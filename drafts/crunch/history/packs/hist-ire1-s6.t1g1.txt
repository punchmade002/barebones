[hist-ire1-s6.0.b] The franchise (the right to vote) decided how much weight tenant farmers could have in politics. Between 1829 and 1850 the Irish electorate was cut, nudged up and then widened in the counties, but voting stayed open and landlords and clergy could still lean on voters.

[hist-ire1-s6.0.1] The Irish Reform Act of 1832 raised the number of Irish seats in the Westminster Parliament, which Irish MPs had joined after the Act of Union (1800), from 100 to 105. It gave the borough vote to £10 householders, but the electorate stayed small beside Britain's.
[hist-ire1-s6.0.2] Voting was open, not secret, until the Ballot Act of 1872 introduced secret voting. Until then landlords, agents and priests could see how people voted and pressure tenants and parishioners, which is why a bigger electorate did not mean a free one.
[hist-ire1-s6.0.3] The Irish Franchise Act of 1850 gave the county vote to occupiers rated at £12 or more and the borough vote to occupiers rated at £8 or more. Being 'rated' means the property was valued for local tax.
[hist-ire1-s6.0.4] The 1850 Act made a big difference in the counties, where the electorate more than doubled, because many tenant farmers now qualified. The borough electorate remained small.
[hist-ire1-s6.0.5] A larger county electorate of tenant farmers helped Tenant League candidates do well at the general election of 1852. Even so, the Famine (1845-50), emigration and eviction had already thinned out the small tenants, so the new voters were a smaller class than before.
[hist-ire1-s6.1.b] Sectarianism, hostility between Catholic and Protestant communities that shapes politics, ran through Irish public life at the end of the 1840s. Orange marches were the most visible sign, and Dolly's Brae showed how quickly they could turn violent.

[hist-ire1-s2.0.b] O'Connell's career after 1829 only makes sense against the settlement he inherited. By 1830 he had won Catholic Emancipation and was now trying to use his popularity to change how Ireland was governed.
[hist-ire1-s2.0.0] The Act of Union, in force from 1 January 1801, abolished the Irish Parliament, so Irish MPs sat at Westminster (the British Parliament in London) and Ireland was governed through Dublin Castle, the seat of the British administration.
[hist-ire1-s2.0.1] The Church of Ireland was the established (state-supported) church, even though most Irish people were Catholic. This is why tithes, a tax for its clergy, became such a bitter grievance in the 1830s.
[hist-ire1-s2.0.2] Catholic Emancipation came with the Roman Catholic Relief Act of 13 April 1829, which let Catholics sit in Parliament. It was won by O'Connell through the Catholic Association (founded 1823), the Catholic Rent (1824) and the Clare election of 1828.
[hist-ire1-s2.0.3] The price of Emancipation was that the county franchise was raised to a £10 freehold, so the 40-shilling freeholders lost the vote. O'Connell, born near Cahersiveen, Co. Kerry, in 1775, was now known as 'the Liberator'.
[hist-ire1-s2.0.4] Two British parties matter here. The Whigs were led by Earl Grey (prime minister 1830-34), Lord Melbourne (1835-41) and later Lord John Russell (from June 1846). The Tories, now called Conservatives, were led by Sir Robert Peel (prime minister 1841-46).
[hist-ire1-s2.1.b] In the 1830s O'Connell led a group of Irish Repeal MPs at Westminster. Repeal (ending the Union and restoring an Irish Parliament under the Crown) got nowhere, so he worked with the Whigs to win practical reforms instead.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.0] Re-elected unopposed for Clare in July 1829, O'Connell took his seat in the Commons in 1830 and gave up his legal practice. Supporters therefore funded him through an annual national collection, the O'Connell Tribute.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.1] He supported Earl Grey's Whig government over parliamentary reform. The Irish Reform Act of 1832 raised Ireland's MPs from 100 to 105 but left the Irish electorate small.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.2] At the 1832 election about 40 MPs pledged to Repeal were returned, yet his Repeal motion of April 1834 was defeated by 523 votes to 38. This showed how little support Repeal had at Westminster.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.3] The Lichfield House Compact (1835) was an informal bargain, not a written treaty. O'Connell and his MPs kept Lord Melbourne's Whigs in office in return for Irish reforms and fair appointments.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.4] Under the Lord Lieutenant Lord Mulgrave, Chief Secretary Lord Morpeth and Under-Secretary Thomas Drummond (1835-1840), Catholics and liberals were appointed to office and stipendiary (paid, professional) magistrates were used. The police were centralised in the Irish Constabulary of 1836.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.5] Drummond told the Tipperary magistrates in 1838 that "property has its duties as well as its rights", a warning to landlords that they could not expect the state to protect them without fulfilling their own obligations.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.6] The Municipal Corporations (Ireland) Act 1840 abolished most of the closed Protestant town corporations (councils that chose their own members). About ten were reformed, with an elected council on a £10 householder franchise.
[hist-ire1-s2.1.7] O'Connell became Lord Mayor of Dublin in 1841, the first Catholic to hold the office since the reign of James II. But the alliance won no Repeal and only limited tithe and municipal reform, so when Peel's Conservatives took office in 1841 he returned to agitation.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.b] Tithe was a tax of about a tenth of agricultural produce, paid in money by the occupiers of land to support the clergy of the Church of Ireland, the church of a small minority. Catholics resented paying for a church they did not attend, and from 1830 they began to refuse.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.0] The Tithe Composition Act of 1823 converted tithe in kind (a share of the crop) into fixed money payments.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.1] The campaign of non-payment began at Graiguenamanagh, Co. Kilkenny, in 1830-31. Farmers organised by the Catholic clergy refused to pay and protected each other's livestock from seizure.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.2] Collectors (tithe proctors) and process servers seized cattle and goods for arrears, escorted by police and soldiers. Crowds gathered at the sound of horns and chapel bells to block them.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.3] At Newtownbarry (Bunclody), Co. Wexford, in June 1831 yeomanry (part-time volunteer cavalry) fired on a crowd resisting a seizure and killed about a dozen people.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.4] At Carrickshock, Co. Kilkenny, on 14 December 1831 a crowd attacked a process server and his police escort, killing the process server and about ten or eleven of the police.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.5] At Gortroe near Rathcormac, Co. Cork, on 18 December 1834 soldiers fired on a crowd trying to stop a seizure from a widow, killing about twelve people.
[hist-ire1-s2.2.6] The protest made tithe uncollectable in much of the south, so arrears mounted and the government had to advance money to clergy left without income.
[hist-ire1-s2.3.b] After years of resistance the government could neither collect tithe nor ignore the problem. The delay came from a split inside the Whig party, and the final settlement changed who paid rather than whether tithe existed.
[hist-ire1-s2.3.0] The Whigs split over appropriation, the idea of using surplus Church of Ireland revenues for non-religious purposes, which O'Connell supported. The disagreement delayed a settlement.
[hist-ire1-s2.3.1] The Tithe Rentcharge Act (August 1838) cut the charge by about a quarter and made landlords responsible for paying it as part of the rent. This removed the direct clash between clergy and tenants, so the open conflict ended.
[hist-ire1-s2.3.2] The settlement had limits: tithe was not abolished but moved into rent, and the Church of Ireland stayed established until disestablishment by the Irish Church Act of 1869 (in effect 1871).
[hist-ire1-s2.4.b] Ireland was a very poor country, but before 1838 it had no national, rate-funded system of poor relief. The Whigs chose to copy England's workhouse system, even though an Irish commission had advised against it.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.0] Before 1838 relief depended on charity, a few houses of industry (institutions for the poor) and county infirmaries, so most of the destitute had no reliable help.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.1] A royal commission chaired by Richard Whately, the Church of Ireland Archbishop of Dublin (1833-1836), argued that Irish poverty was too widespread for workhouses. It recommended assisted emigration, public works and agricultural improvement.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.2] The Whig government rejected the Whately report as too costly. It sent George Nicholls, an English poor law commissioner, who toured Ireland in 1836 and recommended workhouses on the English model.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.3] The Irish Poor Law Act (July 1838) divided Ireland into 130 unions, each with a workhouse run by a board of guardians elected by ratepayers, with local magistrates sitting ex officio (automatically, because of their office).
[hist-ire1-s2.4.4] Relief was given only inside the workhouse (indoor relief). Conditions were made deliberately harsher than the life of the poorest labourer so that only the truly destitute would apply, a rule known as the workhouse test.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.5] Families were separated by sex and age on entry, inmates wore a uniform and worked, and admission carried a stigma, so the poor deeply resented the workhouse.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.6] The cost was met by a poor rate on property, shared between landlord and occupier. From 1843 landlords paid the whole rate on holdings valued at under £4.
[hist-ire1-s2.4.7] The workhouses held about 100,000 people in all and were designed for normal destitution, not mass famine. Most had opened only in the early 1840s, so the system was unready when the potato failed in 1845.
[hist-ire1-s2.5.b] After the Whig alliance disappointed him, O'Connell turned to the method that had won Emancipation: peaceful mass pressure within the law, known as moral force. This time the aim was Repeal, and 1843 was to be the decisive year.
[hist-ire1-s2.5.0] O'Connell founded the Repeal Association in April 1840, with a Repeal Rent of a penny a month. It was modelled on the Catholic Rent.
[hist-ire1-s2.5.1] Repeal meant restoring a separate Irish Parliament under the Crown, not separation from Britain. O'Connell often hinted he would accept a federal compromise or substantial reform instead.
[hist-ire1-s2.5.2] Support grew slowly until Dublin Corporation, led by O'Connell, debated Repeal in early 1843. He then declared 1843 Repeal Year.
[hist-ire1-s2.5.3] The Catholic clergy again acted as local organisers. Father Theobald Mathew's temperance movement, though separate, supplied disciplined crowds and bands.

[hist-ire1-s2.5.6] In May 1843 Peel told the Commons that he would risk any alternative, even war, rather than see the empire dismembered. The government dismissed Repeal magistrates and sent reinforcements to Ireland.

[hist-ire1-s2.6.0] The government banned the monster meeting called for Clontarf on 8 October 1843 only on the evening before (7 October), and troops and artillery occupied the site. Do not confuse this with the medieval battle of Clontarf.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.1] O'Connell cancelled the meeting and sent people home, fearing a massacre. This preserved his moral force policy but exposed the fact that he would not go beyond the law.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.2] He and other leaders, including Charles Gavan Duffy, were arrested in October 1843 and convicted of conspiracy in 1844 by a jury from which Catholics had been excluded.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.3] He was sentenced to a year in prison and a £2,000 fine. He spent about three months in the Richmond Bridewell before the House of Lords quashed the verdict in September 1844.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.4] Peel combined coercion with conciliation: the Devon Commission on land (1843), the Charitable Bequests Act (1844), a larger Maynooth College grant (1845) and the Queen's Colleges (1845). These offered Catholics concessions, so Repeal lost some of its force.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.5] O'Connell was 68 in 1843, and after prison his energy and health declined. He proposed a federal alternative in 1844, which confused supporters because it seemed to give up full Repeal.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.6] From 1845 the Famine, which began when potato blight was first reported that year, destroyed the Repeal Rent and the mass following. O'Connell renewed his alliance with the Whigs when Lord John Russell took office in 1846.
[hist-ire1-s2.6.7] Why Repeal failed: both British parties, Parliament and Protestant Ulster opposed it, and Peel called O'Connell's bluff at Clontarf. Within the movement, it was overwhelmingly Catholic, and the Famine and the Young Ireland split followed.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.b] Young Ireland was a group of mostly young, middle-class writers within the Repeal movement. They developed a cultural idea of the Irish nation, meaning a people bound by shared history, culture and language rather than by religion.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.0] Charles Gavan Duffy, a Catholic journalist from Co. Monaghan, Thomas Davis, a Protestant barrister from Mallow, and John Blake Dillon, a Catholic barrister, founded The Nation, first published on 15 October 1842.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.1] The Nation became one of the most widely read papers in Ireland, passed from hand to hand and read aloud in reading rooms. It published history, ballads and essays.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.2] Its idea of nationality was cultural and non-sectarian: a nation shared history, culture and, ideally, the Irish language, and included Protestants as well as Catholics.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.3] The Library of Ireland, a series of cheap books on Irish history and literature launched by Duffy in 1845, extended the paper's educational work.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.4] Young Ireland disagreed with O'Connell over federalism (1844), over his alliance with the Whigs, and over the Queen's Colleges (1845), which Davis welcomed as mixed education and O'Connell condemned as godless.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.5] The final break came over the peace resolutions of July 1846, which O'Connell proposed so that the Association would declare the use of force never justified, clearing the way for his Whig alliance.
[hist-ire1-s2.7.6] Thomas Francis Meagher's Sword Speech (28 July 1846) refused to condemn force in all circumstances, and Young Ireland walked out of the Repeal Association. Meagher's speech is not to be confused with the peace resolutions it answered.
[hist-ire1-s2.8.b] Having left the Association, the Young Irelanders organised on their own. Events in Europe and a harsher government response pushed them from argument towards rebellion, and this readiness to use force is what is meant by physical force republicanism.
[hist-ire1-s2.8.0] The Young Irelanders founded the Irish Confederation in January 1847, led by William Smith O'Brien, a Protestant landowner and MP, with Duffy and Meagher.
[hist-ire1-s2.8.1] James Fintan Lalor argued in The Nation that the land question should be linked to national independence, so that tenants' grievances would drive the national cause. This idea influenced John Mitchel.
[hist-ire1-s2.8.2] Mitchel broke with the Confederation's moderates and founded the United Irishman in February 1848 to preach open revolution. He urged tenants to refuse rent and resist food exports.
[hist-ire1-s2.8.3] The overthrow of Louis Philippe in Paris in February 1848 encouraged the Confederates to plan a rising, and Smith O'Brien and Meagher went to Paris with an address of support.
[hist-ire1-s2.8.4] Mitchel was convicted under the new Treason Felony Act (April 1848) in May 1848 and transported for fourteen years. In July 1848 the government suspended habeas corpus, the right not to be held without trial, forcing the leaders into a hasty rising.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.b] The 1848 rising was small and quickly over, but it mattered for what it passed on. It linked Young Ireland to the physical force tradition (the belief that independence needs armed rebellion) later taken up by the Fenians.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.0] On 29 July 1848 Smith O'Brien's small band besieged police in the Widow McCormack's house at Ballingarry, Co. Tipperary. About two rebels were killed and the rest dispersed.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.1] Reasons for failure: no preparation or weapons, a starving and demoralised population, strong opposition from the Catholic clergy, government foreknowledge, and leaders who would not risk lives or property.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.2] Smith O'Brien, Meagher, Terence MacManus and Patrick O'Donohoe were sentenced to death at Clonmel in 1848. The sentences were commuted (reduced) to transportation to Van Diemen's Land, while Dillon escaped to America.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.3] James Stephens, wounded at Ballingarry, and John O'Mahony, who also took part in the rising, escaped to France. They later founded the Fenian movement, so 1848 became a link in the physical force tradition.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.4] Young Ireland's other legacy was cultural: The Nation, the Library of Ireland and the ballads shaped the later idea of the Irish nation.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.b] O'Connell, a barrister born in Co. Kerry in 1775 and known as the Liberator, spent his last seventeen years trying to turn the mass movement that won Emancipation into political change. The years covered here are 1830-47 only.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.0] Aims after 1829: Repeal of the Union, the end of tithes, municipal reform, fair government and a share of office for Catholics, all by constitutional means (moral force).
[hist-ire1-s2.10.1] Participant: he led the Irish party at Westminster, made the Lichfield House Compact, founded the Repeal Association, called Repeal Year and decided to cancel Clontarf.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.2] He was shaped by events as much as he shaped them: Peel's firmness in 1843, the Famine from 1845 and Young Ireland's challenge set the limits of what he could achieve.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.3] Strengths, for example: an unrivalled orator and organiser, master of peaceful mass agitation, skilled parliamentarian and lawyer, and committed to religious liberty for all, including Jews and dissenters, and to the abolition of slavery.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.4] Weaknesses, such as his reliance on bluff, his willingness to drop Repeal for Whig favours, rigid control of the movement, the patronage given to his family, and a Catholic-centred movement that alienated Protestants.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.5] He died at Genoa on 15 May 1847 on his way to Rome. His heart was buried in Rome and his body in Glasnevin Cemetery, Dublin.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.6] Contemporaries: to Catholic Ireland he was the Liberator; to the Times and British Tories he was a dangerous agitator; to Young Ireland in 1846 he was a tired leader who had sold Repeal to the Whigs.
[hist-ire1-s2.10.7] Historians: Oliver MacDonagh, in a two-volume biography (1988 and 1989), sees him as a founder of mass democratic politics. Critics note that Repeal failed and that his movement deepened the link between Catholicism and nationalism.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.b] Davis was the thinker of Young Ireland. He was a witness to and participant in the Repeal movement through ideas and writing rather than a political leader, and he died before the Famine and the split.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.0] Born on 14 October 1814 in Mallow, Co. Cork, the son of an army surgeon, Davis was a Protestant who studied at Trinity College Dublin and was called to the bar in 1838.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.1] He joined the Repeal Association in 1841 and co-founded The Nation in 1842, becoming its leading writer.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.2] His essays argued for a nationality above religion, for the Irish language, for Irish history and for self-reliance. He wrote in 1843 that "a people without a language of its own is only half a nation".
[hist-ire1-s2.11.3] His ballads, including A Nation Once Again and The West's Asleep, made romantic nationalism popular among the young.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.4] He backed mixed, non-denominational education and welcomed the Queen's Colleges in 1845, which led to a bitter public clash with O'Connell.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.5] He died of scarlet fever on 16 September 1845, aged 30, before the Famine took hold and before the peace resolutions split the movement. He never saw the split or the rising.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.6] He shaped the movement through ideas, and was shaped by it: his secular views brought him into conflict with O'Connell's Repeal Association. Contemporaries such as Duffy, his friend, revered him as the soul of Young Ireland, while O'Connell's Catholic supporters distrusted his mixed-education views.
[hist-ire1-s2.11.7] Historians see him as the founder of cultural nationalism, whose ideas the Gaelic League generation drew on, and note that his ideal of a nation uniting Protestant and Catholic was never realised.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.b] Examiners reward a clear verdict. The achievements and limits below are paired so each can be turned into a judgement.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.0] O'Connell's achievement: he created mass constitutional politics, won practical reforms with the Whigs and gave Irish Catholics political confidence.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.1] O'Connell's limit: Repeal, his central aim after 1829, failed completely, and the movement collapsed after Clontarf, his ill health and the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.2] Tithe War achievement: popular resistance forced the 1838 settlement. Limit: tithe was only shifted into rent, and the established Church survived until 1869.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.3] Poor Law achievement: Ireland gained a national relief system and local elected boards. Limit: the workhouse model failed when the Famine created mass rather than individual destitution.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.4] Young Ireland's achievement: The Nation created a cultural, non-sectarian idea of the nation and a body of history and ballads that shaped later nationalism.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.5] Young Ireland's limit: it split the national movement in 1846, its rising of 1848 was a fiasco, and its non-sectarian ideal won few Protestants.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.6] Contemporary and later views: to Catholic Ireland O'Connell was the Liberator but Young Ireland saw a tired leader; Davis was revered by Duffy, while historians now see him as the founder of cultural nationalism.
[hist-ire1-s2.12.7] Contribution to nationalism: O'Connell left the constitutional tradition later taken up by Butt and Parnell; Young Ireland left the cultural and physical force traditions taken up by the Fenians and the Gaelic revival.

CANDIDATES hist-ire1-s4: 4. Private responses to Famine and the Famine's impact
[hist-ire1-s4.0.b] The potato blight that struck from September 1845 left many people without food, and the government's response was limited. Private relief, meaning help from individuals, committees and charities rather than the state, grew up to fill the gaps.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.0] Government relief was limited by laissez-faire (the belief that the state should not interfere in the economy) and by the rule that Irish property should pay for Irish poverty, so charity filled gaps in food, clothing, seed and medical care.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.1] Private relief came from Irish local committees, landlords, the churches, the Society of Friends (Quakers), British charities, emigrants and donors across the world, so it was never the work of one group alone.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.2] Under Peel in 1845–1846, local relief committees of landlords, clergy and merchants raised subscriptions, and the government added grants to the sums they collected.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.3] The worst-hit western districts had the fewest resident gentry and merchants, so the areas that most needed local charity could raise the least.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.4] Newspaper reports and drawings from places such as Skibbereen, Co. Cork, in the winter of 1846–1847, especially in the Illustrated London News, made the Famine known in Britain and America and set off the great appeals of 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.b] The Society of Friends, a small Christian group known for plain living and charity, ran the most trusted private relief effort. Its success came from practical help, honest reporting and a refusal to use food to win converts.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.0] Irish Quakers, a small community of prosperous merchants, set up the Central Relief Committee in Dublin on 13 November 1846, with Joseph Bewley and Jonathan Pim among its secretaries.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.1] English Quakers formed a London committee. William Forster toured the west in the winter of 1846–1847 and published letters and reports, and James Hack Tuke also visited and wrote about the west in 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.2] Their published letters and reports described cabins full of the dying, so British readers gained the most trusted eyewitness accounts of the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.3] The Friends opened soup kitchens in Cork and elsewhere in the winter of 1846–1847, and gave boilers, food and money to local committees regardless of religion.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.4] For example, American Quakers and other donors sent food shipments to the Committee, which distributed them through its network of local correspondents.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.5] The Friends refused to proselytise (try to convert people) and kept careful accounts, which earned them trust among Catholics and lasting gratitude.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.6] In 1847, after the government's soup kitchens opened, they withdrew from mass feeding, concluding that only the government had the resources, and turned to longer-term projects: seed distribution, fisheries and spinning schemes.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.7] Their Transactions of the Central Relief Committee (1852) became a key source on conditions and relief because it recorded their work in detail.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.b] Where the Quakers worked through a religious network, the British Relief Association raised money from the wider British public, and donors abroad sent aid too. Together they showed that the Famine had become a world news story.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.0] The British Relief Association (formally the British Association for the Relief of Extreme Distress in Ireland and Scotland) was founded in London on 1 January 1847 by bankers and merchants including Lionel de Rothschild, to raise funds for Ireland and the Scottish Highlands.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.1] A royal letter from Queen Victoria was read in churches in January 1847, the Queen gave £2,000, and the national day of fast and humiliation on 24 March 1847 brought in large collections.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.2] The Association raised about £470,000, among the largest of the private funds, and worked closely with the government so that the two systems of relief supported each other.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.3] So that aid reached the west, its agent, the Polish explorer Count Paul Strzelecki, worked from Westport in the west and used the funds to feed schoolchildren in the poorest unions in 1847–1848.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.4] American relief included two warships lent by Congress, the Jamestown, which reached Cork in April 1847, and the Macedonian, which carried food later in 1847. Both were loaded with food raised by public subscription.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.5] Donations also came from Irish emigrants in America and Britain, from India, Australia and Canada, from the Choctaw Nation in the United States and from the Ottoman Sultan, Abdulmejid.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.6] Because many Irish were Catholic, Pope Pius IX issued the encyclical (formal letter to the Church) Praedecessores Nostros in March 1847, calling for prayers and collections for Ireland, and Catholic communities in Europe and America sent money.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.b] Clergy lived among the starving and so were drawn into relief and suffered with their people. Yet religious rivalry also poisoned some charity, leaving a bitter memory known as souperism.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.0] Because priests lived among the poor, Catholic priests sat on relief committees, wrote public letters describing conditions, ran collections and buried the dead; many died of fever.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.1] Church of Ireland clergy, often the only resident educated men in poor parishes, were the backbone of many local committees and also died in large numbers.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.2] Religious orders such as the Sisters of Charity and the Sisters of Mercy nursed fever victims and fed the poor in towns.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.3] Souperism was the charge that some evangelical Protestants (those who stressed spreading the Gospel and converting others) offered food, clothing or schooling only to those who attended Protestant services or sent children to Protestant schools.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.4] Proselytising missions had begun before the Famine, as at Edward Nangle's Achill colony founded in 1834, and the Irish Church Missions, founded by Rev. Alexander Dallas in 1849, expanded them in Connemara during and after the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.5] Most Protestant relief, including the Quakers' and most Church of Ireland committees', was given without religious conditions, but the actions of a few tainted Protestant charity as a whole.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.6] The legacy was lasting bitterness: taking the soup became a mark of shame in Catholic memory, converts were often ostracised, and the issue sharpened sectarian division.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.b] Landlords owned the land and paid the poor rate (the local tax for relief of the poor), so the Famine hit their incomes as well as their tenants. Their responses ranged from generous relief to mass eviction.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.0] Some landlords reduced or cancelled rents, imported food, set up soup kitchens and employed tenants, and a number of them were ruined by the cost or died of fever.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.1] Others, burdened by debt, rent arrears and poor rates, cleared their estates. They used the Gregory clause (which denied relief to anyone holding more than a quarter acre) and the rule that landlords paid the rates on holdings valued under £4, which made small tenants expensive to keep.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.2] Mass evictions were recorded especially in Clare, Mayo and Galway; in the Kilrush union, Co. Clare, the poor law inspector Captain Arthur Kennedy reported the destruction of whole townlands.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.3] Police returns, which began in 1849, recorded tens of thousands of families evicted between 1849 and 1854, so the scale of clearance can be measured.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.4] Assisted emigration meant the landlord paid for tenants to leave. Major Denis Mahon of Strokestown, Co. Roscommon, assisted about 1,500 tenants to Canada in 1847; many died on the voyage or in quarantine, and Mahon was shot dead in November 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.5] Larger schemes followed, such as Lord Lansdowne's assisted emigration from his Kenmare estate, Co. Kerry, in the early 1850s, and many landlords found shipping tenants cheaper than supporting them in the workhouse.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.b] For many families the only escape from hunger was to leave. Emigration was both a private response, paid for by tenants, relatives or landlords, and one of the Famine's lasting results.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.0] Over one million people left Ireland between 1846 and 1851, mostly for the United States, Britain and Canada, with some going to Australia.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.1] The poorest, who could afford only the cheapest passage, went to Britain and Canada.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.2] Conditions on the crowded sailing ships were so bad, with typhus (a deadly fever spread by lice) breaking out among passengers, that they were called 'coffin ships', and many died on the voyage.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.3] In 1847 ships bound for Canada were held in quarantine (isolation to stop disease spreading) at Grosse Île near Quebec, where many more died.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.4] Emigration was assisted when a landlord or other body paid the passage, as Major Mahon did in 1847, and unassisted when families paid for themselves.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.5] Money and prepaid tickets sent home by the first emigrants paid for the passage of later ones, which is how Famine emigration grew into a habit.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.b] Asenath Nicholson was an American woman who gave relief on her own account and wrote down what she saw. She matters because she is both a participant in relief and one of the best eyewitnesses of the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.0] Born Asenath Hatch in 1792 in Chelsea, Vermont, she was a teacher who, as a widow in New York, worked among the Irish poor and promoted temperance (avoiding alcohol) and a vegetarian diet.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.1] She first came to Ireland in 1844 and walked through almost every county, staying in cabins and reading the Bible to the people she met.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.2] Her account of that tour, Ireland's Welcome to the Stranger (1847), described the poverty and the dependence on the potato before the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.3] She returned in 1846 to give relief, set up a soup kitchen in Dublin's Liberties, then travelled in the west, including Mayo and Erris, distributing food and clothing paid for by appeals in New York papers.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.4] As participant and witness, she gave relief directly and independently rather than through a committee, and recorded what she saw at first hand.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.5] Her Lights and Shades of Ireland (1850), whose Famine section was published as Annals of the Famine in Ireland (1851), condemned landlords, officials and proselytisers alike.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.6] Contemporaries found her eccentric: some officials and clergy suspected her as a Protestant Bible reader, while the poor welcomed her; she left Ireland in 1848 and died in Jersey City in 1855.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.7] Historians now value her as one of the most vivid and sympathetic eyewitnesses of the Famine, a woman relief worker who blamed the system rather than its victims.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.b] Judged against the size of the disaster, private relief did real good but could not do enough, and it faded just when need was greatest. Explaining that fall is as important as describing what it achieved.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.0] Private relief saved many thousands of lives in 1846–1847, especially through Quaker soup kitchens and the British Relief Association's feeding of children.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.1] It fed people faster than government schemes and reached remote places, so it saved lives that official schemes missed, and its reports exposed the scale of suffering to the public.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.2] It could not match the scale of the crisis: total private giving was small beside the government's spending of about £7 million.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.3] Giving fell sharply after 1847 because of donor fatigue (people tiring of appeals), the British commercial crisis of 1847, the belief that the Famine was over, and anger at the Young Ireland rising and at Irish ingratitude in 1848.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.4] The Quakers concluded that private charity could not replace the state and withdrew from mass feeding, so by 1848 relief depended on the Poor Law.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.b] The Famine changed who lived in Ireland and who owned and worked the land. The poorest classes were swept away, and farms and estates were reshaped.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.0] Population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.6 million in 1851 and about 5.4 million in 1871, because of death and emigration.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.1] The cottier (a tenant with a tiny plot) and labourer classes were the hardest hit by death and emigration, and the potato-dependent class largely disappeared.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.2] Consolidation, the joining of small holdings into larger farms, followed: holdings of 1 to 5 acres fell sharply in number, while farms over 15 acres increased, as landlords and surviving farmers took over the vacated land.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.3] The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) set up a court to sell estates whose owners were bankrupt; by 1857 about 3,000 estates, very roughly 5 million acres or a fifth to a quarter of the land, had been sold.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.4] Most buyers were Irish land speculators and businessmen rather than the British capitalists the government hoped for, and many new landlords raised rents and cleared tenants more readily than the old.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.5] Agriculture shifted from tillage (growing crops) to pasture (grazing): cattle and sheep needed less labour and rising British demand made them more profitable.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.b] Fewer, larger farms changed family life. Because the farm now had to stay in one piece, marriage, inheritance and emigration all followed from it, and the Famine's memory shaped language and politics too.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.0] Subdivision (splitting the farm among sons) ended: farmers now passed the whole farm to one son, while the other children emigrated, entered the Church or married into other farms.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.1] Marriage came later and fewer people married, because a son had to wait to inherit the farm; the arranged match between farming families became the norm.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.2] The dowry (money or stock the bride's family gave the groom's family) became central to the match, and was often used to give a dowry to his sister.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.3] Emigration became a habit: remittances (money sent home) and prepaid tickets from relatives abroad paid the passage of later emigrants.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.4] The Irish language declined fastest, because its heartlands in the west and south lost most people; the 1851 census recorded about 1.5 million speakers, and English became the language of advancement.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.5] The Famine left lasting hatred of landlordism (the system of landlord ownership) and of British government among Irish Americans and at home, which fed Fenianism and, later, the Land War.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.6] The Catholic Church gained from the changed society: the surviving farming class was more devout and better able to support priests, nuns and church building.
[hist-ire1-s4.10.b] A good judgement weighs what private relief did against what it could not do, and separates the Famine's immediate impact from its long-term effects.
[hist-ire1-s4.10.0] Achievement of private relief: speed, compassion and practical aid, above all by the Quakers, whose non-sectarian work is still remembered with respect.
[hist-ire1-s4.10.1] Limit of private relief: it was too small, short-lived and unevenly spread, weakest where it was most needed, and shrank after 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.10.2] Damage: souperism and landlord clearances meant that some private responses deepened suffering and division rather than relieving them.
[hist-ire1-s4.10.3] Impact during the Famine: about a million deaths, mass eviction and flight; impact after: a smaller, older, more commercial and more Catholic-devout rural society.
[hist-ire1-s4.10.4] Contemporary British opinion often saw the Famine as an opportunity to modernise Irish agriculture; Irish nationalists, led by John Mitchel, saw it as a crime of British government. Mitchel wrote: 'The Almighty, indeed, sent the potato blight, but the English created the famine.'
[hist-ire1-s4.10.5] Historians stress continuity as well as change: consolidation, pasture and emigration had begun before 1845, but the Famine greatly speeded them up.

CANDIDATES hist-ire1-s5: 5. Post-Famine Ireland: economy and society, 1850–1870
[hist-ire1-s5.0.b] The Great Famine (1845 to about 1850, caused by potato blight) killed and drove out about a quarter of the population and devastated the cottiers, the poorest tenants who rented tiny plots. Before it, Ireland had depended on the potato, and holdings had been subdivided into ever smaller plots. What was left was a smaller, more commercial farming economy built on livestock.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.0] Population fell steadily: about 8.2 million in 1841, about 6.5 million in 1851, about 5.8 million in 1861 and about 5.4 million in 1871. Deaths and emigration in the Famine started the fall, and it did not stop.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.1] Tillage (growing crops) declined and pasture (grass for animals) expanded, because cattle, sheep and dairy produce rose in price relative to grain and needed far less labour. The repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 had already pushed grain prices down relative to livestock prices.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.2] Live cattle were exported to Britain, where the growing towns were the market. The trade was carried by the new railways and steamships, which is why a pasture economy paid so well after the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.3] Small holdings disappeared as cottiers died or emigrated and landlords joined their plots into larger farms. The number of cottiers and tiny holdings fell sharply, and larger farms became the norm.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.4] Agricultural labourers fell in number because pasture needed fewer hands, yet those who remained were still poor. The small farmers of the west stayed close to subsistence, meaning they grew barely enough to feed their families.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.b] Not everyone lost from the Famine. A class of stronger farmers, holding large farms, gained most, while the landlords (a small class who owned almost all the land and let it to tenants) kept their power.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.0] The stronger farmers held large farms on long tenure (a secure, long-lasting right to farm the land), so they could profit from rising livestock prices. They were the main gainers of the period.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.1] They dominated parish life and later supplied much of the support for land and nationalist movements, because they had both the wealth and the standing to lead local opinion.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.2] The Encumbered Estates Act (1849), a Famine-era measure, let debt-ridden estates be sold off. The Landed Estates Court (1858) replaced the Encumbered Estates Court and continued the sales, so do not confuse the two.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.3] Some buyers were commercial investors who raised rents and cleared tenants; others were existing landlords or wealthy tenants.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.4] Landlordism survived the Famine: landlords still owned almost all the land, and tenants had no legal security of tenure (no legal right to stay on the farm so long as they paid rent). The Land Act of 1870 gave only limited protection.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.5] Evictions fell from their Famine peak but remained a threat, so tenant right (the tenants' claim to security and to compensation for their improvements) stayed a central grievance of the 1850s and 1860s.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.b] The Famine hardened a pattern of late marriage, celibacy and single-heir inheritance. It kept farms intact, and it pushed other children abroad.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.0] A dowry was money or land that a bride's family gave on her marriage. Farmers used it to portion (provide for) a sister, to buy out siblings or to pay debts.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.1] Farms usually passed intact to one son, so a farmer could marry only when his father handed over the holding and a bride with a dowry was found.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.2] Matchmaking was widespread, so families arranged the match, and it depended on land and money more than on affection.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.3] Marriage was late, often in the thirties, and a large share of men and women never married (celibacy), especially in the west. The wait for the farm and the need for a dowry caused this.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.4] Younger sons and daughters without a dowry had to choose between emigration, farm labour, the priesthood or religious life, and domestic service.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.5] Late marriage and celibacy kept the birth rate down, so the population did not recover after the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.b] Large-scale emigration continued long after the Famine and became part of normal life. Here the focus is its scale, who left and its effects on those who stayed.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.0] About a million people left Ireland in the 1850s, and emigration stayed high through the 1860s.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.1] The United States was the main destination, followed by Britain, Canada and Australia. Liverpool and Cork (Queenstown, now Cobh) were major ports of departure.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.2] Emigrants were mostly young and unmarried, and women left in numbers roughly equal to men. This is why the countryside lost its future workers and parents.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.3] Chain migration meant that relatives and neighbours already abroad sent news and prepaid passages, so each emigrant helped the next to follow.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.4] Remittances (money sent home) helped families pay rent and passage costs for further relatives. Chain migration is the mechanism and remittances are the money.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.5] Emigration relieved pressure on land, but it drained the countryside of young people and fed the idea of exile in nationalist politics (the belief that people had been forced out by British rule).
[hist-ire1-s5.4.b] The national school system, set up in 1831, became the main provider of primary education and a major influence on literacy and language.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.0] The system was set up in 1831 through a letter from the Chief Secretary, Edward Stanley, to the Duke of Leinster. It was run by a Board of Commissioners of National Education.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.1] Its aim was combined literary and moral education for children of all denominations, with religious instruction given separately by each church.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.2] The state paid grants, supplied textbooks and inspected schools, and a teacher-training establishment opened in Dublin.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.3] The churches contested the plan. The Church of Ireland set up its own Church Education Society (1839), and Presbyterians objected at first.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.4] The Catholic bishops were divided: Archbishop Daniel Murray of Dublin co-operated, while Archbishop John MacHale of Tuam opposed.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.5] In practice most schools became denominational in character, because local clergy managed them and most pupils in each district were of one faith. After 1850 Cardinal Cullen pressed for greater Catholic control of education.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.b] Alongside the national schools, other bodies taught the poor, and the language of the classroom had a lasting effect on Irish.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.0] The Christian Brothers, founded in Waterford in 1802 by Edmund Rice, ran their own free schools for poor Catholic boys. They stayed outside the national system and spread in the towns.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.1] Do not confuse the two: the national schools were the state-backed primary system of 1831, while the Brothers' schools were a separate Catholic network.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.2] Lessons were in English, so Irish had no place in school. The system reinforced the decline of the Irish language by teaching through English and treating Irish as no help to a career.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.3] School attendance and literacy rose steadily, though no reliable figure for the number of pupils should be given.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.b] Higher education divided on religious lines. The state founded colleges that were open to all, the Catholic Church objected, and a rival Catholic institution followed.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.0] Peel's government founded the Queen's Colleges at Belfast, Cork and Galway by an Act of 1845. They opened in 1849 and formed the Queen's University of Ireland in 1850.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.1] They were non-denominational and had no chapel or chair of theology, so critics called them 'godless colleges'.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.2] The Catholic hierarchy was divided, but Rome condemned the colleges in 1847 and the Synod of Thurles in 1850 forbade Catholics to attend. Cullen backed a Catholic alternative.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.3] The Catholic University opened in Dublin in 1854 with John Henry Newman as first rector. It gave no state-recognised degrees and had few students, and Newman resigned in 1858.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.4] Trinity College Dublin, an Anglican institution, remained the main university for Protestants. Catholics could take degrees but not fellowships.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.5] The Queen's College in Belfast served the Presbyterian middle class of Ulster, which is why it was the most successful of the three.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.b] The railway network was built from 1834 and its core was largely complete by about 1860. It changed trade, towns and communications.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.0] The Dublin and Kingstown Railway opened on 17 December 1834 and ran about six miles from Westland Row in Dublin to Kingstown (now Dún Laoghaire). It was the first railway in Ireland.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.1] Growth began slowly and then became rapid in the late 1840s and 1850s: the network reached about 1,000 miles by the mid-1850s and the main network was complete by about 1860.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.2] The Great Southern and Western reached Cork in 1849 and the Midland Great Western reached Galway in 1851. The Killarney line opened in 1853.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.3] The Dublin to Belfast through route was completed in 1855, so the two cities were joined, crossing the Boyne by the viaduct at Drogheda.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.4] Ireland adopted a gauge (the distance between the rails) of 5 feet 3 inches, fixed in the 1840s, which was different from the British standard gauge.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.b] Railways mattered because they made moving goods and people cheaper and faster. Their effects were uneven, which is the mark of a good answer.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.0] Railways cut the cost and time of moving cattle, butter, grain and fish. They tied farmers to British markets through the ports.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.1] Fairs and markets shifted to railway towns, and junction towns such as Mallow, Limerick Junction and Athlone grew in importance.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.2] Some small ports lost trade as goods moved inland by rail instead of by coast.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.3] Newspapers, mail and ideas travelled faster, so national newspapers reached the provinces and political movements could organise nationally.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.4] Tourism grew, for example with the Killarney line opening in 1853. The rail and steamer link to Britain also eased travel and emigration.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.b] Dargan was the most successful Irish railway contractor of his day and a public figure who promoted Irish industry. His career rose with the railway boom and ended with the failure of some of his ventures and a fall from his horse in 1866.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.0] Born in 1799 near Carlow town, he trained as a surveyor and engineer in England and worked under Thomas Telford on the Holyhead road.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.1] He won the contract for the Dublin and Kingstown Railway, which opened in 1834, and went on to build lines including the Dublin and Drogheda and the Ulster Railway. He became the leading contractor in Ireland and employed thousands of workers.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.2] He also built the Ulster Canal and dredged the Victoria Channel at Belfast in the 1840s. The spoil created Queen's Island, later the Harland and Wolff site.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.3] In 1853 he paid for the Dublin Industrial Exhibition on Leinster Lawn, which drew over a million visitors, and Queen Victoria visited it. Do not confuse it with the Great Exhibition in London (1851).
[hist-ire1-s5.9.4] He lost money on the Exhibition, and his reputation as an Irish industrial pioneer was the main reward. He reportedly refused a baronetcy.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.5] A fall from a horse in 1866 and the failure of some of his ventures ended his career. He died in Dublin on 7 February 1867.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.6] Contemporaries praised him as a self-made Catholic businessman who acted from public spirit. A Dargan testimonial fund helped to fund the National Gallery of Ireland, which opened in 1864, and his statue stands outside it.
[hist-ire1-s5.9.7] Historians see him as a symbol of Irish enterprise, but they note limits: his work rested on railway investment and not on a wider Irish industrial base.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.b] Belfast became an industrial city of linen, engineering and shipbuilding, unlike the largely agricultural south. The key is to link each cause to its result.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.0] Mechanised flax spinning arrived in the 1820s and 1830s, and linen became the main industry of Belfast and the Lagan valley.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.1] The American Civil War (1861–65) cut off cotton supplies, a crisis called the cotton famine, so demand and prices for linen rose.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.2] Harbour works, including Dargan's Victoria Channel, allowed larger ships to reach the port, and engineering firms grew up to serve the mills.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.3] Edward Harland took over Robert Hickson's shipyard on Queen's Island in 1858, and in 1861 he and Gustav Wolff formed Harland and Wolff.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.4] Ulster had skilled Protestant labour, capital from the linen trade, local business networks and strong trade links with Britain.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.5] Belfast had no local coal or iron, but cheap imports by sea and rail made industry viable.
[hist-ire1-s5.10.6] Belfast's population grew from about 100,000 in 1851 to over 170,000 by 1871, with migrants coming mainly from the Ulster countryside and Famine-hit areas.
[hist-ire1-s5.11.b] Industry did not spread across the island, and Belfast's growth brought tensions of its own.
[hist-ire1-s5.11.0] Industry in the south declined under competition from British factory goods once tariffs were gone. Dublin and Cork kept only brewing, distilling and food processing.
[hist-ire1-s5.11.1] Catholics, mostly migrants from the countryside, made up about a third of Belfast's population. They were concentrated in unskilled jobs and in certain districts such as the Pound.
[hist-ire1-s5.11.2] Sectarian riots broke out in Belfast in 1857 and 1864, tied to Orange marches and to Catholic rivalry for housing and work.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.b] Judgements differ on whether post-Famine Ireland was a story of recovery or of continuing weakness. Good answers pair each gain with its limit.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.0] Achievement: farm incomes and living standards of the stronger farmers rose, and railways opened national markets.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.1] Limit: the population kept falling, emigration continued and the rural poor of the west gained little.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.2] Achievement: the national school system spread literacy to most of the population.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.3] Limit: schooling in English weakened the Irish language.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.4] Achievement: Belfast industrialised and grew faster than any other Irish town.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.5] Limit: industrialisation was confined to the north-east, and the rest of Ireland depended on agriculture and on exporting people.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.6] Contemporary view: many saw the stronger farmers' prosperity and the railways as evidence of improvement under the Union.
[hist-ire1-s5.12.7] Later view: historians argue that the Famine made Ireland a more commercial but more unequal and emigrant society, with a prosperous farmer class above a shrinking labouring poor.
