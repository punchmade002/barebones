[hist-ire1-s2.9.5] Young Ireland's limit: it split the national movement in 1846, its rising of 1848 was a fiasco, and its non-sectarian ideal won few Protestants.
[hist-ire1-s2.9.6] Contribution to nationalism: O'Connell left the constitutional tradition later taken up by Butt and Parnell; Young Ireland left the cultural and physical force traditions taken up by the Fenians and the Gaelic revival.

CANDIDATES hist-ire1-s4: 4. Private responses to Famine and the Famine's impact
[hist-ire1-s4.0.0] Government relief was limited by laissez-faire and by the rule that Irish property should pay, so charity filled gaps in food, clothing, seed and medical care.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.1] Private relief came from Irish local committees, landlords, the churches, the Society of Friends, British charities, emigrants and donors across the world.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.2] Under Peel in 1845–1846 local relief committees of landlords, clergy and merchants raised subscriptions, and the government added grants to the sums they collected.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.3] The worst-hit western districts had the fewest resident gentry and merchants, so the areas that most needed local charity could raise the least.
[hist-ire1-s4.0.4] Newspaper reports and drawings from places such as Skibbereen, Co. Cork, in the winter of 1846–1847, especially in the Illustrated London News, made the Famine known in Britain and America and set off the great appeals of 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.0] Irish Quakers, a small community of prosperous merchants, set up the Central Relief Committee in Dublin on 13 November 1846, with Joseph Bewley and Jonathan Pim among its secretaries.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.1] English Quakers formed a London committee, and William Forster and others, including the young James Hack Tuke, toured the west in the winter of 1846–1847 and reported on what they saw.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.2] Their published letters and reports described cabins full of the dying and gave British readers the most trusted eyewitness accounts of the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.3] The Friends opened soup kitchens in Cork and elsewhere in the winter of 1846–1847, and gave boilers, food and money to local committees regardless of religion.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.4] American Quakers and other donors sent food shipments to the Committee, which distributed them through its network of local correspondents.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.5] The Friends refused to proselytise and kept careful accounts, which earned them trust among Catholics and lasting gratitude.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.6] From mid-1847 they withdrew from mass feeding, concluding that only the government had the resources, and turned to longer-term projects: seed distribution, fisheries and spinning schemes.
[hist-ire1-s4.1.7] Their Transactions of the Central Relief Committee (1852) became a key source on conditions and relief.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.0] The British Relief Association was founded in London on 1 January 1847 by bankers and merchants including Lionel de Rothschild to raise funds for Ireland and the Scottish Highlands.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.1] A royal letter from Queen Victoria was read in churches in January 1847, the Queen gave £2,000, and the national day of fast and humiliation in March 1847 brought in large collections.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.2] The Association raised about £470,000, the largest single private fund, and worked closely with the government, distributing food through Routh's commissariat.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.3] Its agent, the Polish explorer Count Paul Strzelecki, worked in Westport and the west and used the funds to feed schoolchildren in the poorest unions in 1847–1848.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.4] American relief included two warships lent by Congress, the Jamestown, which reached Cork in April 1847, and the Macedonian, both loaded with food raised by public subscription.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.5] Donations also came from Irish emigrants in America and Britain, from India, Australia and Canada, from the Choctaw Nation in the United States and from the Ottoman Sultan.
[hist-ire1-s4.2.6] Pope Pius IX issued the encyclical Praedecessores Nostros in March 1847, calling for prayers and collections for Ireland, and Catholic communities in Europe and America sent money.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.0] Catholic priests sat on relief committees, wrote public letters describing conditions, ran collections and buried the dead; many died of fever.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.1] Church of Ireland clergy, often the only resident educated men in poor parishes, were the backbone of many local committees and also died in large numbers.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.2] Religious orders such as the Sisters of Charity and the Sisters of Mercy nursed fever victims and fed the poor in towns.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.3] Souperism was the charge that some evangelical Protestants offered food, clothing or schooling only to those who attended Protestant services or sent children to Protestant schools.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.4] Proselytising missions had begun before the Famine, as at Edward Nangle's Achill colony from the 1830s, and the Irish Church Missions founded by Rev. Alexander Dallas in 1849 expanded them in Connemara during and after the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.5] Most Protestant relief, including the Quakers' and most Church of Ireland committees', was given without religious conditions, but the actions of a few tainted Protestant charity as a whole.
[hist-ire1-s4.3.6] The legacy was lasting bitterness: taking the soup became a mark of shame in Catholic memory, converts were often ostracised, and the issue sharpened sectarian division.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.0] Some landlords reduced or cancelled rents, imported food, set up soup kitchens and employed tenants, and a number of them were ruined by the cost or died of fever.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.1] Others, burdened by debt, rent arrears and poor rates, cleared their estates, using the Gregory clause and the rule that landlords paid the rates on holdings under £4.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.2] Mass evictions were recorded especially in Clare, Mayo and Galway; in the Kilrush union, Co. Clare, the poor law inspector Captain Arthur Kennedy reported the destruction of whole townlands.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.3] Police returns, which began in 1849, recorded tens of thousands of families evicted between 1849 and 1854.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.4] Assisted emigration: Major Denis Mahon of Strokestown, Co. Roscommon, paid passage to Canada for about 1,500 tenants in 1847; many died on the voyage or in quarantine, and Mahon was shot dead in November 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.4.5] Larger schemes followed, such as Lord Lansdowne's assisted emigration from his Kenmare estate, Co. Kerry, in the early 1850s, and many landlords found shipping tenants cheaper than supporting them in the workhouse.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.0] Born Asenath Hatch on 24 February 1792 in Chelsea, Vermont, she was a teacher who, as a widow in New York, worked among the Irish poor and promoted temperance and a vegetarian diet.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.1] She first came to Ireland in May 1844 and walked through almost every county, staying in cabins and reading the Bible to the people she met.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.2] Her account of that tour, Ireland's Welcome to the Stranger (1847), described the poverty and the dependence on the potato before the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.3] She returned in 1846 to give relief, set up a soup kitchen in Dublin's Liberties, then travelled to Ulster, Mayo, Erris and Arranmore, distributing food and clothing paid for by appeals in New York papers.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.4] Participant and witness: she gave relief directly and independently rather than through a committee, and recorded what she saw at first hand.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.5] Her Lights and Shades of Ireland (1850), whose Famine section was published as Annals of the Famine in Ireland (1851), condemned landlords, officials and proselytisers alike.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.6] Contemporaries found her eccentric: some officials and clergy suspected her as a Protestant Bible reader, while the poor welcomed her; she left Ireland in 1848 and died in Jersey City in 1855.
[hist-ire1-s4.5.7] Historians now value her as one of the most vivid and sympathetic eyewitnesses of the Famine, a woman relief worker who blamed the system rather than its victims.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.0] Private relief saved many thousands of lives in 1846–1847, especially through Quaker soup kitchens and the British Relief Association's feeding of children.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.1] It fed people faster than government schemes and reached remote places, and its reports exposed the scale of suffering to the public.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.2] It could not match the scale of the crisis: total private giving was a small fraction of the roughly £7 million spent by the government, about half of it as loans.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.3] Giving fell sharply after 1847 because of donor fatigue, the British commercial crisis of 1847, the belief that the Famine was over, and anger at the Young Ireland rising and at Irish ingratitude in 1848.
[hist-ire1-s4.6.4] The Quakers concluded that private charity could not replace the state and withdrew from mass feeding, so by 1848 relief depended on the Poor Law.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.0] Population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.6 million in 1851 and about 5.4 million in 1871.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.1] The cottier and labourer classes were the hardest hit by death and emigration, and the potato-dependent class largely disappeared.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.2] Holdings of 1 to 5 acres fell sharply in number, while farms over 15 and 30 acres increased, as landlords and surviving farmers consolidated land.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.3] The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) set up a court to sell estates whose owners were bankrupt; by the late 1850s several thousand estates, perhaps a quarter of the land of Ireland, had been sold.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.4] Most buyers were Irish land speculators and businessmen rather than the British capitalists the government hoped for, and many new landlords raised rents and cleared tenants more readily than the old.
[hist-ire1-s4.7.5] Agriculture shifted from tillage to pasture: cattle and sheep needed less labour and rising British demand made them more profitable.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.0] Subdivision ended: farmers now passed the whole farm to one son, while the other children emigrated, entered the Church or married into other farms.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.1] Marriage came later and fewer people married, because a son had to wait to inherit the farm; the arranged match between farming families became the norm.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.2] The dowry became central to the match: the bride's family paid money or stock to the groom's family, often used to give a dowry to his sister.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.3] Emigration became a habit: remittances and prepaid tickets from relatives abroad paid the passage of later emigrants (see the notes on post-Famine Ireland).
[hist-ire1-s4.8.4] The Irish language declined fastest, because its heartlands in the west and south lost most people; the 1851 census recorded about 1.5 million speakers, and English became the language of advancement.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.5] The Famine left lasting hatred of landlordism and of British government among Irish Americans and at home, which fed Fenianism and, later, the Land War.
[hist-ire1-s4.8.6] The Catholic Church gained from the changed society: the surviving farming class was more devout and better able to support priests, nuns and church building.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.0] Achievement of private relief: speed, compassion and practical aid, above all by the Quakers, whose non-sectarian work is still remembered with respect.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.1] Limit of private relief: it was too small, short-lived and unevenly spread, weakest where it was most needed, and shrank after 1847.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.2] Damage: souperism and landlord clearances meant that some private responses deepened suffering and division rather than relieving them.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.3] Impact during the Famine: about a million deaths, mass eviction and flight; impact after: a smaller, older, more commercial and more Catholic-devout rural society.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.4] Contemporary British opinion often saw the Famine as an opportunity to modernise Irish agriculture; Irish nationalists, led by Mitchel, saw it as a crime of British government.
[hist-ire1-s4.9.5] Historians stress continuity as well as change: consolidation, pasture and emigration had begun before 1845, but the Famine greatly speeded them up.

CANDIDATES hist-ire1-s5: 5. Post-Famine Ireland: economy and society, 1850–1870
[hist-ire1-s5.0.b] The Famine left fewer, larger and more commercial farms, with livestock replacing tillage and a more prosperous stronger-farmer class.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.0] Population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 6.5 million in 1851, about 5.8 million in 1861 and about 5.4 million in 1871.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.1] Tillage declined and pasture expanded, because cattle, sheep and dairy produce rose in price relative to grain and needed less labour.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.2] Cattle were exported live to Britain, a trade carried by the new railways and steamships, and the growing British towns were the market.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.3] Small holdings disappeared as cottiers died or emigrated and landlords consolidated plots; farms above fifteen acres became far more common.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.4] The stronger farmers, holding large farms on long tenure, were the main gainers; they dominated parish life and later supplied much of the support for land and nationalist movements.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.5] Agricultural labourers fell in number and remained poor, and the small farmers of the west stayed close to subsistence.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.6] The Encumbered Estates Act (1849) and the Landed Estates Court (1858) sold bankrupt estates; some new owners were commercial investors who raised rents and cleared tenants.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.7] Landlordism survived: the landlord class still owned almost all the land, and tenants had no legal security of tenure until the Land Act of 1870 gave limited protection.
[hist-ire1-s5.0.8] Evictions fell from their Famine peak but remained a threat, so tenant right stayed a central grievance of the 1850s and 1860s.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.b] The Famine hardened a pattern of late marriage, celibacy and single-heir inheritance that kept farms intact and pushed other children abroad.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.0] A dowry was money or land that a bride's family gave on her marriage; farmers used it to portion a sister, to buy out siblings or to pay debts.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.1] Farms usually passed intact to one son, so a farmer could marry only when his father handed over the holding and a bride with a dowry was found.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.2] Matchmaking was widespread, and the match depended on land and money more than on affection.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.3] Marriage was late, often in the thirties, and a large share of men and women never married, especially in the west.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.4] Younger sons and daughters without a dowry had to choose between emigration, farm labour, the priesthood or religious life, and domestic service.
[hist-ire1-s5.1.5] Late marriage and celibacy kept the birth rate down, so the population did not recover after the Famine.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.b] Large-scale emigration continued long after the Famine and became part of normal life; the diaspora is covered in detail in the notes on emigration and the Irish abroad.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.0] About a million people left Ireland in the 1850s, and emigration stayed high through the 1860s.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.1] The United States was the main destination, followed by Britain, Canada and Australia; Liverpool and Cork (Queenstown) were major ports of departure.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.2] Emigrants were young and unmarried, and women left in numbers roughly equal to men.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.3] Chain migration meant that relatives and neighbours already abroad sent news and prepaid passages, so people followed established routes to particular towns.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.4] Remittances sent home helped families pay rent and passage costs for further relatives.
[hist-ire1-s5.2.5] Emigration relieved pressure on land, but it drained the countryside of young people and fed the idea of exile in nationalist politics.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.b] The national school system, set up in 1831, became the main provider of primary education and a major influence on literacy and language.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.0] The system was set up in 1831 through a letter from the Chief Secretary, Edward Stanley, and run by a board of Commissioners of National Education.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.1] Its aim was combined literary and moral education for children of all denominations, with religious instruction given separately by each church.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.2] The state paid grants, supplied textbooks and inspected schools, and a training college for teachers opened in Dublin.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.3] The churches contested the plan: the Church of Ireland set up its own Church Education Society (1839), Presbyterians objected at first, and the Catholic bishops were divided between Archbishop Daniel Murray, who co-operated, and Archbishop John MacHale of Tuam, who opposed.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.4] In practice most schools became denominational in character, because local clergy managed them and most pupils in each district were of one faith.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.5] After 1850 Cardinal Cullen pressed for greater Catholic control of education.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.6] The Christian Brothers, founded in Waterford in 1802 by Edmund Rice, ran their own free schools for poor Catholic boys and spread in the towns.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.7] Lessons were in English, and the system reinforced the decline of the Irish language by teaching through English and treating Irish as no help to a career.
[hist-ire1-s5.3.8] School attendance and literacy rose steadily, and by 1870 roughly a million children were on the rolls.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.b] Higher education divided on religious lines, and the dispute fed into the Synod of Thurles.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.0] Peel's government founded the Queen's Colleges at Belfast, Cork and Galway by an Act of 1845; they opened in 1849 and formed the Queen's University of Ireland in 1850.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.1] They were non-denominational and had no chapel or chair of theology, so critics called them godless colleges.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.2] The Catholic hierarchy was divided, but Rome condemned the colleges in 1847 and the Synod of Thurles in 1850 forbade Catholics to attend, and Cullen backed a Catholic alternative.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.3] The Catholic University opened in Dublin in 1854 with John Henry Newman as first rector; it gave no state-recognised degrees and had few students, and Newman resigned in 1858.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.4] Trinity College Dublin, an Anglican institution, remained the main university for Protestants, and Catholics were admitted to degrees but not to fellowships.
[hist-ire1-s5.4.5] The Queen's College in Belfast served the Presbyterian middle class of Ulster and was the most successful of the three.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.b] The railway network was built from 1834 and was largely complete by about 1860, changing trade, towns and communications.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.0] The Dublin and Kingstown Railway opened in December 1834 and ran about six miles from Westland Row in Dublin to Kingstown (now Dún Laoghaire); it was the first railway in Ireland.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.1] Growth began slowly: only about 65 miles were open in 1845, but the network grew to nearly 1,000 miles by 1855 and the core was largely in place by 1860.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.2] The Great Southern and Western reached Cork in 1849, the Midland Great Western reached Galway in 1851, and Belfast was linked to Dublin in the 1850s.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.3] Ireland adopted a broad gauge of 5 feet 3 inches, a decision of the 1840s that set a different gauge from Britain.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.4] Railways cut the cost and time of moving cattle, butter, grain and fish, and tied farmers to British markets through the ports.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.5] Fairs and markets shifted to railway towns, and junctions such as Mallow, Limerick Junction and Athlone grew in importance.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.6] Newspapers, mail and ideas travelled faster, so national newspapers reached the provinces and political movements could organise nationally.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.7] Tourism grew, with the line to Killarney opening in 1853, and the rail and steamer link to Britain eased travel and emigration.
[hist-ire1-s5.5.8] Some small ports lost trade as goods moved inland by rail.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.b] Dargan was the most successful Irish railway contractor of his day and a public figure who promoted Irish industry.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.0] Born in 1799 near Carlow town, he trained as a surveyor and engineer in England and worked under Thomas Telford on the Holyhead road.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.1] He won the contract for the Dublin and Kingstown Railway, which opened in 1834, and went on to build lines including the Dublin and Drogheda and the Ulster Railway, becoming the leading contractor in Ireland.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.2] He also built the Ulster Canal and dredged the Victoria Channel at Belfast, which created Queen's Island, later the Harland and Wolff site.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.3] In 1853 he financed the Dublin Industrial Exhibition on Leinster Lawn, which drew over a million visitors, and Queen Victoria visited it.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.4] He lost heavily on the Exhibition, and his reputation as an Irish industrial pioneer was the main reward; he reportedly refused the offer of a baronetcy.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.5] A fall from a horse in 1866 and the failure of some of his ventures ended his career; he died in Dublin on 7 February 1867.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.6] Contemporaries praised him as a self-made Catholic businessman who employed thousands and acted from public spirit, and the National Gallery of Ireland (1864) was founded partly as his memorial.
[hist-ire1-s5.6.7] Historians see him as a symbol of Irish enterprise within the British economy, but his work depended on railway investment more than on a wider Irish industrial base.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.b] Belfast became an industrial city of linen, engineering and shipbuilding, unlike the largely agricultural south.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.0] Mechanised flax spinning arrived in the 1820s and 1830s, and linen became the dominant industry of Belfast and the Lagan valley.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.1] The American Civil War (1861–65) cut off cotton supplies, and the cotton famine raised demand and prices for linen.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.2] Belfast's population grew from about 100,000 in 1851 to over 170,000 by 1871, with migrants from the Ulster countryside and Famine-hit areas.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.3] Harbour works, including Dargan's Victoria Channel, allowed larger ships to reach the port, and engineering firms grew up to serve the mills.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.4] Edward Harland took over Robert Hickson's shipyard on Queen's Island in 1858, and in 1861 he and Gustav Wolff formed Harland and Wolff.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.5] Ulster had skilled Protestant labour, capital from the linen trade, local business networks and strong trade links with Britain.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.6] Belfast had no local coal or iron, but cheap imports by sea and rail made industry viable.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.7] Industry in the south declined under competition from British factory goods once tariffs were gone, and Dublin and Cork kept only brewing, distilling and food processing.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.8] Catholics, mostly migrants from the countryside, made up about a third of Belfast's population, and they were concentrated in unskilled jobs and certain districts such as the Pound.
[hist-ire1-s5.7.9] Sectarian riots broke out in Belfast in 1857 and 1864, tied to Orange marches and Catholic rivalry for housing and work.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.b] Judgements differ on whether post-Famine Ireland was a story of recovery or of continuing weakness.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.0] Achievement: farm incomes and living standards of the stronger farmers rose, and railways opened national markets.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.1] Limit: the population kept falling, emigration continued and the rural poor of the west gained little.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.2] Achievement: the national school system spread literacy to most of the population.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.3] Limit: schooling in English weakened the Irish language.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.4] Achievement: Belfast industrialised and grew faster than any other Irish town.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.5] Limit: industrialisation was confined to the north-east, and the rest of Ireland depended on agriculture and on exporting people.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.6] Contemporary view: many saw the stronger farmers' prosperity and the railways as evidence of improvement under the Union.
[hist-ire1-s5.8.7] Later view: historians argue that the Famine made Ireland a more commercial but more unequal and emigrant society, with a prosperous farmer class above a shrinking labouring poor.
