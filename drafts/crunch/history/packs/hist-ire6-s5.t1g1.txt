MODE: cost and links
TARGET hist-ire6-s5: 5. Anglo-Irish relations, 1949–1989
[hist-ire6-s5.0.b] Anglo-Irish relations after 1949 grew out of how Ireland had been divided and how the South's links with Britain were loosened. These background facts explain why a declaration of a republic in 1949 caused such a reaction in London and Belfast.
[hist-ire6-s5.0.0] The Government of Ireland Act 1920 set up Northern Ireland with its own parliament at Stormont (in Belfast). The Irish Free State followed in 1922, so the island was divided into two states.
[hist-ire6-s5.0.1] A unionist majority (people who wanted Northern Ireland to stay in the United Kingdom) governed the North, and nationalists (who wanted Irish unity) were a large minority there. This is why partition stayed a live dispute.
[hist-ire6-s5.0.2] The External Relations Act of 1936 left the Crown with a role in Irish external affairs, so Ireland was still linked to the British monarch for diplomatic purposes.
[hist-ire6-s5.0.3] The 1937 Constitution (Bunreacht na hÉireann) claimed the whole island in Articles 2 and 3, which set the Irish state's official position against partition.
[hist-ire6-s5.0.4] The First Inter-Party Government of 1948 to 1951 took office in February 1948 under John A. Costello as Taoiseach, with Seán MacBride of Clann na Poblachta as Minister for External Affairs, and moved to declare a republic.
[hist-ire6-s5.0.5] Ireland was neutral in the Second World War and did not join NATO. Even so, Britain, Northern Ireland and the Republic stayed closely linked by trade, migration and sterling (the British currency).
[hist-ire6-s5.1.b] The declaration of a republic completed the separation from Britain in the South but hardened partition in British law. Britain's reply, the Ireland Act, is the starting point for the whole story.
[hist-ire6-s5.1.0] Costello announced the repeal of the External Relations Act while in Canada (Ottawa) in September 1948. The Republic of Ireland Act 1948 came into force on 18 April 1949 (Easter Monday).
[hist-ire6-s5.1.1] That Irish Act ended the Crown link under the External Relations Act, and Ireland formally left the Commonwealth because a republic could no longer share a monarch with the other members.
[hist-ire6-s5.1.2] Britain responded with the Ireland Act 1949. It declared that the Republic was not a foreign country for British law and that Irish citizens in Britain were not treated as aliens, so they kept their rights, including to vote, work and receive benefits.
[hist-ire6-s5.1.3] The Act also stated that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of the Parliament of Northern Ireland, which gave unionists a veto over any change.
[hist-ire6-s5.1.4] The Irish government saw the Ireland Act as a guarantee of partition and a betrayal of nationalists.
[hist-ire6-s5.1.5] The Common Travel Area, which allowed free movement between the two islands, continued after 1949. This mattered to the large number of Irish emigrants living in Britain.
[hist-ire6-s5.1.6] Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 Constitution claimed the whole island as the national territory. Unionists and the British government cited them as proof that Dublin did not accept Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s5.2.b] In the years after 1949 Irish governments tried to end partition by publicity and diplomacy. When that failed, the IRA turned to force, and by the late 1950s the Irish state changed course.
[hist-ire6-s5.2.0] The 1948 to 1951 government launched an anti-partition campaign with the support of all the main parties, aiming to win international sympathy for Irish unity.
[hist-ire6-s5.2.1] Irish ministers spoke abroad, especially in the United States, and complained that nationalists in Northern Ireland were treated unfairly.
[hist-ire6-s5.2.2] Britain and the unionist government at Stormont refused to discuss the question, so the campaign produced no practical gain.
[hist-ire6-s5.2.3] The IRA's Border Campaign ran from December 1956 to 1962. It had little support in the Republic, and the Irish government interned (jailed without trial) suspects at the Curragh from 1957 to 1959.
[hist-ire6-s5.2.4] By the late 1950s Irish governments moved to a more pragmatic, economic approach and concentrated on economic recovery, because rhetoric about partition had achieved nothing.
[hist-ire6-s5.3.b] Ireland's economy was tied to Britain's, and this shaped how far Irish governments could afford to quarrel with London. It explains much of the move to cooperation from the 1960s.
[hist-ire6-s5.3.0] In the late 1950s about 70 to 75 per cent of Irish exports, mainly live cattle and other farm produce, went to Britain, so Irish farmers depended on the British market.
[hist-ire6-s5.3.1] Britain was also the main source of imports. The Irish pound was tied to sterling until Ireland joined the European Monetary System in 1979, so Irish prices and interest rates followed British conditions.
[hist-ire6-s5.3.2] This dependence is why Irish policy-makers sought to join the EEC (the European Economic Community, forerunner of the European Union) only when Britain did, and why the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement of 1965 mattered.
[hist-ire6-s5.3.3] Both countries joined the EEC in 1973, and Ireland joined the European Monetary System in 1979 while Britain stayed out. The share of exports going to Britain then fell steadily, to under half by the early 1980s.
[hist-ire6-s5.4.b] In 1965 relations between Dublin, Belfast and London became more cooperative than at any time since partition.
[hist-ire6-s5.4.0] Seán Lemass, the Taoiseach, went to Stormont on 14 January 1965 and met Terence O'Neill, the Prime Minister of Northern Ireland. The visit was arranged in secret. O'Neill then visited Dublin on 9 February 1965.
[hist-ire6-s5.4.1] These were the first meetings between the heads of government of the two parts of Ireland since partition, and the agenda was trade, tourism and electricity rather than partition.
[hist-ire6-s5.4.2] Lemass accepted the North's existence in practice and talked of cooperation, which marked a change from the anti-partition rhetoric of the 1950s. The Irish government's declaration on consent came later, in 1973 and 1985.
[hist-ire6-s5.4.3] Ian Paisley and other unionists protested at the meetings, and some in O'Neill's own party mistrusted them.
[hist-ire6-s5.4.4] The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965. Tariff cuts began in 1966 and were phased in over a number of years, so trade between Ireland and Britain became freer gradually.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.b] Violence in Northern Ireland from 1969 tested relations between the Irish and British governments, and Jack Lynch, the Taoiseach, had to balance public feeling in the South against the need to keep working with London. Each crisis produced a response from Dublin that you should be able to date.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.0] After rioting in Derry and Belfast in August 1969, Lynch said in a broadcast on 13 August that the Irish government could no longer stand by. He refused to send troops across the border, and the British army was deployed on the streets on 14 August 1969.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.1] Ireland raised the crisis at the United Nations in August 1969 without result. On 4 September 1969 Patrick Hillery, the Minister for External Affairs, visited the Falls Road in Belfast.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.2] The Arms Crisis of May 1970, when Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney on 6 May, showed the division inside Fianna Fáil over how to respond to the North.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.3] In September 1971 Lynch met Edward Heath, the British Prime Minister, and Brian Faulkner, the Northern Ireland Prime Minister, at Chequers. Lynch argued that an Irish dimension (a role for Dublin) was needed to end the violence.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.4] Internment (jailing suspects without trial) was introduced on 9 August 1971 and condemned by the Irish government. Ireland took the United Kingdom to the European Commission and Court of Human Rights, and in January 1978 the Court found that the interrogation techniques used were inhuman and degrading treatment (not torture).
[hist-ire6-s5.5.5] On Bloody Sunday, 30 January 1972, soldiers of the Parachute Regiment shot dead thirteen people at a civil rights march in Derry, and a fourteenth died later. The Irish ambassador in London was recalled and 2 February 1972 was a day of mourning in the Republic.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.6] Later judgements on Bloody Sunday differed. The Widgery Report of April 1972 was widely seen as a whitewash, but the Saville Inquiry report of June 2010 found the killings unjustified, and David Cameron apologised.
[hist-ire6-s5.5.7] On 2 February 1972 a crowd burned the British embassy in Merrion Square, Dublin, and the British government protested. In March 1972 Heath suspended Stormont and introduced direct rule from Westminster, which Dublin welcomed as a step towards a new settlement.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.b] In 1973 Britain and Ireland entered the EEC together and the two governments, with Northern Ireland's moderate parties, tried to agree a settlement. It failed within months, and security strains continued afterwards.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.0] Ireland and Britain, with Denmark, joined the EEC on 1 January 1973. Their ministers began to meet regularly in Brussels, which improved personal contacts between the two governments.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.1] Liam Cosgrave led a Fine Gael-Labour coalition from March 1973, with Garret FitzGerald as Minister for Foreign Affairs from 1973 to 1977.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.2] The Sunningdale Agreement of December 1973 (agreed at a conference at Sunningdale, Berkshire) involved Heath for the British government and Cosgrave for the Irish government. The Northern parties were the Ulster Unionists under Faulkner, the SDLP under Gerry Fitt and the Alliance Party under Oliver Napier.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.3] It provided for a power-sharing executive (unionists and nationalists governing together), with Faulkner as chief executive and Fitt as deputy, and a Council of Ireland to give the Republic a say in North-South cooperation.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.4] The Irish government declared that there would be no change in the status of Northern Ireland until a majority there wished it. This did not end the constitutional claim, because Articles 2 and 3 stayed until 1998.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.5] Loyalists opposed the Council of Ireland because it gave Dublin a role in the North. The Ulster Workers' Council strike, from 15 to 28 May 1974, ended the executive, so Sunningdale collapsed within about five months.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.6] On 17 May 1974, during the strike, car bombs in Dublin and Monaghan killed 33 people. They were attributed to loyalists and no one was convicted.
[hist-ire6-s5.6.7] The IRA killed Christopher Ewart-Biggs, the British ambassador to Ireland, near Dublin on 21 July 1976. A state of emergency was declared and further emergency legislation followed in 1976, which shows the security strain of the mid-1970s.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.b] Relations moved from security cooperation after IRA attacks in 1979 to a promising summit in 1980 and then a rift in 1982. Charles Haughey and Margaret Thatcher were very different leaders, and a quarrel over the Falklands War ended their dialogue.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.0] On 27 August 1979 the IRA killed Lord Mountbatten at Mullaghmore, Co. Sligo. The same day eighteen British soldiers were killed at Warrenpoint, Co. Down. Lynch met Thatcher in September 1979 and security cooperation was agreed.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.1] Thatcher became Prime Minister in May 1979 and Haughey became Taoiseach on 11 December 1979. Haughey met her in London in May 1980 and in Dublin on 8 December 1980.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.2] The Dublin summit's joint communique spoke of studies of 'the totality of relationships within these islands'. Haughey presented this as a breakthrough, but Thatcher said constitutional status was not in question, so the two leaders read it differently.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.3] The 1981 hunger strikes, in which ten republican prisoners died, including Bobby Sands on 5 May 1981, strained relations between the two governments.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.4] In November 1981 Thatcher and FitzGerald, by then Taoiseach, set up the Anglo-Irish Intergovernmental Council. Do not confuse it with the Haughey-Thatcher summit of December 1980.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.5] In the Falklands War (April to June 1982) Ireland, then a member of the UN Security Council, at first supported EEC sanctions on Argentina. After the Argentine cruiser General Belgrano was sunk on 2 May 1982, Haughey's government withdrew its support in early May and called for a ceasefire.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.6] Thatcher was angered and saw the withdrawal as a breach of solidarity, and trust between the two leaders did not recover while Haughey was in office.
[hist-ire6-s5.7.7] The dispute set Irish neutrality (staying out of other countries' wars) against solidarity with Britain as a fellow EEC member. Haughey's government acted on the first, while Thatcher judged by the second.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.b] Garret FitzGerald built on the Forum to reach the main achievement of Anglo-Irish diplomacy in this period, the agreement signed at Hillsborough. It gave Dublin a formal consultative role in Northern Ireland affairs without granting it authority.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.0] The New Ireland Forum opened in Dublin Castle on 30 May 1983 and reported on 2 May 1984. It brought together Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael, Labour and the SDLP.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.1] Its report offered three options: a unitary state (the preferred option), a federal or confederal state, and joint authority (shared British and Irish rule).
[hist-ire6-s5.8.2] Thatcher publicly rejected all three at a press conference on 19 November 1984 ('out, out, out'). Even so, the Forum had strengthened the SDLP and shown that nationalists had a common position.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.3] Negotiations between officials followed, and FitzGerald and Thatcher signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement at Hillsborough Castle, Co. Down, on 15 November 1985.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.4] Both governments affirmed the consent principle: the status of Northern Ireland would change only with the consent of a majority there. The Intergovernmental Conference was co-chaired by the Secretary of State for Northern Ireland and the Irish Minister for Foreign Affairs, with a secretariat at Maryfield, near Belfast.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.5] The Irish government could put forward views on Northern Ireland affairs through the Conference, but it could not exercise authority, so this was a consultative role and not joint authority.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.6] Unionists protested strongly. A huge rally at Belfast City Hall in November 1985 was followed by the resignation of fifteen unionist MPs in January 1986 to force by-elections.
[hist-ire6-s5.8.7] Fianna Fáil under Haughey opposed the agreement. Desmond O'Malley had been expelled from Fianna Fáil in February 1985 over a vote on family planning law. He and Mary Harney founded the Progressive Democrats in December 1985, in opposition to Haughey's leadership.
[hist-ire6-s5.9.b] The agreement drew on motives from both governments, and nationalists and unionists drew opposite conclusions from it. These contrasting reactions are what examiners ask you to name.
[hist-ire6-s5.9.0] Thatcher accepted the agreement partly for security reasons, because she wanted closer cooperation with Dublin against the IRA.
[hist-ire6-s5.9.1] She also hoped that a role for Dublin would strengthen the SDLP, the nationalist party that rejected violence, against Sinn Féin.
[hist-ire6-s5.9.2] Contemporary nationalists saw the agreement as recognition of their rights and of Dublin's concern for them.
[hist-ire6-s5.9.3] Contemporary unionists saw it as a betrayal and a threat to the union, because it was made without their consent, which is why they protested and later boycotted the Conference.
[hist-ire6-s5.10.b] By 1989 relations were far closer than in 1949, although the basic dispute over Northern Ireland remained unresolved. The agreement had survived and changed how the two governments dealt with each other.
[hist-ire6-s5.10.0] The Intergovernmental Conference met regularly and cooperation on security and cross-border matters had grown, although unionists continued to boycott it.
[hist-ire6-s5.10.1] Friction continued over extradition (handing suspects to the other state), over alleged miscarriages of justice affecting Irish people in British courts, and over the British army's conduct in Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s5.10.2] The IRA's campaign continued, and the North remained divided and violent.
[hist-ire6-s5.10.3] Haughey had opposed the agreement in 1985, but he left it in place when Fianna Fáil formed a coalition with the Progressive Democrats in July 1989, after the general election of June 1989.
[hist-ire6-s5.10.4] Joint EEC membership, the growth of North-South contacts and the habit of regular meetings meant that relations were closer than in 1949, even though the fundamental dispute remained.
[hist-ire6-s5.11.b] Examiners reward a judgement on how far Anglo-Irish relations improved. The evidence falls into paired points: what was achieved against what was not, and how contemporaries judged events against how historians judge them now.
[hist-ire6-s5.11.0] Achievement: from the hostility of the Ireland Act in 1949, Ireland moved to regular contact with London and a formal consultative role in 1985.
[hist-ire6-s5.11.1] Limit: Sunningdale collapsed within months, the 1985 agreement did not end the violence, and the two governments kept different aims.
[hist-ire6-s5.11.2] Contemporary view: nationalists saw the Anglo-Irish Agreement as recognition of their rights.
[hist-ire6-s5.11.3] Contemporary view: unionists saw it as betrayal, a threat to the union made without their consent.
[hist-ire6-s5.11.4] Later view: historians see the agreement as a foundation for the Downing Street Declaration of December 1993 and the Good Friday Agreement of 10 April 1998, although its immediate effect was limited.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s2: 2. Economic planning and the move to free trade; case study: the First Programme for Economic Expansion, 1958–1963
[hist-ire6-s2.0.b] Four ideas run through this subsection. Together they describe the shift from a closed, state-shielded economy to one that planned for growth and competed abroad.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.0] Economic planning means the government sets targets for several years ahead and directs public investment and incentives towards them, so that growth is pursued on purpose rather than left to chance. The First Programme was Ireland's first plan of this kind.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.1] Protectionism is the use of tariffs (taxes on imported goods) and controls to shield home industry from foreign competition. Fianna Fáil used it from the 1930s in the hope of making Ireland more self-sufficient.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.2] Free trade is the removal of tariffs and other barriers between countries, so that firms sell across borders on equal terms. It is the opposite of protection, and Ireland moved towards it in the 1960s.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.3] The balance of payments is the gap between what a country earns abroad, mainly from exports, and what it spends abroad on imports. A deficit (spending more than it earns) forced governments to cut back at home, so it shaped policy throughout the period.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.b] By 1957 the policy of protection that Fianna Fáil had begun in the 1930s was widely seen to have run out of steam. Small factories, one export customer and mass emigration showed that the economy was not growing.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.0] Tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934 had created many small factories that sold only on the home market. The home market was too small to support efficient industry, so firms stayed small and costly to run.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.1] The Control of Manufactures Acts required that a majority of the shares in a new company (51 per cent) be held by Irish citizens. This discouraged foreign firms from setting up in Ireland, since they could not keep control of their own businesses.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.2] Exports were mostly live cattle and farm produce sold to Britain, so the economy depended on one customer and on farming.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.3] Net emigration in the 1950s was about 400,000 (over the decade 1951–61), and the population fell to about 2.82 million in 1961, while the rest of western Europe was growing fast.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.4] Balance of payments crises in 1951–52 and 1955–56 were met by deflation (cutting spending and demand to reduce imports). This cut demand without improving productive capacity.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.5] Because each crisis was met by deflation and then by recovery, the economy moved in a repeating stop-go pattern.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.6] Fianna Fáil returned to government in March 1957, with Seán Lemass at Industry and Commerce (and as Tánaiste, the deputy head of government) and James Ryan at Finance. They were ready to listen to new ideas from officials.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.b] Economic Development was a study by civil servants, led by Whitaker, that changed the direction of policy. It is the link between the failure of the 1950s and the First Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.0] Thomas Kenneth Whitaker became Secretary of the Department of Finance (its top civil servant) in 1956 at the age of 39. He gathered a group of younger officials to examine the economy, and the department that had long resisted spending changed its attitude.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.1] Economic Development was written in the Department of Finance under Whitaker. It was completed in May 1958 and submitted to Ministers, and it was published to the public later in 1958, around the time of the Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.2] It argued that protection had failed to produce growth or jobs, and that emigration was a symptom of an economy that was not expanding.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.3] It recommended shifting public investment from social projects such as housing and hospitals towards directly productive investment in agriculture, industry and tourism.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.4] It welcomed foreign capital and technology, a break with the Control of Manufactures Acts, and it argued for export-led growth (growth driven by selling more abroad).
[hist-ire6-s2.2.5] The government accepted its analysis, and in November 1958 it published the Programme for Economic Expansion, usually called the First Programme, which turned the proposals into a policy with targets.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.b] The First Programme ran for five years, from 1959 to 1963, and set out an agenda for growth. Its measures aimed to attract investment and to reward firms that sold abroad.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.0] Aim: to raise national output and living standards and to halt emigration, because jobs in export industry and farming would give people a reason to stay.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.1] Target: a rise of about 2 per cent a year in gross national product (the total value of what the country's people produce). It was set deliberately modest, so that it could be achieved and confidence rebuilt.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.2] The Industrial Development (Encouragement of External Investment) Act 1958 ended the rule that Irish citizens must hold most of the shares in new firms, so it opened the way to foreign-owned factories.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.3] Export Profits Tax Relief, which exempted profits from increased exports from tax, was introduced in 1956 by the Second Inter-Party Government. Fianna Fáil kept it and widened it, so it began before the Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.4] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA), set up in 1949, was given a larger role and larger grants in the late 1950s to firms setting up new plant. Incentives favoured less developed areas, such as the west.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.5] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company was set up in 1959 and built an industrial estate at Shannon to attract foreign firms.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.6] In agriculture, An Foras Talúntais (the agricultural research institute) was set up in 1958, and farmers were encouraged to raise productivity and exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.7] The state shifted its capital spending (spending on lasting assets such as roads and equipment) towards productive investment, though spending on housing and health rose again as growth took hold.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.b] Judged by its own targets, the First Programme succeeded, and it did so by a wide margin. The results are best shown as target against outcome, with the limits and the outside help set beside them.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.0] Real national output grew by about 4 per cent a year between 1958 and 1963, about double the 2 per cent target and faster than in the previous decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.1] Exports of manufactured goods rose sharply, and industrial production grew because new, mostly foreign-owned firms began to operate.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.2] Net emigration fell to about 80,000 in 1961–66, from about 200,000 in 1956–61, so the haemorrhage of people had slowed.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.3] The 1966 census recorded a population of about 2.88 million, up from about 2.82 million in 1961. This was the first rise since the early 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.4] Unemployment fell but remained a serious problem, and total employment barely rose because jobs in farming continued to decline.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.5] The balance of payments remained fragile, and by the mid-1960s wage claims and inflation were raising difficulties.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.6] Part of the success lay outside Ireland. The late 1950s and 1960s were years of strong growth in Britain and Europe, which raised the demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.b] Historians agree that the First Programme mattered, but they disagree about how much of the change it caused. The question is whether it was a turning point or part of a longer story.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.0] The Programme was the start of regular economic planning in Ireland, so it was followed by a Second Programme published in 1963 and a Third Programme covering 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.1] Contemporaries welcomed it as a sharp break with the pessimism of the 1950s. The idea of a plan for growth was supported across the parties and by the trade unions and employers.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.2] Those who see it as a turning point stress the end of protection, the openness to foreign investment and the change in public confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.3] Sceptical historians stress that the world economy was booming, which lifted Irish exports whatever the policy. They add that the Programme built on measures begun by the Inter-Party governments, such as Export Profits Tax Relief, and that its targets were modest.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.4] Critics on the left argued that foreign firms took profits out of the country, so Ireland kept less of the gain, and that growth was uneven, with the west benefiting least.
[hist-ire6-s2.5.5] Whitaker himself stressed that the Programme was a framework, and that its success depended on private investors and on a favourable international climate.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.b] Lemass became Taoiseach (head of government) in June 1959, when de Valera was elected President, and he carried the Programme through. His record is a mix of modernizing steps and unfinished business.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.0] Lemass became Taoiseach on 23 June 1959 and held the office until 10 November 1966, when Jack Lynch succeeded him. He had been a minister since 1932 and was a pragmatic modernizer.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.1] His style was practical, businesslike and impatient for change, in contrast to de Valera, the older leader he succeeded.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.2] He built closer links with the trade unions and employers, called social partnership, so that pay could be settled by agreement. The national wage rounds, in which unions, employers and the government bargained over general pay increases, began in 1964.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.3] The government set up the Committee on Industrial Organisation in the early 1960s and, from 1963, adaptation councils to help industries prepare for competition from free trade, since protected firms would soon lose their tariff shelter.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.4] The Second Programme for Economic Expansion, published in 1963, aimed for growth of about 4 per cent a year to 1970, but it was abandoned in 1967 after wage rises and a balance of payments deficit. A Third Programme followed for 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.5] He used the new television service, Telefís Éireann, to explain government policy and to project an image of a modern state. There is more on this in the RTÉ case study.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.6] He is also remembered for easing the state's attitude to Northern Ireland, as in his 1965 meetings with Terence O'Neill, who was Prime Minister of Northern Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.b] Lemass saw that the future of Irish industry lay in competing in open markets, not in protection. Britain was both the main customer and the key to any trade deal.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.0] Ireland applied to join the European Economic Community (EEC, the common market of western European states) on 31 July 1961, nine days before Britain did, because Britain was the market for most Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.1] President de Gaulle of France vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which ended the negotiations for the time being. Ireland's application remained on the table.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.2] With EEC entry delayed, Lemass turned to a trade agreement with Britain. The Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965 and took effect on 1 July 1966.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.3] The agreement removed tariffs on trade between the two countries over ten years and kept access for Irish farm exports to the British market.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.4] Free trade threatened protected Irish firms in textiles, footwear and other consumer goods, and many small firms closed over the next decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.5] Supporters argued that free trade would bring cheaper inputs, larger markets and new investment, and that protected industries could not survive in the long run.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.6] Ireland joined the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT, an international agreement to lower tariffs) in 1967, the year after Lemass left office. It was the end point of the move to lower tariffs, not an act of his government.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.7] Lemass visited Terence O'Neill at Stormont on 14 January 1965, and O'Neill visited Dublin on 9 February 1965. These were the first meetings between the heads of the two governments since the 1920s.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.b] Whitaker was a civil servant, not a politician, and the most influential public official of his generation. He supplied the ideas behind the First Programme.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.0] Whitaker was born in 1916 in Rostrevor, Co. Down, and joined the civil service in 1934.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.1] As Secretary of the Department of Finance from 1956 to 1969 he was the main author of Economic Development and the First Programme, working with Lemass and with ministers such as James Ryan.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.2] He shaped events by using the authority of the Department of Finance, which had traditionally resisted spending, to argue for investment. This carried weight because Finance had always been the department that said no.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.3] He later served as Governor of the Central Bank from 1969 to 1976, as a senator from 1977 to 1982 and as chairman of several commissions, including the Constitution Review Group of the mid-1990s. He died in 2017 at the age of 100.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.4] As author of the report, he was a participant in events. He later said that he and his colleagues did the work without extra pay.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.5] Contemporaries praised his clarity and courage in questioning protection, though some in his own department doubted how far growth could be planned.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.6] Historians see him as the architect of the new approach, while stressing that Lemass and de Valera supplied the political will. Some argue that his role has been overstated by the idea that planning alone made the economy grow.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.b] Lemass was the minister and then Taoiseach who turned the new thinking into government policy. He is unusual because he built the system of protection and then helped to end it.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.0] Lemass was born in Dublin in 1899, fought in the Rising in 1916 and on the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, and his brother Noel was killed in 1923.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.1] He co-founded Fianna Fáil in 1926. He was Minister for Industry and Commerce from 1932 to 1939, 1941 to 1948, 1951 to 1954 and 1957 to 1959, and Minister for Supplies from 1939 to 1945.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.2] As the architect of the protectionist industrial policy of the 1930s, he was able later to argue that protection had served its purpose and should now give way to competition. The failure of protection had convinced a former protectionist.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.3] As Taoiseach he backed the First Programme because he now believed in growth through exports, applied to join the EEC and signed the Free Trade Agreement.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.4] Contemporaries saw him as a practical, impatient leader who got things done, so he was respected for results, though some in Fianna Fáil mistrusted his break with protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.5] Historians and biographers judge him a modernizer who changed the tone of Irish politics, but they note that he left rural poverty, urban housing problems and partition unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.b] The change of direction needed both an adviser with ideas and a leader with the will to act. The achievements and limits below give the material for a balanced judgement.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.0] Whitaker supplied the analysis and Lemass the political leadership. The Programme needed both, and their partnership is the central reason for the change of direction.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.1] Compared directly, Whitaker was a civil servant and Lemass a politician. Whitaker was cautious and analytical by temperament, and Lemass was driven and impatient for results.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.2] Achievements of the First Programme: growth about twice the target, so it beat its aim, falling emigration, rising exports, a shift in confidence and the habit of planning.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.3] Limits: total employment barely increased because farm jobs kept falling, farming and the west lagged, the balance of payments remained fragile and wage claims raised inflation. The Second Programme was abandoned in 1967, and partition and rural poverty were left unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.4] Contemporary view: the Programme was seen as a success, and Fianna Fáil did well in the 1965 election and stayed in office.
[hist-ire6-s2.10.5] Later view: historians accept that it was a turning point but debate how much of the growth came from policy and how much from the European boom.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s1: 1. Alternating governments and their economic and social policies, 1948–1959
[hist-ire6-s1.0.b] To understand why governments changed so often in the 1950s, you first need to know what Ireland inherited from the 1930s and the war years. The country was poor, politically divided and closely tied to Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.0] Fianna Fáil under de Valera governed from 1932 to February 1948 and built a protected, self-sufficiency economy, using tariffs (taxes on imports) and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934, which required Irish citizens to hold a majority of shares in most firms.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.1] The party system was Fianna Fáil against Fine Gael and Labour, and the Civil War still shaped loyalties. Proportional representation (voters rank candidates, so smaller parties win seats) made coalitions possible when no party had a majority.
[hist-ire6-s1.0.2] Ireland was neutral in the Second World War, which it called 'the Emergency'. It came out of the war poor, isolated and tied to the British market and to sterling (the British pound).
[hist-ire6-s1.0.3] The Catholic Church had strong influence on social policy, and its position was recognised in the Constitution of 1937. This is why a clash between a minister and the bishops, as in 1951, could decide government policy.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.b] Two economic terms run through the whole decade. Between them they explain why governments of every party kept cutting spending, even when it made life harder.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.0] The balance of payments is the record of a country's payments to and from the rest of the world. If more money goes out (mainly to buy imports) than comes in (mainly from exports), the country has a deficit.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.1] A deficit drained Ireland's reserves of foreign currency, because the money to pay for imports had to come from those reserves. Since the Irish pound was linked to sterling, the government could not simply let its currency fall.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.2] The usual cure was deflation: raising taxes and cutting government spending or credit to reduce demand, so that people bought fewer imports. The cost was jobs, because falling demand led firms to cut staff.
[hist-ire6-s1.1.3] This is why MacEntee's 1952 budget was unpopular, and why deflation (including the measures of 1955–56) helped drive both unemployment and emigration.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.b] After sixteen years of Fianna Fáil rule, a coalition of the opposition parties took office in 1948. An Inter-Party Government is simply a coalition of several parties that agreed to govern together; there were two, in 1948–51 and 1954–57.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.0] In the February 1948 general election Fianna Fáil, in power since 1932, remained the largest party but lost its majority, so it could no longer govern alone.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.1] Clann na Poblachta, a republican party founded in 1946 by Seán MacBride, won only 10 seats in 1948 but held the balance of power in the Dáil, meaning the coalition needed its votes.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.2] The government that took office on 18 February 1948 united Fine Gael, Labour, the National Labour Party, Clann na Poblachta, Clann na Talmhan and some independents.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.3] Richard Mulcahy, the Fine Gael leader, stood aside because of his Civil War record, so John A. Costello became Taoiseach. William Norton of Labour was Tánaiste (deputy prime minister).
[hist-ire6-s1.2.4] Seán MacBride was Minister for External Affairs, and Noël Browne of Clann na Poblachta became Minister for Health in 1948.
[hist-ire6-s1.2.5] The coalition formed to end Fianna Fáil's long hold on office and had no common economic programme. It contained both conservative and radical members, which made bold policy hard.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.b] The government's best-known act was constitutional, but it also made economic and health decisions that mattered for the rest of the decade.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.0] Costello announced in Ottawa, Canada, in September 1948 that the government would repeal the External Relations Act of 1936 and declare a republic.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.1] The Republic of Ireland Act, passed in 1948, came into force on 18 April 1949 (Easter Monday). It ended the state's last formal link with the Crown and its Commonwealth membership.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.2] Britain replied with the Ireland Act 1949, which guaranteed that Northern Ireland would not cease to be part of the United Kingdom without the consent of its parliament.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.3] The Ireland Act also said the Republic was not a foreign country and its citizens were not aliens in Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.4] Ireland declined an invitation to join NATO (the Western military alliance) in 1949, with MacBride citing partition as the obstacle.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.5] The Industrial Development Authority (IDA) was set up in 1949 to encourage new industry, including investment from abroad, though in the 1950s it had little money or power.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.6] Ireland took part in Marshall Aid (the European Recovery Programme, American aid for rebuilding after the war) and received about 146 million dollars, mostly as loans. Much went on land reclamation and agriculture rather than industry, which later critics saw as a missed chance.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.7] Noël Browne led a campaign against tuberculosis (TB), a major killer. New sanatoria (hospitals for TB patients) were paid for partly from the Irish Hospitals' Sweepstake, and a BCG vaccination programme began. TB deaths fell steeply during the 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s1.3.8] The government also expanded house building.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.b] Browne's plan for free health care for mothers and children became the clearest example of the Catholic hierarchy's power over social policy in the 1950s. Its defeat also helped end the government.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.0] Browne's scheme of 1950 used the Health Act 1947 to offer free care for mothers before and after childbirth and for children up to the age of 16, available to all without a means test (a check on income).
[hist-ire6-s1.4.1] The Irish Medical Association opposed it as a threat to doctors' private practice and independence.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.2] The Catholic hierarchy (the bishops) objected that the scheme gave the state too much power over family life, that it might lead to state-provided education in sexual matters, and that it undermined the rights of parents.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.3] The hierarchy wrote to Costello in late 1950 and again in April 1951, saying the scheme was contrary to Catholic social teaching.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.4] MacBride, who wanted to protect his government from a clash with the church, asked Browne to resign. Browne resigned in April 1951 and then published the correspondence, which made the church's role public.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.5] Costello and the cabinet accepted the bishops' view, so the scheme was dropped.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.6] The affair helped bring down the government. Costello called a general election for 30 May 1951, in which Clann na Poblachta fell from 10 seats to 2.
[hist-ire6-s1.4.7] The Fianna Fáil government that took office replaced the scheme with the Health Act 1953, which offered means-tested maternity, child and hospital services, so many people were left without free care.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.b] McQuaid was Archbishop of Dublin from 1940 to 1972 and the most influential churchman in the state in the 1950s. Here the focus is only his part in the crisis of 1950–51.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.0] John Charles McQuaid was born in 1895 in Cootehill, Co. Cavan, joined the Holy Ghost Fathers and was president of Blackrock College before he became Archbishop of Dublin in 1940.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.1] His influence rested on close personal contact with ministers, notably de Valera, whom he had advised on the religious article of the Constitution of 1937.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.2] In 1950–51 he was a participant: he advised Browne and the government on the church's objections and was the most influential voice in arguing that the scheme conflicted with Catholic social teaching. The letters themselves were the bishops' collective act, headed by the primate, Cardinal D'Alton.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.3] His principle was that the family and the church, not the state, should direct the care of mothers and children. He objected to the principle, not only to medical detail.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.4] Contemporaries who agreed with him saw a defender of Catholic social teaching. Browne and his supporters saw an interfering prelate who used his power to defeat a good scheme.
[hist-ire6-s1.5.5] John Whyte, in Church and State in Modern Ireland, 1923–1979 (1971), concluded that the government deferred to the hierarchy. Later work using archives stresses Browne's weak political position and the doctors' opposition, while still treating the bishops' stance as decisive.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.b] De Valera returned as Taoiseach in June 1951, but without a majority and with an economy short of foreign currency. The government's answer to the balance of payments problem was deflation.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.0] Fianna Fáil formed a minority government with de Valera as Taoiseach, Seán Lemass as Tánaiste and Minister for Industry and Commerce, and Seán MacEntee as Minister for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.1] The minority government depended on the votes of independent deputies, which limited how far it could go with unpopular measures.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.2] MacEntee's 1952 budget was deflationary: it cut subsidies on basic foods and raised duties, which pushed prices up, in order to reduce imports and the balance of payments deficit. It was unpopular.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.3] The Underdeveloped Areas Act 1952 gave grants for new industry in the poorer western counties.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.4] The Health Act 1953 provided a framework of means-tested maternity, child and hospital care in place of the Mother and Child Scheme.
[hist-ire6-s1.6.5] Emigration continued at a high level and unemployment stayed high. In the 1954 general election Fianna Fáil lost seats.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.b] Costello returned as Taoiseach in 1954 at the head of a coalition that again had no programme for growth. It then faced a balance of payments crisis.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.0] The second coalition contained Fine Gael, Labour and Clann na Talmhan, with Clann na Poblachta supporting it from outside government, meaning it voted for the government but held no ministries.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.1] Gerard Sweetman, the Minister for Finance, was a strong believer in private enterprise and low state spending.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.2] A balance of payments crisis built up in 1955 and 1956 because imports ran well ahead of exports and foreign currency reserves fell.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.3] The government responded with higher taxes, credit restrictions and cuts in current spending. This reduced demand and pushed up unemployment.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.4] The Capital Investment Advisory Committee, set up in 1956, reviewed proposals for public capital spending. It was an early step towards planning public investment.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.5] In December 1956 the IRA began its Border Campaign, and the government faced pressure from Clann na Poblachta over its handling of the North and its response to the campaign.
[hist-ire6-s1.7.6] Clann na Poblachta withdrew its support in January 1957, which left Costello without a majority, and he called an election.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.b] While most of western Europe boomed, the Irish economy grew only slowly and people left in large numbers. Unemployment stayed high throughout the decade.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.0] The census showed a population of 2,960,593 in 1951 and 2,818,341 in 1961, a fall of about 142,000 despite a high birth rate, because so many people emigrated.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.1] Net emigration (people leaving minus people arriving) in the 1950s was about 400,000, roughly 200,000 in each half of the decade. Most of those who left were young people heading for Britain.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.2] Britain attracted them because it offered work in construction, factories, the health service and transport, and its economy was growing faster than the Irish.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.3] Agriculture still employed about two-fifths of the workforce, mostly on small farms of low productivity, and cattle exports to Britain were the main earner.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.4] Industry sheltered behind protective tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts, which required that most shares in new firms be held by Irish citizens and so discouraged foreign investment.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.5] Irish growth was a fraction of the growth in western Europe, so the gap with other countries widened.
[hist-ire6-s1.8.6] The Irish pound was linked to sterling, so Irish interest rates and trade followed British conditions, including the British government's alternating cycles of expansion and restriction, known as 'stop-go'.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.b] The 1957 election ended the second coalition. Historians use the phrase 'wasted years' for the 1950s, a decade they see as one of missed opportunity, and they disagree about who or what was to blame.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.0] In the March 1957 general election Fianna Fáil won 78 seats and an overall majority, and de Valera became Taoiseach for the last time (to 1959).
[hist-ire6-s1.9.1] Lemass returned to Industry and Commerce and also served as Tánaiste, and James Ryan became Minister for Finance.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.2] T.K. Whitaker had become Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 and began the work that led to Economic Development.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.3] View 1: historians such as J.J. Lee (Ireland 1912–1985) and Tom Garvin (Preventing the Future, 2004) criticise the leaders of the 1950s for lacking ambition and for preferring stability to change, which gave rise to the term 'wasted years'.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.4] View 2: others point to constraints, such as a small economy that depended on the British market and the sterling link, and weak foreign currency reserves.
[hist-ire6-s1.9.5] View 3: the Inter-Party governments did lay groundwork, through the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and public investment, that Fianna Fáil later built on. Planning tools were developed only late in the decade, so policy-makers earlier had little to work with.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement that pairs successes with failures and explains why governments kept changing. Instability and economic failure were linked: poor results brought defeat, and short-lived governments could not plan.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.0] Achievements: the Republic of Ireland Act completed the move to a republic, while the IDA, the Underdeveloped Areas Act and Marshall Aid laid foundations for later industrial policy. The anti-tuberculosis campaign saved lives.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.1] Limits: no government drew up a long-term plan, and each balance of payments crisis led to deflation, which made unemployment and emigration worse.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.2] Limits: the Mother and Child affair showed the Church's power over social policy, since the state could not act when the church and the medical profession opposed it.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.3] Contemporary view: voters punished each government in turn in 1951, 1954 and 1957 for high taxes and emigration, and the sharp swings between coalition and Fianna Fáil governments reflected that dissatisfaction.
[hist-ire6-s1.10.4] Later view: historians generally accept that the decade was one of slow growth and missed opportunities, but they differ about how far this was caused by policy mistakes rather than by Ireland's dependence on Britain.

CANDIDATES hist-ire6-s4: 4. Economic and social challenges of the 1970s and 1980s: Lynch, Haughey and FitzGerald
[hist-ire6-s4.0.b] The leaders of the 1970s and 1980s worked within a fixed party system and faced two new pressures, conflict in the North and a changing economy. Three ideas recur in this period, so each is defined below.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.0] Lynch became Taoiseach (prime minister) on 10 November 1966, after Seán Lemass resigned, and so inherited the economic boom of the 1960s.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.1] Three parties dominated politics: Fianna Fáil, Fine Gael and Labour. Fianna Fáil usually governed alone, so the alternative was a Fine Gael-Labour coalition (a government of two parties sharing power).
[hist-ire6-s4.0.2] The Troubles (the conflict in Northern Ireland) began in 1968-69, which forced the Republic to decide how to respond to violence on its border.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.3] Ireland, together with the United Kingdom, joined the EEC (the European Economic Community, the forerunner of the European Union) on 1 January 1973.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.4] The balance of payments is the record of a country's dealings with the rest of the world. A deficit means more money flows out, for example to pay for imports, than flows in.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.5] Liberalisation means relaxing legal or social controls, for example on contraception and divorce. Pluralism means a society of several groups and beliefs, which the state accommodates rather than favouring one.
[hist-ire6-s4.0.6] The national debt is the total the state owes. It rose rapidly in these years because governments paid for spending by borrowing.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.b] The decade that began with EEC entry brought a series of external shocks that exposed the weaknesses of the Irish economy. Ireland was a small economy that depended on imports and exports, so what happened abroad hit it hard.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.0] Ireland imported nearly all of its oil, so it was badly exposed when Arab oil producers (members of OPEC) cut supplies and raised prices after the Yom Kippur War of October 1973.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.1] The oil price rise pushed up inflation (the general rise in prices) in the mid-1970s and ended the long post-war boom in Europe, which reduced demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.2] The National Coalition of Fine Gael and Labour under Liam Cosgrave governed from March 1973 until 5 July 1977. It faced recession, rising unemployment and a large balance of payments deficit.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.3] The Iranian Revolution of 1979 caused a second oil shock, raising fuel costs and prices again. Inflation reached about 20 per cent around 1981.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.4] The European Monetary System (a scheme linking European currencies) began in March 1979. Ireland joined and Britain did not, so Ireland broke its link with sterling (the British pound) in 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.5] Governments responded to rising unemployment by raising public spending and borrowing, since the state hoped to protect jobs and services until growth returned. The national debt rose rapidly as a result.
[hist-ire6-s4.1.6] By the early 1980s a large share of tax revenue was being spent on interest on the national debt, which left less money for services.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.b] Lynch succeeded Lemass and led Fianna Fáil through the first years of the Troubles, then returned to power in 1977. His career shows a leader who was stronger on external affairs than on the economy.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.0] John Mary Lynch was born in Cork in 1917. He played hurling and football with Cork and won six All-Ireland medals in a row (1941-46: five in hurling, one in football), which made him famous; he qualified as a barrister and was first elected to the Dáil in 1948.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.1] He was Minister for Education from 1957, for Industry and Commerce from 1959 and for Finance from 1965, and became Taoiseach on 10 November 1966 as a compromise choice between rival candidates.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.2] He won the June 1969 general election with a majority of seats but lost the February 1973 election to the National Coalition of Fine Gael and Labour. He won again in June 1977 and was Taoiseach from 5 July 1977 to December 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.3] On 13 August 1969, after violence in Derry and Belfast, he said in a broadcast that the government could "no longer stand by". The army set up field hospitals near the border, but he ruled out sending troops across it.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.4] His Northern policy was unity by consent and opposition to violence. He condemned internment (jailing without trial) when it began on 9 August 1971, and after Bloody Sunday on 30 January 1972 called a day of mourning and recalled the Irish ambassador from London.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.5] After bombs in Dublin on 1 December 1972, so as to deal with the IRA, his government secured passage of the Offences Against the State (Amendment) Act to deal with the IRA.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.6] He held Fianna Fáil together through the Arms Crisis, but was brought down by the economic position after 1977 and by the loss of two by-elections in 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.7] Strengths: he was popular and conciliatory, kept the party together and stopped the state being drawn into the conflict in the North. Weaknesses: critics said he was indecisive, slow to remove Haughey and Blaney, and that the 1977 programme he led was financially reckless.
[hist-ire6-s4.2.8] Contemporaries admired his decency and moderate approach but doubted his firmness. Historians assess him as a skilful party manager and a moderating influence, whose record was stronger externally than internally and whose time was dominated by the North and the Arms Crisis.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.b] The Arms Crisis was the most serious split in Fianna Fáil during the Troubles and tested Lynch's authority.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.0] In April 1970 Special Branch (the police intelligence unit) told Lynch that senior ministers might be involved in a plan to import arms for Northern nationalists.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.1] On 6 May 1970 Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey, Minister for Finance, and Neil Blaney, Minister for Agriculture. Kevin Boland resigned from the cabinet in protest.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.2] Haughey, Blaney, Captain James Kelly of army intelligence, John Kelly from Belfast and the Belgian businessman Albert Luykx were charged with conspiracy to import arms illegally.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.3] The case against Blaney was dropped before the trial, and Haughey and the other accused were acquitted in October 1970, so no one was convicted.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.4] Haughey always maintained that the government had known about the plan, while Lynch's version was that he acted as soon as he had evidence. Historians still dispute who knew what.
[hist-ire6-s4.3.5] The crisis left a lasting division between Lynch's supporters and Haughey's in Fianna Fáil, which continued into the 1980s, and it kept Haughey out of the cabinet for the years that followed.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.b] Lynch returned to power in 1977 with a promise of more spending, and the consequences brought down his government within two and a half years.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.0] Fianna Fáil's manifesto for the June 1977 general election promised to abolish domestic rates (a local tax on property) and car tax, and to create jobs by increasing spending.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.1] The party won 84 of the 148 seats, then the largest majority in the history of the state. Lynch became Taoiseach on 5 July 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.2] The new government cut taxes and increased public spending, financed by borrowing, in the hope of lifting growth. This is why the current budget deficit and the national debt rose sharply.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.3] Growth was strong at first, but inflation, a second oil crisis and the cost of the manifesto turned the position into a crisis by 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.4] Fianna Fáil lost two by-elections in Cork in November 1979, which weakened Lynch's authority, and he announced his resignation in December 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.4.5] Haughey defeated George Colley for the leadership of the party by 44 votes to 38 and became Taoiseach on 11 December 1979.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.b] Haughey dominated Irish politics in the 1980s and divided opinion more than any other leader. He first warned that the country was overspending, then in 1987 made cuts that, with the Programme for National Recovery, helped prepare recovery.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.0] Charles Haughey was born in 1925 in Castlebar, Co. Mayo, trained as an accountant, became very wealthy and married Maureen Lemass, a daughter of Seán Lemass.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.1] He was Minister for Justice (1961-64), Agriculture (1964-66) and Finance (1966-70). Dismissed in the Arms Crisis, he rebuilt his support in the party in the 1970s, so he could return as Minister for Health and Social Welfare in 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.2] He was Taoiseach from 1979 to June 1981, from March to December 1982, and from March 1987 to 1992. His first term began on 11 December 1979 because he had defeated Colley for the leadership.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.3] In a television address on 9 January 1980 he said the country was "living away beyond our means" and called for sacrifice. Yet his government's spending and borrowing continued to rise.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.4] In 1987 he led a minority government (one without a majority of seats). Fine Gael under Alan Dukes promised not to oppose cuts if they stayed on course, the Tallaght Strategy of September 1987, so Minister for Finance Ray MacSharry could cut spending sharply.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.5] The Programme for National Recovery, agreed in October 1987 by the government, trade unions, employers and farmers, held pay increases low in return for reducing the deficit, so workers accepted restraint in exchange for a firmer economy.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.6] Strengths (which explain his loyal following): charisma, drive, loyalty from followers and a talent for tactics. Weaknesses: secrecy, extravagance and the use of power for personal advantage.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.7] Contemporaries divided between those who saw a bold nationalist who got things done and those who saw a corrupting influence. Tribunals in the 1990s and 2000s later found he had received large sums from businessmen.
[hist-ire6-s4.5.8] Historians judge him gifted but flawed: his record on the public finances was poor before 1987, but his later cuts and the PNR helped prepare recovery.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.b] Three general elections in eighteen months (June 1981, February 1982 and November 1982) produced weak governments and a sense of crisis. The reason was that no party or coalition held a secure majority.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.0] The June 1981 election produced a Fine Gael-Labour coalition under Garret FitzGerald, which depended on the votes of independent TDs.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.1] That government fell in January 1982 when its budget, which proposed VAT on children's clothes and shoes, was defeated. A new election followed in February 1982.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.2] Haughey returned as Taoiseach in March 1982 with the support of the Workers' Party, some independents and Tony Gregory, who won investment for inner-city Dublin in return.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.3] In 1982 a series of scandals followed: phone-tapping of journalists, a leadership challenge against Haughey in October, and on 14 August the arrest of the murderer Malcolm MacArthur in the apartment of the Attorney General, Patrick Connolly.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.4] The phrase "grotesque, unbelievable, bizarre and unprecedented" was applied to these events. Conor Cruise O'Brien turned it into the acronym GUBU, which became shorthand for the political culture of the time.
[hist-ire6-s4.6.5] Haughey's government fell in November 1982 when the Workers' Party and Tony Gregory withdrew their support, and FitzGerald returned as Taoiseach in December 1982.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.b] FitzGerald, an economist and Fine Gael leader, headed two coalitions with Labour and tried to modernise the state. He is remembered for his vision of a more tolerant Ireland more than for his economic results.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.0] Garret FitzGerald was born in 1926, the son of Desmond FitzGerald, a minister in the first Free State government. He worked as an economist and lecturer before becoming Minister for Foreign Affairs from 1973 to 1977.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.1] He became leader of Fine Gael in 1977 and was Taoiseach from June 1981 to March 1982 and from December 1982 to March 1987.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.2] In 1981 he announced a constitutional crusade to make the Republic more pluralist, with divorce reform and fewer Catholic influences on the law. The aim was to make it more attractive to Northern unionists.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.3] The crusade had limited results. The 1983 referendum inserted the Eighth Amendment, which FitzGerald had reservations about in that wording, and the 1986 divorce referendum was defeated.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.4] In 1985 his government liberalised the law on contraceptives (the Health (Family Planning) (Amendment) Act), allowing non-medical contraceptives to be sold without prescription to people over 18.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.5] He set up the New Ireland Forum in 1983 and signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Margaret Thatcher on 15 November 1985.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.6] His economic record was weak: his coalition tried to reduce the deficit but unemployment and the debt kept rising, and Labour left the government in January 1987 over spending cuts.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.7] Strengths: intellect, integrity and a vision of a modern, tolerant Ireland. Weaknesses: a tendency to overreach and difficulty holding a coalition together on economic policy.
[hist-ire6-s4.7.8] Contemporaries admired his honesty and energy but saw him as unworldly. Historians credit him with opening Irish politics to new ideas but judge his economic record the weakest part.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.b] FitzGerald hoped to liberalise the Constitution, but two referendums showed how far public opinion still lagged behind. Comparing them shows how limited pluralism remained.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.0] The Eighth Amendment, approved on 7 September 1983 by about two to one, acknowledged the right to life of the unborn, with due regard to the equal right to life of the mother.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.1] It was backed by anti-abortion campaigners, and FitzGerald had reservations about the wording, so it was not simply his own policy.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.2] The divorce referendum of 26 June 1986 sought to remove the constitutional ban on divorce and was defeated by about two to one.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.3] The results showed that the church still had great influence over social questions in the 1980s, and that liberalisation had not progressed as FitzGerald had hoped.
[hist-ire6-s4.8.4] Debates about pluralism and the Constitution in the 1980s are an important background to the changes of the 1990s.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.b] The economic problems of the early 1980s hit ordinary people directly through jobs, emigration and taxes. The public finances eventually forced a change of direction.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.0] Unemployment rose from about 7 per cent in 1979 to over 15 per cent by the mid-1980s, and the live register (the list of people claiming unemployment payments) reached about a quarter of a million by 1987.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.1] Emigration returned on a large scale, as jobs were scarce, with about 200,000 more people leaving than arriving over the decade. Many were well-educated young people heading for Britain, the United States and continental Europe.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.2] The national debt passed 100 per cent of national income by 1987, and interest payments absorbed a large part of tax revenue.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.3] Tax rates rose to pay for services, which discouraged investment and made the burden on workers heavy.
[hist-ire6-s4.9.4] Cuts in spending in 1987 and the PNR brought the deficit down, which laid the base for the recovery of the 1990s.
[hist-ire6-s4.10.b] Judging these leaders means weighing what each achieved against what each left undone. The best answers pair successes with limits and contemporary views with later ones.
[hist-ire6-s4.10.0] Lynch: success in keeping the state out of the Northern conflict and holding the party together, but a limit in leaving the economy in difficulty after 1977 because the 1977 programme relied on borrowing.
[hist-ire6-s4.10.1] Haughey: success in presiding over a turn towards financial discipline in 1987, but a limit in his earlier government, which was marked by deficits and scandal, such as GUBU.
[hist-ire6-s4.10.2] FitzGerald: success in putting pluralism and the Northern question on the agenda, but a limit in being unable to deliver social change in referendums or economic recovery.
[hist-ire6-s4.10.3] Contemporary view: voters repeatedly turned governments out, partly because each had to impose or defend painful measures.
[hist-ire6-s4.10.4] Later view: historians describe the 1980s as a lost decade for growth, though they see the late 1980s as the beginning of recovery. They debate how far credit belongs to Haughey, MacSharry, Dukes and the European funds.
