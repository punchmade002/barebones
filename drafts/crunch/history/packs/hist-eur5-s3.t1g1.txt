TARGET hist-eur5-s3: 3. British withdrawal from Palestine, the origins of the Arab–Israeli conflict and the Suez Crisis, 1956
[hist-eur5-s3.0.b] Before 1917 Palestine belonged to the Ottoman Empire. Britain's wartime deals about its future pointed in different directions, and that contradiction lies behind much of the later conflict.
[hist-eur5-s3.0.0] Palestine was part of the Ottoman Empire until Britain captured it in 1917-18, during the First World War. Arabs were the large majority of the population and Jews a small minority.
[hist-eur5-s3.0.1] Britain promised support to the Arabs in the McMahon-Hussein correspondence of 1915-16, between Sir Henry McMahon (British high commissioner in Egypt) and Hussein, sharif of Mecca, so that the Arabs would revolt against the Ottomans. Whether Palestine was included in the promised Arab independence was and is disputed.
[hist-eur5-s3.0.2] In 1916 the Sykes-Picot agreement between Britain and France planned to divide the Ottoman Middle East between them, which sat uneasily with promises of Arab independence.
[hist-eur5-s3.0.3] Britain then issued the Balfour Declaration in 1917, backing a Jewish national home in Palestine. Three wartime commitments therefore pulled in different directions, so Arabs and Zionists could each claim Britain had promised them Palestine.
[hist-eur5-s3.0.4] After the war the Mandate system, set up by the League of Nations (the international organisation of the 1920s), let a victorious power govern a former Ottoman territory on the League's behalf. Britain governed Palestine under it until 1948.
[hist-eur5-s3.0.5] The San Remo conference of April 1920 assigned the Mandate for Palestine to Britain, and the League confirmed it in 1922, so Britain's rule rested on international approval.
[hist-eur5-s3.1.b] Zionism supplied the aim of a Jewish homeland, and Britain's Declaration and Mandate gave it official backing. Both set the Jewish national movement against the Arab majority in the same land.
[hist-eur5-s3.1.0] Zionism was the movement for a Jewish national homeland in Palestine. It took its political form under Theodor Herzl, who led the first Zionist Congress at Basel in 1897.
[hist-eur5-s3.1.1] The Balfour Declaration of 2 November 1917 was a letter from Arthur Balfour, the British foreign secretary, to Lord Rothschild. It said Britain viewed ‘with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people’.
[hist-eur5-s3.1.2] The Declaration added a safeguard: nothing was to be done to prejudice the civil and religious rights of the existing non-Jewish communities. This was a promise to the Arab majority, but it left their political rights unmentioned and contradicted the wartime promises made to the Arabs.
[hist-eur5-s3.1.3] The Mandate for Palestine, granted to Britain, came into force in September 1923. Transjordan, the land east of the River Jordan, was separated from the area west of the river in the early 1920s, so the Mandate then covered Palestine west of the Jordan.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.b] As Jewish immigration grew, Arab opposition turned to open revolt. Britain tried partition first and then restriction, and satisfied neither side.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.0] Jews were about 11 per cent of Palestine's population in the 1922 census and roughly 30 per cent by 1939. This rise alarmed the Arab majority, who feared losing land and control of their country.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.1] Nazi rule in Germany from 1933 raised Jewish immigration sharply, because Jews were fleeing persecution and Palestine was the place Zionists wanted them to go.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.2] The Arab Revolt of 1936-39 was a rising against Mandate rule and Jewish immigration. British forces put it down. Haj Amin al-Husseini, the Mufti of Jerusalem, was the leading Palestinian Arab figure.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.3] The Peel Commission of 1937 proposed partition into a small Jewish state, an Arab state joined to Transjordan and a British-held enclave including Jerusalem. Arab leaders rejected it, and the British soon dropped it.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.4] The White Paper (a government policy statement) of 17 May 1939 limited Jewish immigration to 75,000 over five years, after which it would depend on Arab consent. It also restricted Jewish land purchases and promised an independent Palestine within ten years.
[hist-eur5-s3.2.5] Zionists rejected the White Paper because it closed Palestine to Jews just as persecution in Europe worsened. Many Arabs were also unhappy because it did not go further.
[hist-eur5-s3.3.b] Four terms recur in this topic. Examiners ask for each as a short definition plus an example, so learn a one-line definition for each.
[hist-eur5-s3.3.0] Zionism is the movement for a Jewish national homeland in Palestine. Theodor Herzl founded it in its political form, and its first Congress met at Basel in 1897.
[hist-eur5-s3.3.1] Terrorism is violence against civilians or political targets to create fear and advance a political aim. Jewish and Arab groups in Palestine both used it. Examples are the Irgun bombing of the King David Hotel in 1946 and the attack on Deir Yassin in 1948.
[hist-eur5-s3.3.2] Arab nationalism is the movement for Arab independence and unity, opposed to Western control and to the state of Israel. Nasser was its leading figure in the 1950s.
[hist-eur5-s3.3.3] Nationalisation is a government taking over a privately owned business or resource. Nasser nationalised the Suez Canal Company in 1956, so its profits went to Egypt.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.b] The Holocaust made the Jewish demand for a state far more urgent. Britain's immigration limits then led some Jewish groups to turn their weapons against British rule.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.0] The Holocaust killed about six million Jews in Europe between 1941 and 1945. It intensified the demand for a Jewish state and for refugee admission to Palestine, which Britain's White Paper limits blocked.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.1] The Haganah, founded in 1920, was the main Jewish defence force. It served the Jewish Agency (the body that represented the Jewish community), which David Ben-Gurion led.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.2] The Irgun was formed in 1931 by Revisionist Zionists and led from 1943 by Menachem Begin. Lehi, or the Stern Gang, was a small splinter group founded in 1940 by Avraham Stern. Both attacked British targets.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.3] From October 1945 to mid-1946 the Haganah, Irgun and Lehi co-operated in the Jewish Resistance Movement, although in 1944-45 the Haganah had handed Irgun members to the British in the 'Saison'.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.4] On 22 July 1946 the Irgun bombed the south wing of the King David Hotel in Jerusalem, headquarters of the British administration. It killed 91 people, Arabs, Britons and Jews. Britain condemned it and the Haganah distanced itself.
[hist-eur5-s3.4.5] In July 1947 the Irgun hanged two British sergeants in reprisal for the execution of its members, which showed how violent the conflict with British forces had become.
[hist-eur5-s3.5.b] Britain held out against admitting large numbers of refugees, and the cost of doing so grew. By 1947 the government wanted to end a commitment it could not make work.
[hist-eur5-s3.5.0] The Anglo-American Committee of Inquiry recommended in April 1946 that 100,000 Jewish refugees be admitted to Palestine. Britain refused to agree without Arab consent.
[hist-eur5-s3.5.1] In 1947 the ship Exodus carried about 4,500 Holocaust survivors. Britain turned it back and the passengers were returned to Germany. This drew world sympathy for the Zionist cause and damaged Britain's reputation.
[hist-eur5-s3.5.2] Britain kept tens of thousands of troops in Palestine, and holding the country was costly. Attacks on British forces, such as the King David Hotel bombing and the hanging of two sergeants, added to the pressure.
[hist-eur5-s3.5.3] No solution acceptable to both Arabs and Jews could be found, and the United States pressed Britain over the refugee question. Together these reasons explain why Britain decided in 1947 to give up its Mandate.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.b] Unable to reconcile Arab and Jewish claims, Britain handed the problem to the United Nations in 1947. The UN's partition plan was rejected by the Arabs, and Britain left the fighting to others.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.0] In February 1947 the British government, with Ernest Bevin, announced that it would refer the Palestine question to the United Nations. The UN Special Committee on Palestine (UNSCOP) recommended partition in 1947, and in September Britain announced that the Mandate would end.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.1] Resolution 181 of the UN General Assembly, passed on 29 November 1947 by 33 votes to 13 with 10 abstentions, divided Palestine into an Arab state and a Jewish state, with Jerusalem under international control. Britain abstained.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.2] The plan gave the Jewish state about 55 per cent of the land, although Jews were only about a third of the population. This is why Arabs saw it as unjust.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.3] The United States and the Soviet Union both voted for partition, an unusual agreement in the early Cold War.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.4] The Arab Higher Committee and the Arab states rejected partition, and fighting between Arab and Jewish forces began in December 1947.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.5] Britain refused to enforce partition itself, so no authority was left to keep order. The Mandate ended on 14 May 1948, the day Israel was proclaimed.
[hist-eur5-s3.6.6] In the civil war that followed, Jewish forces under Plan Dalet took Tiberias, Haifa and Jaffa in April-May 1948, and most of the Arab inhabitants of these cities fled.
[hist-eur5-s3.7.b] The Mandate's end and Israel's declaration were followed at once by invasion. The war that began decided the new state's shape and created the refugee problem.
[hist-eur5-s3.7.0] David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the State of Israel in Tel Aviv on 14 May 1948 and became prime minister. The United States recognised Israel within minutes, de facto, and the Soviet Union recognised it three days later. The declaration did not fix Israel's borders.
[hist-eur5-s3.7.1] Egypt, Jordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon sent forces into Palestine on 15 May 1948, the day after the Mandate ended, and the first Arab-Israeli war began. Israel survived and held more territory than the UN plan gave it.
[hist-eur5-s3.7.2] About 6,000 Israelis died in the war, roughly 1 per cent of the Jewish population, so the new state paid a heavy price for survival.
[hist-eur5-s3.7.3] On 9 April 1948 Irgun and Lehi fighters attacked the village of Deir Yassin near Jerusalem and killed about 100 to 120 villagers, although early reports said 254. Both sides used the attack for propaganda, and it fuelled panic among Arabs.
[hist-eur5-s3.7.4] Four days later Arab forces killed about 78 Jews in a convoy travelling to the Hadassah hospital on Mount Scopus.
[hist-eur5-s3.8.b] The war left a lasting refugee problem and new borders that no one fully accepted. It also shaped Israel as a state of immigration.
[hist-eur5-s3.8.0] About 700,000 Palestinian Arabs fled or were expelled from what became Israel; estimates range roughly from 600,000 to 750,000. Some left in fear, as after Deir Yassin, and some were driven out.
[hist-eur5-s3.8.1] UN General Assembly Resolution 194 of December 1948 said refugees who wished to return and live in peace should be allowed to. Israel has not accepted a general right of return, so the refugee question stayed unresolved.
[hist-eur5-s3.8.2] The armistice agreements of 1949 ended the fighting without a peace. Jordan held the West Bank, which it annexed in 1950 although few states recognised this, and Egypt administered the Gaza Strip.
[hist-eur5-s3.8.3] Israel's Law of Return of 1950 gave Jews the right to immigrate, which brought large numbers to the new state. Many Jews also left or were pushed out of Arab states after 1948.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.b] Nasser took power in Egypt in 1952 and set out to free Egypt from British influence. His clashes with Britain, Israel and the West then led towards the Suez Crisis.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.0] The Free Officers overthrew King Farouk on 23 July 1952. Gamal Abdul Nasser became the leading figure, became prime minister in April 1954 and was elected president by referendum in June 1956.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.1] The Anglo-Egyptian Agreement of October 1954 provided for British troops to leave the Suez Canal base, a British military base beside the canal. They had left by June 1956.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.2] Nasser attended the Bandung Conference of Asian and African states in April 1955 and supported anti-colonial movements in Algeria and elsewhere, so Arab nationalism for him meant opposing Western control.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.3] The Baghdad Pact of 1955 linked Britain, Iraq, Turkey, Iran and Pakistan. Nasser saw it as an attempt to isolate Egypt, so he opposed it.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.4] Egypt barred Israeli ships from the canal and the Straits of Tiran. Fedayeen (Palestinian raiders) attacked Israel from Gaza, and an Israeli raid on Gaza in February 1955 killed 38 people. This pushed Nasser to look for arms.
[hist-eur5-s3.9.5] In 1955 Nasser bought arms from the Soviet bloc through Czechoslovakia. This made Egypt a point of Cold War rivalry, because Western powers now feared Soviet influence in the Middle East.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.b] In 1956 the loss of Western funding for the Aswan High Dam pushed Nasser into a bold response. The canal he seized mattered greatly to Britain and France.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.0] Egypt recognised communist China in May 1956. The United States withdrew its offer to finance the Aswan High Dam on 19 July 1956 and Britain followed within days.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.1] The Suez Canal opened in 1869. Britain bought the Khedive's shares, about 44 per cent, in 1875 under Disraeli, and in the 1950s about two-thirds of Western Europe's oil passed through it. It was the sea route between Europe and Asia.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.2] On 26 July 1956 Nasser announced the nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company, the private company that owned the canal. The dam was to be financed from canal revenues, so the nationalisation answered the Western withdrawal.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.3] Anthony Eden was Britain's prime minister. Guy Mollet, France's prime minister, blamed Nasser for backing the Algerian rebels. Israel wanted the Tiran blockade ended and the fedayeen raids stopped.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.4] Diplomacy came first, but attempts to negotiate a settlement failed, so the three countries moved to military plans. The Anglo-French plan was called Operation Musketeer.
[hist-eur5-s3.10.5] The Soviet Union later helped to build the dam, so the Aswan project became a symbol of Egypt's turn towards the Soviet bloc.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.b] Britain, France and Israel prepared the attack in secret and gave it a public excuse. The invasion then followed in a set order.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.0] The Protocol of Sèvres was signed secretly near Paris on 24 October 1956 by Britain, France and Israel, with Ben-Gurion attending. Israel would attack Sinai; Britain and France would then demand that both sides withdraw and occupy the canal to ‘separate’ the combatants.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.1] This pretext was false, because the attack had been planned together. The secrecy later made the collusion a political scandal, which is why critics at home condemned it.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.2] 1. On 29 October Israel invaded Sinai.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.3] 2. On 30 October Britain and France issued an ultimatum telling both sides to withdraw about 10 miles from the canal. Israel accepted and Egypt refused.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.4] 3. On 31 October the Anglo-French bombing of Egyptian airfields began.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.5] 4. On 5 November British and French paratroopers landed at Port Said and Port Fuad, and on 6 November seaborne landings followed.
[hist-eur5-s3.11.6] 5. A ceasefire took effect at midnight on 6-7 November, while the Anglo-French force was still advancing down the canal.
[hist-eur5-s3.12.b] The invasion was stopped by pressure from the two superpowers and by the UN rather than by Egypt's army. Britain, dependent on American support, could not carry on.
[hist-eur5-s3.12.0] President Eisenhower of the United States opposed the action. Britain faced a run on the pound (heavy selling of the currency), and the US withheld support at the IMF (the International Monetary Fund) until Britain agreed to withdraw.
[hist-eur5-s3.12.1] Soviet leaders threatened rocket attacks on London and Paris on 5 November, and the Soviet Union supported Egypt in the UN.
[hist-eur5-s3.12.2] Soviet troops crushed the Hungarian rising on 4 November 1956. This weakened the Anglo-French complaint against Soviet behaviour at the UN and made Eisenhower angrier at his allies.
[hist-eur5-s3.12.3] The UN set up the United Nations Emergency Force (UNEF) in November 1956 on the proposal of Lester Pearson, Canada's foreign minister, to supervise the withdrawal. General E. L. M. Burns commanded it.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.b] Suez ended in political defeat for Britain and France and a victory for Nasser, although his army had lost. It also moved power in the Middle East to the United States.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.0] Eden resigned on 9 January 1957, in ill health and politically discredited, and Harold Macmillan succeeded him. The crisis showed that Britain could not act against American wishes, so Britain became more dependent on the US.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.1] Britain and France lost their position as the main Western powers in the Middle East. The Eisenhower Doctrine of January 1957 pledged US help to Middle Eastern states against communism, so the US took over the role.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.2] Egypt's army was beaten in Sinai, but Nasser kept the canal and became a hero of Arab nationalism. His prestige in the Arab world rose sharply because he had defied Britain, France and Israel.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.3] Egypt blocked the canal with a number of sunken ships. A UN clearance operation reopened it in April 1957, under Egyptian control, and Egypt seized British and French property.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.4] Israel withdrew from Sinai and Gaza by March 1957 after US assurances about the Straits of Tiran, so Israel gained some security. UNEF was stationed on the Sinai frontier.
[hist-eur5-s3.13.5] The Iraqi monarchy, Britain's close ally, was overthrown in July 1958, and Iraq left the Baghdad Pact in 1959. This showed how British influence among Arab states was falling.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.b] Ben-Gurion led the Jewish community in Palestine and became the first prime minister of Israel. He was a labour Zionist leader who shaped the state as it was founded.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.0] Born David Grün in 1886 in Plonsk in the Russian Empire, he emigrated to Ottoman Palestine in 1906 and became a leader of labour Zionism.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.1] He led the Jewish Agency executive from 1935 and accepted the UN partition plan of 1947 as a first step towards a Jewish state.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.2] He was shaped by events: the Holocaust and the White Paper shaped his outlook, and the war of 1948 tested the new state.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.3] He proclaimed Israel on 14 May 1948 as a participant and was prime minister in 1948-54 and 1955-63, also holding the defence ministry. He retired for a time to Sde Boker kibbutz.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.4] He created the Israel Defence Forces in May 1948 and ordered the shelling of the Irgun arms ship Altalena in June 1948 to assert a single army. In October 1956 he attended the secret talks at Sèvres with Britain and France.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.5] Contemporaries differed: most Jews saw him as the founder of the state, while Arabs and Palestinians saw him as the architect of a state that displaced them.
[hist-eur5-s3.14.6] Historians of the ‘new history’ school such as Benny Morris argue there was no overall expulsion plan in 1948 but that expulsions took place, including from Lydda and Ramle in July 1948. How far Ben-Gurion approved them is debated.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.b] Nasser led Egypt from 1952 and became the most important figure of Arab nationalism. The Suez Crisis was the central event of his career.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.0] Born on 15 January 1918 in Alexandria, he trained as an army officer and was one of the Free Officers who overthrew King Farouk in 1952.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.1] He was prime minister from 1954 and president from 1956. He pursued land reform and the Aswan High Dam, and nationalised the canal. He formed the United Arab Republic with Syria in 1958-61.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.2] He was shaped by events: the Gaza raid, the Baghdad Pact and the withdrawal of the dam offer each pushed him towards confrontation.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.3] In the Suez Crisis he was a participant. His army was defeated in Sinai, but he survived the attack with control of the canal confirmed.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.4] Eden and some British commentators compared him with Mussolini and Hitler, because he seemed an aggressive dictator, while Arab nationalists saw a hero who had defied the West.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.5] Historians credit him with land reform, the dam and Arab self-confidence. They criticise his one-party rule, the suppression of the Muslim Brotherhood and the defeat of 1967.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.6] He intervened in the Yemeni civil war from 1962 and blocked the Straits of Tiran in 1967. Egypt lost Sinai in the Six-Day War of June 1967.
[hist-eur5-s3.15.7] He offered to resign after the defeat, withdrew the offer after mass demonstrations, and died on 28 September 1970.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.b] Examiners want a judgement about how far Britain caused the troubles of the Middle East and how far its actions succeeded. Use each pair of points to argue both sides.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.0] Britain's contribution, limit: the Mandate's contradictory promises, its handling of immigration, the 1939 White Paper and the abrupt withdrawal in 1948 all left a legacy of refugees and hostility.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.1] Britain was not the only cause, because Arab and Jewish nationalism, the Holocaust, the US and UN, Arab rejection of partition and Nasser's own actions also drove events.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.2] Withdrawal, achievement: Britain ended a costly commitment and passed the question to the UN instead of fighting both communities, as the attacks on British forces had become too great.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.3] Withdrawal, limit: the Mandate's conflicting promises to Arabs and Jews were not reconciled, so the 1948 war left refugees and hostility.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.4] Suez, achievement: Israel overran Sinai in days and the Anglo-French force was advancing down the canal before the ceasefire, so the operation was largely successful as far as it went.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.5] Suez, limit: the operation failed politically. The ceasefire was forced by the United States, the Soviet threat and the UN, and the canal remained in Egyptian hands.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.6] Contemporary view: Eden presented the action as a police action to separate Israel and Egypt, while Labour leader Hugh Gaitskell condemned it and Anthony Nutting, a Foreign Office minister, resigned in protest.
[hist-eur5-s3.16.7] Later view: many historians treat Suez as the moment that exposed the end of Britain's great-power pretensions, while others argue that decolonisation was already under way.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s1: 1. World War II and Asia; case study: British withdrawal from India, 1945–1947
[hist-eur5-s1.0.b] To follow the withdrawal of 1945–47 you need to know what Britain was leaving. India was Britain's largest colony, ruled by an outside power, and by the 1930s its nationalists were demanding self-rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.0] Britain ruled India directly through the Crown from 1858, after the rising of 1857; this system was called the Raj. The Viceroy (the Crown's representative in India) headed the government.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.1] Over 500 princely states (Indian-ruled states under British paramountcy, meaning Britain controlled their foreign affairs and defence) remained outside direct British rule, so any transfer of power also had to settle their future.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.2] The Indian National Congress, founded in 1885, began by petitioning the British for a say in government, but by the 1930s it was demanding self-rule, which is why it became the main opponent of the Raj.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.3] Britain granted reforms in stages, in 1919 and 1935, giving Indians a growing share in provincial government without handing over control of India as a whole.
[hist-eur5-s1.0.4] Hindus formed the majority, and Muslims were about a quarter of the population of British India, concentrated in the north-west and the east. Many Muslims feared their rights would be weak under majority rule, and this fear underlies the later demand for Pakistan.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.b] Three ideas run through this whole topic. Learn them as they are used here, because they are used throughout.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.0] Colonial rule is control of a territory by an outside power. It is governed for the benefit of the imperial state, usually without the consent of the people ruled, which is why nationalists called it illegitimate.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.1] De-colonisation is the ending of European rule and the creation of independent states, mainly from 1945 to the 1960s. India's independence in 1947 was one of its first and largest cases.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.2] The (British) Commonwealth is an association of Britain and its former dominions and colonies. A dominion is a self-governing state that keeps the Crown as head of state; dominion status was what Britain offered India in 1942 and what India and Pakistan first received in 1947.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.3] In 1949 the Commonwealth changed so that republics could stay in; the London Declaration of April 1949 let India remain a member as a republic.
[hist-eur5-s1.1.4] Partition means dividing British India into two states, India and Pakistan, along religious lines. Other terms used below are satyagraha (non-violent resistance), Quit India, the Cabinet Mission, the Muslim League and the Indian National Congress.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.b] In the war against Japan, European armies lost one colony after another within months. This weakened the claim that Europeans were strong enough to rule Asians, and it gave nationalists new arguments.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.0] Japan attacked Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941 (8 December in local time in Asia) and landed in British Malaya the same day. Hong Kong surrendered on 25 December 1941.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.1] Singapore, Britain's main base in the East, surrendered on 15 February 1942. About 80,000 British, Indian and Australian troops became prisoners of war, so the defeat was not only large but humiliating.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.2] Japan overran Burma in 1942, closing the Burma Road that supplied China and threatening India's eastern frontier, so the war now reached the edge of India itself.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.3] Japan also overran the Dutch East Indies in early 1942.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.4] Japanese troops entered northern Indochina in 1940, and Japan removed the French administration on 9 March 1945.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.5] Japanese propaganda promoted a ‘Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere’ and the slogan ‘Asia for the Asians’. This encouraged anti-colonial nationalists across the region.
[hist-eur5-s1.2.6] In short, European rule in Asia had been shown to be breakable, and Britain emerged exhausted. This is the main implication of the war for Britain's place in Asia.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.b] India was not only a battlefield but a source of soldiers and supplies. The war changed India's army, its politics and its attitude to British rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.0] About 2.5 million men served in the British Indian Army during the war, all of them volunteers, and the army expanded greatly as a result.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.1] The Indian National Army (INA) was first formed in 1942 under Mohan Singh from Indian prisoners taken at Singapore. Subhas Chandra Bose, a former Congress president, took it over in 1943, and it fought alongside Japan.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.2] Bose died in August 1945. After the war the British put INA officers on trial at the Red Fort in Delhi, beginning in November 1945, because they had fought against the Crown.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.3] The Red Fort trials caused a public outcry that united Congress and League opinion, so punishing the officers was politically risky. The sentences were not carried out.
[hist-eur5-s1.3.4] The Bengal famine of 1943 killed an estimated two to three million people and damaged British prestige in India. Wartime requisitioning, the loss of Burmese rice imports, inflation and a poor government response all contributed, and historians debate the causes.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.b] Mohandas Gandhi turned Congress from a body that petitioned into a mass movement. His method was non-violent civil disobedience.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.0] Gandhi returned from South Africa in January 1915 and became the dominant Congress leader after 1920.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.1] Satyagraha, meaning ‘truth-force’, was non-violent civil disobedience. Gandhi first used it in India in peasant campaigns at Champaran in Bihar (1917, indigo growers) and Kheda in Gujarat (1918, over land revenue).
[hist-eur5-s1.4.2] At Amritsar on 13 April 1919, General Dyer ordered troops to fire on an unarmed crowd at Jallianwala Bagh. The British counted 379 dead and Indian estimates exceed 1,000, so say hundreds or give both; it turned many Indians against British rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.3] The Non-Cooperation movement of 1920–22 urged boycotts of British goods, courts, schools and councils. After the Chauri Chaura incident of February 1922, when a mob killed policemen, Gandhi called it off and was jailed in 1922.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.4] The Salt March ran from 12 March to 6 April 1930, about 240 miles (about 385 km) from the Sabarmati ashram near Ahmedabad to Dandi on the coast, where Gandhi made salt in defiance of the British salt monopoly.
[hist-eur5-s1.4.5] Tens of thousands were arrested in the civil disobedience that followed the Salt March, which shows how far the movement had spread.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.b] Constitutional reform brought Congress into government for the first time. The outbreak of war then tested the relationship again, and each wartime offer or demand ended in confrontation.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.0] The Government of India Act 1935 gave Indians self-government in the provinces. In the elections of early 1937, in eleven provinces, Congress won outright majorities in five and went on to form ministries (provincial governments) in seven.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.1] On 3 September 1939 the Viceroy, Lord Linlithgow, declared war on India's behalf without consulting Indian leaders. Congress ministers resigned in October–November 1939 in protest.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.2] The Cripps Mission of March 1942 offered dominion status after the war, with the right to secede and the right of provinces to opt out. Congress rejected it because it allowed provinces to opt out and gave no immediate control of defence, so the offer was withdrawn.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.3] On 8 August 1942 at Bombay Congress passed the Quit India resolution, demanding British withdrawal. Gandhi's slogan was ‘Do or die’.
[hist-eur5-s1.5.4] Gandhi and the Congress leaders were arrested on 9 August. Widespread unrest and sabotage followed and were crushed within weeks; Gandhi was released in May 1944 because of ill health.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.b] Partition grew out of the Muslim League's campaign for separate Muslim states. The Congress leadership would not accept this for the whole of India, so each side's claim to speak for India's Muslims became the central dispute.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.0] The All-India Muslim League was founded at Dhaka in December 1906 to protect Muslim political interests, twenty-one years after Congress.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.1] Muhammad Ali Jinnah, a barrister and one-time Congress member, took the League's leadership in 1934 and was known as Quaid-i-Azam (great leader).
[hist-eur5-s1.6.2] The name Pakistan was coined in 1933 by Choudhry Rahmat Ali, a student at Cambridge. The League did poorly among Muslim voters in the 1937 elections.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.3] The League used the years of Congress rule to claim that Muslims could not be safe in a Hindu-majority state. Jinnah called a Day of Deliverance on 22 December 1939, when the Congress ministries resigned.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.4] The Lahore Resolution of 23 March 1940 called for independent states in the Muslim-majority areas of the north-west and east of India. It never used the word Pakistan.
[hist-eur5-s1.6.5] Jinnah argued that Hindus and Muslims were two nations, and that Congress claimed to speak for all Indians while representing mainly Hindus. This is the two-nation argument.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.b] By 1945 the League could claim real support. This made it impossible for Congress or Britain to bypass it, but it also exposed the hardest problem, the provinces where no clean division was possible.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.0] The Simla Conference, held from 25 June to 14 July 1945 and called by Viceroy Wavell, broke down because Jinnah insisted that only the League could name the Muslim members of the Viceroy's council.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.1] In the 1945–46 elections the League won all 30 Muslim seats in the central assembly and the great majority of Muslim provincial seats, which gave it a claim to speak for Muslim India. Congress won most general seats.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.2] Punjab (Muslim majority, with large Hindu and Sikh minorities) and Bengal (Muslim majority, with a large Hindu minority) had mixed populations, so any border would leave large minorities on the wrong side.
[hist-eur5-s1.7.3] For this reason Punjab and Bengal became the central problem in the partition settlement, and they were the places where the worst violence later occurred.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.b] The Labour government that took office in 1945 was committed to Indian self-rule, and Britain's post-war weakness made it hard to resist. Within a year events in India confirmed that the old system could not continue.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.0] Clement Attlee's Labour Party won a landslide in the general election of July 1945, and Attlee became prime minister on 26 July 1945, committed to self-rule for India.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.1] Britain had spent heavily on the war. It borrowed about 3.75 billion dollars from the United States in 1945–46, and its debts and the cost of occupation left it unable to afford a large army in India.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.2] By 1945 Britain also owed India over one billion pounds in sterling balances (credits for wartime supplies), which reversed the old financial relationship and reduced the economic value of the Raj.
[hist-eur5-s1.8.3] In February 1946 (18–23 February) ratings of the Royal Indian Navy mutinied at Bombay over pay, conditions and racial discrimination. The mutiny spread to other ports and was suppressed, but it showed that the armed forces could no longer be relied on to support British rule.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.b] In 1946 Britain tried to agree a plan for a united India with both parties. The attempt failed, and the failure turned into communal violence.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.0] The Cabinet Mission (Pethick-Lawrence, Cripps and A. V. Alexander) was in India from March to June 1946. Its plan of 16 May 1946 proposed a loose federation with three groups of provinces and a weak centre, so it rejected the full Pakistan demand.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.1] Both sides accepted the plan at first, then disagreed over the grouping of provinces, so the League withdrew its acceptance in July 1946 and the mission failed to win agreement.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.2] The League called Direct Action Day for 16 August 1946. Communal killings in Calcutta left thousands dead (estimates range from about 4,000 to 10,000) and many more injured.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.3] Violence spread to Noakhali in East Bengal and Bihar in October–November 1946, and to the Punjab in spring 1947, so fear of civil war grew.
[hist-eur5-s1.9.4] An interim government headed by Jawaharlal Nehru took office on 2 September 1946, and the League joined it on 26 October 1946. The two parties still could not work together.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.b] Early in 1947 Britain set a deadline and sent a new Viceroy to meet it. Once Mountbatten judged that unity was impossible, the timetable was shortened sharply.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.0] On 20 February 1947 Attlee announced that British rule would end by June 1948 at the latest, and Lord Wavell was replaced as Viceroy by Lord Mountbatten.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.1] Mountbatten, Attlee's choice, was a royal cousin and wartime Supreme Allied Commander in South-East Asia. He arrived in Delhi on 22 March 1947 and was sworn in as Viceroy on 24 March.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.2] He judged that a united India was no longer possible and pressed for a quick transfer of power, partly because he feared civil war if he delayed.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.3] Under the 3 June 1947 plan the assemblies of Punjab and Bengal voted on dividing their provinces, and a referendum in the North-West Frontier Province and Sylhet decided their future.
[hist-eur5-s1.10.4] The plan accepted partition. The date of 15 August 1947 was announced on 4 June, ten months earlier than the June 1948 deadline, once the principal Indian leaders agreed.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.b] Independence on 15 August 1947 created two new states and divided the Punjab and Bengal. Because the borders had to be drawn so quickly, the settlement was rushed.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.0] The Indian Independence Act received royal assent on 18 July 1947 and ended British rule, creating two independent dominions, India and Pakistan.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.1] Power was transferred at midnight on 14–15 August 1947 (Pakistan's day was 14 August, India's 15 August). Jinnah became Governor-General of Pakistan, Mountbatten stayed as India's first Governor-General and Nehru became prime minister.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.2] Pakistan was formed in two parts, West Pakistan and East Bengal (renamed East Pakistan in 1955), separated by about 1,600 km of Indian territory.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.3] Cyril Radcliffe, a London barrister who had never been to India, chaired the commissions that drew the Punjab and Bengal boundaries. He had about five weeks, and the award was completed about 12 August.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.4] The Radcliffe Line was published on 17 August 1947, two days after independence.
[hist-eur5-s1.11.5] The Indian Army, the civil service, the railways and other assets had to be divided between the two dominions in a few weeks, which put the administration under heavy strain.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.b] Partition caused mass forced migration, with killing on a huge scale. Alongside it came a dispute over the princely states, which has continued to the present.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.0] About 10 to 15 million people were displaced as Hindus and Sikhs moved from West Pakistan into India and Muslims moved into Pakistan. Give the range, because nobody knows the exact number.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.1] Communal violence in the Punjab and Bengal killed many people. Estimates of the death toll range from about 200,000 to about 2 million, so do not pick a single figure.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.2] The Punjab was the worst affected area, and trains carrying refugees were attacked, which is why the journeys themselves became a source of mass death.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.3] The princely states, over 500 in number, had to join India or Pakistan. The Nizam of Hyderabad and the Maharaja of Kashmir were the most contested cases.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.4] Hyderabad was a Hindu-majority state ruled by a Muslim Nizam, so its choice was disputed, and India annexed it by military action in September 1948.
[hist-eur5-s1.12.5] Kashmir had a Muslim-majority population under a Hindu Maharaja, Hari Singh. After a tribal invasion from Pakistan he signed the Instrument of Accession to India on 26 October 1947, and Indian troops were flown in, so fighting over Kashmir between India and Pakistan began that year.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.b] Independence did not end the conflicts that partition created, but India and Britain found a lasting arrangement. India stayed in the Commonwealth as a republic.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.0] Gandhi fasted in Calcutta (September 1947) and Delhi (January 1948) to stop communal violence. He was shot dead in Delhi on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.1] Jinnah, Governor-General of Pakistan, died in September 1948, only a year after the state he had campaigned for was created.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.2] The first Kashmir war of 1947–49 ended with a UN-brokered ceasefire from 1 January 1949, leaving a line that divided the state and is still disputed.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.3] The London Declaration of April 1949 allowed India to become a republic while remaining in the Commonwealth, accepting the King as symbolic head of the association.
[hist-eur5-s1.13.4] India became a republic on 26 January 1950, with Jawaharlal Nehru as prime minister, and under Nehru followed non-alignment (staying out of the rival blocs of the Cold War).
[hist-eur5-s1.13.5] India remained a major trading partner of Britain, with the sterling area (a group of countries whose currencies were tied to the pound) linking the two economies.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.b] The end of the Raj mattered differently to each side. For Britain it was a loss of power and a change in the Commonwealth, while for India and Pakistan it brought both freedom and a long legacy of conflict.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.0] For Britain, India was the largest and most valuable of its possessions, so its loss ended the Raj, which had ruled India directly since 1858.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.1] The loss reduced Britain's role in Asia and made the Commonwealth its main continuing link with former colonies.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.2] Burma (January 1948) and Ceylon (February 1948) became independent soon afterwards, so India's independence was an example for other colonies.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.3] India's decision to remain in the Commonwealth as a republic established that membership did not depend on accepting the monarch as head of state.
[hist-eur5-s1.14.4] For India and Pakistan the withdrawal brought independence and, in India, democracy, but it also brought partition, mass migration, killing and the Kashmir dispute.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.b] Historians offer several causes, and the best answers weigh them rather than listing them. The main explanations can be set out as paired causes.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.0] Nationalist explanation: decades of Congress agitation, from non-cooperation to Quit India, made British rule impossible to maintain without a degree of repression the British public would not accept.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.1] Economic explanation: Britain was exhausted by war and owed India over one billion pounds in sterling balances, which reversed the old financial relationship and reduced the value of holding the Raj.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.2] Political explanation: Labour's 1945 government was committed to Indian self-rule, and Attlee argued that Britain could not hold India against the will of its people.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.3] Security explanation: the INA trials and the 1946 naval mutiny led some British officials, including Viceroy Wavell, to doubt that the Indian Army and police could be relied on to suppress a mass rising.
[hist-eur5-s1.15.4] Communal explanation: the failure of Congress and the League to agree, and the violence in Calcutta, Bihar and the Punjab, led Mountbatten to fear civil war if he delayed.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.b] Leaving India proved far harder than deciding to leave. Each difficulty can be tied to a date and a person.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.0] Congress and the League could not agree: the Cabinet Mission failed in 1946 and the interim government could not work as a unit.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.1] Communal violence, beginning with Direct Action Day in Calcutta in August 1946 and spreading to Noakhali, Bihar and the Punjab, made the transfer of power more dangerous.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.2] The princely states, over 500 in number, had to be brought into India or Pakistan, and Hyderabad and Kashmir were disputed.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.3] Speed and the boundary: Mountbatten's critics argue that bringing the date forward by ten months left too little time to plan the boundary and policing, while his defenders argue that delay would have meant more violence.
[hist-eur5-s1.16.4] Radcliffe had about five weeks to draw the Punjab and Bengal boundaries, and the army, civil service and railways were divided in a few weeks, so the administration was under severe strain.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.b] Gandhi was the leading figure of the Indian independence movement and the most influential Indian voice in the British withdrawal. His life and methods show what non-violent mass politics could and could not achieve.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.0] Gandhi was born on 2 October 1869 at Porbandar in Gujarat. He trained as a barrister at the Inner Temple in London (called to the bar in 1891) and practised law in South Africa from 1893 to 1914.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.1] In South Africa he led campaigns against discriminatory laws affecting Indians, and developed the method of non-violent resistance that he later called satyagraha.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.2] After 1915 he built a mass movement among peasants and urban workers, wore simple dress and promoted khadi (hand-spun cloth) as a symbol of self-reliance.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.3] His stated aims were swaraj (self-rule), Hindu–Muslim unity, ending untouchability and non-violence. In Hind Swaraj (1909) he rejected industrial civilisation, and he called the untouchables Harijans, a term now regarded as patronising.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.4] At the London Round Table Conference of autumn 1931 he attended as Congress's sole representative; the talks failed over separate electorates and minorities.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.5] After the 1932 Communal Award gave Dalits separate electorates, Gandhi fasted in Yerwada jail, and the Poona Pact (September 1932) gave Dalits reserved seats in general electorates instead. B. R. Ambedkar led the Dalit side and remained a critic.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.6] He took part in every major Congress campaign and was jailed several times, including from August 1942 to May 1944.
[hist-eur5-s1.17.7] In late 1946 he walked through villages in Noakhali to calm communal violence. On 15 August 1947 he was in Calcutta fasting and praying rather than celebrating independence, and he was shot dead on 30 January 1948.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.b] Gandhi was seen very differently by those who lived through the period, and historians still disagree about how much he achieved. This is the material for any judgement of his importance.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.0] Many Indians revered him as the Mahatma (great soul).
[hist-eur5-s1.18.1] British officials often treated him as an obstacle, and Jinnah saw him as a Hindu leader who spoke for Hindus, not all Indians.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.2] Ambedkar was one of his sharpest critics, because he led the Dalit side in the clash over separate electorates.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.3] Judith Brown, in Gandhi: Prisoner of Hope (1989), presents him as a skilled leader who built a mass movement but whose achievement was limited.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.4] Other historians stress war, the INA, naval unrest, economics and Labour's policy over Gandhi's role, and Yasmin Khan, in The Great Partition (2007), stresses the haste and the part played by the state in the violence.
[hist-eur5-s1.18.5] Critics also point to his limited success on caste and his failure to prevent partition. In 1947 he was a participant, not the decision-maker, so he could not stop it.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.b] A good judgement sets what the independence movement achieved against what it could not do. The points below pair each achievement with a limit, and end with the views of the time and of later historians.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.0] Achievement: non-violent mass protest became the central method of Indian politics, and campaigns such as the Salt March drew in very large numbers of people.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.1] Limit: the Gandhian campaigns did not secure a united India, and could not prevent partition or the violence that followed.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.2] Achievement: the Quit India movement and the 1942 unrest showed the British that their authority could not rest on force alone.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.3] Limit: partition was agreed by the Congress and League leaders in June 1947, not by Gandhi, who opposed it, so the movement did not control the outcome.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.4] Achievement: the transfer of power ended British rule without a colonial war of the kind France fought in Indochina and Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.5] Limit: withdrawal was rushed in 1947, and the boundary settlement was made within weeks, which left the population to bear the cost.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.6] Limit: the Gandhian method relied on the moral restraint of the British government and on disciplined followers, and it did not stop the communal killings that followed Direct Action Day in 1946.
[hist-eur5-s1.19.7] Contemporary view: Gandhi was revered as the Mahatma by many Indians but criticised by Jinnah and Ambedkar. Later view: many historians judge Mountbatten's haste and the rushed Radcliffe work as major contributors to the scale of the violence.
