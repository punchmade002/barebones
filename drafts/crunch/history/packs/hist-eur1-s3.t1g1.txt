[hist-eur1-s2.1.3] Nationalists wanted a German nation state, but disagreed whether it should include the German lands of the Habsburg Empire.

[hist-eur1-s2.2.7] Peasant risings in the south-west forced governments to begin abolishing feudal dues.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.0] The Vorparlament (pre-parliament), a self-appointed gathering of liberal politicians, met at Frankfurt from 31 March to 3 April 1848.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.1] It rejected the radicals' call for a republic and arranged elections to a national assembly by broad manhood suffrage, although each state set its own rules and some excluded servants and men on poor relief.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.2] Disappointed radicals in Baden, led by Friedrich Hecker and Gustav Struve, launched a republican rising in April 1848, which federal troops defeated at Kandern on 20 April.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.3] The Frankfurt National Assembly opened on 18 May 1848 in the Paulskirche (St Paul's Church).
[hist-eur1-s2.3.4] About 585 deputies were elected, and some 800 men sat at some point including substitutes; most were lawyers, judges, civil servants, teachers and professors, so it was nicknamed the professors' parliament.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.5] Craftsmen were few, and there were almost no peasants or workers among the deputies.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.6] The Assembly elected Heinrich von Gagern, a moderate liberal from Hesse-Darmstadt, as its president.
[hist-eur1-s2.3.7] On 29 June 1848 it chose Archduke John of Austria as Reichsverweser (imperial regent) at the head of a Provisional Central Power, but this government had no army, civil service or tax revenue of its own.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.0] The Basic Rights of the German People, passed in December 1848, guaranteed equality before the law, freedom of the press, speech, religion, assembly and movement, and trial by jury.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.1] They abolished the nobility as a privileged estate, landlords' private courts and feudal dues, and abolished the death penalty except under martial law.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.2] Grossdeutsch (Greater German): a Germany including the German-speaking Habsburg lands, favoured by Catholics, Austrians and many southerners.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.3] Kleindeutsch (Lesser German): a Germany united under Prussia without Austria, favoured by Gagern and most Protestant northern liberals.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.4] The Grossdeutsch plan failed when Austria's new chief minister, Prince Felix Schwarzenberg, insisted in March 1849 that the whole multinational Habsburg Empire must enter any German state or none of it would.
[hist-eur1-s2.4.5] The Assembly also claimed mixed areas ruled by others, such as parts of Prussian Poland (Posen), showing that liberal nationalism could deny other nations' rights.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.0] The duchies of Schleswig and Holstein were ruled by the King of Denmark; Holstein, wholly German, belonged to the German Confederation, while Schleswig had a mixed Danish and German population and did not.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.1] When the new Danish king, Frederick VII, moved to incorporate Schleswig into Denmark in March 1848, the German population rose and set up a provisional government at Kiel.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.2] Prussian troops under General Wrangel invaded on the Confederation's behalf, which the Assembly hailed as a national war.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.3] Under pressure from Britain and Russia, Prussia signed the Malmö armistice in August 1848 without consulting Frankfurt.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.4] The Assembly first rejected the armistice and then, having no army of its own, accepted it on 16 September 1848.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.5] Radicals rioted in Frankfurt on 18 September and murdered two conservative deputies, Prince Felix Lichnowsky and General Hans von Auerswald, and the Assembly had to be saved by Prussian and Austrian troops.
[hist-eur1-s2.5.6] Fighting resumed in 1849, but Prussia made peace with Denmark in 1850, the duchies' army was defeated at Idstedt that July, and the London Protocol of 1852 confirmed the Danish king's rule.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.b] By late 1848 the old governments had regained control of their capitals through force, while the liberals at Frankfurt were still debating.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.0] In June 1848 the Austrian general Windischgrätz bombarded Prague and crushed the Czech rising, and in July Radetzky defeated Piedmont at Custoza.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.1] A radical rising in Vienna in October 1848 was crushed when Windischgrätz and the Croatian commander Jelačić retook the city on 31 October.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.2] Robert Blum, a radical Frankfurt deputy from Saxony, was shot in Vienna on 9 November 1848, despite his immunity as a member of the Assembly.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.3] Schwarzenberg became Austrian chief minister in November, and on 2 December 1848 the 18-year-old Franz Joseph replaced Emperor Ferdinand.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.4] In Berlin, Frederick William IV appointed a conservative ministry under Count Brandenburg, and General Wrangel's troops entered the city on 10 November 1848 without resistance.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.5] On 5 December 1848 the king dissolved the Prussian National Assembly and imposed a constitution of his own, which kept royal control of the army and ministers.
[hist-eur1-s2.6.6] In May 1849 Prussia brought in the three-class franchise, which divided voters into three classes by the taxes they paid; each class chose a third of the electors, so a small wealthy minority had a vastly greater say than the poor majority.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.0] The Frankfurt constitution, completed on 27–28 March 1849, created a federal German Empire with a hereditary emperor, a lower house elected by manhood suffrage and an upper house representing the states.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.1] On 28 March 1849 the Assembly elected Frederick William IV Emperor of the Germans by 290 votes, with 248 deputies abstaining.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.2] Frederick William gave an evasive answer to the Assembly's delegation on 3 April and formally refused the crown on 28 April 1849, because he would not accept a crown from an elected assembly rather than from his fellow princes.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.3] In private he scorned it as a crown picked up from the gutter of revolution.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.4] Austria rejected the constitution and recalled its deputies in April 1849.
[hist-eur1-s2.7.5] Twenty-eight smaller states accepted the constitution by mid-April, but Prussia, Bavaria, Saxony and Hanover rejected it, so it never came into force.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.0] In May 1849 radicals rose in Saxony, the Palatinate and Baden to force the princes to accept the constitution; Richard Wagner and Mikhail Bakunin took part in the Dresden rising.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.1] Prussian troops crushed the Dresden rising in May and the Baden and Palatinate risings by July 1849, when the fortress of Rastatt surrendered and many rebels were executed.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.2] The moderates left Frankfurt, and about 100 radical deputies moved to Stuttgart as a rump parliament until Württemberg troops dispersed them on 18 June 1849.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.3] Thousands of revolutionaries emigrated, many to the United States, where the Forty-Eighters included Hecker and Carl Schurz.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.4] Prussia's Erfurt Union plan, drawn up by Joseph von Radowitz, tried in 1849–50 to unite Germany under Prussia by agreement of the princes, and an Erfurt Parliament met in March–April 1850.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.5] Austria, backed by Russia, threatened war, and by the Punctation of Olmütz (29 November 1850) Prussia abandoned the union; Prussians remembered it as a humiliation.
[hist-eur1-s2.8.6] The old Confederation and its Diet were restored in 1851 under Austrian presidency, with Bismarck as Prussia's delegate at Frankfurt from that year.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.0] The Frankfurt Assembly had no army, money or administration, so it depended on the very governments it was trying to limit.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.1] The rulers kept control of their armies and bureaucracies, which stayed loyal and were used to retake Prague, Vienna and Berlin once the shock of March had passed.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.2] Liberals were divided from radicals and workers: middle-class deputies feared a social revolution and a republic more than they feared the princes.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.3] Peasants lost interest once governments abolished most feudal dues in 1848, as Austria did in September 1848.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.4] Months of debate on the Basic Rights and the Grossdeutsch–Kleindeutsch question gave Austria and Prussia time to recover.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.5] National rivalries with Danes, Poles and Czechs split the revolutionaries and gave the Habsburg army willing allies, such as Jelačić's Croats.
[hist-eur1-s2.9.6] The refusal of the crown by Frederick William IV left the constitution without the one state strong enough to enforce it.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.0] Failure: no united Germany was created, the Basic Rights were repealed by the restored Diet in 1851, and the old Confederation returned.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.1] Achievement: feudal dues and serfdom were abolished, and Prussia kept a constitution and parliament after 1850, however limited.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.2] Failure: liberals were discredited as impractical, and many concluded that unity would come only through Prussian power, which Bismarck's later methods confirmed.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.3] Achievement: the Assembly was Germany's first national parliament, created political parties and provided a model for the constitutions of 1919 and the Basic Law of 1949.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.4] Contemporary views: conservatives celebrated the restoration of order, while radicals and exiles, including Marx and Engels, blamed the cowardice of the liberal middle class.
[hist-eur1-s2.10.5] Later views: A. J. P. Taylor wrote that in 1848 German history reached its turning point and failed to turn, a view linked to the idea of a German special path (Sonderweg) away from democracy; more recent historians stress the revolution's lasting gains in political culture and participation.

CANDIDATES hist-eur1-s4: 4. Italy moves towards unity, 1815–1871
[hist-eur1-s4.0.b] The Congress of Vienna left Italy divided into several states, most of them under Austrian influence or Habsburg relatives, with the Pope ruling the central lands.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.0] The Kingdom of Piedmont-Sardinia, ruled by the House of Savoy, was the largest independent state in the north-west and the only major Italian state ruled by a native dynasty.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.1] Austria directly ruled Lombardy and Venetia in the north, and Habsburg princes ruled Tuscany, Modena and Parma.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.2] The Papal States covered central Italy from Rome to Bologna, and the Pope's government was an absolute monarchy with clerical control of administration.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.3] The Kingdom of the Two Sicilies, ruled by the Bourbons from Naples, covered the south and Sicily.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.4] Secret societies, most notably the Carbonari, spread liberal and nationalist ideas among army officers, students and professionals in the 1820s.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.5] The Carbonari took part in the revolts of 1820 in Naples and 1821 in Piedmont, which were crushed by Austrian troops, leaving the leaders imprisoned or exiled.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.6] Revolts in 1831 in Modena, Parma and the Papal Legations of central Italy were defeated with Austrian help, and the Modenese rising's leader Ciro Menotti was executed.
[hist-eur1-s4.0.7] Napoleonic rule had introduced the Code Napoleon, a single administration and the idea of a united Italy, which restored rulers could not entirely undo.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.b] Mazzini was the most influential theorist and organiser of Italian nationalism before 1848. His ideal of a republican, unified Italy shaped the movement even when his methods failed.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.0] Mazzini was born in Genoa in 1805, trained as a lawyer and joined the Carbonari before his arrest and exile in 1830.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.1] In 1831 he founded Young Italy at Marseille, a secret society that aimed at a unified republic and whose members were mostly young.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.2] He wanted a unitary republic, with the people as the sovereign, and his motto was God and the People (Dio e Popolo); his later book The Duties of Man (1860) stressed duty to the nation over individual rights.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.3] He organised a failed invasion of Savoy in 1834 with other exiles, which was easily defeated, and he later founded Young Europe (1834) to link revolutionary movements.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.4] Mazzini lived in exile in Switzerland, France and England, and from 1837 he remained based in London, where he ran his network of exiles and conspiracies.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.5] In 1848 he returned to Italy and took part in the Roman Republic of 1849, where he was one of the triumvirs who governed the city.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.6] Mazzini was a participant in the revolutionary movement rather than a witness: he planned risings and sent agents into Italy, but his ideas also inspired ordinary followers.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.7] Contemporaries were divided: radicals and republicans admired him, while Cavour and the monarchists disliked his republicanism, and the Pope and Austria saw him as a dangerous conspirator.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.8] Mazzini remained a republican and rejected the monarchical Italy of 1861 as a betrayal; in 1870 he was arrested in Sicily while trying to start a republican rising, and he died in Pisa in 1872.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.9] Historians now see Mazzini as a key ideologue of Italian nationalism whose ideas shaped the Risorgimento, though his conspiracies largely failed and his republic was not achieved.
[hist-eur1-s4.1.10] Mazzini's national ideas relied on the people's own insurrection and education, not on foreign help or kings, and his Young Italy members swore an oath to work for an independent, united republic.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.0] Revolutions broke out across Italy in early 1848, starting at Palermo in January; after the February revolution in Paris, risings followed in Milan and Venice, and Pius IX granted a constitution in the Papal States.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.1] The Five Days of Milan (18–22 March 1848) drove the Austrian garrison out of the city before Radetzky withdrew to the fortresses of the Quadrilateral.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.2] Charles Albert of Piedmont declared war on Austria in March 1848, the first of the Italian wars of independence, and Piedmont's army was defeated at Custoza in July 1848.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.3] Pope Pius IX, who had won a liberal reputation in 1846, withdrew from the war against Austria in April 1848 and fled to Gaeta in November 1848 after the murder of his minister Pellegrino Rossi.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.4] The Roman Republic was proclaimed in February 1849, and Mazzini, Saffi and Armellini governed the city, with Garibaldi commanding its defence.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.5] The Republic of San Marco in Venice, led by Daniele Manin, held out against Austria until August 1849.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.6] Charles Albert was defeated at Novara in March 1849, abdicated and was succeeded by his son Victor Emmanuel II, who made peace with Austria.
[hist-eur1-s4.2.7] French troops sent by Louis-Napoleon, then President of France, captured Rome in July 1849, restored the Pope and kept a garrison there.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.0] Piedmont kept the Statuto Albertino, a constitution granted by Charles Albert in 1848, with an elected chamber, and it was the only Italian state to keep its constitution after 1849.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.1] The Siccardi Laws of 1850 removed the separate church courts and some clerical privileges, causing conflict with the Papacy.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.2] Count Camillo di Cavour, a landowner and journalist who had served as minister of agriculture and then finance, became Prime Minister of Piedmont in November 1852 and pursued economic modernisation, free trade and railway building.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.3] Cavour made Piedmont a credible leader of the national cause by sending about 15,000 troops to the Crimean War in 1855, which gave it a seat at the Congress of Paris in 1856.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.4] At the Congress of Paris, Cavour raised the Italian question and the problem of Austria's presence in the peninsula before the Great Powers.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.5] Cavour and Napoleon III met secretly at Plombières in July 1858, agreeing that France would support Piedmont in a war against Austria, provided Austria appeared the aggressor, in return for Nice and Savoy; the plan also envisaged a marriage between Princess Clotilde of Savoy and Napoleon III's cousin Prince Napoleon.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.6] The cession of Nice and Savoy to France in 1860 angered some nationalists, including Garibaldi, who was born in Nice.
[hist-eur1-s4.3.7] The Italian National Society, founded in 1857 by Manin, La Farina and Garibaldi among others, backed Piedmont's leadership in place of Mazzini's republicanism.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.0] Austria, goaded by Piedmontese mobilisation, issued an ultimatum and declared war on Piedmont in April 1859, and France came to Piedmont's support under Napoleon III.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.1] The French and Piedmontese armies won at Magenta on 4 June 1859 and at Solferino on 24 June 1859, bloody battles that, together with Prussian mobilisation on the Rhine and Catholic anger at home, led Napoleon III to seek peace.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.2] Napoleon III made the armistice of Villafranca with Austria on 11 July 1859 without consulting Cavour, who resigned in anger; Lombardy went to Piedmont but Venetia stayed under Austrian rule.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.3] The Treaty of Zürich (November 1859) gave Lombardy to Piedmont, while Austria kept Venetia.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.4] Tuscany, Modena, Parma and the Romagna held plebiscites in March 1860 and voted to join Piedmont, which Napoleon III accepted in return for Nice and Savoy.
[hist-eur1-s4.4.5] Nice and Savoy were ceded to France by the Treaty of Turin (March 1860).
[hist-eur1-s4.5.0] Garibaldi led about 1,000 volunteers, the Thousand, who sailed from Quarto near Genoa on 5 May 1860.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.1] They landed at Marsala in Sicily on 11 May 1860, won the battle of Calatafimi, and took Palermo, where Garibaldi governed as dictator in the name of Victor Emmanuel II.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.2] Garibaldi crossed to the mainland and entered Naples on 7 September 1860, as the Bourbon king Francis II withdrew.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.3] The Piedmontese army invaded the Papal Marches and Umbria in September 1860, and Garibaldi handed his conquests to Victor Emmanuel II near Teano in October 1860, with plebiscites in the south approving annexation.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.4] The Kingdom of Italy was proclaimed on 17 March 1861, with Victor Emmanuel II as king.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.5] Cavour died on 6 June 1861, months after the proclamation, and his successors struggled to resolve the southern question.
[hist-eur1-s4.5.6] Garibaldi's attempt to march on Rome in 1862 was stopped at Aspromonte by Italian troops, and his 1867 attempt was defeated at Mentana by French troops.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.0] Italy allied with Prussia in April 1866 and fought Austria in the Third War of Independence, but its defeats at Custoza and Lissa did not stop the Prussian victory.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.1] Austria ceded Venetia to Napoleon III, who passed it to Italy under the Treaty of Vienna (October 1866) and a plebiscite.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.2] The French garrison in Rome was withdrawn in August 1870 after the outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.3] Italian troops entered Rome on 20 September 1870 after a brief bombardment, and a plebiscite in October 1870 approved the annexation of Rome.
[hist-eur1-s4.6.4] Rome became the capital of the Kingdom of Italy in 1871 (after Turin and then Florence from 1865); the Law of Guarantees offered the Pope privileges, but Pius IX rejected it and declared himself a prisoner of the Vatican.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.0] Austrian control of Venetia and its military strength in the Quadrilateral were the main obstacles until 1866.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.1] French protection of the Papacy, and the garrison in Rome, prevented the unification of the peninsula until 1870.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.2] Regional rivalries, the strength of local identities and the lack of a common language among most people made national unity hard to achieve.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.3] The divisions between republicans, led by Mazzini, and monarchists, led by Cavour and the Savoy dynasty, weakened earlier attempts.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.4] After 1852 Piedmont offered a constitutional model, a modern economy and a professional army, which made it the leader of the movement.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.5] Cavour used diplomacy to isolate Austria and to gain French support, while the Crimean War helped Piedmont gain a place among the Great Powers.
[hist-eur1-s4.7.6] Garibaldi's volunteers provided the military force that could move quickly through the south, which the regular army alone could not have achieved.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.0] Achievement: by 1871 the peninsula was united under a single monarchy, with Rome as its capital, which had been the aim of the Risorgimento since the 1820s.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.1] Limit: the new state was a centralised monarchy under Piedmontese control, with a franchise so narrow that only about 2 per cent of the population could vote.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.2] Achievement: the Piedmontese model of constitutional monarchy and a modern economy was adopted in the new Kingdom.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.3] Limit: the southern peasantry gained little from unification, and brigandage in the south was suppressed by the army after 1861.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.4] Historians differ: some stress Cavour's diplomacy and Piedmont's leadership, Denis Mack Smith saw Cavour and Garibaldi as rivals as well as partners, and Antonio Gramsci described the Risorgimento as a passive revolution that left the peasants out.
[hist-eur1-s4.8.5] Overall: unity came through Piedmontese diplomacy, French support and Garibaldi's volunteers, but fell short of Mazzini's republican and popular ideal, and he was left disappointed.
