TARGET hist-eur4-s13: 13. The impact of advances in the biological sciences, nuclear power and the computer
[hist-eur4-s13.0.b] Scientific advances after 1945 transformed medicine, and the new drugs and vaccines changed the health of Europeans.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.0] Penicillin, discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1928, was developed into a mass-produced drug during the Second World War by Howard Florey and Ernst Chain at Oxford.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.1] Antibiotics were used widely in the 1950s, which cut deaths from bacterial infections such as pneumonia and tuberculosis in Western Europe.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.2] Jonas Salk's polio vaccine was announced on 12 April 1955, and Albert Sabin's oral vaccine was widely used from the early 1960s.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.3] The oral contraceptive pill, approved in the USA in 1960, spread through Western Europe in the 1960s and changed reproductive choice (see the notes on women and the family).
[hist-eur4-s13.0.4] The first successful kidney transplant between identical twins took place in Boston in 1954, and the first human heart transplant was performed by Christiaan Barnard in Cape Town on 3 December 1967.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.5] Streptomycin, discovered in 1943 by Selman Waksman, was the first effective drug against tuberculosis, and TB deaths fell sharply in Europe in the 1950s.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.6] Vaccination campaigns against diphtheria, tuberculosis (BCG), polio and measles reduced child deaths, and smallpox was declared eradicated worldwide by the World Health Organization in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.7] The welfare state made new drugs and hospital treatment available to all, and life expectancy in Western Europe rose by over a decade between 1945 and 1990.
[hist-eur4-s13.0.8] Surgery advanced with the heart pacemaker, kidney dialysis and hip replacements (developed by John Charnley in England in the 1960s), and the CT scanner developed by Godfrey Hounsfield in 1971.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.b] The discovery of the structure of DNA opened the way to genetic engineering and new understanding of heredity.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.0] James Watson and Francis Crick published the double-helix structure of DNA in April 1953, using X-ray crystallography data gathered by Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.1] Watson, Crick and Wilkins shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1962, and Franklin, who had died in 1958, was not included.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.2] In 1973 Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer demonstrated recombinant DNA techniques, which allowed genes to be moved between organisms.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.3] The first in-vitro fertilisation baby, Louise Brown, was born in Oldham in England on 25 July 1978, after work by Robert Edwards and Patrick Steptoe.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.4] Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photograph of DNA, Photo 51 (taken in 1952 at King's College London), gave key evidence for the helical structure, and Watson and Crick worked at the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.5] The genetic code was cracked in the 1960s, and in 1977 Frederick Sanger at Cambridge developed a method of sequencing DNA.
[hist-eur4-s13.1.6] Recombinant DNA techniques led to the first genetically engineered drugs, such as human insulin produced by bacteria in 1978, and to the founding of the biotechnology industry in the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s13.2.b] Advances in biology brought benefits and new moral questions.
[hist-eur4-s13.2.0] The thalidomide scandal of 1961 showed the risks: the drug, sold in West Germany from 1957 as a sedative and for morning sickness, caused birth defects in thousands of children and led to stricter drug testing.
[hist-eur4-s13.2.1] The ethics of IVF, embryo research and genetic engineering were debated; the Warnock Report in Britain in 1984 recommended regulation, and Pope John Paul II's Church opposed IVF.
[hist-eur4-s13.2.2] DNA fingerprinting, developed by Alec Jeffreys at Leicester in 1984, was first used in a criminal case in Britain in 1986.
[hist-eur4-s13.2.3] Debates about genetic engineering, embryo research and drug safety led governments to set up regulatory bodies and ethics committees in the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.b] Nuclear power was developed for both weapons and electricity, and in the 1950s and 1960s governments promoted it as a source of cheap energy.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.0] The Soviet Union's Obninsk reactor, which began supplying electricity to the grid in June 1954, was the first nuclear power station connected to a public electricity network.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.1] Calder Hall in Cumbria, England, opened in October 1956 and was the world's first nuclear power station to supply electricity on a commercial scale; it also produced plutonium for weapons.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.2] France developed a national nuclear programme, and in 1974 the Messmer Plan committed the country to building a large number of reactors, which by the 1980s provided most of its electricity.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.3] CERN, the European Organisation for Nuclear Research, was founded in Geneva in 1954 by twelve European states to promote cooperative research in physics.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.4] Atoms for Peace, an address by US President Eisenhower to the UN in December 1953, promoted civil nuclear power and led to the International Atomic Energy Agency in 1957.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.5] The Treaty of Rome of 1957 created Euratom to coordinate nuclear energy in the six EEC states.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.6] The Windscale fire in Cumbria in October 1957 was Britain's worst nuclear accident and released radioactive iodine over the countryside.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.7] After the oil crisis of 1973 many governments, including France, West Germany, Sweden and the USSR, expanded nuclear programmes, and nuclear power came to supply around three quarters of French electricity by the 1990s.
[hist-eur4-s13.3.8] The USSR built reactors across the bloc, including the RBMK reactors at Chernobyl, and nuclear weapons stockpiles grew on both sides.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.b] The growth of nuclear power produced a strong opposition movement, and serious accidents in 1979 and 1986 strengthened it.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.0] Greenpeace was founded in 1971 in Vancouver, Canada, and it campaigned against nuclear testing and nuclear power at sea.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.1] The accident at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania on 28 March 1979 was the most serious in the USA, and it halted new nuclear orders there for many years.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.2] The explosion at the Chernobyl reactor in Soviet Ukraine on 26 April 1986 released radioactive material across Europe, and the nearby town of Pripyat was evacuated.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.3] Chernobyl led to the creation of a 30-kilometre exclusion zone around the plant, and its effects on health and the environment were the subject of long disputes.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.4] Anti-nuclear protests in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands grew in the early 1980s, alongside protests against NATO missile deployments.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.5] The Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament (CND) was founded in Britain in 1958 and organised marches to Aldermaston, using the peace symbol designed in 1958.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.6] In Ireland a plan to build a nuclear plant at Carnsore Point, Co. Wexford, was dropped after large protests in 1978–79 and was never built.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.7] Sweden held a referendum in 1980 on the future of nuclear power, and Austria's Zwentendorf plant, completed in 1978, never operated after a referendum rejected it.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.8] Chernobyl's explosion followed a safety test and design flaws in the RBMK reactor; the Soviet authorities were slow to admit it, and Swedish monitors detected the radioactive cloud, which forced an announcement on 28 April.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.9] many hundreds of thousands of people were eventually resettled from the affected areas.
[hist-eur4-s13.4.10] Chernobyl led to greater international safety cooperation and strengthened the case for glasnost; in Western Europe it prompted a re-think in Italy, which voted to end nuclear power in 1987.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.b] Computers moved from wartime code-breaking and large mainframes to personal machines in the 1980s, changing work and daily life.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.0] Colossus, built at Bletchley Park in 1943–44 by Tommy Flowers' team, was used to break German teleprinter codes, and it was one of the first programmable electronic computers.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.1] ENIAC, completed in the USA in 1945, was one of the first general-purpose electronic digital computers.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.2] The transistor was invented at Bell Laboratories in the USA in December 1947, and it replaced vacuum tubes in computers.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.3] The integrated circuit, or microchip, was developed by Jack Kilby and Robert Noyce around 1958 and 1959, and the Intel 4004 microprocessor followed in 1971.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.4] IBM's System/360 mainframe, launched in 1964, set the standard for business computing in Europe and the USA.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.5] The IBM Personal Computer, launched on 12 August 1981, and the Apple II of 1977 spread personal computing into offices and homes in the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.6] The ARPANET, a US network, was first connected in 1969, and it became the basis of the internet.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.7] Alan Turing's theoretical work in the 1930s and wartime code-breaking laid the foundations of computing, and the Manchester Baby of 1948 was the first stored-program computer to run.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.8] The first commercial computer for business use was LEO, built for the Lyons tea shops in Britain in 1951, and IBM dominated the market for mainframes.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.9] Minicomputers and microprocessors from the 1970s made computing cheaper; personal computers such as the Sinclair ZX Spectrum (1982) and the Commodore 64 were bought for homes in Britain and Europe.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.10] In France the Minitel online terminal was launched in 1982 and was given free to telephone subscribers.
[hist-eur4-s13.5.11] Tim Berners-Lee, working at CERN, proposed the World Wide Web in 1989, and it was launched publicly in 1991.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.b] Advances in science and computing changed working life and the balance between East and West, as technology became central to economic power.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.0] Computers automated routine office and factory work, and they reduced demand for some clerical and manufacturing jobs in Western Europe.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.1] Western export controls, coordinated through COCOM from 1949, restricted the sale of advanced computing technology to the Soviet bloc.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.2] The Soviet bloc fell behind the West in computing and electronics, a gap that Soviet planners tried to close through copying and espionage.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.3] Information technology became a key concept in the economy, and the pace of change in the 1980s prepared the ground for the global networks that followed.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.4] Banks introduced cash machines from 1967, supermarkets adopted bar codes in the 1970s, and word processors and spreadsheets transformed office work in the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.5] Automation reduced employment in manufacturing, while service jobs in banking, insurance and administration grew, contributing to deindustrialisation.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.6] By the 1980s Soviet leaders recognised that the Soviet Union lagged badly in computing and microelectronics, which was one of the reasons for the push for economic reform.
[hist-eur4-s13.6.7] Europe lagged behind the USA and Japan in computing and electronics, and the EEC launched programmes such as ESPRIT in 1984 to support research.
[hist-eur4-s13.7.b] Historians and contemporaries have judged these advances by the benefits they brought and the new dangers and inequalities they created.
[hist-eur4-s13.7.0] Achievements: longer life expectancy, the control of infectious disease, cheap electricity from nuclear plants and the transformation of communication and work.
[hist-eur4-s13.7.1] Limits: nuclear accidents, the risks of radioactive waste, the ethical questions raised by reproductive and genetic technology, and job losses from automation.
[hist-eur4-s13.7.2] Contemporaries in the 1950s often celebrated science as the key to progress, while the anti-nuclear and environmental movements of the 1970s and 1980s challenged that view.
[hist-eur4-s13.7.3] Later historians stress that the impact of these technologies depended on political decisions about how they were used, not only on their scientific potential.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s2: 2. The main crises of the Cold War, Khrushchev and détente
[hist-eur4-s2.0.b] Khrushchev led the Soviet Union from Stalin's death in 1953 to 1964 and set the terms of the Cold War between de-Stalinisation and nuclear confrontation.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.0] Born in 1894 in Kalinovka, a village in Kursk province, he rose through the Communist Party under Stalin, becoming first secretary of the Moscow party in 1935 and of the Ukrainian party in 1938.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.1] He was a participant in the Great Purge in Ukraine and Moscow in the late 1930s, which later made his attacks on Stalin's terror open to challenge.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.2] After Stalin's death in March 1953 he became First Secretary of the Central Committee in September 1953, and he defeated rivals such as Georgy Malenkov and Lavrentiy Beria in power struggles.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.3] In his secret speech to a closed session of the 20th Party Congress on 25 February 1956 he denounced Stalin's cult of personality and terror; the text was published in the West within months.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.4] He argued at the same congress that war with the capitalist states was not inevitable, which became known as peaceful coexistence.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.5] Contemporaries in the West saw him as unpredictable and aggressive, while Mao Zedong's China criticised him as a revisionist who had abandoned Marxist-Leninist purity.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.6] Historians such as William Taubman, in Khrushchev: The Man and His Era (2003), describe a reformer whose policies were improvised and often failed, but whose de-Stalinisation opened Soviet society.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.7] Rise to power, 1953–57: Beria was arrested in June 1953 and executed that December; Malenkov was forced to resign as premier in 1955; in June 1957 Khrushchev defeated the 'anti-party group' of Molotov, Malenkov and Kaganovich with help from Marshal Zhukov and the Central Committee, and in March 1958 he added the premiership to his party post.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.8] Foreign initiatives of the thaw: he agreed to the Austrian State Treaty and withdrew Soviet troops from Austria in 1955, visited Tito's Yugoslavia in 1955 to mend the 1948 split, and attended the Geneva summit of July 1955.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.9] At the 1959 American National Exhibition in Moscow Khrushchev and Vice-President Nixon held the 'kitchen debate' on the merits of communism and capitalism.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.10] On 14 October 1964 the Central Committee removed him from the posts of First Secretary and chairman of the Council of Ministers, while he was on holiday in the Black Sea.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.11] Leonid Brezhnev became First Secretary of the party and Alexei Kosygin chairman of the Council of Ministers.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.12] His critics cited the Cuban humiliation, the failed agricultural and economic reforms, and his erratic style of leadership.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.13] He was pensioned off and lived in retirement, largely at a dacha outside Moscow, until his death in 1971.
[hist-eur4-s2.0.14] His fall confirmed that the Soviet leadership would not tolerate a single figure dominating policy, which shaped the more collective style of the Brezhnev years.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.b] Khrushchev's domestic programme loosened Stalinist controls but its economic experiments produced mixed results.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.0] De-Stalinisation released many political prisoners, reduced the role of the secret police and allowed more open discussion in literature.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.1] The Virgin Lands campaign from 1954 ploughed up grassland in Kazakhstan and Siberia to grow grain, and it produced good harvests in its early years.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.2] Droughts and poor management later brought failures, most seriously in 1963, when the harvest fell and grain had to be imported.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.3] In 1957 industry was decentralised into regional economic councils, a reform that was later reversed by his successors.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.4] The Novocherkassk strike of June 1962 over food prices and wages was suppressed by troops who fired on the crowd, a sign of the limits of his reforms.
[hist-eur4-s2.1.5] Space achievements boosted prestige: Sputnik 1 was launched on 4 October 1957, and Yuri Gagarin became the first human in space on 12 April 1961.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.b] The crises before Khrushchev set the pattern of confrontation without direct war.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.0] Berlin Blockade (1948–49): the first major crisis, ended by the airlift and followed by the founding of NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.1] Korean War (1950–53): the North Korean invasion of the South in June 1950 persuaded Western governments that communism was expansionist, and led to the rearmament of NATO, the creation of a unified NATO command under Eisenhower and the push to rearm West Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.2] Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a more flexible phase; an armistice in Korea followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.b] Berlin was the most dangerous flashpoint in Europe, because West Berlin was an enclave inside East Germany and a window to the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.0] On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev issued an ultimatum giving the Western powers six months to agree to a settlement that would end their occupation of West Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.1] Khrushchev's visit to the USA in September 1959, including talks with Eisenhower at Camp David, produced a brief thaw that the U-2 incident later ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.2] The Paris summit of May 1960 collapsed after the U-2 spy plane piloted by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union on 1 May 1960.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.3] At the Vienna summit in June 1961 Khrushchev pressed the new US president John F. Kennedy on Berlin, and the crisis deepened over the summer.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.4] East German forces began building the Berlin Wall on 13 August 1961, closing the border between the two halves of the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.5] The Western powers did not use force against the Wall, accepting it as the price of avoiding war; Kennedy is reported to have said that a wall was better than a war.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.6] Between 1961 and 1989 the Wall and the border fortifications around it were guarded, and people who tried to cross were shot or arrested.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.b] The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war, and it was resolved by a compromise that Europeans watched closely.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.0] In October 1962 US reconnaissance photographs showed Soviet medium-range missiles being installed in Cuba, within range of much of the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.1] President Kennedy announced a naval quarantine of Cuba on 22 October 1962 and demanded the removal of the missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.2] For about a week the two superpowers were on the brink of war; Kennedy sent former secretary of state Dean Acheson to brief the French president de Gaulle, who gave firm support, while most European governments learned of the crisis only as it unfolded.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.3] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.4] The secret part of the deal was a US promise to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was not made public at the time.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.5] The crisis shaped later arms control: a Moscow–Washington hotline was set up in 1963, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty followed in August 1963.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.6] Khrushchev's motives were to defend Castro's Cuba after the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961, to offset US missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to narrow the Soviet Union's weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.7] The crisis began on 16 October 1962 when Kennedy was shown U-2 photographs; Kennedy's advisers (ExComm) weighed an air strike against a naval blockade and chose the blockade, described publicly as a quarantine.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.8] Seen from Europe, the crisis showed that Berlin and Cuba were linked, and made West European leaders more aware that decisions on nuclear war would be taken in Washington and Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.b] Each superpower relied on nuclear weapons to deter attack, and limited agreements grew out of the fear that deterrence could fail.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.0] Nuclear deterrence meant that each side kept enough weapons to destroy the other after a first strike, a condition later called mutually assured destruction.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.1] The Soviet Union tested the 50-megaton Tsar Bomba on 30 October 1961, the largest nuclear explosion ever set off.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.2] The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union, banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.3] The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 committed non-nuclear states not to acquire nuclear weapons, while the nuclear powers agreed to pursue disarmament.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.4] Western Europe depended on the US nuclear umbrella under NATO, and debates in France and Britain over independent deterrents reflected doubts about that protection.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.5] France tested its first atomic bomb in 1960 and developed its own deterrent, which it kept outside NATO's military command from 1966.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.6] The ideological dispute with Mao's China grew from the late 1950s and became open in 1960, when Soviet advisers were withdrawn from China.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.7] The Sino-Soviet split meant the Soviet Union faced two rival communist powers and could not count on Chinese support in Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.8] Albania sided with China and in 1968 became the only member state to leave the Warsaw Pact.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.9] Soviet leaders regarded the Berlin Wall as a means of stabilising the German Democratic Republic, which had lost about 2.7 million people to the West since 1945.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.b] Détente was a period of easing tension, from about 1969 to the late 1970s, in which the superpowers and European states signed agreements on arms and borders.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.0] Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik sought better relations with Eastern Europe; the Treaty of Moscow of 12 August 1970 renounced the use of force between the Federal Republic and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.1] The Four Power Agreement on Berlin of 3 September 1971 guaranteed access between West Berlin and West Germany and confirmed the Western presence in the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.2] The Basic Treaty of 1972 established relations between the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.3] SALT I, signed on 26 May 1972, limited anti-ballistic missile systems and placed interim limits on strategic offensive missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.4] The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe produced the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, signed by 35 states including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.5] The Final Act recognised existing borders in Europe and committed signatories to respect human rights, a commitment that later dissidents used to press their governments.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.b] Détente broke down in the late 1970s, and a new arms race in Europe led to the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in the early 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.0] On 24 December 1979 Soviet forces invaded Afghanistan, and the USA responded with a grain embargo and a boycott of the Moscow Olympics in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.1] NATO's double-track decision of 12 December 1979 agreed to deploy Pershing II and cruise missiles in Western Europe unless arms talks with the Soviet Union succeeded.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.2] The Soviet Union had deployed SS-20 intermediate-range missiles in the late 1970s, which threatened Western Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.3] Large peace demonstrations took place in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands in 1981–83 against the deployment of the new missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.4] The Soviet leadership's fear of a Western first strike led to a serious misreading during the NATO exercise Able Archer 83 in November 1983.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.5] The crisis was defused by arms negotiations, and the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty was signed in December 1987 under Gorbachev.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.b] Examiners ask which events changed the course of the Cold War, so know the order and what each changed.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.0] Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: confirmed the division of Germany and led to NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.1] Hungary, 1956: showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep its bloc together.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.2] Berlin Wall, 1961 and Cuba, 1962: the high points of confrontation, after which both sides sought safeguards such as the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.3] Prague Spring, 1968: the invasion of Czechoslovakia confirmed the Brezhnev Doctrine but did not end détente.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.4] Helsinki, 1975: the high point of détente, which gave dissidents in the East a human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.5] Afghanistan and the Euromissiles, 1979: ended détente and began the second Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.6] Reagan's election in 1980 brought a hard line: in March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an evil empire, and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.7] Gorbachev from 1985, the Reykjavik summit of October 1986 and the INF Treaty of 1987 reversed the arms race, and 1989 ended Soviet control of eastern Europe (see the notes on reform movements and the collapse of communism).
[hist-eur4-s2.9.b] Khrushchev's record is judged by contrasting his bold moves with the setbacks that ended his rule.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.0] Achievements: de-Stalinisation, the Partial Test Ban Treaty, the hotline, the Austrian State Treaty of 1955 and the space successes.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.1] Failures: the Virgin Lands harvest crises, the reversal of his administrative reforms, and the Cuban settlement, which Soviet leaders saw as a retreat.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.2] Contemporary Western views often focused on his unpredictability, while his Soviet colleagues attacked his rash 'hare-brained schemes' and his boasting.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.3] Later historians have judged him as a reformer who opened the system but never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.4] The Berlin Wall, which his policy helped to produce, was for many observers the most lasting symbol of his era, and later assessments weigh it heavily against his reform record.
