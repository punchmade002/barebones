TARGET hist-eur6-s5: 5. The decline of Cold War certainties, 1973–1989: withdrawal from Vietnam, détente, SALT and Star Wars
[hist-eur6-s5.0.b] By 1973 American foreign policy rested on ideas set in the late 1940s, and Vietnam had begun to shake them. Three ideas help the later blocks make sense: containment, internationalism and technological development.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.0] Containment was the policy, in place since 1947, of stopping the spread of communism by resisting Soviet expansion. The arms race and mutual deterrence (each side stayed safe because the other feared nuclear retaliation) grew out of it.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.1] Internationalism means a continuing US commitment to active engagement in world affairs. After Vietnam Americans debated how far that commitment should go, which is why this period matters for the whole story of US foreign policy.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.2] Technological development means weapons and space systems that shaped the arms race, such as MIRVs (missiles carrying several warheads, each aimed at a different target), missile defence and verification satellites, which let each side check the other's arsenal.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.3] Vietnam is the background to the period. Johnson's escalation, the Tet Offensive of 1968, the anti-war movement and Nixon's Vietnamisation (handing the fighting to South Vietnamese forces) had already weakened support for the war.
[hist-eur6-s5.0.4] Nixon resigned over Watergate on 9 August 1974, so three presidents followed in this period: Gerald Ford, Jimmy Carter (1977 to 1981) and Ronald Reagan (1981 to 1989).
[hist-eur6-s5.1.b] The United States left Vietnam in 1973 through a negotiated settlement, and Congress then closed off the option of fighting again. This weakened the consensus behind containment.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.0] The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973 by the United States, North Vietnam, South Vietnam and the Provisional Revolutionary Government (the Viet Cong). They provided for a ceasefire, US withdrawal and the release of prisoners of war.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.1] Kissinger and Le Duc Tho negotiated the Accords and were awarded the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize, though Le Duc Tho declined it. North Vietnamese troops were allowed to remain in the South, which left South Vietnam exposed once the Americans had gone.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.2] The last US combat troops left by 29 March 1973, and American prisoners of war were released in the same weeks.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.3] Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto on 7 November 1973 (House 284 to 135, Senate 75 to 18). It requires the President to consult and report to Congress and to withdraw forces within 60 days, with a 30-day extension for withdrawal, unless Congress authorises them.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.4] The War Powers Resolution was meant to stop another undeclared war like Vietnam, because it put limits on a President sending troops abroad without Congress. Do not confuse it with the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution of 1964, which had given Johnson wide powers.
[hist-eur6-s5.1.5] The Case-Church amendment barred funds for US combat in Indochina from 15 August 1973. So when the North later broke the Accords, the United States could not intervene, and South Vietnam relied on its own forces and on aid from Congress.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.b] Two years after American troops left, South Vietnam collapsed. The defeat left Americans cautious about overseas commitments and changed the debate about internationalism.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.0] The North Vietnamese offensive of 1975 overran South Vietnam. In April 1975 Congress refused President Ford's request for emergency aid for South Vietnam, which showed how far the American consensus behind the war had collapsed.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.1] Ford, not Nixon, was president when Saigon fell. Speaking at Tulane University, New Orleans, on 23 April 1975, he said that the war was finished as far as America was concerned.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.2] Saigon fell on 30 April 1975. Operation Frequent Wind evacuated Americans and many South Vietnamese by helicopter on 29 and 30 April, so images of the evacuation became the symbol of the defeat.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.3] Phnom Penh, the capital of Cambodia, fell to the Khmer Rouge on 17 April 1975, and the communist Pathet Lao took power in Laos in December 1975. All three Indochinese states therefore had communist governments by the end of 1975.
[hist-eur6-s5.2.4] About 58,000 Americans died in the war. The defeat produced the Vietnam syndrome, a reluctance to commit US forces abroad, which made later presidents cautious about overseas military commitments.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.b] Détente means the easing of tensions through negotiation, based on a balance of power rather than on trust. Nixon and Kissinger used it to manage rivalry with both communist giants while the US was weakened by Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.0] Henry Kissinger was national security adviser from 1969 and Secretary of State as well from 1973. He aimed for a stable balance of power in which no side could easily gain an advantage.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.1] His policy of linkage tied arms control and trade to Soviet behaviour in other regions. In other words, the US would offer the Soviet Union agreements it wanted only if Moscow behaved well elsewhere.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.2] Nixon visited China from 21 to 28 February 1972 and met Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai. The Shanghai Communiqué set out a process to normalise relations, which also put pressure on the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.3] Formal US recognition of the People's Republic of China came on 1 January 1979 under Carter, building on Nixon's opening.
[hist-eur6-s5.3.4] Nixon was in Moscow from 22 to 30 May 1972, meeting Leonid Brezhnev, the Soviet leader. The first major arms control agreements, SALT I, were signed there on 26 May 1972, and trade agreements were discussed.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.b] The Strategic Arms Limitation Talks (SALT) aimed to limit the superpowers' nuclear arsenals. SALT I was the first agreement and it slowed the arms race without ending it.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.0] SALT I was signed in Moscow on 26 May 1972 by Nixon and Brezhnev, with Kissinger as the key American negotiator. It had two parts: the Anti-Ballistic Missile (ABM) Treaty and an Interim Agreement.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.1] The ABM Treaty limited missile defence systems. Because each side stayed open to attack, neither could hope to launch a first strike and be safe from retaliation, so deterrence was preserved. The Senate approved it by 88 votes to 2 in August 1972.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.2] The Interim Agreement froze the numbers of land-based ICBMs (intercontinental ballistic missiles) and submarine-launched missile launchers for five years.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.3] It did not limit the number of warheads each missile carried, so MIRVs (multiple independently targetable re-entry vehicles) let each side add warheads without breaking the limits.
[hist-eur6-s5.4.4] Both sides therefore kept building up their arsenals, so SALT I limited growth without reducing the number of warheads.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.b] Détente brought real agreements, but it also drew attacks from critics and was strained by events in the Middle East and Africa.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.0] The Helsinki Final Act was signed on 1 August 1975 by 35 states, including the United States and the Soviet Union. It recognised European borders as they stood and set out human rights principles, which the Soviet Union later failed to honour.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.1] Ford and Brezhnev agreed a framework for a second SALT agreement at Vladivostok in November 1974, which kept arms control going after Nixon's fall.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.2] In October 1973 the Yom Kippur War in the Middle East led Nixon to put US forces on alert (DEFCON 3) after Soviet threats of intervention, showing that détente did not remove the risk of confrontation.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.3] The Jackson-Vanik Amendment of 1974 linked US-Soviet trade to freedom of emigration, notably for Soviet Jews. It angered Moscow.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.4] Critics of détente, including conservative Democrats such as Senator Henry Jackson and others on the right, argued that the Soviet Union was gaining military strength while the United States made concessions.
[hist-eur6-s5.5.5] Soviet and Cuban backing for the MPLA in Angola in 1975 and 1976 convinced critics that the Soviet Union was exploiting détente to gain influence in the developing world while talking of peace.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.b] Carter came to office stressing moral principles in foreign policy, but events in Iran and Afghanistan pushed him into a harder line.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.0] Carter stressed human rights from 1977. He criticised Soviet treatment of dissidents (people who openly oppose their government) such as the physicist Andrei Sakharov, which annoyed Moscow.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.1] He gave full diplomatic recognition to the People's Republic of China on 1 January 1979, building on Nixon's opening.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.2] The Shah of Iran, a US ally who had been deposed, was admitted to the United States in October 1979. In response the US embassy in Tehran was seized on 4 November 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.3] The hostages were held for 444 days and were released on 20 January 1981, the day Reagan took office.
[hist-eur6-s5.6.4] Operation Eagle Claw, a rescue attempt in April 1980, failed: helicopters broke down, and at a desert staging site a helicopter collided with a transport aircraft, killing eight US servicemen. The failure contributed to Carter's defeat in the 1980 election.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.b] SALT II was meant to build on SALT I, but within months of its signing the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan ended détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.0] SALT II was signed in Vienna on 18 June 1979 by Carter and Brezhnev. It set an equal ceiling of 2,400 strategic delivery vehicles for each side, to fall to 2,250, and limited multiple-warhead missiles.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.1] Critics, led by Senator Henry Jackson and the Committee on the Present Danger, said SALT II let the Soviet Union keep a lead in heavy land-based missiles, so ratification was in doubt.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.2] Soviet forces entered Afghanistan on 24 and 25 December 1979. Carter condemned the invasion as an unacceptable threat to regional stability, and it ended the era of détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.3] SALT II was never ratified. In January 1980 Carter asked the Senate to postpone consideration, after the invasion of Afghanistan.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.4] Carter announced a grain embargo against the Soviet Union in January 1980, which cut off supplies the Soviet Union wanted from the US.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.5] In his State of the Union address in January 1980 he set out the Carter Doctrine: the United States would resist any outside attempt to control the Persian Gulf region.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.6] Carter led a boycott of the Moscow Olympics: the US Olympic Committee voted in April 1980 not to send a team, and dozens of other countries also stayed away. Some national teams, including Britain's, attended despite government pressure.
[hist-eur6-s5.7.7] The Soviet Union and the United States both said they would observe SALT II's limits informally after 1980, but Reagan's government ceased to observe the limits in 1986.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.b] Reagan rejected the logic of détente and deterrence, arguing that the United States should deal with Moscow from strength. This opened what is often called a new Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.0] Reagan described the Soviet Union as an evil empire in his "evil empire" speech, given to the National Association of Evangelicals on 8 March 1983, a phrase that showed how far he had moved from Nixon's approach.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.1] Defence spending rose sharply from 1981 to 1985. Programmes included the B-1 bomber, the MX missile and Pershing II missiles in Western Europe.
[hist-eur6-s5.8.2] American Pershing II and cruise missiles began arriving in Western Europe from late 1983, which led to protests, including at Greenham Common in Britain.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.b] Reagan wanted a defence against nuclear missiles instead of relying on the threat of retaliation. His proposal was a big step in technological development, and it also changed the arms talks that followed.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.0] Reagan announced the Strategic Defense Initiative (SDI) in a television address on 23 March 1983, proposing space-based and other systems to destroy incoming missiles.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.1] Critics gave it the nickname Star Wars, after the film, partly because many scientists doubted it could work and partly because of its cost.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.2] It was never deployed, but it alarmed Soviet leaders, who feared an American technological lead they could not match.
[hist-eur6-s5.9.3] SDI also differed from the ABM Treaty of 1972, which had limited defences, and critics said SDI undermined that treaty.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.b] A new generation of Soviet leadership made talks possible, and Reagan and Gorbachev then agreed the first treaty to abolish a whole class of nuclear weapons.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.0] Brezhnev died in November 1982, and the leadership changed rapidly: Yuri Andropov (to February 1984), Konstantin Chernenko (to March 1985), then Mikhail Gorbachev, who became General Secretary in March 1985.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.1] Gorbachev's policies of glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring of the economy) loosened Soviet control over Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.2] Reagan and Gorbachev met at Geneva in November 1985 and at Reykjavik in October 1986. At Reykjavik they came close to agreeing deep cuts in nuclear weapons.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.3] The Reykjavik talks broke down over SDI: Gorbachev wanted it confined to the laboratory and Reagan refused.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.4] The Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces (INF) Treaty was signed in Washington on 8 December 1987. It eliminated all US and Soviet land-based missiles with ranges between 500 and 5,500 kilometres, with on-site inspection.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.5] It was the first treaty to abolish a whole class of nuclear weapons, unlike SALT, which only limited growth. The Senate ratified it in May 1988, and Reagan visited Moscow for a fourth summit in May and June 1988.
[hist-eur6-s5.10.6] Reagan spoke at the Berlin Wall (the Brandenburg Gate) on 12 June 1987 and said: "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall."
[hist-eur6-s5.11.b] In 1989 the division of Europe that defined the Cold War broke up, largely because the Soviet Union stopped using force to hold it together.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.0] Soviet troops finished leaving Afghanistan in February 1989, ending the costly war that had begun in December 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.1] The Soviet Union did not intervene when communist governments in Eastern Europe fell during 1989, which was a break with earlier practice, so the changes could go ahead.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.2] The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989, and the collapse of communist governments across Eastern Europe ended the division of the continent.
[hist-eur6-s5.11.3] Reagan's successor, George H. W. Bush, met Gorbachev off Malta on 2 and 3 December 1989, and the two leaders described a new era in relations.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.b] Examiners reward judgements that weigh US policy against Soviet internal failure and the choices of Gorbachev, and that also weigh the successes and failures of détente.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.0] Achievements of détente and arms control: SALT I, Helsinki, the opening to China and later the INF Treaty. Limits: arsenals kept growing, SALT II was never ratified, and détente had collapsed by 1979.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.1] Supporters of Reagan argue that his build-up and the Strategic Defense Initiative forced the Soviet Union into an arms race it could not afford, and that negotiating from strength created the conditions for Gorbachev's reforms.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.2] Critics of Reagan argue that the Soviet economy was already stagnating before 1980 and that Gorbachev's own reforms were the decisive cause of the change.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.3] Reagan's turn to negotiation owed much to Gorbachev, so critics say the talks were not simply a reward for American strength.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.4] Continuity: détente and arms control created the framework and the habit of verification used in 1987, so the INF Treaty built on the 1970s.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.5] The costs of the Afghan war and falling oil revenues in the mid-1980s added to the Soviet Union's economic difficulties.
[hist-eur6-s5.12.6] Vietnam reduced US willingness to intervene abroad, which affected internationalism. Yet US power and ideas outlasted the Soviet system.

CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s3: 3. Case study: Lyndon Johnson and Vietnam, 1963–1968
[hist-eur6-s3.0.b] Johnson did not start the American involvement in Vietnam. He inherited a commitment built up over two earlier presidencies, driven by Cold War fear of communist expansion.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.0] Containment was the Cold War policy of stopping communism spreading. In 1954 Eisenhower put forward the domino theory: if one country fell to communism its neighbours would fall one after another, like a row of dominoes. This is why Americans thought South Vietnam mattered so much.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.1] The United States gave aid to France in its war in Vietnam and then to South Vietnam directly. Vietnam was divided in 1954 at the 17th parallel (a line of latitude), which left a communist state in the North and a US-backed state in the South.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.2] Ho Chi Minh, a communist, led North Vietnam, and Ngo Dinh Diem led South Vietnam with American support. Diem's government was weak, which is why it needed outside help to survive.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.3] The Viet Cong were communist guerrillas (fighters who use ambush and hit-and-run rather than open battle) operating inside South Vietnam against Diem's government. They were separate from the North Vietnamese Army, the regular army of the North.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.4] Kennedy raised the number of US military advisers from under 1,000 in 1961 to about 16,000 by late 1963, but he sent no combat troops. So the United States was committed to South Vietnam's survival without yet fighting the war itself.
[hist-eur6-s3.0.5] The Great Society was Johnson's domestic programme of civil rights, the War on Poverty, health and education. He announced it in May 1964, and it is the reason he wanted Vietnam kept in the background.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.b] Johnson came to the presidency as one of the most skilful legislators in American history. His methods worked on Congress but not on an enemy abroad, which helps to explain why Vietnam beat him.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.0] Lyndon Baines Johnson was born on 27 August 1908 near Stonewall, Texas. He became Texas director of the National Youth Administration (a New Deal agency that gave work to young people) in 1935 and was elected to the House of Representatives in 1937.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.1] He won the 1948 Democratic Senate runoff in Texas by only 87 votes, which earned him the mocking nickname Landslide Lyndon. He took his Senate seat in January 1949.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.2] He became Democratic leader in the Senate in 1953 and Majority Leader from 1955 to 1961. After losing the 1960 Democratic nomination to Kennedy he accepted the vice presidency (1961 to 1963).
[hist-eur6-s3.1.3] He was a master of Senate politics and persuasion, using personal pressure on colleagues (the Johnson treatment) to pass legislation. This worked because colleagues needed his favour.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.4] He could not apply those methods to a war against an enemy who would not bargain on his terms, because Hanoi had nothing to gain from his favour and could simply refuse to negotiate.
[hist-eur6-s3.1.5] Kennedy was shot in Dallas on 22 November 1963, and Johnson was sworn in as president the same day. He remained president until January 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.b] Johnson took over a war that was going badly, in a country with an unstable government. He chose continuity, and his own fears of political damage made him hold on.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.0] Diem was overthrown by a military coup on 1 to 2 November 1963 and killed, so the Saigon government was unstable when Johnson took office. The Viet Cong controlled large parts of the countryside, and about 16,000 US advisers were in the country.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.1] Within days Johnson told his ambassador in Saigon that he would not lose Vietnam. National Security Action Memorandum 273 (26 November 1963) confirmed the policy of supporting South Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.2] He kept Kennedy's advisers: Robert McNamara (Secretary of Defense), Dean Rusk (Secretary of State) and McGeorge Bundy (national security adviser, replaced by Walt Rostow in 1966). So the same thinking about the war carried on.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.3] Johnson feared that losing Vietnam would repeat the loss of China, when Truman's party had been attacked as soft on communism. He feared a right-wing backlash that would wreck his domestic programme.
[hist-eur6-s3.2.4] He also believed that losing South Vietnam would damage US credibility worldwide, because other allies would doubt American promises. He was a firm anti-communist who was also aware of the domestic cost of war.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.b] Two incidents in the Gulf of Tonkin gave Johnson congressional authority for a larger war, and the 1964 election confirmed his mandate while he presented himself as the candidate of restraint.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.0] On 2 August 1964 North Vietnamese torpedo boats attacked the US destroyer USS Maddox, which was on a naval intelligence patrol near the North Vietnamese coast. South Vietnamese raids on that coast (Operation 34A) had taken place days before, and Hanoi linked them to the patrol.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.1] A second attack reported on 4 August 1964, on the Maddox and the destroyer USS Turner Joy, is now believed not to have happened. Sonar and radar readings in bad weather were misread, and later documents showed that officials doubted the report even as it was used.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.2] On the evening of 4 August Johnson announced retaliation, and air strikes hit North Vietnamese naval bases on 5 August.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.3] The Tonkin Gulf Resolution, passed on 7 August 1964 by 416 votes to 0 in the House and 88 to 2 in the Senate, authorised the President to take 'all necessary measures'. This gave Johnson a free hand without a declaration of war.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.4] Senator J. William Fulbright helped steer it through the Senate. Senators Wayne Morse and Ernest Gruening voted against it. A draft resolution had been prepared before the incidents, and the second incident was doubtful, which later made the resolution a target of criticism.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.5] In the election of 3 November 1964 Johnson won about 61 per cent of the popular vote against Barry Goldwater, who had called for stronger action in Vietnam and was portrayed as reckless. Johnson presented himself as the candidate of restraint.
[hist-eur6-s3.3.6] The restraint was a political strategy. Johnson publicly promised not to widen the war while privately preparing for a larger one, which is the gap between campaign promises and later action that critics later seized on.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.b] In the first months of 1965 Johnson moved from reprisal to a sustained bombing campaign and then to ground troops. This turned an advisers' war into an American war.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.0] On 7 February 1965 a Viet Cong attack on the American base at Pleiku killed eight Americans. Johnson ordered reprisal air raids (Flaming Dart), which led on to sustained bombing.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.1] Operation Rolling Thunder, a sustained bombing campaign against North Vietnam, began on 2 March 1965 and continued in stages until 2 November 1968. Its aims were to put pressure on Hanoi and to support the Saigon government.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.2] Its limit was that bombing did not stop the war. North Vietnam refused to negotiate while the bombing continued, and the Viet Cong kept fighting in the South, supplied through the Ho Chi Minh Trail (a network of routes through Laos and Cambodia).
[hist-eur6-s3.4.3] About 3,500 US Marines landed at Da Nang on 8 March 1965, the first American ground combat troops in Vietnam. They were sent to protect the air base, but they began the build-up of ground forces.
[hist-eur6-s3.4.4] Johnson's strategy was to hold the South while signalling willingness to negotiate. In his Johns Hopkins University speech in Baltimore on 7 April 1965 he offered unconditional discussions and a development plan for the Mekong region (about one billion dollars).
[hist-eur6-s3.4.5] Hanoi did not accept, so the offer of talks was not followed up, and the build-up went on.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.b] Johnson committed large ground forces in the summer of 1965, but he did it quietly and against some advice. He wanted to protect the Great Society legislation then passing through Congress.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.0] General William Westmoreland, commander of US forces from June 1964, argued that a war of attrition with large forces would wear down the North Vietnamese.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.1] Johnson's advisers were not united. Under Secretary of State George Ball argued in memos in 1964 and 1965 that the United States would be dragged into an unwinnable land war, but Johnson, McNamara, Rusk and Bundy chose escalation.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.2] On 28 July 1965 Johnson announced that US forces would rise from 75,000 to 125,000 at once and that more would follow as commanders requested. He asked for no declaration of war and did not call up the reserves.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.3] He kept the escalation low-key and avoided a formal declaration of war because he did not want to endanger his Great Society legislation, which was passing through Congress in 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.5.4] US troop numbers were about 184,000 at the end of 1965, about 385,000 at the end of 1966 and about 485,000 at the end of 1967. They were over 500,000 in 1968 and peaked at about 540,000 in 1969 before falling.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.b] Westmoreland's methods turned the war into a contest of firepower and numbers. They killed many people but did not break communist will, and they carried heavy costs for Vietnamese civilians and Americans alike.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.0] Westmoreland's strategy was attrition (wearing the enemy down by killing more than they could replace) through search and destroy. Large units hunted communist forces in the countryside, using helicopters, artillery and air power, and success was measured by the body count.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.1] Attrition failed because the communists could choose when to fight and replaced losses from a large population and from supplies carried down the Ho Chi Minh Trail. So the killing did not break their will.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.2] Heavy firepower, including napalm (a sticky burning fuel) and the defoliant Agent Orange (a chemical that strips leaves so the enemy cannot hide), killed civilians and drove peasants into government-controlled camps or towards the Viet Cong.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.3] Pacification (winning over the countryside) and the building of a stable South Vietnamese government lagged behind. The Saigon regime was led by generals, and Nguyen Van Thieu became president in September 1967 after elections criticised as flawed.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.4] In November 1967 Westmoreland told Washington that the end was in view. This set up the shock of the Tet Offensive.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.5] On 16 March 1968 American soldiers killed several hundred unarmed villagers at My Lai. The massacre was exposed publicly in November 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.6.6] More than 30,000 Americans were dead by the end of 1968. The draft (compulsory military service) gave deferments to most college students, so it fell on those without them and spread the war's costs through American families.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.b] As the war grew, Johnson's public statements were increasingly doubted. From 1965 his critics in Congress, universities, churches and the press challenged the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.0] Journalists used the term credibility gap from the mid-1960s for the gap between official statements on the war and what reporters found in Vietnam. Johnson played the war down in public to protect the Great Society, so the later gap between his optimism and events hurt him.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.1] The first teach-in (a meeting of lectures and debate against the war) was held at the University of Michigan on 24 to 25 March 1965. Student and church opposition grew steadily through 1966 and 1967.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.2] Students for a Democratic Society (SDS) led a march of about 20,000 in Washington on 17 April 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.3] Senator J. William Fulbright, chairman of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee, held televised hearings in February 1966 that questioned the war's basis. He had helped steer the 1964 resolution through, so his change of mind carried weight.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.4] His book The Arrogance of Power (1966) argued that the United States was acting as a great power imposing its will on a small nation.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.5] On 4 April 1967 Martin Luther King spoke against the war in his Beyond Vietnam speech at Riverside Church, New York, linking the war to poverty and racism.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.6] On 28 April 1967 Muhammad Ali refused induction into the army in Houston and was stripped of his heavyweight title, which made the draft a public issue.
[hist-eur6-s3.7.7] On 21 October 1967 about 50,000 people marched on the Pentagon, showing that opposition had spread well beyond students.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.b] By 1967 Americans were arguing about why the country was in Vietnam at all. Three ideas shaped the argument: that the war was imperialism, that opinion was turning, and that defence interests drove it.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.0] Imperialism was the critics' charge that a great power was imposing its will on a small nation. Fulbright used this argument, and it challenged the claim that the United States was only defending a free people.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.1] Critics argued that the war was costly and immoral and was draining money and attention from the Great Society.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.2] Supporters argued that withdrawal would damage America's credibility abroad and that the domino theory meant that other countries would fall if South Vietnam did.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.3] The military-industrial complex was the interest shared by defence contractors, the armed forces and government in high defence spending. Eisenhower had warned of it in his farewell address on 17 January 1961, and critics linked it to the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.8.4] Public opinion means the views of ordinary Americans, measured by polls and shaped by television and the press. It had turned against the war and Johnson from 1967, and by late 1967 Johnson's approval was about 40 per cent or below.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.b] Johnson tried to fund both the war and his domestic programme, and the cost of the war weakened his reform agenda.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.0] The Great Society's biggest laws came first: the Civil Rights Act (2 July 1964), Medicare and Medicaid (July 1965) and the Voting Rights Act (6 August 1965). They were passed before the war's costs became acute, and they are Johnson's most secure achievements.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.1] Johnson tried to pay for the war and the Great Society together, a policy critics called guns and butter. He delayed asking Congress for a tax increase until January 1967.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.2] Federal deficits grew and inflation rose after 1965, passing 4 per cent in 1968. Congress passed a 10 per cent income tax surcharge in June 1968 to restrain spending.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.3] The War on Poverty (declared January 1964) was cut or diverted as military spending grew, so many programmes were underfunded as they expanded.
[hist-eur6-s3.9.4] Urban riots, such as Watts in 1965 and Detroit in 1967, added to the loss of support. By 1967 the war's rising cost and the riots had turned public opinion against new domestic spending, so the Great Society lost momentum.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.b] The Tet Offensive of January 1968 did not defeat the Americans militarily, but it shattered their optimism and ended Johnson's chance of re-election.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.0] Tet began on 30 January 1968 (the night of 30 to 31 January), during the Tet holiday. Viet Cong and North Vietnamese forces attacked more than a hundred towns and cities across South Vietnam, including Saigon and Hue, which was retaken after about a month of fighting.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.1] On 31 January a Viet Cong squad entered the US embassy compound in Saigon and held out for about six hours. Television pictures shocked American opinion, even though the communists failed to hold it.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.2] Westmoreland had said in November 1967 that the end was in view and the enemy could no longer mount major offensives. Tet therefore looked like proof that official optimism was false, an American political defeat though a heavy communist military one.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.3] On 27 February 1968 the television newsman Walter Cronkite broadcast a report calling the war a stalemate. It was widely reported as a sign of changing opinion.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.4] In February 1968 Westmoreland and the Joint Chiefs asked for about 206,000 more troops. Clark Clifford, Secretary of Defense from 1 March 1968, advised against it. The Wise Men (senior outside advisers) told Johnson in late March that the war could not be won at an acceptable cost.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.5] In the New Hampshire primary on 12 March 1968 the anti-war Democrat Eugene McCarthy ran Johnson close. Robert Kennedy entered the race in mid-March, so Johnson was weakened within his own party.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.6] On 31 March 1968 Johnson announced a partial halt to the bombing north of the 20th parallel, offered talks and said he would not seek re-election. Peace talks began in Paris in May, but the war continued.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.7] Martin Luther King was assassinated on 4 April 1968. Robert Kennedy was shot on 5 June and died on 6 June. Rioting between police and protesters marked the Democratic convention in Chicago in August.
[hist-eur6-s3.10.8] Hubert Humphrey lost narrowly to the Republican Richard Nixon in November, who won the presidency after promising an honourable end to the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.b] A huge American effort failed to defeat a determined enemy. Historians and contemporaries give several linked reasons, and the best answers back each with evidence.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.0] The war was a limited one. The US avoided invading North Vietnam, mining Haiphong harbour (the North's main port) or using nuclear weapons, partly from fear of bringing in China or the Soviet Union, and this limited the pressure on Hanoi.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.1] North Vietnam and the Viet Cong used bases in Laos and Cambodia and the Ho Chi Minh Trail, which the US was reluctant to attack. So they could withdraw and rebuild, and their determination did not break.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.2] The South Vietnamese government was weak and corrupt, and the Army of the Republic of Vietnam could not hold the territory cleared by American forces.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.3] The strategy of attrition and the body count measure were criticised within the US army and in the press as misleading about the progress of the war.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.4] Johnson's gradual escalation and his refusal to consider a clear withdrawal kept options open while costs and divisions at home grew. He never explained clear aims to the public, so home support drained away.
[hist-eur6-s3.11.5] Historians differ on the cause. Some say Johnson was trapped by the assumptions of the Cold War, while others argue that he made avoidable choices on escalation in 1964 and 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.b] Johnson was the president who made Vietnam an American war and also the president of the Great Society. He was a decision-maker at the centre of events, not a witness.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.0] His career: House of Representatives 1937, Senate 1949, Majority Leader 1955 to 1961, vice president 1961 to 1963, and president from 22 November 1963 to January 1969.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.1] His domestic record was one of the greatest legislative achievements of any president: the Civil Rights Act (1964), the Voting Rights Act (1965), Medicare, Medicaid and federal aid to education.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.2] In Vietnam he took the decisions of 1964 and 1965 to bomb North Vietnam and send combat troops, always in steps. He hoped to avoid a wider war with China or the Soviet Union, as in Korea in 1950.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.3] He shaped events by making those decisions and was shaped by them. The Tet shock and falling support forced him out in 1968, and protesters outside the White House chanted at him about how many children he had killed that day.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.4] Contemporaries saw him as a forceful, domineering leader who was crude, devious and driven. The credibility gap made many doubt his word, though supporters pointed to his achievements for the poor and for black Americans.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.5] Historians in the 1970s, such as the journalist David Halberstam in The Best and the Brightest (1972), blamed the arrogance of the advisers he inherited.
[hist-eur6-s3.12.6] Later historians, such as Robert Dallek in Flawed Giant (1998) and Fredrik Logevall in Choosing War (1999), stress that Johnson had alternatives in 1964 and 1965 and chose escalation, partly to protect his domestic reputation.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.b] Examiners reward a clear judgement, stated at the start, that weighs Johnson's domestic achievements against his failure in Vietnam and sets contemporary views against later ones.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.0] Success at home: the Civil Rights Act, the Voting Rights Act, Medicare and Medicaid are judged to be major achievements of twentieth-century social reform, passed in 1964 and 1965.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.1] Failure abroad: the United States did not win in Vietnam, and about 58,000 Americans died in the whole war. The war damaged the Great Society and the public's trust in government.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.2] Contemporaries were divided. In 1964 and 1965 most Americans admired him as a strong, effective leader, but by late 1967 his approval had fallen to about 40 per cent or below. Protesters chanted about how many children he had killed that day.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.3] Later historians disagree. Halberstam blamed the arrogant advisers, while Dallek and Logevall argue that Johnson made avoidable choices. The debate is whether he was trapped by Cold War assumptions or chose escalation.
[hist-eur6-s3.13.4] Overall he was a great domestic president whose war undermined his presidency. The credibility gap was his lasting legacy, a problem of trust between president and public that continued to shape politics after Vietnam.
