TARGET hist-eur3-s5: 5. Case study: Stalin's show trials
[hist-eur3-s5.0.b] The trials of 1936 to 1938 followed a pattern set by earlier public trials, and they served several purposes for Stalin at once.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.0] The Shakhty trial of 1928 tried engineers from a Donbas coal town on charges of sabotage and foreign conspiracy, and the Industrial Party trial of 1930 repeated the charges against leading technical specialists.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.1] In April 1933 six British engineers of the Metropolitan-Vickers company were tried in Moscow for espionage and wrecking, a case that showed the regime blaming foreigners for industrial failures.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.2] The costs of the First Five-Year Plan and collectivisation, including shortages, accidents and the famine of 1932-33, created a need for scapegoats among managers, engineers and officials, who were accused of 'wrecking' and sabotage.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.3] Stalin's rivals from the succession struggle, Trotsky in exile and former oppositionists such as Zinoviev, Kamenev and Bukharin, still had prestige as old Bolsheviks and could be presented as a potential alternative leadership.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.4] The 17th Party Congress of January and February 1934, known as the Congress of Victors, celebrated the success of the plans, yet most of its delegates did not survive the Terror.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.5] Historians differ on motive: some stress Stalin's paranoia and desire for total personal power, while others point to real tensions over the pace of industrialisation, fear of foreign attack as Hitler rose, and the pressure on local officials to find enemies.
[hist-eur3-s5.0.6] A party purge or chistka began in 1933, when members judged unreliable were expelled, and in 1935 a check on party documents led to further expulsions; these purges were not at first fatal.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.b] The shooting of a leading Stalinist gave Stalin the pretext for the repression that followed.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.0] Sergei Kirov, party leader in Leningrad and a member of the Politburo, was shot dead on 1 December 1934 at the Smolny Institute, the party headquarters in Leningrad, by Leonid Nikolaev, a young party member who had been dismissed from his job.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.1] Nikolaev was tried in secret and shot in December 1934, and the NKVD at first blamed a plot by 'white guards' and then by former supporters of Zinoviev.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.2] A decree of 1 December 1934, issued the day of the murder and apparently drafted by Stalin, ordered that terrorism cases be heard in days, with no defence lawyer, no appeal and immediate execution of death sentences.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.3] Zinoviev and Kamenev were tried in secret in January 1935 and given prison terms of ten and five years; in 1936 they were brought back for a public trial on much graver charges.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.4] Historians disagree about the murder: some, such as Robert Conquest, suspected that Stalin or the NKVD arranged it, while others, including J. Arch Getty and Matthew Lenoe, find evidence that Nikolaev acted alone; no document proving Stalin's involvement has been found.
[hist-eur3-s5.1.5] Either way, Stalin exploited the killing: he claimed that a network of conspirators existed and that the opposition had been drawn into terrorism, and he used this claim to justify the show trials.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.b] The Trial of the Trotskyite-Zinovievite Terrorist Centre opened the public series.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.0] The trial was held from 19 to 24 August 1936 in the Hall of Columns of the House of Unions in Moscow, with sixteen defendants led by Grigory Zinoviev and Lev Kamenev, two of Lenin's closest colleagues and former members of the Politburo.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.1] They were charged with forming a terrorist centre under Trotsky's direction, murdering Kirov, and plotting to kill Stalin and other leaders.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.2] Before the trial Zinoviev and Kamenev had held out for months; Stalin reportedly promised that their lives and their families would be spared if they confessed, and the NKVD interrogators told them that confession would serve the party.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.3] All sixteen defendants confessed in open court and all were sentenced to death; they were shot on 25 August 1936, within hours of the verdict, despite the promise.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.4] Trotsky, living in Norway, and his son Lev Sedov were named as the organisers of the plot and were condemned in their absence.
[hist-eur3-s5.2.5] The trial opened a campaign against 'Trotskyite-Zinovievite' enemies and led to the arrest of thousands of former oppositionists, and Mikhail Tomsky, a Right Oppositionist named during the trial, shot himself on 22 August 1936 before he could be arrested.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.b] The trial of the so-called Anti-Soviet Trotskyite Centre moved the charges on to industrial sabotage.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.0] It was held from 23 to 30 January 1937 and tried seventeen defendants, among them Yuri Pyatakov, a deputy commissar for heavy industry, and the journalist Karl Radek.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.1] The defendants were accused of wrecking in factories and mines, causing railway accidents and plotting with Trotsky and with Germany and Japan to restore capitalism; the charges gave an explanation for the failures of the plans.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.2] Pyatakov claimed to have flown to Oslo in December 1935 to meet Trotsky, but the Norwegian authorities reported that no foreign aircraft had landed there at the time, an inconsistency that foreign critics noticed.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.3] Thirteen defendants, including Pyatakov, were shot; Radek and Grigory Sokolnikov received ten years in prison for co-operating with the prosecution, and two others received eight years.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.4] Radek died in 1939 in prison; the circumstances are unclear, and the usual account is that he was killed by a fellow prisoner, probably on NKVD orders.
[hist-eur3-s5.3.5] Sergo Ordzhonikidze, head of heavy industry and Pyatakov's superior, shot himself in February 1937, an event officially described as a heart attack.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.b] The destruction of the army leadership took place largely in secret, unlike the public trials.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.0] Marshal Mikhail Tukhachevsky, Deputy Defence Commissar and the leading advocate of modern mechanised warfare, was arrested in May 1937 with other senior officers.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.1] They were tried by a closed military tribunal on 11 June 1937 on charges of treason and spying for Germany, and were shot the following day; the evidence was fabricated and relied on confessions.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.2] Three of the five marshals of the Soviet Union, most army commanders and a large share of corps and divisional commanders were arrested, as were many naval and air force officers.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.3] In total roughly 30,000 to 35,000 officers were dismissed or arrested between 1937 and 1939, although some were later released, and political commissars regained control of the army.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.4] The purge left the Red Army poorly led at the time of the Winter War against Finland (1939-40) and the German invasion of June 1941, though historians debate how much the purge explains the defeats of 1941.
[hist-eur3-s5.4.5] Tukhachevsky was rehabilitated in 1957.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.b] The Trial of the Right-Trotskyite Bloc was the last and the largest of the public trials.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.0] It was held from 2 to 13 March 1938 and tried twenty-one defendants, including Nikolai Bukharin (former editor of Pravda and Comintern chairman), Alexei Rykov (former Prime Minister), the former NKVD chief Genrikh Yagoda, and Nikolai Krestinsky.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.1] The defendants were charged with treason, spying for foreign powers, wrecking, planning to dismember the USSR, and conspiring to murder Lenin, Stalin, Kirov and the writer Maxim Gorky; Yagoda and a group of doctors were accused of causing deaths by medical murder.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.2] On the first day Krestinsky retracted his confession, saying it was false, but he reversed himself the next day after a night in NKVD hands.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.3] Bukharin accepted overall political responsibility for the 'bloc' but denied the specific charges of espionage and murder; this has been read as an attempt to signal his innocence to a careful reader.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.4] Eighteen defendants, including Bukharin, Rykov and Yagoda, were shot on 15 March 1938; three others received prison terms.
[hist-eur3-s5.5.5] Bukharin was rehabilitated by the Soviet government in 1988.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.b] The trials were scripted from start to finish, and Stalin himself reviewed the charges and the testimony.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.0] The prosecutor Andrei Vyshinsky, a former Menshevik and lawyer, prepared the cases with the NKVD and argued that a confession was sufficient proof of guilt.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.1] The NKVD obtained confessions by sleep deprivation, long interrogations, threats against family members, false promises of leniency and, from 1937, with Stalin's approval, physical torture.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.2] Defendants rehearsed their parts, and some seem to have been persuaded that confession served the party's interest, an argument used by interrogators on loyal communists.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.3] The trials took place in public in the Hall of Columns or the October Hall of the House of Unions before an invited audience, with foreign journalists and diplomats present and the full transcripts published in several languages.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.4] The presiding judge Vasily Ulrikh, a military judge, helped to keep the proceedings tightly controlled, and Soviet newspapers demanded the death penalty before the verdicts.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.5] Weaknesses in the evidence, such as the Oslo flight, an alleged meeting in the Hotel Bristol in Copenhagen that had been demolished, and the near-identical wording of confessions, were noticed by foreign observers.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.6] Joseph Davies, the American ambassador, who attended parts of the 1937 and 1938 trials, reported to Washington that the evidence appeared to establish guilt, and the German writer Lion Feuchtwanger, who visited Moscow in 1937, defended the trials in a book.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.7] Some other diplomats and journalists were doubtful, and many liberals and socialists condemned the proceedings as a travesty of justice.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.8] In 1937 an international commission chaired by the American philosopher John Dewey heard Trotsky's evidence in Mexico, in April, and in December reported that the charges were false; its report was titled Not Guilty.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.9] Western communist parties defended the trials as necessary to destroy fascist agents, and some members left the party over the issue; the trials damaged the reputation of the Soviet system among liberals.
[hist-eur3-s5.6.10] Stalin used the verdicts to discredit Trotsky, who was murdered in Mexico by an agent of the NKVD, Ramón Mercader, in August 1940.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.b] Show trials were the visible tip of a mass campaign run by the NKVD and carried out largely in secret.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.0] Nikolai Yezhov replaced Yagoda as head of the NKVD in September 1936, and the years 1937 and 1938 are often called the Yezhovshchina.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.1] NKVD Order 00447 of 30 July 1937 set quotas by region for the arrest of 'former kulaks, criminals and anti-Soviet elements', with the first category to be shot and the second sent to camps; local officials asked for higher quotas, and the targets were exceeded.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.2] National operations targeted ethnic groups with foreign connections, such as Poles, Germans and Latvians, and the Polish operation of 1937-38 was the largest.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.3] NKVD records opened after 1989 show about 1.6 million arrests for 'counter-revolutionary' crimes in 1937 and 1938 and about 680,000 executions; some historians believe the true total of deaths was higher once camp deaths are added.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.4] Many victims were ordinary workers, peasants and minorities rather than officials, and neighbours and colleagues were encouraged to denounce 'enemies of the people'.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.5] According to Khrushchev's secret speech of 1956, 1,108 of the 1,966 delegates to the 1934 Congress were arrested and 98 of 139 members and candidates of the Central Committee elected at the Congress were shot.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.6] By 1939 the Gulag held well over a million prisoners.
[hist-eur3-s5.7.7] The Terror slowed in late 1938: Yezhov was removed in November 1938 and replaced by Lavrentiy Beria, and he was arrested in 1939 and shot in February 1940.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.b] The trials illustrate three of the syllabus concepts.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.0] Totalitarianism: the secret police, the control of the courts and the press, and the ability to make leading communists confess to invented crimes show a state that left no area outside political control.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.1] Propaganda: newspapers, radio and public meetings across the USSR demanded death for the 'enemies of the people', and the confessions were used to explain shortages and accidents.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.2] Personality cult: the trials presented Stalin as the vigilant leader who protected the people, Lenin's true heir, and denied the old Bolsheviks their share in the revolution.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.3] The trials and the Terror were followed by the Short Course history of the party in 1938, which presented the defendants as agents of foreign powers from the beginning of the revolution.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.4] The party's old guard was destroyed; Stalin's rivals and potential rivals were dead or in exile, and the new officials who replaced them owed their positions to him.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.5] The economy lost managers, engineers and planners, and fear of being accused of wrecking made officials cautious and encouraged them to falsify results or avoid decisions.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.6] The army and the security services were purged, and the scale of the loss of experienced officers is widely linked to the poor performance against Finland in 1939-40 and the German advance in 1941.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.7] Society was affected through fear, denunciations and the arrest of relatives, and families of those arrested were stigmatised as relatives of enemies of the people.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.8] Younger men promoted into the vacancies in the party, army and industry owed their careers to Stalin, which made the new elite loyal to him.
[hist-eur3-s5.8.9] In 1956 Khrushchev condemned the Terror in his secret speech; mass rehabilitations followed, and Bukharin's was one of the last in 1988.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.b] Judgements balance the consolidation of Stalin's personal power against the damage the Terror did to the state.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.0] Achievement for Stalin: by 1939 the party, army and state were in the hands of officials loyal to him, and no organised opposition survived.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.1] Limit for the regime: the loss of officers, managers and specialists weakened the army and the economy in the years before Barbarossa.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.2] Contemporary supportive view: Soviet propaganda and foreign communists presented the trials as the exposure of real traitors, and visitors such as Davies and Feuchtwanger accepted the evidence.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.3] Contemporary critical view: the Dewey Commission, liberal and socialist critics and many diplomats treated the trials as frame-ups.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.4] Later view: after the opening of the archives in the 1990s historians treat the trials as stage-managed fabrications; they still debate how far Stalin planned the Terror, how much was driven from below by local officials, and how far it reflected paranoia or political calculation.
[hist-eur3-s5.9.5] Later revisionist view: J. Arch Getty and others stress the disorder and local initiative in the Terror, while Robert Conquest and others emphasise central planning by Stalin.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s4: 4. Communism in Russia: Lenin, Stalin and the Soviet alternative
[hist-eur3-s4.0.b] After the Bolsheviks seized power in 1917 (see the notes on the Russian Revolution), Lenin built a one-party state and fought a civil war that shaped the Soviet system.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.0] The Cheka, the Bolshevik secret police set up in December 1917 under Felix Dzerzhinsky, used arrest and execution against opponents, including the Socialist Revolutionaries and Mensheviks, and after the attempt on Lenin it carried out the Red Terror from September 1918.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.1] The Russian Civil War (1918-1921, with fighting continuing in the Far East until 1922) pitted the Red Army, led by Leon Trotsky as War Commissar, against the White armies and foreign intervention forces.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.2] War Communism (1918-1921) nationalised industry and banks, forced peasants to hand over grain at fixed low prices, and rationed food to towns.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.3] Peasant revolts and famine in 1920-1921, combined with the grain requisitioning, showed how unpopular the policy had become.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.4] The Bolsheviks banned rival parties during the civil war, and the Communist Party became the only legal party in the Soviet state.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.5] Lenin survived an assassination attempt in August 1918, after which the Cheka's use of terror was greatly expanded.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.6] In March 1921 sailors and workers at the naval base of Kronstadt revolted against Bolshevik rule, demanding free elections and an end to the monopoly of power; the Red Army crushed the revolt.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.7] At the Tenth Party Congress in March 1921 the Bolsheviks adopted the New Economic Policy (NEP), which allowed small private trade, peasant sales of surplus grain and some private small-scale industry.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.8] The NEP restored food supplies and the economy recovered by the mid-1920s, while large industry and banking remained under state control.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.9] The NEP was seen by many Communists as a retreat, and critics on the left argued that it produced a new class of traders and wealthy peasants called Nepmen and kulaks.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.10] The Union of Soviet Socialist Republics was formally created on 30 December 1922, bringing together Russia, Ukraine, Belarus and the Transcaucasian republics.
[hist-eur3-s4.0.11] Lenin suffered strokes in 1922 and 1923, the first in May 1922, and died on 21 January 1924, leaving the party without a clear successor.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.b] Stalin's rise as General Secretary, and his defeat of rival factions, decided who would lead the Soviet Union in the years of the Five-Year Plans.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.0] Stalin had become General Secretary of the Communist Party in April 1922, a post that gave him control of party appointments.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.1] Lenin's Testament, dictated in late 1922 and early 1923, criticised Stalin's rudeness and called for his removal from the post of General Secretary; the Central Committee did not publish it at the time.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.2] Zinoviev and Kamenev first allied with Stalin against Trotsky in 1923-25; Trotsky's Left Opposition argued for rapid industrialisation and world revolution, and when Zinoviev and Kamenev joined him in the United Opposition of 1926 it was defeated, with its leaders expelled from the party by December 1927.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.3] Trotsky was expelled from the party in 1927, sent to internal exile in Kazakhstan in 1928 and exiled from the Soviet Union in 1929; Stalin's agent killed him in Mexico in 1940.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.4] The Right Opposition led by Nikolai Bukharin, Alexei Rykov and Mikhail Tomsky favoured the NEP and gradual growth, and was defeated in 1928-29 after Stalin, who had used it to beat the Left, turned to the Left's policy of rapid industrialisation and forced grain collection.
[hist-eur3-s4.1.5] Stalin's defeat of rivals in the party allowed him to launch the First Five-Year Plan, and the Trotsky and Bukharin factions were both removed from political influence by 1929.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.b] The First Five-Year Plan (1928-1932) set targets for heavy industry, and later plans extended the drive for rapid industrial growth.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.0] Gosplan, the state planning agency, drew up the First Five-Year Plan, which was launched in October 1928 and aimed to transform the Soviet Union into an industrial power.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.1] Targets were set for coal, steel, electricity, oil and machinery, with heavy industry given priority over consumer goods.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.2] The steel city of Magnitogorsk was built in the southern Urals, becoming a symbol of the drive for industrialisation and of the labour that went into it.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.3] Alexei Stakhanov, a Donbas coal miner, was reported to have cut 102 tonnes of coal in a single shift in August 1935, and the Stakhanovite movement was used to promote higher output, though the feat was prepared with help and made norms harder for ordinary workers.
[hist-eur3-s4.2.4] Steel output rose from about 4 million tonnes in 1928 to about 18 million in 1940, but official statistics were inflated and the costs were very high: wages fell, housing was overcrowded, quality was poor and forced labour was used on projects such as the White Sea-Baltic Canal (1931-33).
[hist-eur3-s4.2.5] The Second Five-Year Plan (1933-1937) and the Third (1938-1941) continued the emphasis on heavy industry, and the Soviet economy was militarised by the late 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.b] Collectivisation aimed to bring peasant agriculture under state control, and its enforcement caused famine, mass deaths and the destruction of the kulaks as a class.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.0] Collectivisation was launched in late 1929, when Stalin announced the 'liquidation of the kulaks as a class', and peasants were forced to join collective farms (kolkhozy) in which land and livestock were pooled and run to state orders.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.1] Kulaks, the richer peasants, were described as enemies of the state and were deported or killed in a campaign of dekulakisation.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.2] Peasants resisted by slaughtering livestock, and grain requisitions were set at levels that left villages short of food.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.3] The harvest failures and forced requisitions of 1932 and 1933 produced a famine that killed several million people, mainly in Ukraine, the North Caucasus and Kazakhstan.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.4] The Soviet authorities denied the famine and restricted movement to prevent starving peasants from reaching the towns.
[hist-eur3-s4.3.5] Collectivisation transformed agriculture into a system of state-controlled production, but it produced lower output of meat and grain for several years.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.b] The regime pursued a programme of social change that promoted literacy, changed family life and sought to replace religion with atheism and Marxist culture.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.0] Literacy campaigns in the 1920s and 1930s raised reading levels greatly, and education was expanded to train workers and engineers.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.1] The Bolsheviks legalised abortion in 1920 and promoted equality for women in work and politics, but abortion was restricted again in 1936.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.2] The League of the Militant Godless, founded in 1925, campaigned against religion, and many churches and monasteries were closed or destroyed.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.3] Socialist realism became the official style of art and literature in 1932, and the Union of Soviet Writers, formed in 1934, enforced its standards.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.4] Cultural life was controlled by the party, and artists and writers who did not follow the official line faced censorship, expulsion or arrest.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.5] The Soviet system promoted a new Soviet man and woman and a culture of collective achievement, expressed in parades, posters and public monuments.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.6] Western visitors toured Soviet factories and collective farms in the early 1930s, and some returned with favourable reports about rapid industrial growth and the absence of unemployment.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.7] The Webbs, Sidney and Beatrice, British socialists, published Soviet Communism: A New Civilisation? in 1935, which presented the Soviet system as a model for social planning.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.8] The Depression made the planned economy look more attractive to some socialists, intellectuals and trade unionists in Britain, France and the United States.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.9] Critics in the West, including many liberal and conservative commentators, pointed to the famine, the show trials and the absence of political freedom.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.10] Contemporary opinion was therefore divided, and the Soviet alternative was a source of debate between supporters of planning and defenders of liberal democracy.
[hist-eur3-s4.4.11] The Soviet Union's diplomatic recognition by the United States in 1933 and its membership of the League of Nations from 1934 gave the regime greater international standing.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.b] The German invasion of June 1941 tested the Soviet system, and Stalin's wartime leadership combined the mobilisation of resources with terror against soldiers and civilians.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.0] Germany invaded the Soviet Union on 22 June 1941 in Operation Barbarossa, and the Red Army suffered heavy losses in the first months of the war.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.1] Stalin chaired the State Defence Committee set up on 30 June 1941 to direct the war economy, and became Supreme Commander in August 1941; he stayed in Moscow during the German advance in the autumn of 1941.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.2] Factories were moved east to the Urals and Siberia in 1941, and output of tanks, aircraft and munitions rose sharply by 1942.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.3] The Battle of Stalingrad, from August 1942 to February 1943, marked the turning point on the Eastern Front, when the German Sixth Army was encircled and surrendered.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.4] Stalin used the Red Army and the NKVD to enforce discipline, including executions of deserters and punishment of soldiers who had been captured.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.5] The Soviet Union's victory in the war was used to strengthen Stalin's personal authority, and he was given the rank of Generalissimo in 1945.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.6] Order 227 of July 1942, 'Not one step back', led to blocking detachments behind the front line and penal battalions for those who retreated.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.7] Soviet industry relocated about 1,500 factories east of the Urals in 1941 and 1942, and Lend-Lease supplies from Britain and the United States, including trucks and food, supported the Red Army from 1941.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.8] Soviet victories at Stalingrad (February 1943) and Kursk (July 1943) turned the war, and the Red Army reached Berlin in April-May 1945.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.9] Soviet deaths in the war are usually estimated at about 27 million, military and civilian combined, far more than any other combatant.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.10] Prisoners of war who returned from German captivity and whole nationalities such as the Chechens and Crimean Tatars, accused of collaboration, were punished by imprisonment or deportation.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.11] In 1943 Stalin eased pressure on the Orthodox Church and appealed to Russian nationalism, calling the conflict the Great Patriotic War, to win popular support.
[hist-eur3-s4.5.12] In 1945 Stalin joined Churchill and Roosevelt at Yalta and Potsdam, and the USSR emerged as a superpower with control over Eastern Europe.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.b] Lenin led the Bolsheviks from 1903 to 1924, and he created the institutions that Stalin inherited.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.0] Born Vladimir Ulyanov in 1870 at Simbirsk, he had an elder brother who was executed in 1887 for plotting against Tsar Alexander III.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.1] He was a participant in the 1917 revolutions, returning to Russia in April 1917 and issuing the April Theses calling for the transfer of power to the soviets.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.2] He led the Bolsheviks in seizing power in October 1917 and presided over the Council of People's Commissars, the first Soviet government.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.3] As leader in the civil war, he authorised the use of terror, the grain requisitioning of War Communism and the suppression of the Kronstadt revolt.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.4] Contemporaries in the party saw him as the authority on Marxist theory and tactics, and his death in 1924 was marked by an embalmed body displayed in a mausoleum in Moscow.
[hist-eur3-s4.6.5] Historians judge him as the architect of the one-party state, though some stress that the NEP shows a pragmatic side, and others argue that his methods made the later terror possible.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.b] Stalin's leadership from the late 1920s to his death in 1953 transformed the Soviet economy and society, and he built a cult of personality around himself.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.0] Born Ioseb Dzhugashvili in 1878 at Gori in Georgia, he was trained for the priesthood before joining the revolutionary movement and taking the name Stalin.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.1] As General Secretary he built a network of loyal appointees, and by 1929 he had defeated his main rivals in the party.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.2] He directed the Five-Year Plans and collectivisation, and his decisions set the timetable and targets for the whole economy.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.3] Stalin was the central figure of Soviet propaganda, with his portraits, statues and sayings displayed across the country, and cities such as Stalingrad were named after him.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.4] Western admirers in the 1930s, such as the Webbs, praised his industrial achievements, while the famine, the show trials and the Terror provoked criticism from liberal and socialist opponents.
[hist-eur3-s4.7.5] After Stalin's death, Nikita Khrushchev denounced the cult of personality in a secret speech to the 20th Party Congress in February 1956, and historians have since debated how far his policies reflected ideology, paranoia or the pressures of industrialisation.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.b] A personality cult presented Stalin as the infallible successor of Lenin, and a system of terror and control gave the state its totalitarian character.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.0] The cult began with the celebrations of Stalin's fiftieth birthday in December 1929, when Pravda hailed him as Lenin's heir and the 'leader' of the party.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.1] Official history was rewritten: the History of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Short Course) of 1938 gave Stalin the central role in the revolution, while Trotsky and other rivals were written out and removed from photographs.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.2] Towns, factories and streets were named after Stalin, and poets, artists and film-makers portrayed him as the wise father of the people.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.3] The 1936 Constitution, called the Stalin Constitution, promised universal suffrage, a secret ballot and civil rights, while the Communist Party's monopoly of power was left untouched.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.4] The NKVD and the Gulag network of forced-labour camps held millions of prisoners, many of them peasants and political prisoners, who built canals, railways and mines.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.5] Internal passports introduced in 1932 and 1933 and the registration of residence controlled movement, tying peasants to the countryside and keeping 'undesirables' out of the cities.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.6] The Treaty of Rapallo with Germany (1922) restored relations between the two outcast states and allowed secret military co-operation on German rearmament on Soviet soil.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.7] The Comintern, founded in 1919, directed foreign communist parties; its policy of denouncing Social Democrats as 'social fascists' split the German left in the early 1930s.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.8] After Hitler took power, the USSR joined the League of Nations (1934), signed pacts with France and Czechoslovakia (1935) and backed the Popular Front policy of co-operation against fascism.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.9] The USSR gave aid to the Spanish Republic in the Civil War from 1936, but was excluded from the Munich conference in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.10] The Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939 gave Stalin time and a buffer: the Red Army entered eastern Poland on 17 September 1939, the USSR attacked Finland in the Winter War of November 1939, and the Baltic states were annexed in 1940.
[hist-eur3-s4.8.11] The pact left the USSR unready when Germany attacked in June 1941, despite many intelligence warnings which Stalin discounted.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.b] Judgements of the Soviet system balance its industrial and military achievements against the human costs of its methods.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.0] Achievement: by 1941 the Soviet Union had built a large heavy industry base, and its war production helped defeat Germany.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.1] Limit: collectivisation destroyed much of the peasant farming system and caused a famine that killed millions in 1932 and 1933.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.2] Achievement: literacy and education expanded greatly, and women gained formal equality in work and the professions.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.3] Limit: the cult of personality, the suppression of rival parties and the arrests of opponents removed any independent political life.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.4] Contemporary view: many Western socialists and intellectuals saw the Soviet Union as a model for planning during the Depression.
[hist-eur3-s4.9.5] Later view: historians after 1945 stressed the costs and the totalitarian features of the system, while more recent work has examined its social change and the views of ordinary people.
