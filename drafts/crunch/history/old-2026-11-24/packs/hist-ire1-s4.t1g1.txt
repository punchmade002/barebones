[hist-ire1-s3.0.b] Land was the basis of wealth and power, and rural society was a ladder running from a small, mainly Protestant landlord class down to millions of landless labourers.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.0] About 10,000 landlords, mostly Protestant, owned nearly all the land; many held estates in more than one county.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.1] Absentee landlords lived in England or Dublin and ran their estates through agents, so rents left the locality and improvements were rare.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.2] Middlemen leased large farms from landlords on long leases and sublet them in smaller lots at higher rents; landlords increasingly ended such leases after 1815.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.3] Strong farmers held 30 acres or more, employed labour and often grazed cattle; they were the most secure and prosperous tenants.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.4] Smallholders held between about 5 and 30 acres, mostly as tenants-at-will with no lease, who could be evicted at short notice.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.5] Cottiers rented a cabin and a potato patch, paying their rent mainly in labour for the farmer or landlord.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.6] Landless labourers hired conacre, a patch of manured ground taken for one season to grow potatoes, often at very high rents.
[hist-ire1-s3.0.7] Most tenants had no legal security of tenure, and any improvement they made to their holding belonged to the landlord, which discouraged investment.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.0] Ireland's population rose from about 5 million around 1800 to about 8.2 million at the 1841 census.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.1] Early marriage and large families were common among the poor, because a couple needed only a potato patch and a cabin to set up home.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.2] Holdings were subdivided among sons, so that by 1841 nearly half of all holdings were under 5 acres.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.3] The potato yielded more food per acre than any grain, grew on poor soil and, with buttermilk, gave a nutritious diet.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.4] By the 1840s about 3 million people depended on the potato almost entirely; an adult labourer ate up to about 12–14 lb a day.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.5] The heavy-cropping but poor-quality lumper variety had spread widely among the poor, and potatoes could not be stored from one season to the next.
[hist-ire1-s3.1.6] The 1841 census classed about 40% of all houses as fourth-class: one-roomed mud cabins, the lowest category of housing.
[hist-ire1-s3.2.0] Wartime demand had pushed up grain prices and rents during the French Wars; after Waterloo in 1815 prices fell steeply, but rents fell slowly.
[hist-ire1-s3.2.1] Tenants fell into arrears and many landlords, themselves heavily in debt from wartime spending and family settlements, could not afford to invest.
[hist-ire1-s3.2.2] Landlords began clearing smallholders and middlemen to consolidate farms, especially for more profitable pasture.
[hist-ire1-s3.2.3] Rural industry declined: hand spinning and weaving of linen outside north-east Ulster collapsed against factory competition, and the Irish cotton industry declined after protective duties ended in 1824.
[hist-ire1-s3.2.4] Partial famines and fever followed harvest failures in 1816–1817, 1822 and the early 1830s, as well as several local failures in the late 1830s.
[hist-ire1-s3.2.5] Agrarian secret societies such as the Whiteboys, Rockites and Ribbonmen attacked landlords, agents and new tenants who took farms after evictions.
