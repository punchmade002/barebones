TARGET hist-eur5-s7: 7. Economic consequences of decolonisation: the Lomé Conventions, trade, aid and famine, and Nyerere's ujamaa
[hist-eur5-s7.0.b] Colonial rule built economies around exporting raw materials to Europe, and these structures outlived independence.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.0] Colonial economies were organised around cash crops and minerals: cocoa in the Gold Coast, groundnuts in Senegal, cotton in Uganda and copper in Katanga.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.1] Railways and ports were built to carry exports to the coast, so most African transport networks linked interior mines and farms to ports rather than to each other.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.2] Colonial powers controlled trade through their own companies and currencies, and African states inherited trading patterns that tied them to a few markets.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.3] Development economists such as Raúl Prebisch and Hans Singer argued from 1950 that the terms of trade, the ratio of export prices to import prices, tend to move against primary producers, so African countries have to export more to buy the same manufactured goods; commodity prices fell sharply in the 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.4] Dependence on one or two commodities made African economies vulnerable to price falls, as with copper prices in the 1970s and cocoa and coffee prices in the 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.5] Colonial governments in the Belgian Congo and French West Africa required peasants to grow set quantities of cotton and other cash crops, which were bought at fixed low prices.
[hist-eur5-s7.0.6] Multinational companies such as Unilever's United Africa Company, Shell-BP and the Union Minière kept control of trade, oil and mining after independence, which is why the topic is linked to the idea of a transnational economy.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.b] The European Economic Community set up trade and aid agreements with former African colonies, which later became the Lomé Conventions.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.0] The Treaty of Rome of 1957 associated the overseas territories of the EEC members, mostly in Africa, with the Community.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.1] The Yaoundé Convention of 1963 (with a second in 1969) between the EEC and 18 associated African states gave their exports duty-free access to European markets in return for limited reciprocal opening of their own markets.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.2] Lomé I was signed on 28 February 1975 in Lomé, Togo, between the EEC and 46 African, Caribbean and Pacific (ACP) states, after Britain's entry in 1973 brought in Commonwealth countries; the ACP group as an organisation was formed later that year.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.3] Lomé I gave ACP exports largely duty-free access to the EEC without requiring reciprocal concessions, which was a new departure in trade relations.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.4] STABEX was a fund set up under Lomé I to compensate ACP countries when earnings from specified agricultural exports fell below a set level.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.5] Later Conventions were signed in 1979 (Lomé II), 1984 (Lomé III) and 1989 (Lomé IV), and they increasingly added conditions on human rights and governance.
[hist-eur5-s7.1.6] Lomé I also included a Sugar Protocol, which guaranteed quotas for ACP sugar at European prices, and Lomé II (1979) added SYSMIN, a fund to help mineral exporters; the Conventions were replaced by the Cotonou Agreement in 2000.
[hist-eur5-s7.2.0] The IMF and the World Bank were created at the Bretton Woods conference of 1944; the IMF lent to countries with balance-of-payments problems and the World Bank funded development projects.
[hist-eur5-s7.2.1] Oil price rises in 1973 and 1979 raised import costs for oil-importing African states, and many governments borrowed heavily from banks and international lenders in the 1970s.
[hist-eur5-s7.2.2] When interest rates rose in the early 1980s and commodity prices fell, African countries struggled to repay, and Mexico's default in 1982 marked the start of the wider debt crisis.
[hist-eur5-s7.2.3] From the early 1980s the IMF and World Bank made new loans conditional on structural adjustment programmes: devaluation, cuts in public spending, removal of subsidies and the sale of state enterprises.
[hist-eur5-s7.2.4] The World Bank's Berg Report of 1981 blamed African governments' own policies, such as overvalued currencies and state marketing boards, while critics argued that structural adjustment reduced spending on health and education; UNICEF's 1987 report 'Adjustment with a Human Face' made the case for protecting the poor.
[hist-eur5-s7.2.5] Ghana under Jerry Rawlings adopted an IMF-backed recovery programme in 1983, which was widely cited as an early success even though it brought hardship.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.0] Aid came in two forms: bilateral aid given government to government by a donor state, and multilateral aid channelled through bodies such as the World Bank and the UN.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.1] Tied aid required recipients to spend the money on goods and services from the donor country, which increased its cost and reduced its value.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.2] Many former colonies received aid from their former rulers, so aid often reinforced existing trade and political links rather than diversifying them.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.3] Aid was criticised for supporting corrupt or authoritarian governments, such as Mobutu's Zaire, and for creating dependence on donors for budgets and food.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.4] Some African governments tried to reduce dependence by promoting local industry and regional trade, but many national markets remained small and fragmented.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.5] The United Nations set a target in 1970 that rich countries give 0.7 per cent of national income as aid; few donors met it.
[hist-eur5-s7.3.6] Cold War rivalry shaped aid: China built the TAZARA railway linking Zambia to the Tanzanian port of Dar es Salaam between 1970 and 1975, and the Soviet Union and the United States each aided their allies.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.b] Famines in the post-colonial period were caused by war, drought and state policy, and they drew international relief efforts.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.0] During the Biafran War, blockade and fighting caused a famine in Biafra in 1968–70, and the images of starving children drew wide attention in Europe and North America.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.1] Relief flights into Biafra ran at night from São Tomé to the Uli airstrip, and they were limited by the federal government's control of the airspace; in June 1969 a Red Cross aircraft was shot down.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.2] A drought in the Sahel from the late 1960s to the mid-1970s caused a famine across Mali, Niger, Chad, Mauritania, Upper Volta (now Burkina Faso) and Senegal, with large loss of livestock; the Wollo famine of 1972-74 helped bring down Ethiopia's Emperor Haile Selassie in 1974.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.3] The Ethiopian famine of 1983-85 killed hundreds of thousands, with estimates from about 300,000 to one million; drought combined with the Derg regime's policies and the wars in Eritrea and Tigray, and a BBC report by Michael Buerk in October 1984 brought it to Western attention.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.4] The British singer Bob Geldof organised the Band Aid charity single 'Do They Know It's Christmas?', released in December 1984, and the Live Aid concerts in London and Philadelphia on 13 July 1985 raised about 150 million pounds for famine relief.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.5] Famine relief was also criticised for distorting local markets and for prolonging wars when food aid went to armed groups.
[hist-eur5-s7.4.6] The Ethiopian government's resettlement programme of 1984-86 moved about 600,000 people from the drought-hit north, and tens of thousands died during the moves.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.0] Non-governmental organisations (NGOs) are non-state charities and campaigning bodies that deliver aid and work on development projects.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.1] Oxfam was founded in Oxford in 1942 as the Oxford Committee for Famine Relief, and it later expanded into development work across Africa and Asia.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.2] Concern was founded in Dublin in 1968 by John and Kay O'Loughlin Kennedy in response to the Biafran famine, and it became one of Ireland's main aid agencies.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.3] Trócaire was founded in 1973 by the Catholic bishops of Ireland as the Irish Catholic development agency, and it works through local partners in Africa.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.4] NGOs distinguish between emergency relief and long-term development, and many shifted in the 1980s towards community projects and advocacy.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.5] Trócaire's annual Lenten campaign raised money and awareness in Irish parishes, and it became one of the best-known development campaigns in Ireland.
[hist-eur5-s7.5.6] GOAL was founded in Dublin in 1977 by John O'Shea, and Médecins Sans Frontières was founded in France in 1971 by doctors who had worked in Biafra.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.b] Julius Nyerere tried to build African socialism in Tanzania through state control and rural villages.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.0] The Arusha Declaration of 5 February 1967 committed Tanzania to ujamaa (familyhood or African socialism), self-reliance and state control of the main means of production.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.1] The government nationalised the banks and major industries and insurance companies in 1967, and it introduced a policy of ‘Education for Self-Reliance’ to shape schooling around rural work.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.2] The ujamaa villages were voluntary cooperative settlements at first, but from 1973-74 the government moved millions of people into villages, often by force, in a policy called villagisation.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.3] Villagisation disrupted farming, and food shortages followed; the oil price rise, drought and the cost of the war to overthrow Idi Amin of Uganda in 1978-79 added to the crisis, the government had to import food and the economy declined by the early 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.4] Tanzania made important gains in health and literacy, with adult literacy rising to a high proportion of the population by the early 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.5] Nyerere stepped down as president in 1985, and his successor Ali Hassan Mwinyi accepted an IMF programme in 1986 that reversed many of the socialist policies.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.6] Nyerere, known as Mwalimu (teacher), died in 1999; supporters stress his gains in education, health and national unity and his refusal to rule by ethnic favour, while critics stress the collapse of farm output, the use of compulsion and the one-party state.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.7] TANU's Mwongozo guidelines of 1971 required party members to work for their living and to avoid exploitation and privilege.
[hist-eur5-s7.6.8] Universal primary education launched in 1974 aimed to put every child in school by 1977, and primary enrolment rose rapidly.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.b] Development workers, including missionaries, volunteers and agency staff, carried out most of the day-to-day aid work in rural Africa from the 1960s.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.0] Development workers came from churches, voluntary bodies such as Britain's Voluntary Service Overseas (1958), government programmes such as the American Peace Corps (1961), and NGOs such as Concern and Trócaire.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.1] Their typical work included well-digging, agricultural advice, primary health care and vaccination, the supply of food in famines, and training of local staff.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.2] Many arrived as young volunteers on short contracts, and they often lived in rural communities with limited access to medicine, transport and communications.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.3] Their problems included poor roads and supplies, cultural misunderstandings with local communities, the risk of violence during civil wars, and the difficulty of keeping projects running after the donor left.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.4] They were participants in the aid system but also witnesses to its results, and their reports were important sources for NGO campaigns in Europe and North America.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.5] Contemporaries in Europe often saw development workers as humanitarian heroes, while African critics sometimes saw them as agents of dependence on foreign money.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.6] Historians have criticised ‘top-down’ development projects of the 1960s and 1970s, and they have praised the shift in the 1980s towards projects planned with local people.
[hist-eur5-s7.7.7] Irish missionary orders such as the Holy Ghost Fathers and the Medical Missionaries of Mary ran schools, hospitals and clinics in Nigeria, Kenya, Tanzania and other countries, and the Irish government began an official aid programme in 1974.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.0] Achievement: the Lomé Conventions gave ACP states preferential access to European markets and a stabilisation fund, which was a recognised innovation in trade policy.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.1] Limit: despite these trade preferences, most ACP exports stayed concentrated on a few primary commodities, and commodity prices fell in the 1980s.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.2] Achievement: international health campaigns achieved the global eradication of smallpox by 1977, the last natural case being in Somalia, and aid and NGO work improved access to water and primary schooling in many areas.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.3] Limit: debt and structural adjustment left many governments with cuts in public services, and the famines of the 1970s and 1980s showed how fragile food supply remained.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.4] Achievement: Tanzania's rise in literacy and primary education showed what a government with limited resources could achieve in social services.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.5] Limit: villagisation and the collapse of agricultural output showed that state-directed development could cause serious hardship.
[hist-eur5-s7.8.6] Later view: dependency writers such as Walter Rodney (How Europe Underdeveloped Africa, 1972) blamed colonial exploitation, while the World Bank's Berg Report of 1981 blamed domestic policies; most historians now combine colonial legacies, falling commodity prices, Cold War pressures and government choices.

CANDIDATES hist-eur5-s5: 5. Africa: Nigeria, the Congo, Tanzania and Angola
[hist-eur5-s5.0.b] Britain joined separate northern and southern territories into one colony in 1914, and independence in 1960 was given to a federation of three regions.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.0] Lord Lugard amalgamated Northern and Southern Nigeria into one colony in 1914, and the colonial government ruled much of the north through existing emirs under the system of indirect rule.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.1] Nigeria's three main groups were the Hausa-Fulani in the north, the Yoruba in the west and the Igbo in the east; the north was predominantly Muslim, and the south had large Christian populations.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.2] The 1954 constitution created a federation of three regions, Northern, Western and Eastern, with Lagos as a federal territory.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.3] Nigeria became independent on 1 October 1960 as a member of the British Commonwealth, with Abubakar Tafawa Balewa as prime minister; the Queen remained head of state, represented by a governor-general, Nnamdi Azikiwe from November 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.4] The regions had their own parliaments and revenue, and the regional parties drew support on ethnic lines, which made federal politics tense.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.5] Nigeria became a republic on 1 October 1963 with Azikiwe as president, and a fourth region, the Mid-West, was created in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.6] In the 1959 federal elections the Northern People's Congress won the most seats and formed a coalition with Azikiwe's National Council of Nigeria and the Cameroons, and its leader Abubakar Tafawa Balewa became federal prime minister.
[hist-eur5-s5.0.7] Nigeria's oil came from Oloibiri, discovered by Shell-BP in 1956, and exports began in 1958; the oil lay in the Eastern Region, which became a source of dispute in 1967.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.0] On 15 January 1966 a group of army officers led by Major Chukwuma Nzeogwu killed the federal prime minister and several regional leaders, and the coup was seen as dominated by Igbo officers.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.1] General Johnson Aguiyi-Ironsi took power, but a counter-coup in July 1966 brought Lieutenant-Colonel Yakubu Gowon to power.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.2] Anti-Igbo massacres in the north in 1966 killed many thousands of Igbo people, and large numbers fled to the east.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.3] The eastern region declared itself the Republic of Biafra on 30 May 1967 under Colonel Odumegwu Ojukwu, and the federal government responded with a blockade.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.4] The Biafran War, from July 1967 to 15 January 1970, caused widespread famine in Biafra; estimates of the total death toll range from several hundred thousand to more than two million, most from starvation and disease.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.5] Biafra was recognised by only Tanzania, Gabon, the Ivory Coast, Zambia and Haiti, while Britain and the Soviet Union supplied arms to the federal government; France and Portugal gave covert support to Biafra.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.6] Gowon created twelve states on 27 May 1967, three days before Biafra's declaration, which broke up the Eastern Region and cut the Igbo off from most of the oil; an earlier attempt to settle the crisis, the Aburi meeting in Ghana in January 1967, had failed.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.7] Gowon announced a policy of reconciliation, rehabilitation and reconstruction at the end of the war on 15 January 1970 and declared that there was no victor and no vanquished.
[hist-eur5-s5.1.8] Many outside Nigeria were moved by images of starving Biafran children; the relief flights of Joint Church Aid, Irish missionaries among them, kept supplies reaching the enclave.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.b] Belgium ran the Congo as a colony for profit, and the country had very few trained Congolese when independence came suddenly in 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.0] King Leopold II ruled the Congo Free State from 1885 as his personal possession, and forced rubber collection caused widespread abuses, exposed by Roger Casement and E. D. Morel, that led to international protest.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.1] The Belgian government took over the colony in 1908, and it was ruled by a paternalistic administration that gave little political role to Congolese.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.2] The colony's wealth lay in copper, cobalt, tin and diamonds, much of it extracted in Katanga by the Union Minière du Haut-Katanga, a Belgian company.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.3] By 1960 only a handful of Congolese had a university degree, and the Force Publique, the colonial army, had no Congolese officers, with Belgians commanding all the ranks above sergeant.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.4] Congolese political parties emerged in the late 1950s, including Patrice Lumumba's National Congolese Movement (MNC), which won the most seats in the 1960 elections.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.5] Riots in Léopoldville in January 1959 shocked Belgian opinion, and King Baudouin soon promised independence at an unspecified date.
[hist-eur5-s5.2.6] The Round Table in Brussels in January-February 1960 fixed independence for 30 June 1960, only about five months later.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.0] Joseph Kasa-Vubu became head of state and Patrice Lumumba prime minister at independence on 30 June 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.1] At the independence ceremony King Baudouin praised Belgium's colonial work; Lumumba replied with a speech attacking the humiliations of colonial rule, which offended Belgian opinion.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.2] Soldiers of the Force Publique mutinied against their Belgian officers on 5–6 July 1960, and attacks on Europeans led Belgium to send troops back to the Congo.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.3] Katanga seceded on 11 July 1960 with Belgian backing, and South Kasai declared its own state in August 1960.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.4] The UN Security Council authorised the UN operation in the Congo (ONUC) in July 1960; at its height it had about 20,000 troops, and it was the largest UN operation of its time.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.5] Kasa-Vubu dismissed Lumumba on 5 September 1960 and Lumumba tried to dismiss Kasa-Vubu, which gave Mobutu his pretext to intervene.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.6] Colonel Joseph Mobutu, army chief of staff, took power on 14 September 1960; Lumumba, who had appealed to the Soviet Union for help, was confined to his residence under UN guard.
[hist-eur5-s5.3.7] Lumumba left his residence in late November 1960, was captured in December, and was flown to Katanga and murdered there on 17 January 1961 in the presence of Belgian officers; Belgium's parliament concluded in 2002 that it bore a moral responsibility.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.0] Mobutu seized power in a second coup on 24 November 1965, with Kasa-Vubu deposed and the army in charge of the state.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.1] The country was renamed Zaire in 1971, and Mobutu's regime promoted authenticité, which banned Christian first names and European dress for men in favour of the abacost.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.2] In 1973 Zairianisation transferred foreign-owned plantations and businesses to Zairian owners, mostly Mobutu's associates, and the economy collapsed as the copper price fell in 1974-75.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.3] The one-party state, the Popular Movement of the Revolution (MPR), was the only legal party, and political opposition was suppressed.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.4] Mobutu was supported by the United States, Belgium and France as a barrier to communism in central Africa, and their military help defeated the Katangan exile invasions of Shaba in 1977 and 1978.
[hist-eur5-s5.4.5] Mobutu announced the end of the one-party state in April 1990 under pressure from protests and from the end of Cold War support, though he stayed in power until 1997.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.b] Tanganyika moved to independence without armed conflict under Julius Nyerere and then united with Zanzibar.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.0] Tanganyika had been German East Africa until 1918, then a British mandate under the League of Nations and a UN trust territory after 1946.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.1] Julius Nyerere founded the Tanganyika African National Union (TANU) in 1954 and led it to a majority in the 1960 elections.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.2] Tanganyika became independent on 9 December 1961 with Nyerere as prime minister, and it became a republic with Nyerere as president in December 1962.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.3] Zanzibar became independent in December 1963, but a revolution in January 1964 overthrew its Arab-led government, and the new regime was dominated by the Afro-Shirazi Party.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.4] Tanganyika and Zanzibar united on 26 April 1964 to form the United Republic, renamed Tanzania in October 1964; Zanzibar kept its own government and president, Abeid Karume of the Afro-Shirazi Party.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.5] Tanzania was a multi-ethnic state with more than a hundred ethnic groups and no dominant majority, and Nyerere made Swahili the national language and avoided ethnic parties.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.6] In January 1964 the Tanganyika Rifles mutinied against British officers; Nyerere asked Britain for help and Royal Marines put down the mutiny, after which the army was rebuilt.
[hist-eur5-s5.5.7] Nyerere broke off diplomatic relations with Britain in December 1965 over its failure to act against the white minority regime in Southern Rhodesia, though Tanzania stayed in the Commonwealth.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.0] Portugal under the Estado Novo regime of António de Oliveira Salazar regarded Angola as an overseas province rather than a colony, and it refused to grant independence.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.1] Armed attacks in Luanda on 4 February 1961 began the Angolan war of independence, followed by a rising in the north on 15 March 1961 in which many Europeans and Africans were killed and Portuguese reprisals were severe; the war lasted until 1974.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.2] Three nationalist movements fought the Portuguese: the MPLA, led by Agostinho Neto, founded in 1956 and strongest among the Mbundu and in Luanda; the FNLA, led by Holden Roberto and based among the Bakongo in the north; and UNITA, founded in 1966, led by Jonas Savimbi and based among the Ovimbundu.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.3] The Carnation Revolution of 25 April 1974 in Lisbon overthrew the Portuguese regime, and the new government agreed to grant independence to Angola.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.4] The Alvor Agreement of January 1975 set up a transitional government in which the three movements shared power, but it broke down into fighting.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.5] Angola became independent on 11 November 1975, with the MPLA under Neto holding Luanda; the civil war that followed between the MPLA and UNITA and the FNLA lasted until 2002, with Cuban troops and Soviet aid for the MPLA, and South African and American support for its opponents.
[hist-eur5-s5.6.6] Most of the roughly 300,000 Portuguese settlers left Angola in 1975, taking skills and capital with them, and oil from Cabinda and diamonds became the main sources of revenue.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.0] Nigeria kept the Queen as head of state until becoming a republic in 1963, and stayed in the Commonwealth, with British companies such as Shell-BP dominating the oil industry.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.1] Belgium retained large economic interests in the Congo through companies such as the Union Minière, and Mobutu nationalised the Union Minière's Katanga assets in 1966-67 before reaching a settlement with it in 1969.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.2] Portugal's refusal to decolonise until 1974 meant that Angola faced a long war, and Portugal's exit left Angola with few trained administrators.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.3] Tanzania remained in the Commonwealth and received British aid, but Nyerere pursued non-alignment and criticised Western economic influence; Britain also supplied arms to the federal side in Nigeria's war.
[hist-eur5-s5.7.4] France kept influence in its former West and Equatorial African colonies through co-operation accords, the franc zone and military agreements, an arrangement later called Françafrique.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.0] Colonial rulers defined and fixed ethnic identities through census categories, languages and indirect rule, and the term tribalism was used by colonial officials to describe ethnic loyalty.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.1] In Nigeria, the three main parties each drew their support from one region and one ethnic group, and the army's officer corps divided on regional lines, which contributed to the coups of 1966 and the massacres that followed.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.2] In the Congo, the leading politicians drew their support from particular regions and ethnic communities, such as Lumumba's base in the east, Kasa-Vubu's Kongo base in the west and the Lunda of Katanga.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.3] Katanga's secession was partly based on the claim that the Katangan people had a separate identity from the rest of the Congo, a claim that the national government rejected.
[hist-eur5-s5.8.4] Tanzania's single language, Swahili, and the policy of building a national identity were among the factors that reduced ethnic conflict after independence.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.0] Opportunity: independence gave Africans control of government, and Nyerere's Tanzania pursued national unity and African development without a civil war.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.1] Problem: the Belgian Congo's rapid independence left the new state with almost no trained administrators or officers, and the army's mutiny and the secession of Katanga followed within weeks.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.2] Opportunity: Nigeria's oil revenue, which grew rapidly after the 1973 oil price rise, gave the federal government resources for reconstruction after the Biafran War.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.3] Problem: the Biafran War and the 1966 coups showed how the federal structure could fail to hold ethnic groups together.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.4] Contemporary view: many African leaders saw independence as the end of colonial domination, while the former colonial powers often saw it as the start of a new relationship of trade and aid.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.5] Later view: historians stress that colonial borders, ethnic policies and the Cold War constrained the new states, but they also stress African agency in shaping the outcomes.
[hist-eur5-s5.9.6] Problem: Angola's long war of independence ended in civil war because the three movements had different ethnic bases and different foreign backers.
