[hist-ire5-s7.3.2] The bombing of McGurk's Bar in Belfast on 4 December 1971 killed 15 Catholic civilians; police first blamed the IRA, but the Police Ombudsman concluded in 2011 that the UVF was responsible.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.3] The UVF carried out the bombings in Dublin and Monaghan on 17 May 1974, which killed 33 people and an unborn child and are the largest loss of life in a single day of the Troubles; claims of security force collusion were never proven.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.4] The Greysteel massacre of 30 October 1993 saw the Ulster Freedom Fighters kill eight people at a Halloween dance in the Rising Sun bar, County Londonderry, a week after the Shankill bomb.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.5] Loyalist paramilitaries claimed to be defending the Union, but most of their victims were Catholic civilians with no paramilitary links.
[hist-ire5-s7.3.6] UVF and UDA gunmen carried out attacks on Catholic bars and homes in Belfast during the 1970s, and attacks on Catholic workers on the roads.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.b] Some loyalist groups carried out sectarian murders of Catholics that were marked by torture and brutality, and the Shankill Butchers were the most notorious.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.0] The Shankill Butchers were a UVF gang based on the Shankill Road in west Belfast, who abducted and killed Catholics at night between 1975 and 1977.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.1] The gang was led by Lenny Murphy, and eleven members were convicted in 1979 of a series of murders, receiving many life sentences.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.2] Many victims were killed with knives and hatchets, which led to widespread revulsion in both communities.
[hist-ire5-s7.4.3] The gang's activities showed how sectarian violence could develop outside the formal paramilitary chains of command, and Murphy himself was shot dead in 1982.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.b] Both republicans and loyalists used murals, publications and political statements to shape public opinion, and the British government sought to restrict broadcasts.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.0] Republican murals in the Falls and Bogside celebrated IRA volunteers and the hunger strikers, and they became a central feature of the political landscape.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.1] Loyalist murals in Protestant areas celebrated the UVF and UDA and presented their violence as a defence of loyal communities.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.2] Republicans used publications such as An Phoblacht to present their case, and their statements were sought by international media.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.3] In October 1988 the British government banned the broadcast of the voices of spokesmen for Sinn Féin and ten other organisations, including loyalist groups, and broadcasters used actors' voices; the ban was lifted in 1994.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.4] The ban was intended to deny publicity to terrorist groups, and critics said it restricted freedom of expression.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.5] Irish-American support for republicans provided funds and publicity for the republican campaign.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.6] In the Republic, Section 31 of the Broadcasting Authority Act barred broadcasters from interviewing spokesmen for the IRA and, from 1977, Sinn Féin, and it was allowed to lapse in 1994.
[hist-ire5-s7.5.7] The republican movement published An Phoblacht, and its propaganda stressed the British occupation and the hunger strikers as martyrs.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.b] The Republic's government responded to the violence of August 1969 with a broadcast, emergency aid and a border presence, but the Arms Crisis of 1970 divided the Fianna Fáil government.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.0] On 13 August 1969 the Taoiseach Jack Lynch broadcast that the Irish government 'can no longer stand by' while Catholics were attacked in Belfast and Derry, and he called for a United Nations peacekeeping force.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.1] The Irish army set up field hospitals at the border and the government provided relief for refugees fleeing Belfast and Derry, with camps at Gormanston and elsewhere.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.2] The Arms Crisis of May 1970 followed allegations that ministers had planned to import arms for Northern nationalists using money voted for relief, and Lynch dismissed Charles Haughey and Neil Blaney from the government on 6 May 1970.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.3] Haughey, Captain James Kelly, John Kelly and Albert Luykx were tried for conspiracy to import arms and acquitted in October 1970.
[hist-ire5-s7.6.4] After Bloody Sunday the Irish government declared a day of mourning and recalled its ambassador from London, and the British embassy in Dublin was burned on 2 February 1972 by a crowd, which the government condemned.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.0] The IRA was declared an unlawful organisation in the Republic in 1936, and membership remained illegal under the Offences against the State Act 1939.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.1] The Offences against the State (Amendment) Act 1972 allowed a Garda chief superintendent's belief to be evidence of IRA membership, and it was passed in December 1972 after bombs in Dublin.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.2] The Special Criminal Court was reactivated in 1972 to try offences connected with paramilitary violence without a jury.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.3] The Republic's government under Liam Cosgrave supported the Sunningdale settlement and its Council of Ireland, which was intended to show Dublin's commitment to cross-border cooperation.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.4] Garda cooperation with the RUC increased, but extradition from the Republic of people wanted in Northern Ireland remained a point of tension between Dublin and London.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.5] The Criminal Law (Jurisdiction) Act 1976 allowed some cross-border offences to be tried in the Republic.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.6] The Emergency Powers Act 1976 followed the IRA killing of the British ambassador, Christopher Ewart-Biggs, and led President Cearbhall Ó Dálaigh to refer the bill to the Supreme Court and then resign.
[hist-ire5-s7.7.7] The Extradition (European Convention on the Suppression of Terrorism) Act 1987 was passed after the Enniskillen bombing.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.0] Garret FitzGerald, Taoiseach from 1981 to 1982 and from 1982 to 1987, pursued the New Ireland Forum and signed the Anglo-Irish Agreement with Margaret Thatcher in 1985.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.1] Charles Haughey, Taoiseach from 1987 to 1992, returned Fianna Fáil to power and kept the Anglo-Irish Agreement's framework in place.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.2] Unionists complained that the Republic had too much influence through the Agreement's Intergovernmental Conference.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.3] Extradition and cross-border security cooperation were recurring issues, and the Anglo-Irish Agreement put security cooperation on a formal footing.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.4] Haughey and Thatcher held a summit in Dublin in December 1980 that agreed to study the 'totality of relationships' between the two islands.
[hist-ire5-s7.8.5] Fianna Fáil under Haughey opposed the Anglo-Irish Agreement in the Dáil in 1985, but kept its framework when he returned to government in 1987.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.0] Impact: terrorism killed large numbers of civilians and security force members, and it made political compromise harder by hardening attitudes on all sides.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.1] Impact: republican and loyalist campaigns shaped British policy, with security measures and political initiatives both shaped by the need to contain violence.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.2] Failure: neither the IRA nor the loyalist groups achieved their stated aims, and the main ceasefires came in 1994, after the period covered here.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.3] Failure: terrorism deepened sectarian divisions, and its victims in both communities created lasting grievances.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.4] Contemporary view: the British and Irish governments condemned terrorism and sought to isolate paramilitaries from political influence.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.5] Contemporary view: republicans argued that the IRA was defending the Catholic community and fighting an occupying power, while loyalists claimed to defend the Union.
[hist-ire5-s7.9.6] Later view: historians point out that most of the killing was done by paramilitary groups, not the state, and that the long campaign persuaded many in each group that violence could not win.
