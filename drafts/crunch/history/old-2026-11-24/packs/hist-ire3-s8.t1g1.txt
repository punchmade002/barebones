[hist-ire3-s7.5.2] The government paid bounties on exports and subsidised wheat and beet to make up for lost income, financed by higher taxes.
[hist-ire3-s7.5.3] The Coal–Cattle Pact of 1935 allowed more Irish cattle into Britain in return for British coal.
[hist-ire3-s7.5.4] Farmers in Munster and Leinster, with their large cattle farms, were hit more than small western farmers.
[hist-ire3-s7.5.5] The 1938 agreement ended the dispute, but exports did not return to their former level before the war.
[hist-ire3-s7.5.6] Dairy farmers and small farmers were affected by the fall in prices, and the cost of the trade war fell on the whole rural economy.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.b] Fianna Fáil spent more than its predecessor on housing and welfare, but resources were limited and poverty remained widespread.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.0] The Housing Acts of 1932 and 1936 raised state subsidies, and local authorities built tens of thousands of new houses, helped by slum clearance in Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.1] The Unemployment Assistance Act of 1933 gave relief to those who had run out of insurance benefit.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.2] The Widows' and Orphans' Pensions Act of 1935 introduced a contributory pension scheme.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.3] Tenements in Dublin remained overcrowded, and the tuberculosis death rate was high.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.4] Emigration fell in the early 1930s as the Depression closed American and British job markets, then rose again in the late 1930s and during the Second World War.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.5] A marriage bar for national teachers, introduced in 1932, obliged women teachers to resign on marriage; a similar bar already applied in the civil service from the 1920s.
[hist-ire3-s7.6.6] Emigrants' remittances were an important source of income for families in the west, and their reduction in the Depression hurt small farmers.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.b] Neither free trade nor protection produced rapid growth. Judgements depend on whether the aim is stability, self-sufficiency or living standards.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.0] Cumann na nGaedheal's achievement: financial stability, a sound currency, an efficient farm export trade and the Shannon Scheme.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.1] Cumann na nGaedheal's limit: dependence on the British market and low spending left little help for the poor when the Depression came.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.2] Fianna Fáil's achievement: new industries and jobs, a larger state role through semi-state companies and more social housing.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.3] Fianna Fáil's limit: the Economic War, a small and protected market, continued emigration and low living standards.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.4] Why success was hard: a small home market, few natural resources, dependence on Britain, the Depression and then the Second World War.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.5] Contemporary view: supporters of protection held that it was necessary for independence; critics held that it hurt consumers and farmers.
[hist-ire3-s7.7.6] Later view: historians such as Mary Daly and Joe Lee say that protection created jobs but not an efficient industry, while the Finance Department's caution restricted investment.
