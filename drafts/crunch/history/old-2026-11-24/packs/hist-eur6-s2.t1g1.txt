[hist-eur6-s2.0.b] After 1945 the United States abandoned its pre-war isolation and committed itself to resisting Soviet expansion, a policy known as containment.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.0] The United States emerged from the Second World War as the richest industrial power and, from 1945, the only country with atomic weapons.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.1] The atomic bombs were dropped on Hiroshima on 6 August 1945 and Nagasaki on 9 August 1945, on Truman's authority, and the Soviet Union tested its own bomb on 29 August 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.2] George Kennan, a diplomat in Moscow, sent his Long Telegram in February 1946 arguing that Soviet expansion could be checked by firm, long-term resistance rather than by negotiation.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.3] Kennan's article The Sources of Soviet Conduct, published in Foreign Affairs in July 1947 under the signature X, gave containment its name.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.4] The Truman Doctrine of 12 March 1947 asked Congress for about 400 million dollars in aid to Greece and Turkey, and it committed the United States to support free peoples resisting armed minorities or outside pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.5] The Communist seizure of power in Czechoslovakia in February 1948 convinced many in Washington and Western Europe that the Soviet threat was immediate.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.6] Mao Zedong proclaimed the People's Republic of China on 1 October 1949, and Republicans later used the question "Who lost China?" to attack Truman's Asia policy.
[hist-eur6-s2.0.7] In April 1950 the National Security Council paper NSC-68 called for a large rise in defence spending to meet the Soviet threat, and it was adopted after the Korean War began.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.b] Economic aid and a military alliance were the two pillars of containment in Western Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.0] George Marshall, the Secretary of State, proposed a European recovery programme in a speech at Harvard University on 5 June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.1] The European Recovery Program, passed by Congress in April 1948, provided about 13 billion dollars of aid to Western European countries between 1948 and 1952.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.2] The Soviet Union refused Marshall aid and pressured its satellite states in Eastern Europe to refuse it too, so the division of Europe became more fixed.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.3] Most aid was given as grants of food, fuel and machinery rather than loans, and the Organisation for European Economic Co-operation (OEEC) was set up in April 1948 to allocate it; Britain, France and West Germany were the largest recipients.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.4] The North Atlantic Treaty was signed on 4 April 1949 by twelve countries, including the United States, Britain, France, Canada and Italy.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.5] Article 5 of the NATO treaty stated that an armed attack on one member would be treated as an attack on all, the basis of collective defence.
[hist-eur6-s2.1.6] Germany was divided: the Federal Republic of Germany was founded in May 1949 in the Western zones, and the German Democratic Republic followed in October 1949 in the Soviet zone.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.b] Berlin, divided into four sectors, was the most dangerous point of Cold War contact in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.0] The Soviet Union began blocking road, rail and canal access to West Berlin on 24 June 1948, aiming to force the Western powers out of the city.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.1] The Western powers responded with an airlift that flew food, coal and other supplies into West Berlin; more than two million tons were carried before the blockade was lifted on 12 May 1949.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.2] In November 1958 Khrushchev demanded that the Western powers withdraw from Berlin within six months, and the issue was debated at the Vienna summit with Kennedy in June 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.3] The Berlin Wall was built from 13 August 1961, closing the border between East and West Berlin and stopping the flow of East German refugees.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.4] In October 1961 American and Soviet tanks faced each other at Checkpoint Charlie, the crossing point for foreigners, in a tense standoff that ended without shots.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.5] Well over two million East Germans had fled to the West between 1949 and 1961, most of them by crossing from East to West Berlin, which drained the East's skilled workforce.
[hist-eur6-s2.2.6] Kennedy visited West Berlin on 26 June 1963 and declared in German "Ich bin ein Berliner" (I am a Berliner), a statement of solidarity that was widely praised in the West.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.b] The Korean War was the first major armed conflict of the Cold War, fought under a United Nations flag.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.0] Korea had been divided at the 38th parallel in 1945; the Republic of Korea in the South under Syngman Rhee and the Democratic People's Republic in the North under Kim Il Sung were both founded in 1948.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.1] US troops had left South Korea in 1949, and Secretary of State Dean Acheson's speech of January 1950 left Korea outside the American defence perimeter in the Pacific, which communist leaders may have read as a green light.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.2] North Korean forces invaded the South on 25 June 1950, and the UN Security Council, with the Soviet delegate absent, condemned the attack and then recommended that members help South Korea repel it.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.3] Truman sent American forces without asking Congress for a declaration of war, and he described the operation as a police action.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.4] General Douglas MacArthur led the UN forces; his landing at Inchon on 15 September 1950 cut North Korean supply lines and led to the recapture of Seoul on 28 September.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.5] UN forces advanced north of the 38th parallel in October 1950, and Chinese forces entered the war in large numbers from late October and November 1950, driving them back.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.6] Truman dismissed MacArthur on 11 April 1951 after the general publicly argued for attacking China, a decision that caused a major domestic row.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.7] An armistice was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953; the frontier stayed close to the 38th parallel, and no peace treaty was ever signed.
[hist-eur6-s2.3.8] About 36,000 American servicemen died in the war, and US troops remained in South Korea after the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.b] Cuba lay ninety miles from Florida, and the United States had long treated the island as within its sphere of influence.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.0] Under the Platt Amendment of 1901 the United States had the right to intervene in Cuba, a clause repealed in 1934 but leaving a legacy of US economic dominance.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.1] Fidel Castro's revolution took power on 1 January 1959 after Batista fled, and the new government nationalised US-owned sugar, oil and other property in 1960.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.2] Eisenhower cut the Cuban sugar quota in July 1960, imposed a partial trade embargo in October 1960 and broke off diplomatic relations in January 1961; Cuba turned to the Soviet Union for trade and oil.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.3] CIA-trained Cuban exiles landed at the Bay of Pigs on 17 April 1961; they were defeated within three days, a humiliation for Kennedy.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.4] Operation Mongoose, a covert campaign against Castro's government, was authorised in November 1961.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.5] Soviet medium-range missiles were shipped to Cuba from September 1962, and photographs taken by a US U-2 spy plane on 14 October 1962 revealed the launch sites; Kennedy was shown them on 16 October.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.6] Khrushchev's motives for the missiles included defending Cuba from another invasion, answering US Jupiter missiles in Turkey and Italy, and offsetting Soviet weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.7] Kennedy and his advisers (the Executive Committee, ExComm) rejected an air strike or invasion, and on 22 October he announced on television a naval quarantine (blockade) of Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.8] On 24 October Soviet ships turned back from the quarantine line; on 27 October ("Black Saturday") a U-2 was shot down over Cuba, killing its pilot, Major Rudolf Anderson.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.9] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed on Moscow radio to remove the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur6-s2.4.10] Kennedy also secretly agreed to withdraw US Jupiter missiles from Turkey within months, a deal kept private for years; a hotline between Washington and Moscow was installed in 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.b] US involvement in Vietnam grew out of the containment of communism in Asia. Johnson's escalation of 1964 to 1968 is covered in the case study on Johnson and Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.0] Vietnam was part of French Indochina; the Viet Minh under Ho Chi Minh fought the French, who were defeated at Dien Bien Phu in May 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.1] From 1950 Truman gave aid to the French war, and by 1954 the United States was paying about three quarters of its cost.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.2] In April 1954 Eisenhower described the domino theory: if Vietnam fell to communism, the rest of Southeast Asia would follow, like a row of falling dominoes.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.3] The Geneva Accords of July 1954 divided Vietnam at the 17th parallel, with elections planned for 1956 that were never held.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.4] The United States backed Ngo Dinh Diem in South Vietnam and joined the Southeast Asia Treaty Organisation (SEATO) in 1954.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.5] Under Eisenhower the United States trained the South Vietnamese army with a military advisory group, and the first American advisers killed in the conflict died in 1959.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.6] The National Liberation Front, known to the Americans as the Viet Cong, was formed in December 1960 to fight the government in the South.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.7] Kennedy backed the Strategic Hamlet programme (1962), which moved peasants into fortified villages and was deeply resented.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.8] In 1963 Diem's persecution of Buddhists provoked protests, including the self-immolation of the monk Thich Quang Duc in Saigon in June 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.9] Diem was overthrown and killed in a coup that US officials had tacitly encouraged, on 2 November 1963, after which the South suffered a series of unstable governments.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.10] Kennedy increased US military advisers in South Vietnam to about 16,000 by late 1963.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.11] The Nixon Doctrine, announced at Guam in July 1969, stated that Asian nations should take the main responsibility for their own defence, and US troop numbers began to fall.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.12] Nixon's Vietnamisation handed the ground war to the South Vietnamese army while US troops were withdrawn, falling from about 540,000 in early 1969 to under 30,000 by late 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.13] In April 1970 Nixon sent US and South Vietnamese troops into Cambodia to attack communist bases, which sparked protests at home, including Kent State; secret bombing of Cambodia had begun in March 1969.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.14] In 1971 South Vietnamese forces invaded Laos with US air support and were driven back; in 1972 Nixon answered North Vietnam's Easter Offensive by mining Haiphong harbour and the Linebacker bombing raids, and ordered the Christmas bombing of Hanoi in December 1972.
[hist-eur6-s2.5.15] The Paris Peace Accords were signed on 27 January 1973, and the last US combat troops left Vietnam by late March 1973; the fall of Saigon in 1975 is outside this subsection.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.0] Eisenhower's Secretary of State John Foster Dulles relied on massive retaliation (1954): the threat of nuclear attack to deter communist aggression, which was cheaper than large conventional forces.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.1] Eisenhower is said to have signalled through intermediaries in 1953 that he might use nuclear weapons if the Korean talks failed, though historians dispute how far this forced the armistice.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.2] Kennedy replaced massive retaliation with flexible response, building up conventional and special forces (such as the Green Berets) so the United States could fight limited wars.
[hist-eur6-s2.6.3] After the missile crisis Kennedy and Khrushchev signed the Partial Test Ban Treaty (August 1963), banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.b] Truman's domestic record is covered in the notes on US politics. This block covers his foreign policy and how he is judged.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.0] At the Potsdam conference of July and August 1945 Truman met Stalin for the first time, and he authorised the atomic bombings shortly afterwards.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.1] The Truman Doctrine speech to Congress (12 March 1947) set out the principle that the United States would support free peoples against communist pressure.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.2] He recognised the new state of Israel on 14 May 1948, within minutes of its declaration of independence, against the advice of parts of his State Department.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.3] He decided in June 1950 to send American forces to defend South Korea without a congressional vote, a decision that set the pattern for later presidential war-making.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.4] He was a direct participant in every decision: he chose the airlift rather than an armed convoy to Berlin (1948) and accepted a limited war in Korea rather than MacArthur's wider war with China.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.5] A sign on his desk read "The buck stops here", summing up his view that the President must take final responsibility.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.6] Contemporaries were divided: Republicans attacked him over China and the spy scandals of the period, and by 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply.
[hist-eur6-s2.7.7] Revisionist historians from the 1960s, such as William Appleman Williams, argued that US economic expansion and the atomic bomb helped cause the Cold War; post-revisionists such as John Lewis Gaddis stressed Stalin's own responsibility and argued that containment worked in Europe.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.0] Success in Western Europe: the Marshall Plan, NATO and the Berlin airlift contained Soviet power in Europe and helped to create a prosperous Western alliance.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.1] Failure in Asia: the Korean War ended in a stalemate after about three years, with heavy American losses and no victory.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.2] Korea can also be judged a limited success: South Korea survived, and Truman kept the war from spreading to China or becoming nuclear.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.3] The Cuban missile crisis avoided nuclear war, but the Bay of Pigs was a clear failure and the Soviet missiles were removed only in exchange for a secret US concession.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.4] Vietnam showed the limits of containment: a commitment that began with advisers had escalated to over half a million troops by 1968.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.5] Supporters argue that US commitments in Asia deterred further Soviet and Chinese expansion, while critics say they drew the United States into costly wars in places of little strategic importance.
[hist-eur6-s2.8.6] Contemporaries and later historians differ over whether the Cold War could have been managed by diplomacy earlier, and the debate shapes judgements of Truman, Kennedy and Johnson.

[hist-eur6-s1.0.b] The Constitution divides power between the federal government and the states, and that division shaped the civil rights and social policy fights of the period.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.0] Federalism: powers not given to the federal government are reserved to the states under the Tenth Amendment, so states controlled schools, policing, voting rules and marriage law.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.1] Before the 1960s most southern states enforced segregation by state law, and federal action against it was met with the argument that such matters belonged to the states.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.2] In 1948 southern Democrats angry at Truman's civil rights programme left the party and formed the States' Rights Democratic Party (the Dixiecrats) with Strom Thurmond as candidate; it carried four Deep South states.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.3] The Civil Rights Act 1957 was the first civil rights law since Reconstruction; it set up a Civil Rights Commission and gave federal authorities power to act over voting rights.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.4] The Civil Rights Act 1964 rested on federal power to regulate interstate commerce, which allowed Congress to ban segregation in hotels, restaurants and other public places.
[hist-eur6-s1.0.5] In September 1957 Governor Orval Faubus of Arkansas used the National Guard to stop nine black students entering Little Rock Central High School; Eisenhower sent federal troops, including the 101st Airborne Division, to enforce integration.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.b] The Constitution gives Congress, the President and the Supreme Court separate powers, and each can limit the others.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.0] The Constitution (1787) gives Congress the power to make laws, raise taxes and declare war; the President executes the laws, commands the armed forces and conducts foreign policy; the Supreme Court interprets the Constitution.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.1] The President can veto bills, but Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds majority in both houses; the Senate must confirm senior appointments and ratify treaties by a two-thirds vote.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.2] Congress passed the Taft-Hartley Act in 1947, restricting trade union activity such as the closed shop, and overrode Truman's veto in June 1947.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.3] Republicans won control of both houses of Congress in the 1946 midterm elections (slogan: "Had enough?") for the first time since 1928, leaving Truman to govern with a hostile Congress until 1948.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.4] Youngstown Sheet and Tube Co v Sawyer (June 1952): the Supreme Court ruled that Truman had exceeded his powers by seizing the steel mills during a strike in April 1952 without congressional authority.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.5] The Warren Court (Chief Justice Earl Warren, appointed by Eisenhower in 1953) used judicial review to extend civil rights and liberties, e.g. Brown v. Board of Education (1954) and Miranda v. Arizona (1966) on the rights of arrested suspects.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.6] The Twenty-Second Amendment, passed by Congress in March 1947 and ratified in 1951, limits a president to two elected terms, a direct response to Roosevelt winning four elections.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.7] United States v Nixon (July 1974): the Supreme Court unanimously ruled that Nixon must hand over the White House tapes, and his resignation followed in August 1974.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.8] Congress passed the War Powers Resolution over Nixon's veto in November 1973 to limit the President's power to commit troops without congressional approval.
[hist-eur6-s1.1.9] The Boland Amendments (1982 to 1984) restricted US aid to the Contra rebels in Nicaragua; officials in the Reagan administration kept the Contras supplied in breach of them, the core of the Iran-Contra affair.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.b] The President's staff and agencies grew steadily from the 1930s, so that more policy was shaped inside the White House and its agencies.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.0] The Executive Office of the President was created in 1939 under Roosevelt, giving the President a personal staff separate from the government departments.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.1] The National Security Act of July 1947, signed by Truman, created the National Security Council, the Central Intelligence Agency and the National Military Establishment, later renamed the Department of Defense.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.2] The National Security Council gave the President a forum of advisers on foreign and defence policy, often bypassing the State Department and Congress.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.3] The Council of Economic Advisers was created by the Employment Act of 1946, which committed the federal government to promote maximum employment, production and purchasing power.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.4] Kennedy held live televised press conferences from January 1961, using the press conference as a direct channel to public opinion.
[hist-eur6-s1.2.5] Arthur Schlesinger Jr. described the expanded executive in The Imperial Presidency (1973), a phrase that became the standard label for concern over presidential power after Watergate.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.b] Roosevelt's coalition and the wartime economy shaped the politics that Truman inherited in 1945.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.0] Roosevelt won four presidential elections (1932, 1936, 1940 and 1944) and served from March 1933 until his death.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.1] The New Deal coalition combined urban workers, trade unions, Catholics and Jews, farmers, southern whites and black voters who moved to the Democrats from the 1930s.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.2] Roosevelt died on 12 April 1945, and Harry Truman, vice president since January 1945, became president.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.3] Truman had chaired a Senate committee (the Truman Committee, 1941 to 1944) that investigated waste and fraud in wartime production, which gave him a reputation for careful oversight.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.4] Truman inherited a wave of strikes in 1945 and 1946 as the wartime economy was converted to peacetime production.
[hist-eur6-s1.3.5] Truman's party was divided between northern liberals and conservative southern Democrats, who often voted with Republicans in Congress (the conservative coalition) to block reform.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.b] Truman was a participant in the domestic politics of his presidency and a figure whose popularity swung widely. His foreign policy record is covered in the notes on US foreign policy.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.0] Born in Lamar, Missouri, in 1884, he worked as a farmer, a haberdasher and a county court official before being elected to the Senate in 1934 and taking his seat in 1935.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.1] He served as an artillery captain in France in the First World War and owed his early political career to the Kansas City Democratic machine of Tom Pendergast.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.2] As vice president from January 1945 he was not told of the atomic bomb project until he became president, a fact that shaped his early reliance on advisers.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.3] In September 1945 he sent Congress a 21-point programme of reforms that continued the New Deal, but reconversion, inflation and strikes blocked most of it.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.4] His domestic programme, the Fair Deal, was outlined in January 1949 and included national health insurance, a wider Social Security system, a higher minimum wage and public housing.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.5] Congress blocked national health insurance and most other Fair Deal proposals; the Housing Act of 1949 and a rise in the minimum wage were among the main successes.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.6] His civil rights programme began with the report To Secure These Rights (October 1947) from the President's Committee on Civil Rights, and Executive Order 9981 (26 July 1948) ordered the desegregation of the armed forces.

[hist-eur6-s1.4.11] By 1952 his popularity had fallen sharply because of the Korean War, rising prices and corruption scandals in several federal agencies, and he announced in March 1952 that he would not seek re-election.
[hist-eur6-s1.4.12] Historians since the 1970s, and David McCullough's biography Truman (1992), have generally ranked him among the stronger presidents for his decisiveness and his civil rights initiatives, while judging his domestic record more limited than he hoped and his loyalty programme damaging to civil liberties.
