TARGET hist-ire2-s11: 11. Working with evidence: history, the historian and the documents-based question
[hist-ire2-s11.0.b] History is the past itself, the evidence that survives of it, and the written accounts historians build from that evidence.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.0] The past itself is gone; no one can examine the Dublin lockout of 1913 directly, only what it left behind.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.1] Evidence is whatever survives: documents, objects, images, buildings and recorded memories.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.2] A written history is a historian’s account, shaped by the questions asked and the evidence found, so two historians can offer different histories of the same events.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.3] Historical knowledge is provisional: new evidence or new questions can revise an account, as when the Pigott forgeries collapsed in 1889 and showed the letters against Parnell to be false.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.4] A source is the item itself, such as a speech by Gladstone; it becomes evidence only when a historian uses it to answer a particular question, such as why Liberals split in 1886.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.5] Public records (census returns, parliamentary papers, police reports) show official policy and statistics; private institutions’ records (union minute books, company papers, the GAA’s convention reports) show the interests of their members.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.6] Letters, memoirs and eyewitness accounts give personal views, for example Davitt’s Leaves from a Prison Diary or accounts by participants in Bloody Sunday.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.7] Newspapers, speeches and pamphlets show how events were presented to the public at the time.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.8] Maps, photographs and political cartoons are visual evidence; oral testimony and radio or television transcripts are usually recorded long after events.
[hist-ire2-s11.0.9] Repositories include archives (state records and the papers of organisations), libraries (printed books, newspapers and pamphlets) and museums (objects, banners and photographs), and each holds different kinds of source.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.b] A historian cross-examines every source with the same set of questions before using it.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.0] Who made it: a nationalist MP, a unionist editor, a bishop or a trade unionist each saw events from a position that shaped what they recorded.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.1] Why it was made: to inform, persuade, defend an interest, record an event or claim credit afterwards.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.2] When it was made: a report written during the election campaign of 1885 differs from a memoir written decades later.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.3] In what circumstances: a statement made under pressure, in secret or before an inquiry may differ from a public speech.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.4] For what audience: a speech to supporters aims to rally them; a letter to an opponent aims to persuade or warn.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.5] Stages of research: choose a question, locate sources, evaluate them, compare them with each other, form an interpretation and test it against the evidence.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.6] Primary sources were produced at the time of the events (a speech, a letter, a newspaper report, a cartoon); secondary sources are later accounts based on them (a modern book on Home Rule).
[hist-ire2-s11.1.7] Primary does not mean reliable: a participant’s account is close to events but may be biased, and a secondary source may be more balanced but further from the facts.
[hist-ire2-s11.1.8] A source can be both: a historian’s book is secondary for the period it describes but primary evidence for the thinking of the time it was written.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.b] Documents mix checkable facts with opinion and persuasion, and the historian has to separate them.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.0] A fact can be checked against other evidence, for example that Lord Frederick Cavendish and Thomas Burke were killed in Phoenix Park on 6 May 1882.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.1] An opinion is a judgement that evidence cannot settle, for example that the Land Act of 1881 was a betrayal of the tenants.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.2] Bias is a consistent tilt in how a source presents events, shown by loaded words, selective detail and omission, for example a unionist newspaper describing a nationalist rally as a mob.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.3] Propaganda is material designed to persuade a mass audience, using emotion, slogans and selected facts; Home Rule is Rome Rule is a unionist slogan that works this way.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.4] A biased source is not worthless: it is good evidence of what its author and audience believed, even when poor evidence of what happened.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.5] Objectivity is the aim of fair, evidence-based judgement, although no historian is free from all assumptions.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.6] Strengths of a source include closeness to events, first-hand knowledge, precision and corroboration by others; limitations include bias, a narrow viewpoint, gaps, later revision and the survival of only some records.
[hist-ire2-s11.2.7] Revising a view: historians change their accounts when new sources appear, when old ones are tested harder, or when new questions are asked, such as women’s roles in the suffrage movement.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.b] Question 1 on the paper is the documents-based question on the prescribed topic, with four parts: comprehension, comparison, criticism and contextualisation.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.0] Question 1 is worth 100 marks of the 400: part (a) comprehension 20, part (b) comparison 20, part (c) criticism 20 and part (d) contextualisation 40.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.1] The documents, written or visual, come from one of the topic’s three case studies: the elections of 1885–86, Dublin 1913 or the GAA to 1891.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.2] With four equal questions in about 170 minutes, plan roughly 40 minutes for Question 1: a few minutes each for parts (a) to (c) and the biggest share for part (d).
[hist-ire2-s11.3.3] Part (a) asks what a document says or shows; the answer must come from the named document alone.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.4] Step 1: read the source note giving the author, date and publication, then the document.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.5] Step 2: underline the exact points the question asks for, and find the line for each.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.6] Step 3: put each point in your own words with a detail from the document (a name, date, place or figure).
[hist-ire2-s11.3.7] Step 4: do not add outside knowledge; part (a) is marked on what the document tells you, and extra background earns nothing.
[hist-ire2-s11.3.8] Example approach: for a speech on Home Rule, list each argument the speaker gives, in order, with a short reference to each.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.b] Comparison asks how two documents are alike or different on a stated point, and every point needs evidence from both.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.0] Choose the exact point the question names, such as attitude to Home Rule, to the strikers or to the GAA’s bans.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.1] State what the first document says on that point, quoting or referring to a line.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.2] State what the second says, then say whether they agree, differ or agree only in part.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.3] A specific similarity or difference in both documents earns marks; a description of each document separately does not.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.4] Explain the difference where you can, using the authors’ positions: for example a nationalist paper and a unionist paper describing the same crowd would use different words and numbers.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.5] Compare like with like: a private letter and a public speech can differ because of audience rather than disagreement.
[hist-ire2-s11.4.6] Use linking words such as whereas, both and unlike to keep every sentence a comparison.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.b] Criticism asks how far a document can be trusted or how useful it is, and the answer tests provenance, purpose, viewpoint and reliability.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.0] Provenance: who produced the document, when and where; a name and date alone are not an evaluation.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.1] Purpose: why it was produced and for whom; a speech meant to rally supporters will present facts in their favour.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.2] Viewpoint: the author’s party, religion, class or nationality, for example a landlord, a tenant, a priest or an employer.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.3] Reliability: whether the claims are specific and supported by other evidence, or general and unsupported.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.4] Cite a line from the document for each judgement, for example an emotional phrase as evidence of bias.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.5] Balance the answer: say what the document is good evidence for as well as what it cannot show; a biased source can still be useful evidence of attitudes.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.6] Usefulness questions ask how useful a document is for a stated enquiry, so measure it against that enquiry.
[hist-ire2-s11.5.7] Questions on fact and opinion ask you to pick out one example of each, and questions on propaganda ask how the language and imagery persuade the audience.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.b] Visual sources are read with the same questions as written ones, with extra attention to symbols and captions.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.0] Identify every figure and what it stands for: Hibernia or Erin for Ireland, John Bull or Britannia for Britain, a statesman drawn from his known appearance.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.1] Read the caption, labels and dates, because the meaning often rests on the words.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.2] Note the exaggeration: caricature of faces, size, dress or animal imagery shows the artist’s attitude to a person or group.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.3] Identify the message and the target audience: a cartoon in Punch addressed British readers, while one in a nationalist or unionist paper addressed supporters.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.4] A cartoon is opinion, not record: use it as evidence of attitudes and propaganda, not of the facts of an event.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.5] Ask whether a cartoon is hostile to Irish people or to a politician, and support the answer with a detail such as the way a figure is drawn.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.6] A photograph shows what stood in front the camera, but the choice of subject, framing, staging and caption can mislead.
[hist-ire2-s11.6.7] Example: a photograph of the Church Street ruins of September 1913 is good evidence of the state of Dublin housing, but does not show who was to blame.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.b] Part (d) is a short essay on the subject of the case study, using your own knowledge; it is not a retelling of the documents.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.0] Part (d) carries 40 marks, twice as much as any other part, and is marked like a short essay on cumulative relevant points.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.1] It asks about the case study’s subject, such as a leader’s strengths and weaknesses, how an organisation dealt with difficulties, or the causes and outcome of a dispute.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.2] Open with the background and context, then deal with events in order, with dates, names and numbers, then outcomes and significance.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.3] Use the documents sparingly as supporting evidence, referring to them by letter, but do not summarise them.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.4] Cover both sides: for example the arguments of nationalists and unionists in 1885–86, employers and workers in 1913, or the Church, the IRB and the founders in the GAA.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.5] Finish with a judgement that answers the question set, such as how far a leader succeeded or how significant an event was.
[hist-ire2-s11.7.6] Draw on the rest of the topic for context: Home Rule and land for the elections, labour and Dublin’s poverty for 1913, and cultural revival and the Parnell split for the GAA.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.b] Every document can be dated by the people, events and language it contains, and dating it is the first step in using it.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.0] Elections of 1885–86: mention of the Third Reform Act or the new voters points to 1884–85; the balance of power, the Hawarden Kite or Salisbury points to late 1885; a bill, Chamberlain or the Liberal Unionists points to April–June 1886; the Orange card, Belfast rioting or the July poll points to mid-1886.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.1] Typical voices for 1885–86: Parnell and Irish Party speakers, Gladstone, Chamberlain, Hartington, Lord Randolph Churchill, the Freeman’s Journal, the Belfast News-Letter, and Punch.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.2] Dublin 1913: trams and Horse Show week point to late August; O’Connell Street, batons and a banned meeting point to 31 August; the Document, Askwith or AE’s letter to September–October; food ships, Save the Kiddies and the Citizen Army to October–November; return to work to January 1914.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.3] Typical voices for 1913: Larkin, Connolly, William Martin Murphy, Archbishop Walsh, the Irish Worker, the Irish Independent, the TUC and the Dublin Metropolitan Police.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.4] The GAA to 1891: Thurles and Hayes’ Hotel point to November 1884; Croke, Parnell and Davitt as patrons to 1884–85; Cusack’s removal and IRB influence to 1886–87; the first championships to 1887–88; the American tour to 1888; the Parnell split and collapsing membership to 1890–91.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.5] Typical voices for the GAA: Cusack, Davin, Croke, Davitt, Parnell, the nationalist press, the RIC and Dublin Castle.
[hist-ire2-s11.8.6] Once dated, ask who gains from the document’s version of events, and what it leaves out.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.b] Marks separate answers that use the evidence precisely from those that retell it or ignore it.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.0] Strong comprehension answers use only the document and give precise details; weak ones add outside facts or copy whole passages.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.1] Strong comparison answers name a point and give a line from each document; weak ones describe the two documents one after the other.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.2] Strong criticism answers judge provenance, purpose and viewpoint with cited lines and reach a balanced verdict; weak ones say only that the source is biased.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.3] Strong part (d) answers combine dated detail, two or more viewpoints and a clear judgement; weak ones paraphrase the documents or offer a general essay.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.4] A strength of the format is that it rewards close reading; a limit is that it rewards answers that are tied closely to the case study and its documents.
[hist-ire2-s11.9.5] Contemporary views and later historians’ views are both useful in part (d), for example how contemporaries saw Parnell or Larkin and how historians now judge them.
