TARGET hist-ire3-s1: 1. The Home Rule Bill, World War I and the 1916 Rising, 1912–1916
[hist-ire3-s1.0.b] Home Rule became a real prospect because the Liberal government depended on the Irish Parliamentary Party and had removed the House of Lords' power to block bills for good.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.0] After the two general elections of 1910 the Liberals needed the votes of John Redmond's Irish Parliamentary Party to stay in office, and Redmond's price was Home Rule.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.1] The Parliament Act 1911 replaced the Lords' veto with a delaying power of two years: a bill passed by the Commons in three successive sessions became law without Lords' consent.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.2] Asquith introduced the Third Home Rule Bill on 11 April 1912.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.3] The Bill set up an Irish parliament in Dublin of a Senate of 40 and a House of Commons of 164, with an executive responsible to it.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.4] Westminster kept the Crown, war and peace, the army and navy, foreign relations and customs; most taxation remained under Westminster, and the police stayed under British control for six years.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.5] Under the Bill Ireland would send 42 MPs to Westminster, down from the 103 Irish seats of the time.
[hist-ire3-s1.0.6] The Commons passed the Bill in January 1913 and again in July 1913, and the Lords rejected it each time (30 January and 15 July 1913).
[hist-ire3-s1.0.7] The Commons passed it a third time in May 1914, which meant it could become law without the Lords.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.b] Unionists answered the Bill with mass protest and then armed organisation, and nationalists formed their own volunteers in reply. The detail is in the notes on the movements for reform before 1914.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.0] Edward Carson, a Dublin-born barrister, led Irish unionists, and James Craig organised the campaign in Ulster.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.1] On Ulster Day, 28 September 1912, about 237,000 men signed the Ulster Covenant pledging to resist Home Rule, and about 234,000 women signed a parallel Declaration.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.2] The Ulster Volunteer Force (UVF) was formed in January 1913 to resist Home Rule by force; it drilled tens of thousands of men.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.3] In March 1914 the Curragh incident, in which British cavalry officers said they would rather be dismissed than act against Ulster, showed that the army could not be relied on to coerce the unionists.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.4] The UVF landed about 25,000 rifles at Larne, Co. Antrim, on the night of 24–25 April 1914 and the government did not stop it.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.5] The Irish Volunteers were founded at the Rotunda in Dublin on 25 November 1913, with Eoin MacNeill as their public leader; the IRB (Bulmer Hobson and others) helped organise them from behind the scenes.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.6] On 26 July 1914 the Volunteers landed about 900 German rifles at Howth, Co. Dublin, from Erskine Childers's yacht Asgard.
[hist-ire3-s1.1.7] That afternoon soldiers of the King's Own Scottish Borderers fired on a jeering crowd at Bachelor's Walk in Dublin and killed three civilians; nationalists contrasted this with the lack of action at Larne.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.b] By mid-1914 Home Rule was about to become law, but exclusion of Ulster was unresolved and the outbreak of war removed the issue from the agenda.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.0] Asquith offered county option (each Ulster county voting on exclusion for six years) in March 1914; Carson rejected temporary exclusion and Redmond reluctantly accepted it as a concession.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.1] In June 1914 Redmond forced the Irish Volunteers' committee to admit 25 of his own nominees, so he could control the movement.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.2] The Buckingham Palace Conference of 21–24 July 1914 failed to agree a border for excluded counties; the sticking point was Tyrone and Fermanagh.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.3] An Amending Bill to provide for Ulster was introduced in the House of Lords in June 1914 and was changed there to exclude all nine counties of Ulster; it never became law.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.4] Britain declared war on Germany on 4 August 1914, and on 18 September 1914 the Home Rule Act received royal assent.
[hist-ire3-s1.2.5] A Suspensory Act, passed at the same time, postponed the operation of Home Rule until the end of the war, with an undertaking that Ulster would get special treatment by an amending Act.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.b] Redmond tied the Home Rule cause to the British war effort. The result was a split in the Volunteers and, over time, a loss of confidence in his party.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.0] On 3 August 1914 Redmond offered the Irish Volunteers to defend Ireland alongside the Ulster Volunteers.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.1] At Woodenbridge, Co. Wicklow, on 20 September 1914, Redmond urged the Volunteers to join the British army and fight in the war.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.2] The Volunteers split: about 170,000 followed Redmond as the National Volunteers, while about 11,000 stayed with MacNeill as the Irish Volunteers.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.3] About 200,000 Irishmen served in the British forces during the war, and estimates of Irish deaths range from about 30,000 to about 50,000.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.4] The 10th (Irish) Division fought at Suvla Bay, Gallipoli, in August 1915.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.5] The 16th (Irish) Division, raised mostly from nationalist recruits, fought at Guillemont and Ginchy on the Somme in September 1916; the nationalist MP Tom Kettle was killed at Ginchy.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.6] The 36th (Ulster) Division, drawn from the UVF, attacked at Thiepval on the first day of the Somme, 1 July 1916, and suffered over 5,000 casualties in two days.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.7] Farmers gained from high food prices, and shipbuilding and engineering in Belfast boomed, but prices rose faster than wages in the towns, and Dublin's poor suffered.
[hist-ire3-s1.3.8] Redmond's party lost influence because it had delivered a suspended Home Rule Act, and anti-war feeling among separatists grew as the war dragged on.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.b] The Rising was organised by a secret group inside the IRB, which treated Britain's war as the opportunity to strike. German help was promised but badly co-ordinated.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.0] The IRB's Supreme Council set up a Military Council in 1915; it ended up with seven members: Patrick Pearse, Joseph Plunkett, Éamonn Ceannt, Tom Clarke, Seán MacDiarmada, James Connolly and Thomas MacDonagh.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.1] Connolly of the Irish Citizen Army, who had been planning a rising of his own, was brought in by the Military Council in January 1916.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.2] The funeral of the Fenian Jeremiah O'Donovan Rossa on 1 August 1915 was organised as a show of strength and gave Pearse a platform.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.3] Roger Casement went to Germany in autumn 1914 to seek arms and an Irish Brigade from Irish prisoners of war; very few prisoners joined.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.4] A German ship, the Aud (disguised as a Norwegian vessel), carried about 20,000 rifles for the Rising; it was meant to land them in Tralee Bay, Co. Kerry, but arrived without a pilot or signals and was intercepted by the Royal Navy on Good Friday, 21 April 1916.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.5] The Aud's captain scuttled the ship in Cork Harbour on 22 April, and the arms were lost.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.6] Casement landed from a German submarine at Banna Strand, Co. Kerry, on 21 April 1916 and was arrested within hours; three Volunteers drowned that night when their car drove off Ballykissane pier on the way to meet him.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.7] MacNeill, who had been misled about the plans, learned of the Aud's loss and issued a countermand; it was published in the Sunday Independent on Easter Sunday, 23 April.
[hist-ire3-s1.4.8] The Military Council met and put the Rising back by one day to noon on Easter Monday, 24 April, so only a part of the Volunteers turned out.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.b] About 1,200 Volunteers and Citizen Army members took over strong points in Dublin and proclaimed a republic. They were out-gunned and out-numbered, and the fighting was confined mostly to Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.0] On Monday 24 April the rebels seized the General Post Office (GPO) in O'Connell Street as their headquarters, with Pearse as President and Commandant-General and Connolly commanding the Dublin forces.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.1] Pearse read the Proclamation of the Irish Republic outside the GPO; it was addressed to ‘Irishmen and Irishwomen’, guaranteed religious and civil liberty and equal rights, and was signed by the seven members of the Military Council.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.2] The main garrisons were Boland's Mill (Éamon de Valera), the South Dublin Union (Ceannt), Jacob's biscuit factory (MacDonagh), St Stephen's Green and the College of Surgeons (Michael Mallin and Countess Markievicz), and the Four Courts (Edward Daly).
[hist-ire3-s1.5.3] The rebels failed to take Dublin Castle and did not seize the main British barracks, and Trinity College, the Castle and the docks stayed in British hands.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.4] Martial law was proclaimed in Dublin on 25 April and General Sir John Maxwell was sent to take command on 28 April.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.5] British reinforcements arrived from the Curragh and from Britain; at Mount Street Bridge on 26 April a handful of Volunteers inflicted heavy casualties on the Sherwood Foresters.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.6] The gunboat Helga shelled Liberty Hall, and artillery shelling and fires wrecked much of O'Connell Street.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.7] Outside Dublin, Thomas Ashe's Volunteers won a fight at Ashbourne, Co. Meath, on 28 April, Enniscorthy was held for several days, and Liam Mellows led a poorly armed rising in Galway.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.8] The GPO was evacuated on Friday 28 April after fire; the garrison retreated to Moore Street, where The O'Rahilly was killed.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.9] Pearse surrendered to Brigadier-General Lowe on Saturday 29 April and the other garrisons surrendered on Sunday.
[hist-ire3-s1.5.10] About 485 people died: about 260 civilians, about 140 British soldiers and police, and about 80 rebels, with thousands wounded; the figures are research estimates.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.b] The Rising was at first unpopular in Dublin. The speed and manner of the executions, and mass arrests, turned opinion towards the rebels.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.0] As rebel prisoners were marched away, some Dubliners jeered them, and the Irish Parliamentary Party and most newspapers condemned the Rising.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.1] About 3,500 people were arrested and about 1,800 were interned in Britain, many at Frongoch camp in north Wales.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.2] Ninety men were sentenced to death by courts-martial, and 15 were shot between 3 and 12 May 1916: 14 at Kilmainham Gaol, and Thomas Kent at Cork.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.3] Pearse, Tom Clarke and Thomas MacDonagh were shot on 3 May; Joseph Plunkett married Grace Gifford in the prison chapel the night before his execution on 4 May.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.4] James Connolly, wounded in the fighting, was shot on 12 May while seated in a chair, together with MacDiarmada; the other 75 death sentences were commuted, including those of de Valera and Markievicz.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.5] Bishop O'Dwyer of Limerick publicly refused Maxwell's demand to discipline priests and criticised the executions.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.6] Augustine Birrell resigned as Chief Secretary on 3 May 1916, and Asquith visited Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.7] Lloyd George's attempt to settle the Irish question in June and July 1916, with Home Rule at once and temporary exclusion of six counties, failed when Unionist ministers insisted on permanent exclusion.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.8] Casement was tried for treason and hanged at Pentonville Prison on 3 August 1916; the British used his diaries to damage his reputation.
[hist-ire3-s1.6.9] The Frongoch prisoners were released at Christmas 1916, and a general amnesty followed in June 1917; many of them became the core of the new Sinn Féin.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.b] Pearse was the chief ideologue of the Rising and its leading public figure. He is judged both as an educator and cultural figure and as an advocate of armed sacrifice.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.0] Pearse (1879–1916) was born in Dublin to an English father and an Irish mother, joined the Gaelic League in 1896 and qualified as a barrister, though he hardly practised.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.1] He edited the League's paper An Claidheamh Soluis from 1903 and founded St Enda's, a bilingual school, in 1908; his essay The Murder Machine attacked the state education system.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.2] He joined the IRB in 1913, became Director of Military Organisation of the Irish Volunteers, and joined the Military Council in 1915.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.3] In his graveside oration for O'Donovan Rossa on 1 August 1915 he said Ireland unfree would never be at peace; this speech remains his best-known statement.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.4] His 1915 writings praised war as cleansing; he described bloodshed as a cleansing and sanctifying thing, and critics later called this ‘blood sacrifice’, a phrase he did not himself use as a slogan.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.5] He was President of the Provisional Government and Commandant-General in 1916, read the Proclamation, and ordered the surrender on 29 April.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.6] He was executed in Kilmainham Gaol on 3 May 1916 at the age of 36, along with his brother Willie on 4 May.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.7] Contemporaries were divided: Redmond's supporters and many Dubliners condemned him in April 1916, while by autumn he was honoured as a martyr.
[hist-ire3-s1.7.8] Historians differ: Ruth Dudley Edwards's biography (1977) treated him as a gifted but troubled figure, Conor Cruise O'Brien criticised the cult of sacrifice, and Joost Augusteijn (2010) stressed his development as an educator and a revolutionary.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.b] Markievicz was a participant in the Rising and its most prominent female combatant. Her later career is covered in the notes on the second Sinn Féin and the War of Independence.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.0] Constance Gore-Booth (1868–1927) was born in London and brought up at Lissadell, Co. Sligo; she studied art at the Slade School in London and in Paris, and married the Polish count Casimir Markievicz in 1900.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.1] She joined Inghinidhe na hÉireann and Sinn Féin in 1908 and in 1909 helped Bulmer Hobson found Fianna Éireann, a republican youth organisation.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.2] She joined the Irish Citizen Army and in the 1913 Lockout ran the soup kitchen at Liberty Hall.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.3] In the Rising she was second-in-command to Michael Mallin at St Stephen's Green and the College of Surgeons, and she surrendered with the garrison.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.4] At court-martial on 4 May 1916 she was sentenced to death, and General Maxwell commuted it to penal servitude for life on account of her sex; she was held in Aylesbury Prison until the amnesty of June 1917.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.5] Contemporaries admired her courage and also mocked her aristocratic background; Sean O'Casey was critical of her.
[hist-ire3-s1.8.6] Later historians stress her place as an early woman in revolutionary politics and her commitment to labour and women's causes.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.b] Judgements of the Rising depend on whether it is measured by its military result, by the aims of the Proclamation, or by its political effects.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.0] Military failure: the rebels held no major target outside their garrisons, were contained within a week, and had few arms.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.1] Political success: the executions, internment and the Proclamation's republican claim gave the survivors a moral authority the Parliamentary Party could not match.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.2] Contemporary view: unionists and Redmond's party called it a German-backed rebellion; the Catholic press, which at first condemned it, shifted after the executions.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.3] Later view: revisionist historians stressed its small support, secret planning and the fact that civilians were the largest group of dead; others stressed the effect of the British reaction.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.4] Yeats's line ‘A terrible beauty is born’ from his poem Easter, 1916 summed up the sense that Irish politics had changed.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.5] Home Rule was overtaken by events: the suspended Act never came into force, and partition came instead through the Government of Ireland Act of 1920.
[hist-ire3-s1.9.6] The Rising set up a debate over legitimacy that shaped later republican claims, and its fiftieth anniversary in 1966 and centenary in 2016 became public occasions.
