CANDIDATES hist-eur6-s4: 4. Domestic factors in US foreign policy: McCarthyism, the anti-war movement and race relations
[hist-eur6-s4.0.b] Fear of communist subversion in the United States grew after 1945 and shaped the domestic limits within which foreign policy was made.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.0] The House Un-American Activities Committee (HUAC) investigated alleged communist influence in Hollywood, and its hearings in October 1947 led to the Hollywood Ten, who refused to answer questions, being convicted of contempt of Congress in 1948; they served prison terms of up to a year from 1950 after losing their appeals.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.1] The studios adopted the Waldorf Statement in November 1947, agreeing not to employ anyone who was a suspected communist, which created the Hollywood blacklist.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.2] Alger Hiss, a former State Department official, was convicted of perjury in January 1950 after denying that he had passed State Department documents to Whittaker Chambers, a former communist courier who accused him in 1948; the case was highly publicised and Nixon's role in it made his name.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.3] Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were arrested in 1950 for passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union and were executed in June 1953.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.4] The McCarran Internal Security Act of September 1950 required communist organisations to register with the government, and Congress overrode Truman's veto of it.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.5] Truman's loyalty programme of 1947 and the subsequent investigations made many federal employees fear losing their jobs over any past political association.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.6] The Smith Act trial of eleven Communist Party leaders in 1949 led to convictions that the Supreme Court upheld in Dennis v. United States (1951).
[hist-eur6-s4.0.7] Klaus Fuchs, a British physicist who had worked on the Manhattan Project, confessed to passing secrets to the Soviets in January 1950, which strengthened fears of spies.
[hist-eur6-s4.0.8] The Red Scare reached teachers, trade unionists and entertainers: loyalty oaths were widely imposed and many people lost jobs for refusing to name former associates.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.b] McCarthy was a participant who used the Red Scare to build a national following. He was also a figure whose methods were judged harshly by contemporaries and later historians.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.0] Joseph McCarthy was born in 1908 in Grand Chute, Wisconsin, served as a Marine in the Second World War, and was elected a Republican senator for Wisconsin in 1946.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.1] In 1950 he was a little-known senator whose record was thin; Klaus Fuchs's arrest as an atomic spy in Britain, the Hiss conviction and the loss of China made the Wheeling speech a national sensation.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.2] The Tydings Committee of the Senate investigated his claims in 1950 and called them a fraud and a hoax, but McCarthy's attacks helped to defeat Senator Millard Tydings in the November 1950 election.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.3] His methods were guilt by association, unnamed lists, shifting numbers and personal smears, and the term McCarthyism was coined by the cartoonist Herblock in 1950.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.4] He investigated the Voice of America and the overseas libraries of the State Department in 1953, and his aides Roy Cohn and G. David Schine toured Europe checking them.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.5] Edward R. Murrow's television programme See It Now, broadcast on 9 March 1954, used McCarthy's own words and film to criticise him.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.6] In a speech at Wheeling, West Virginia, on 9 February 1950 he claimed to hold a list of communists working in the State Department, and the number he gave changed in later accounts.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.7] He chaired the Senate Permanent Subcommittee on Investigations from 1953, where he made accusations against officials of the State Department and, in 1954, turned his inquiries on the army.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.8] The Army-McCarthy hearings in April to June 1954 were televised, and the lawyer Joseph Welch's question Have you no sense of decency, sir? became a turning point in public opinion against him.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.9] The Senate voted 67 to 22 on 2 December 1954 to censure him for conduct unbecoming a senator, and his influence collapsed after that vote.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.10] Eisenhower avoided naming McCarthy in public for most of 1953 and 1954, and the president's private disapproval was not made clear until later.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.11] McCarthy died in May 1957 of illness made worse by alcoholism; historians still debate how many of his accusations were justified, as the Venona decrypts published in 1995 showed that some Soviet spies were indeed active in the government.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.12] Contemporaries split along party lines, with many Republicans and conservatives supporting him, while many liberals and the press denounced his methods as reckless smears.
[hist-eur6-s4.1.13] Historians since Richard Rovere (1959) have judged him a demagogue; Ellen Schrecker argues that McCarthyism was a wider system, backed by the FBI under J. Edgar Hoover, HUAC and both parties, which McCarthy exploited rather than created, and M. Stanton Evans (2007) offered a minority defence based on Venona.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.b] The climate of suspicion affected what officials said and did about China, the Soviet Union and Asia.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.0] The Republican charge that Truman and the State Department had lost China, after the communist victory in October 1949, was amplified by McCarthy's attacks on State Department officials.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.1] The China expert Owen Lattimore was accused by McCarthy in 1950 of being a leading Soviet agent in the United States; he was never charged with espionage, and perjury charges against him were dropped in 1955.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.2] The State Department's China specialists were moved from their posts or dismissed in the early 1950s, weakening the government's expert knowledge of Asia.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.3] McCarthy's campaign made it politically risky for Democrats to show any willingness to negotiate with communist states, which constrained diplomacy in the Korean and Chinese crises.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.4] Eisenhower's administration was accused of appeasing McCarthy, and his State Department adopted the loyalty security checks that McCarthy had demanded.
[hist-eur6-s4.2.5] Soviet propaganda used images of racial violence and discrimination in the United States, including the Little Rock crisis of 1957, to argue that American democracy was a sham.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.b] Opposition to the Vietnam War grew from a small minority in 1965 into a mass movement that affected the government's choices from 1968 onwards.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.0] The first major teach-in against the war was held at the University of Michigan on 24 March 1965, and teach-ins spread to many campuses in the following weeks.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.1] Students for a Democratic Society (SDS), founded in 1960 and famous for the Port Huron Statement of 1962, organised large anti-war marches and campus protests from 1965.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.2] The first draft lottery, which selected men for call-up by birthdate, was held on 1 December 1969, and it made young men more aware of the war's impact on their lives.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.3] A large march on the Pentagon on 21 October 1967 was recorded by Norman Mailer in The Armies of the Night, which won major literary prizes.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.4] The Vietnam Moratorium demonstrations of 15 October and 15 November 1969 drew very large crowds, and Nixon responded with a speech on 3 November 1969 appealing to a silent majority.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.5] On 4 May 1970, after the US invasion of Cambodia had been announced on 30 April, the Ohio National Guard shot protesters at Kent State University, killing four students.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.6] The Pentagon Papers, a secret government study of the war's history, were published by the New York Times on 13 June 1971, and the Supreme Court upheld the paper's right to publish on 30 June 1971.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.7] Draft resistance spread: many young men burned draft cards or fled to Canada, and the draft deferment for college students meant that poorer and black men were more likely to be sent to Vietnam.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.8] Muhammad Ali refused induction on 28 April 1967 on religious grounds as a Muslim minister, which made the draft a matter of conscience for a mass audience.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.9] The Democratic National Convention in Chicago in August 1968 saw police beat protesters outside the convention hall in front of television cameras, and demonstrators chanted that the whole world was watching.
[hist-eur6-s4.3.10] The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution was repealed by Congress in January 1971.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.b] Racial discrimination at home was a serious problem for US foreign policy, because it weakened America's claim to lead the free world.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.0] The Brown v Board of Education ruling of 17 May 1954 was reported abroad as evidence that the United States was tackling discrimination, although desegregation was slow in the South.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.1] The Little Rock crisis of 1957 was broadcast around the world, and US diplomats argued that it damaged the country's standing in newly independent Asian and African states.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.2] President Truman's Executive Order 9981 of 1948 ordering the armed forces to desegregate was partly motivated by the need to answer communist criticism of US racism.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.3] The Freedom Rides of 1961 and the Birmingham campaign of 1963 were covered in the international press and undermined the US claim to be a model of democracy.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.4] Black Americans such as Malcolm X and Martin Luther King Jr. linked racial injustice to US foreign policy, with King linking the cost of the war in Vietnam to the funds needed for the poor.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.5] The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965 were framed in part as steps needed to maintain US credibility abroad during the Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s4.4.6] The Soviet Union and later the Chinese broadcast stories of lynchings and segregation to the Third World, and the State Department filed briefs in Brown arguing that segregation was an embarrassment in the Cold War.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.b] Eisenhower's administration replaced Truman's loyalty rules with its own security criteria, which kept the anti-communist climate in place.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.0] Eisenhower signed Executive Order 10450 in April 1953, which set new security criteria for federal employment and superseded Truman's loyalty programme.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.1] Under the new criteria many government employees were dismissed as security risks, and past membership in suspect organisations could count against a person as well as proven disloyalty.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.2] Eisenhower's administration did not challenge McCarthy's investigations directly, and his public reticence allowed the charges to spread until the Army hearings of 1954.
[hist-eur6-s4.5.3] Richard Nixon, Eisenhower's vice president, had built his national reputation on the Hiss investigation, which made anti-communism a bipartisan position in 1950s politics.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.b] Opposition in Congress moved from criticism to legislative limits on the war from 1970, and it reduced the President's room for manoeuvre.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.0] The McGovern-Hatfield amendment of 1970, which proposed a date for withdrawing all US troops, was defeated in the Senate, though it attracted substantial support.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.1] The Cooper-Church amendment sought to bar funds for US operations in Cambodia after 30 June 1970; the Senate passed it but the House did not, and a weaker version banning US ground troops in Laos and Thailand became law in December 1970, one of the first formal limits on the war.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.2] The Case-Church amendment, passed in June 1973 and accepted by Nixon in a compromise bill, cut off funds for US combat in Indochina from 15 August 1973.
[hist-eur6-s4.6.3] Congressional opposition was strengthened by the Pentagon Papers and by testimony from veterans, and it made the war a matter of political argument within the government.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.b] Examiners reward judgements that weigh the influence of domestic fears and protests on decisions against the other causes of US policy.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.0] Supporters of McCarthyism argue that the fear of espionage was justified by real Soviet spy networks, such as those uncovered in the Venona decrypts, and that anti-communism kept the country vigilant.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.1] Critics argue that McCarthy's methods damaged the civil liberties of innocent people and weakened expert advice on China, and that the State Department lost its best Asia analysts.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.2] The anti-war movement helped to end the draft and to bring Johnson's withdrawal, but the war continued for four more years after his decision not to run in 1968.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.3] Historians disagree about whether protests changed the outcome of the war or simply sharpened a decision that the government had already made.
[hist-eur6-s4.7.4] The Red Scare was a long-lasting feature of US politics, and the constraints it placed on foreign policy were most severe in the early 1950s.
