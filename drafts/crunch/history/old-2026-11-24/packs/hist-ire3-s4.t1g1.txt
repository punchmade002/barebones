[hist-ire3-s4.0.b] The Truce of 11 July 1921 opened talks about Ireland's status. Months of correspondence were needed before a formal conference could meet, and the central question was already clear: the Crown.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.0] Lloyd George had invited de Valera to talks on 24 June 1921, after George V's call for reconciliation at Belfast, and the Truce came into force at noon on 11 July.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.1] De Valera met Lloyd George in London four times between 14 and 21 July 1921, and neither side could shift the other on principle.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.2] The British proposal of 20 July offered dominion status with restrictions: British control of defence and the ports, free trade, and no change to the position of the six counties.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.3] The Dáil cabinet rejected the offer on 10 August; de Valera's counter-proposal was ‘external association’, in which Ireland would be a separate republic linked to the British Commonwealth, with the Crown recognised only as head of the association.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.4] In an exchange of letters through the late summer Lloyd George insisted that Ireland accept the Crown and remain within the Empire, and de Valera insisted on the right of self-determination.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.5] Lloyd George's invitation of 29 September proposed a conference to see how the association of Ireland with the Empire might be reconciled with Irish national aspirations; the Irish accepted it, and the conference opened on 11 October 1921.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.6] Britain wanted a settlement because the war was costing money and reputation, the Empire's dominion premiers urged compromise, and the cabinet knew that a full-scale reconquest would need a very large army and martial law over the whole island.
[hist-ire3-s4.0.7] The Irish side wanted a settlement because the IRA was short of arms and ammunition, and because of the cost to civilians in the war zones.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.b] The decision to send plenipotentiaries and keep de Valera in Dublin shaped the whole outcome and the debate about it afterwards.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.0] The Irish delegation was agreed by the Dáil cabinet in September 1921: Arthur Griffith (chairman), Michael Collins, Robert Barton, George Gavan Duffy and Éamonn Duggan.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.1] Erskine Childers was the delegation's chief secretary, with Diarmuid O'Hegarty, John Chartres and others as staff.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.2] The delegation lived at 22 Hans Place in Knightsbridge, while Collins stayed separately at Cadogan Gardens.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.3] De Valera stayed in Dublin, saying that as head of state he should be held in reserve and that he had to keep the more extreme republicans on side; Cathal Brugha and Austin Stack also stayed at home.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.4] Critics, including Collins, argued that it was a mistake: the man with most authority and most experience of Lloyd George was absent and later disowned the result.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.5] The delegates' credentials described them as plenipotentiaries, which meant they had full power to negotiate and sign.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.6] Their written instructions were different: they should report to Dublin on the text of any proposed treaty before signing; the exact meaning of this instruction was later disputed.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.7] The British team was Lloyd George (Prime Minister), Austen Chamberlain (Lord Privy Seal), Lord Birkenhead (Lord Chancellor), Winston Churchill (Colonial Secretary), Sir Laming Worthington-Evans (War Secretary), Sir Hamar Greenwood (Chief Secretary for Ireland) and the Attorney General, Gordon Hewart.
[hist-ire3-s4.1.8] Lionel Curtis and the cabinet secretary Tom Jones acted as advisers and go-betweens for the British.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.b] Plenary sessions at Downing Street gave way to small sub-conferences where Griffith and Collins argued about the Crown, Ulster, defence and finance.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.0] The plenary sessions of 11–24 October produced no agreement, and after that small groups met, so most of the bargaining was done privately.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.1] The Crown and the oath: the British demanded an oath of allegiance to the Crown and membership of the Commonwealth, and the Irish tried to substitute external association and an oath to the constitution.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.2] Ulster: the Irish argued that Ireland was one nation and that the six counties should not be allowed to opt out; Lloyd George said he could not coerce them, and he suggested a Boundary Commission, which Griffith and Collins took as a way of reducing the North so much that it could not survive.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.3] Defence: Britain demanded control of Irish ports and coastal defence for naval reasons, which the Irish saw as a limit on sovereignty.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.4] Trade and finance: the British wanted free trade between the two islands and a share of the British public debt, while the Irish claimed offsetting counter-claims for overtaxation.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.5] Craig and the Northern government refused to join the talks, and Lloyd George tried to extract concessions from Craig on the basis of a united Ireland within the Empire.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.6] The delegates travelled to Dublin to consult the cabinet in November and December, so the process was not entirely independent of de Valera and the cabinet.
[hist-ire3-s4.2.7] Throughout, Tom Jones and Lionel Curtis passed messages between Lloyd George and Griffith, and Collins formed a good relationship with Birkenhead.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.b] The final week was a negotiation under deadline pressure. The decision to sign without referring back to Dublin is the central controversy.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.0] On 2 December Griffith privately gave Lloyd George a written assurance that he would not allow the talks to break on the Ulster question, provided the Boundary Commission was set up; Lloyd George later treated this as a commitment and republicans saw it as a concession made without the cabinet's knowledge.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.1] At a cabinet meeting in Dublin on 3 December the cabinet instructed the delegates not to accept the oath as drafted, and the delegates returned to London with that line.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.2] On 4 and 5 December the British pressed for signature; on 5 December Lloyd George told Griffith and Collins that there would be a break and war unless the Treaty was signed that night, and he said his courier was ready to take letters to Craig either way.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.3] Barton later recalled that Lloyd George warned of ‘immediate and terrible war’, and the British minutes described a threat of war within days; whether the threat was a bluff is still disputed.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.4] The delegates returned to Hans Place, where Collins and Griffith argued that the alternative to signing was war, and Barton, who had at first refused, gave way.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.5] The Treaty was signed at Downing Street at about 2.10 am on 6 December 1921 by all five Irish delegates, with the British team.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.6] Collins said afterwards that he had signed his own death warrant, a remark he made in private letters of that month.
[hist-ire3-s4.3.7] De Valera was not told in advance; he learned of the signing on 6 December and was angry that the delegation had not sent the final text to Dublin.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.b] The Treaty created the Irish Free State as a self-governing dominion in the Empire, with the Crown, an oath, British naval bases and partition qualified by a Boundary Commission.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.0] Article 1: the Irish Free State was to have the same constitutional status as Canada, Australia, New Zealand and South Africa.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.1] The King was represented by a Governor-General, and the Free State's parliament and government were to be responsible to the Dáil.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.2] The oath: members of the Free State parliament had to swear to be faithful to the Constitution and to the King, ‘in virtue of the common citizenship of Ireland with Great Britain’.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.3] Defence: Britain would defend the coasts, and kept the use of the ports of Berehaven, Queenstown (Cobh) and Lough Swilly, with the right to use others in war; the Free State army was to be limited in proportion to population.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.4] Finance: the Free State took on a share of the British public debt, subject to counter-claims; the Treaty said nothing of land annuities.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.5] Ulster: Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State within one month, and a Boundary Commission would then settle the border in line with the wishes of inhabitants, as far as compatible with economic and geographic conditions.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.6] A Provisional Government would take over from the British administration until the Free State was established.
[hist-ire3-s4.4.7] The Treaty had to be approved by the Dáil, and by the British Parliament; it was also to be ratified by a meeting of the House of Parliament of Southern Ireland.

[hist-ire3-s4.5.0] On 8 December the Dáil cabinet met and divided four to three in favour of the Treaty; de Valera, Brugha and Stack were against.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.1] De Valera issued a statement to the press saying that he could not recommend the Treaty to the Dáil.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.2] The Dáil debated in private from 14 to 17 December, where de Valera put forward his own alternative text, Document No. 2, based on external association; it was never presented to the British.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.3] The public debate ran from 19 December to 10 January 1922, with an adjournment over Christmas; speakers included Griffith, Collins, Brugha, Mary MacSwiney and Childers.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.4] Collins described the Treaty as giving ‘the freedom to achieve freedom’, a stepping stone to full independence, while Brugha attacked Collins's reputation.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.5] Opponents argued that the oath and the Crown betrayed the republic proclaimed in 1916 and declared in 1919; supporters argued that the people did not want more war.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.6] All six women deputies voted against the Treaty.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.7] The Dáil approved the Treaty on 7 January 1922 by 64 votes to 57.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.8] De Valera resigned as President on 9 January and lost a vote to be re-elected, and Griffith became President of the Dáil on 10 January.
[hist-ire3-s4.5.9] A Provisional Government under Collins was formed on 14 January and took over from the British administration in Dublin Castle on 16 January.

[hist-ire3-s4.6.7] Historians: Michael Laffan and Colum Kenny stress his role in creating the political movement that won independence and his realism in 1921.

[hist-ire3-s4.7.0] Collins was Minister for Finance and head of the IRB, and he warned the cabinet that he would be a poor negotiator; he believed that his name was needed for the military side but that the politicians should lead.

[hist-ire3-s2.7.7] Historians: Tim Pat Coogan (1993) and Diarmaid Ferriter (Judging Dev, 2007) debate whether he was a pragmatist or an ideologue.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.b] This part covers Markievicz after her release in 1917, as a Sinn Féin politician and Dáil minister.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.0] After her release in June 1917 she joined Sinn Féin and the Volunteers' public campaign, and she was elected to the executive.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.1] She was arrested in the German Plot of May 1918 and held in Holloway, where she was elected in December 1918; she was released in March 1919.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.2] She was Minister for Labour in the Dáil ministry from April 1919 to January 1922, the only woman in the cabinet, and one of the first women in Europe to hold cabinet rank.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.3] In September 1920 she was arrested again and sentenced to two years' hard labour; she was released in July 1921, after the Truce.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.4] Her work as minister was limited by the fact that she was in jail or on the run for much of the time.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.5] She opposed the Treaty in the Dáil vote in January 1922; her later career is covered in the notes on the Civil War.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.6] Contemporaries admired her bravery but some Sinn Féin colleagues distrusted her socialism and her flamboyance.
[hist-ire3-s2.8.7] Historians now emphasise her importance as a pioneer of women's participation in revolutionary politics.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.b] Judgements depend on whether the 1918 election, the Dáil and the IRA are credited with winning the settlement or blamed for its costs.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.0] Achievement: the 1918 result gave Sinn Féin a democratic mandate, and the counter-state showed Irish people could run their own affairs.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.1] Achievement: the IRA campaign made Ireland costly and hard to govern and, with Dáil diplomacy, brought Britain to talks.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.2] Limit: the IRA never controlled much territory, and it was short of arms and men by mid-1921.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.3] Limit: the election was won on a minority share of the vote, and Sinn Féin's mandate said nothing about methods; many voters wanted an end to Home Rule rather than war.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.4] Contemporary view: nationalists saw a legitimate government defending itself, while the British government called the Dáil and IRA a ‘murder gang’ before changing course.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.5] Later view: historians debate how far the war was supported by the majority and how far it involved intimidation of informers and loyalists, as in Peter Hart's work on Cork.
[hist-ire3-s2.9.6] The dual strategy of Dáil and IRA left the movement with two sources of authority that the Treaty split in 1922.

CANDIDATES hist-ire3-s3: 3. Partition and Northern Ireland under the Unionist Party, 1912–1949
[hist-ire3-s3.0.b] Partition was the result of unionist resistance, British failure to find an all-Ireland settlement and the pattern of the 1918 vote.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.0] Unionist resistance: the Ulster Covenant of 1912 and the UVF made clear that Home Rule for all Ireland could be enforced only by force.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.1] Exclusion debates: county option for six years (March 1914), the failed Buckingham Palace Conference (July 1914) and the failed Lloyd George proposals of 1916 all assumed the exclusion of some Ulster counties.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.2] The 1916 plan collapsed because unionist ministers in the cabinet insisted that exclusion be permanent, while nationalists were told it would be temporary.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.3] The war and the Rising changed nationalist politics, and the Irish Convention of 1917–18 failed to produce agreement.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.4] The 1918 election confirmed the divide: Sinn Féin won most of the island, while unionists won 26 seats, mostly in the north-east.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.5] Only four of the nine Ulster counties had unionist majorities (Antrim, Down, Armagh and Londonderry), and Tyrone and Fermanagh had narrow nationalist majorities; the chosen six counties gave a safer unionist majority than nine.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.6] Northern Ireland had about 1.25 million people, of whom about two-thirds were Protestant and about one-third Catholic.
[hist-ire3-s3.0.7] British policy: Lloyd George's coalition, with a large Conservative element, was unwilling to coerce Ulster, and the Government of Ireland Act of 1920 gave the unionists the offer of their own parliament.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.b] The Act of 1920 created two Home Rule parliaments. Only the northern one worked, and the Treaty then allowed it to stay out of the Free State.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.0] The Government of Ireland Act (December 1920) set up a parliament for the six counties of Northern Ireland and one for the other 26 counties of Southern Ireland, with a Council of Ireland to link them.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.1] In the Northern election of 24 May 1921 the Unionists won 40 of 52 seats, and Sinn Féin and the Nationalists won six each but would not recognise the parliament.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.2] Craig became Prime Minister in June 1921, and George V opened the Northern parliament in Belfast on 22 June 1921.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.3] Southern Ireland's parliament never worked, since Sinn Féin won 124 of 128 seats and treated the election as one for the Dáil.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.4] Under the Treaty Northern Ireland could opt out of the Free State, and it did so on 7 December 1922, the day after the Free State came into being.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.5] The Treaty provided for a Boundary Commission, chaired by Richard Feetham, with Eoin MacNeill for the Free State and J.R. Fisher for Northern Ireland, since Craig refused to nominate.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.6] The commission's expected report was leaked to the Morning Post in November 1925; MacNeill resigned, and on 3 December 1925 the three governments agreed that the border would stay as it was.
[hist-ire3-s3.1.7] The Tripartite Agreement also released the Free State from its share of the imperial debt and ended the Council of Ireland.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.b] The founding of the state was accompanied by sectarian rioting and an IRA campaign, to which the government responded with exceptional powers.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.0] After the killing of an RIC officer in Cork in July 1920, thousands of Catholic and some Protestant labour supporters were expelled from the Belfast shipyards and engineering works.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.1] Riots in Derry in June 1920 and in Belfast through 1920–22 killed about 500 people, most of them Catholics, and drove many families from their homes.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.2] The Dáil organised a boycott of Belfast goods in 1920, which hurt Catholic workers and shopkeepers more than it hurt the unionist economy.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.3] The Ulster Special Constabulary, formed in late 1920, had full-time A Specials, part-time B Specials and reserve C Specials; it was recruited almost entirely from Protestants.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.4] The Royal Ulster Constabulary replaced the RIC in June 1922; on paper a third of its places were reserved for Catholics, but they never filled them.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.5] The Craig–Collins pacts of January and March 1922 failed, and the IRA offensive on the border in May 1922 led the government to intern republicans.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.6] The Civil Authorities (Special Powers) Act of April 1922 allowed internment, curfews, the banning of meetings and the arrest of suspects without warrant; it was renewed annually and made permanent in 1933.
[hist-ire3-s3.2.7] Several hundred men were interned in 1922, including some held on the prison ship Argenta.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.b] The Ulster Unionist Party governed without interruption and changed the electoral rules to keep it in power. Nationalists took little part in the system.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.0] Nationalists abstained from Stormont at first; the Nationalist Party under Joe Devlin took its seats in 1925, but Sinn Féin remained abstentionist.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.1] Tyrone and Fermanagh county councils and many other nationalist-controlled councils, which refused to recognise the Belfast government in 1920–22, were dissolved and replaced by commissioners.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.2] The Local Government Act of 1922 abolished proportional representation in local elections and redrew electoral areas.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.3] Proportional representation for Stormont elections was abolished in 1929 and single-member constituencies introduced, which helped the Unionists and weakened Labour and independents.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.4] Derry Corporation stayed under unionist control from 1923 until 1969 even though Catholics were a majority of the city's population; this is the best-known case of gerrymandering.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.5] The Unionist Party held every Stormont election from 1921 to 1969 by large margins, and a bloc vote on border issues kept Protestant workers within it.
[hist-ire3-s3.3.6] Stormont Parliament Buildings, on the outskirts of Belfast, were opened by the Prince of Wales in 1932, a symbol of the permanence of the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.b] Nationalists said that the state discriminated against Catholics. Unionists denied it, arguing that Catholics refused to take part in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.0] Catholics were about a third of the population but held a small share of senior posts in the civil service, the judiciary, and local government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.1] The shipyards and heavy industry employed few Catholics, and Protestant workers' networks and the Orange Order helped to secure employment.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.2] Local councils controlled by unionists allocated public housing, and nationalists argued that houses were allocated to Protestant families first.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.3] In the mid-1930s Basil Brooke urged unionists not to employ Catholics, a statement widely quoted as evidence of the attitudes of leading unionists.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.4] On 24 April 1934 Craig told the Stormont parliament that he boasted of a Protestant parliament and a Protestant state; he said this in reply to southern Catholic claims, and it is the best-known statement of the attitudes of the unionist government.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.5] Critics of the system, such as Michael Farrell in The Orange State (1976), see systematic discrimination, while others accept discrimination in some fields but stress that it was not centrally planned.
[hist-ire3-s3.4.6] Nationalist abstention in the early years and the Catholic Church's separate schools left the minority with a limited voice in the state.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.b] Northern Ireland entered the 1920s with high-value industries that depended on export markets, and the post-war slump and the Depression hit them hard.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.0] Shipbuilding at Harland and Wolff and linen were the two big employers, and both fell back after the post-war boom ended in 1920–21.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.1] Unemployment was around 20 per cent for much of the 1920s and reached around 25 per cent in the early 1930s, among the highest in the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.2] Agriculture remained important, with small farms exporting cattle and eggs to Britain.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.3] The government followed a policy of ‘step by step’ with Britain on welfare benefits, which kept pensions and unemployment pay in line with British levels, financed with an imperial contribution.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.4] Housing in the slums of Belfast was poor and overcrowded, and the government built little until the late 1930s.
[hist-ire3-s3.5.5] In October 1932 unemployed Catholic and Protestant workers joined in the Outdoor Relief strike and riots in Belfast against the harsh relief rules; police shot dead two people, and the Board of Guardians increased the relief.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.b] Partition broke up an economy that had been built on all-Ireland trade and weakened both parts.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.0] Customs controls on the border from 1923 interrupted the flow of goods and cattle, and the Free State's tariffs and Economic War of the 1930s made smuggling common.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.1] Belfast's linen, shipbuilding and engineering, which had sold to a UK and world market, lost the southern market they had counted on.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.2] The Free State lost the industrial north-east, which had provided the bulk of Irish manufactured exports, and was left with an economy dependent on agriculture.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.3] Dublin and Belfast, once part of a single banking and railway system, were run from different centres, and railways such as the Great Northern crossed the border.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.4] Society divided as well: Catholics in the North were cut off from the southern state's institutions, while Protestants in the South felt the pressure of the Catholic ethos.
[hist-ire3-s3.6.5] Politically the border became the main theme of Northern nationalism and southern foreign policy, as seen in Articles 2 and 3 of the 1937 constitution.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.b] Craig was a leader of Ulster resistance to Home Rule and the first Prime Minister of Northern Ireland. He is judged as the builder of a stable state and also as a leader of a sectarian one.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.0] Craig (1871–1940) was born in Belfast, the son of a wealthy whiskey distiller, and worked as a stockbroker; he served in the Boer War and became MP for East Down in 1906.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.1] He organised the UVF and the Larne gun-running of April 1914 alongside Carson.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.2] He was a junior minister in the British government from 1916 to 1921, ending as Parliamentary and Financial Secretary to the Admiralty.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.3] He succeeded Carson as Unionist leader in February 1921 and became Prime Minister of Northern Ireland in June 1921, serving until his death on 24 November 1940.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.4] He was made Viscount Craigavon in 1927.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.5] He negotiated with Collins in 1922 and with Lloyd George, but insisted that the border was settled; he refused to nominate to the Boundary Commission.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.6] His government created the RUC and the Special Powers Act, and abolished proportional representation, so critics see him as a sectarian figure.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.7] His defenders note that he held the state together against IRA violence and economic crisis and that his government copied British social policy.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.8] Contemporaries: unionists revered him as their founder, while nationalists considered him responsible for their exclusion.
[hist-ire3-s3.7.9] Historians: Patrick Buckland's biography (1980) stressed his cautious, conservative leadership and his failure to reach out to Catholics, while others criticise the lack of any attempt to win minority consent.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.b] Bates was the minister who ran the machinery of control for more than 20 years, and his name is associated with the security system and electoral changes.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.0] Richard Dawson Bates (1876–1949) was a Belfast solicitor who was secretary of the Ulster Unionist Council from 1906 to 1921 and helped to organise the UVF.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.1] He was Minister of Home Affairs from 1921 to 1943, responsible for the RUC, the Special Constabulary, the Special Powers Act and local government and elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.2] He introduced the Local Government Act of 1922 that abolished PR for local elections and the 1929 change for Stormont elections.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.3] He used his powers to ban republican parades and organisations, to arrest and intern suspects and to censor some publications.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.4] Contemporaries: nationalists described him as the architect of the system of control, and many unionists praised his efficiency.
[hist-ire3-s3.8.5] Historians: Buckland, in The Factory of Grievances (1979), describes how his department's decisions, even if cautious, left a legacy of grievances; Bates left office in 1943 in ill health.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.b] Judgements depend on whether the state is measured by survival and stability or by its treatment of the minority.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.0] Achievement: it survived the violence of 1920–22 and the Depression and secured the position of Northern Ireland within the United Kingdom.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.1] Achievement: it maintained British standards in education, health and welfare through its step-by-step policy.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.2] Limit: it relied on emergency powers, a largely Protestant police and manipulated electoral rules, so the minority never accepted it.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.3] Limit: a one-party government with a permanent majority did not develop real opposition, and unemployment policy was timid.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.4] Contemporary view: unionists regarded it as a bulwark against a Catholic all-Ireland state, while nationalists saw a Protestant state.
[hist-ire3-s3.9.5] Later view: historians disagree on how far the system was planned discrimination, but accept that its legacy shaped the Troubles.
