[hist-ire6-s2.0.b] By 1957 the policy of protection that Fianna Fáil had begun in the 1930s was widely seen to have run out of steam.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.0] Tariffs and the Control of Manufactures Acts of 1932 and 1934 had created many small factories that sold only on the home market, and the home market was too small to support efficient industry.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.1] The Control of Manufactures Acts required that a majority of the shares in a new company be held by Irish citizens, which discouraged foreign firms from setting up in Ireland.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.2] Exports were mostly live cattle and farm produce sold to Britain, so the economy depended on one customer.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.3] Net emigration in the 1950s was about 400,000 and the population was falling, while the rest of western Europe was growing fast.
[hist-ire6-s2.0.4] The balance of payments crises of 1951–52 and 1955–56 had been met by deflation, which cut demand without improving productive capacity (see the notes on alternating governments).
[hist-ire6-s2.0.5] Fianna Fáil returned to government in March 1957 with Seán Lemass at Industry and Commerce and James Ryan at Finance, and they were ready to listen to new ideas from officials.

[hist-ire6-s2.1.0] Thomas Kenneth Whitaker became Secretary of the Department of Finance in 1956 at the age of 39, and he gathered a group of younger officials to examine the economy.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.1] In May 1958 he published Economic Development, a study prepared in the Department of Finance and presented under the authority of the government.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.2] It argued that protection had failed to produce growth or jobs and that emigration was a symptom of an economy that was not expanding.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.3] It recommended shifting public investment from social projects such as housing and hospitals towards directly productive investment in agriculture, industry and tourism.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.4] It welcomed foreign capital and technology, a break with the Control of Manufactures Acts, and it argued for export-led growth.
[hist-ire6-s2.1.5] The government accepted its analysis, and in November 1958 it published the Programme for Economic Expansion, usually called the First Programme, which turned the proposals into a policy with targets.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.b] The First Programme ran for five years from 1959 to 1963 and set out an agenda for growth.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.0] Aim: to raise national output and living standards and to halt emigration by creating jobs in export industry and farming.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.1] Target: a modest rise in gross national product of about 2 per cent a year, set deliberately low so that it could be achieved.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.2] The Industrial Development (Encouragement of External Investment) Act 1958 and related measures removed the rule that Irish citizens must hold most of the shares in new firms, opening the way to foreign-owned factories.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.3] Export Profits Tax Relief, first introduced in 1956, was extended so that profits from increased exports were exempt from tax.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.4] Grants were offered through the IDA, which was given a larger role, to firms setting up new plant, with the best terms in the poorer western areas.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.5] The Shannon Free Airport Development Company was set up in 1959 and built an industrial estate at Shannon to attract foreign firms.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.6] In agriculture, An Foras Talúntais (the agricultural research institute) was established in 1958 and farmers were encouraged to raise productivity and exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.2.7] The state shifted its capital spending towards productive investment, though spending on housing and health rose again as growth took hold.

[hist-ire6-s2.3.0] Real national output grew by about 4 per cent a year between 1958 and 1963, roughly double the target, and the growth was faster than in the previous decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.1] Exports of manufactured goods rose sharply, and industrial production grew as new, mostly foreign-owned firms began to operate.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.2] Net emigration fell to about 80,000 in 1961–66, from about 200,000 in 1956–61.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.3] The 1966 census recorded a population of about 2.88 million, up from 2.82 million in 1961 and the first rise since the early 1950s.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.4] Unemployment fell but remained a serious problem, and total employment grew only modestly because jobs in farming continued to decline.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.5] The balance of payments remained a concern, and by the mid-1960s wage claims and inflation were raising difficulties.
[hist-ire6-s2.3.6] Part of the success lay outside Ireland: the late 1950s and 1960s were years of strong growth in Britain and Europe, which raised the demand for Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.0] The Programme was the start of regular economic planning in Ireland, followed by a Second Programme published in 1963 and a Third Programme for 1969–72.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.1] Contemporaries welcomed it as a sharp break with the pessimism of the 1950s, and the idea of a plan for growth was supported across the parties and by the trade unions and employers.

[hist-ire6-s2.4.3] Sceptical historians stress that the world economy was booming, that the Programme built on measures begun by the Inter-Party governments, such as Export Profits Tax Relief, and that its targets were modest.
[hist-ire6-s2.4.4] Critics on the left argued that foreign firms took profits out of the country and that the growth was uneven, with the west benefiting least.

[hist-ire6-s2.5.6] He is also remembered for easing the state's attitude to Northern Ireland, as in his meetings with Terence O'Neill.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.b] Lemass saw that the future of Irish industry lay in competing in open markets, not in protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.0] Ireland applied to join the European Economic Community on 31 July 1961, nine days before Britain did, because Britain was the market for most Irish exports.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.1] President de Gaulle vetoed British entry on 14 January 1963, which ended the negotiations for the time being, although Ireland's application remained on the table (see the notes on the UN and the EEC).
[hist-ire6-s2.6.2] With EEC entry delayed, Lemass turned to a trade agreement with Britain: the Anglo-Irish Free Trade Agreement was signed on 14 December 1965 and took effect on 1 July 1966.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.3] The agreement provided for the removal of tariffs on trade between the two countries over ten years and kept access for Irish farm exports to the British market.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.4] Free trade threatened protected Irish firms in textiles, footwear and other consumer goods, and many small firms closed over the next decade.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.5] Supporters argued that free trade would bring cheaper inputs, larger markets and new investment, and that protected industries could not survive in the long run.
[hist-ire6-s2.6.6] Lemass visited Terence O'Neill at Stormont on 14 January 1965, and O'Neill visited Dublin on 9 February 1965; these were the first meetings of the leaders of the two parts of Ireland since partition (see the notes on Anglo-Irish relations).
[hist-ire6-s2.7.b] Whitaker was a civil servant, not a politician, and the most influential public official of his generation.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.0] Whitaker was born in 1916 in Rostrevor, Co. Down, and joined the civil service in 1934.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.1] As Secretary of the Department of Finance from 1956 to 1969 he was the main author of Economic Development and the First Programme, working with Lemass and with ministers such as James Ryan.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.2] He was a participant in the change of policy, using the authority of the Department of Finance, which had traditionally resisted spending, to argue for investment.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.3] He later served as Governor of the Central Bank from 1969 to 1976, as a senator from 1977 to 1982 and as chairman of several commissions, including the Constitution Review Group of the 1990s; he died in 2017 at the age of 100.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.4] Contemporaries praised his clarity and courage in questioning protection, though some in his own department doubted how far growth could be planned.
[hist-ire6-s2.7.5] Historians see him as the architect of the new approach, while stressing that Lemass and de Valera supplied the political will, and some argue that his role has been overstated by the idea that planning alone made the economy grow.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.b] Lemass was the minister and then Taoiseach who turned the new thinking into government policy.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.0] Lemass was born in Dublin in 1899, fought in the Rising in 1916 and on the anti-Treaty side in the Civil War, and his brother Noel was killed in 1923.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.1] He was a founder of Fianna Fáil in 1926 and was Minister for Industry and Commerce from 1932 to 1939, from 1941 to 1948, from 1951 to 1954 and from 1957 to 1959, and Minister for Supplies from 1939 to 1945.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.2] As the architect of the protectionist industrial policy of the 1930s, he was able later to argue that protection had served its purpose and should now give way to competition.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.3] As Taoiseach he backed the First Programme, applied to join the EEC and signed the Free Trade Agreement.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.4] Contemporaries saw him as a practical, impatient leader who got things done, though some in Fianna Fáil mistrusted his break with protection.
[hist-ire6-s2.8.5] Historians and biographers judge him a modernizer who changed the tone of Irish politics, but they note that he left rural poverty, urban housing problems and partition unresolved.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.0] Whitaker supplied the analysis and Lemass the political leadership; the Programme needed both, and their partnership is the central reason for the change of direction.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.1] Whitaker was cautious by temperament and was more sceptical than Lemass about the Second Programme's ambitious targets, which were abandoned in 1967.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.2] Achievements of the First Programme: growth about twice the target, falling emigration, rising exports and a shift in confidence.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.3] Limits: total employment barely increased, farming and the west lagged, and the balance of payments remained fragile.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.4] Contemporary view: the Programme was a success, and Fianna Fáil regained an overall majority in the 1965 election.
[hist-ire6-s2.9.5] Later view: historians accept that it was a turning point but debate how much of the growth came from policy and how much from the European boom.
