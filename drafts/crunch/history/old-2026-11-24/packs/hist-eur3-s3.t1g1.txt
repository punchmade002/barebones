[hist-eur3-s2.0.b] The Weimar Republic was founded in 1919 after the German Revolution and the end of the Kaiser's rule, and its constitution contained features that could be used to undermine it.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.0] The National Assembly met at Weimar, away from the unrest in Berlin, and adopted a constitution on 31 July 1919 creating a federal republic with a President elected by the people for seven years and a Chancellor responsible to the Reichstag.

[hist-eur3-s2.0.2] Article 48 allowed the President to issue emergency decrees and to rule without the Reichstag, a power that became central after 1930.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.3] Many judges, civil servants, army officers and university teachers from the imperial era stayed in post and remained hostile to the republic, and the 'stab in the back' myth blamed the November 1918 politicians, not the army, for defeat.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.4] Friedrich Ebert was the first President, from 1919 until his death in 1925; Paul von Hindenburg, a former field marshal, succeeded him in 1925.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.5] The Treaty of Versailles of June 1919 imposed territorial losses, military limits and reparations, and Article 231 placed war guilt on Germany.
[hist-eur3-s2.0.6] The London Schedule of 1921 set Germany's reparations at 132 billion gold marks, a sum that many Germans regarded as impossible and humiliating.
[hist-eur3-s2.1.b] The republic faced armed challenges from left and right in its first years, and its judges and army officers were often sympathetic to the right.

[hist-eur3-s2.1.1] The Kapp Putsch of March 1920, led by Wolfgang Kapp with the Ehrhardt Freikorps brigade, seized Berlin while the army refused to defend the government; it collapsed after four days when trade unions called a general strike.

[hist-eur3-s2.2.0] By November 1923 one US dollar was worth about 4.2 trillion marks, prices rose by the hour, workers were paid daily or twice daily and rushed to spend their wages, and barter replaced money in many places.

[hist-eur3-s2.2.2] Debtors, speculators and large industrialists such as Hugo Stinnes, who borrowed to buy assets, gained from the inflation, while people on fixed incomes and savers lost most.

[hist-eur3-s2.2.4] The Rentenmark, introduced on 15 November 1923 and backed by a mortgage on land and industry, ended the hyperinflation; Hjalmar Schacht, as currency commissioner and then Reichsbank president, kept its supply tightly limited, and it was replaced by the Reichsmark in 1924.

[hist-eur3-s2.3.0] Hitler joined the small German Workers' Party in Munich in September 1919; it was renamed the National Socialist German Workers' Party (NSDAP) in February 1920 with a 25-point programme, and Hitler became its leader with dictatorial powers in July 1921.

[hist-eur3-s2.9.b] Judgements of the republic divide between blaming its structure and the Treaty of Versailles, and emphasising the impact of the Depression and the choices of its leaders.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.0] Structural view: proportional representation, Article 48 and the lack of a stable parliamentary majority left the republic dependent on emergency government.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.1] Versailles view: contemporaries on the right blamed the treaty for national humiliation, and this shaped the appeal of parties promising revision.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.2] Achievement: Stresemann's policies produced a recovery between 1924 and 1929, with inflation defeated and Germany admitted to the League of Nations.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.3] Limit: that recovery depended heavily on American loans, so the Wall Street Crash exposed its fragility and left no reserves to cushion the unemployment that followed.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.4] Later historians such as Henry Ashby Turner stress that Nazi electoral support was falling by late 1932 and that Hitler's appointment was not inevitable but resulted from decisions by a small group of conservative politicians.
[hist-eur3-s2.9.5] Contemporary left-wing and liberal critics blamed the conservative elite for undermining the republic, while the Nazi party presented itself as the only force able to restore order.

CANDIDATES hist-eur3-s1: 1. Origins and growth of the fascist regimes: Mussolini's Italy
[hist-eur3-s1.0.b] Fascism grew where parliamentary government seemed weak and where war, economic crisis and fear of revolution had left people frightened and angry.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.0] Italy's war effort cost heavy casualties and debt, and the peace settlement gave Italy less than the Treaty of London (1915) had promised, feeding the sense of a 'mutilated victory'.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.1] Italy had five prime ministers between 1919 and 1922 (Orlando, Nitti, Giolitti, Bonomi and Facta), and proportional representation, introduced in 1919, produced weak coalitions dependent on the Socialists and the Catholic Popolari.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.2] The Biennio Rosso (1919-1920) brought strikes, peasant land seizures and factory occupations in the north, leading landowners, industrialists and the middle classes to fear a socialist revolution.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.3] Inflation, unemployment and food shortages added to the anger of ex-servicemen, who believed the state had failed to reward them.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.4] Fascism drew support from ex-servicemen, the lower middle class, large landowners in the Po valley and industrialists who wanted an end to strikes and socialist organisation.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.5] The poet Gabriele D'Annunzio's seizure of Fiume with volunteers (September 1919 to December 1920) showed the weakness of the state and gave Fascism its style: black shirts, balcony speeches, the Roman salute and mass ritual.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.6] Characteristics of fascism: extreme nationalism that places the nation above the individual, with power concentrated in one leader who embodies the nation (the leader principle) and is promoted by a personality cult.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.7] A single party and a totalitarian state: Mussolini's own formula, from a 1925 speech, was 'everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state'.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.8] Fascists were hostile to communism, socialism and liberal democracy, which they blamed for class conflict and national weakness.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.9] Fascism glorified violence, war and youth through uniformed squads and mass rallies, and promised corporatism, in which employers and workers would co-operate in state-controlled corporations instead of class conflict.
[hist-eur3-s1.0.10] Comparison only: other interwar authoritarian regimes included Horthy's Hungary (1920), Primo de Rivera's Spain (1923-30), Piłsudski's Poland (1926), Salazar's Portugal (1932) and Metaxas's Greece (1936); most were conservative military or clerical dictatorships rather than mass fascist movements.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.b] The Fascist movement gained power through organised violence against its opponents, often tolerated by local police and army commanders.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.0] Benito Mussolini, a former editor of the socialist newspaper Avanti!, founded the Fasci di Combattimento in Milan in March 1919 with a programme that mixed radical and nationalist ideas.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.1] The squadristi were armed squads, often led by ex-servicemen, who attacked socialist offices, trade union halls and Catholic People's Party premises, especially in the Po valley and Tuscany.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.2] Fascist candidates won 35 seats in the May 1921 election, when Mussolini himself was elected, and Mussolini founded the National Fascist Party (PNF) that November.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.3] Mussolini signed a Pacification Pact with socialist leaders in August 1921 to end the violence, but local squads ignored it and the pact collapsed.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.4] Liberal leader Giovanni Giolitti included Fascists in his 1921 electoral bloc, underestimating their strength and believing he could control them.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.5] The squads' violence was directed against the organisations that had led the 1919-1920 unrest, so Fascism presented itself as the defender of order against socialism.
[hist-eur3-s1.1.6] By 1922 the Fascists had taken control of many local councils in northern and central Italy, giving them local bases of power independent of Rome.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.b] The March on Rome was a show of force that persuaded the King to hand power to Mussolini rather than declare a state of siege.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.0] Between 27 and 30 October 1922, about 30,000 Fascist squads converged on Rome under four leaders, the quadrumvirs (Balbo, De Bono, De Vecchi and Bianchi), while Mussolini waited in Milan; Prime Minister Luigi Facta asked for a state of siege.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.1] King Victor Emmanuel III refused to sign the state of siege decree, fearing civil war and doubting the army's loyalty; he invited Mussolini to form a government on 29 October and Mussolini took office on 30 October 1922.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.2] Mussolini's first government was a coalition in which Fascists held only a minority of posts alongside Liberals, Popolari and nationalists, and parliament granted it emergency powers for a year in November 1922.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.3] The Acerbo Law of 1923 gave two-thirds of the seats in the Chamber of Deputies to any list winning at least 25 per cent of the vote.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.4] In the April 1924 election the Fascist-led national list won about 65 per cent of the vote, in a campaign marked by intimidation and violence against opponents.
[hist-eur3-s1.2.5] The Fascist Grand Council (December 1922) became the party's governing body and the Fascist militia (MVSN, January 1923) turned the squads into a state-paid force loyal to Mussolini rather than the King.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.b] The murder of a socialist deputy in 1924 threatened Mussolini's government and was followed by the removal of parliamentary and press freedom.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.0] On 30 May 1924 Giacomo Matteotti, a Socialist deputy, denounced electoral fraud and violence in the Chamber of Deputies.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.1] Matteotti was abducted in Rome on 10 June 1924 by men connected to the Fascist squads and was murdered; his body was found in August.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.2] Opposition deputies walked out of the Chamber in what became known as the Aventine Secession, hoping the King would dismiss Mussolini; the King did not act.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.3] On 3 January 1925 Mussolini told the Chamber that he accepted political responsibility for the violence, which marked the start of his open dictatorship.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.4] Between 1925 and 1926 the government banned opposition parties, censored the press, and replaced elected mayors with appointed podesta.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.5] The Leggi Fascistissime of 1925-26 made Mussolini responsible only to the King, let him rule by decree, set up a Special Tribunal for the Defence of the State (1926) and created the OVRA secret police; political prisoners were sent to internal exile (confino) on islands such as Lipari.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.6] Violet Gibson, an Englishwoman, shot Mussolini in Rome on 7 April 1926 and wounded his nose; the attack was used to justify further repression.
[hist-eur3-s1.3.7] A 1928 electoral law let the Grand Council draw up a single list of 400 candidates for voters to accept or reject, and the 1929 plebiscite approved it with about 98 per cent voting yes.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.b] Mussolini aimed to bring the economy under state direction through fascist unions and corporations, but the system remained more theory than practice.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.0] The Rocco Law of 1926 allowed only state-recognised syndicates to represent workers and employers, and it banned strikes and lockouts.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.1] The Charter of Labour of 1927 set out the principles of the corporate system, in which employers and workers would be organised together under the state.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.2] Corporations covering the main economic sectors were set up in 1934, and in 1939 the Chamber of Deputies was replaced by the Chamber of Fasces and Corporations.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.3] The Battle for Grain (from 1925) aimed at self-sufficiency in wheat by raising tariffs and expanding cereal production on poor land.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.4] The Battle for Births, promoted from about 1927, offered rewards for large families and penalised celibacy through taxation.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.5] The Pontine Marshes south of Rome were drained and resettled in the 1930s, a project presented as a triumph of the regime.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.6] Quota 90, announced in Mussolini's Pesaro speech of August 1926 and reached in 1927, revalued the lira to 90 to the pound sterling for national prestige; it made exports dearer, deflated the economy and cut wages.
[hist-eur3-s1.4.7] After the 1929 crash the state rescued banks and industry through the Institute for Industrial Reconstruction (IRI, 1933) which left the state owning a large share of banking, shipbuilding and steel.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.b] The regime used mass organisations, the press and film to create consent and an image of Mussolini as the embodiment of the nation.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.0] The Opera Nazionale Balilla (1926) brought boys and girls into a state youth organisation, which was reorganised as the Gioventu Italiana del Littorio in 1937.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.1] The Dopolavoro (after-work) organisation from 1925 provided sport, excursions and leisure for workers under fascist supervision.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.2] The Istituto Luce, founded in 1924, produced newsreels and documentary films that showed Mussolini at work, at rallies and at public works.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.3] The Ministry of Popular Culture, known as Minculpop, was set up in 1937 to control the press, radio, cinema and theatre.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.4] Mussolini's image as Il Duce was promoted through posters, photographs of him harvesting grain or bare-chested, balcony speeches from the Palazzo Venezia in Rome, and slogans painted on walls such as 'Mussolini is always right' and 'Believe, obey, fight'.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.5] The regime used the Catholic Church and public ritual to build legitimacy, although the Church kept its own youth and press organisations.
[hist-eur3-s1.5.6] Elementary schools used a single state textbook from 1929, and university professors had to swear an oath of loyalty to the regime in 1931; only about 12 of some 1,200 refused.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.b] Mussolini used foreign adventures to build prestige, and his ambitions led Italy into alliance with Germany and into the Second World War.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.0] The Corfu incident of August 1923 began when General Enrico Tellini and members of an Italian boundary commission were murdered on the Greek-Albanian border; Italy bombarded and occupied Corfu until Greece paid compensation.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.1] Italy annexed the port of Fiume in 1924 under a treaty with Yugoslavia, ending a long dispute left over from the peace settlement.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.2] Italy invaded Abyssinia (Ethiopia) on 3 October 1935; the League of Nations imposed sanctions, but they did not stop the invasion or cut off oil supplies.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.3] The Hoare-Laval Plan of December 1935, which would have given Italy much of Abyssinia, was abandoned after public protest in Britain and France.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.4] Addis Ababa was captured on 5 May 1936, and Victor Emmanuel III was declared Emperor of Ethiopia.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.5] Italy and Germany drew together after German support during the Abyssinian war and their joint intervention for Franco in Spain (1936-39); Mussolini announced the Rome-Berlin Axis in a Milan speech on 1 November 1936, joined the Anti-Comintern Pact in 1937 and accepted the Anschluss in 1938.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.6] Mussolini posed as a peacemaker at the Munich Conference (September 1938), invaded Albania in April 1939 and signed the Pact of Steel with Germany on 22 May 1939, but declared Italy non-belligerent when war began in September 1939.
[hist-eur3-s1.6.7] Italy declared war on Britain and France on 10 June 1940, when France was already beaten, but suffered defeats in Greece (from October 1940) and North Africa that made it dependent on German help.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.b] Military defeats and an Allied invasion of Sicily brought the regime down in 1943, but Mussolini's rule in northern Italy continued under German control until 1945.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.0] Allied forces landed in Sicily on 10 July 1943, and at a meeting on 24-25 July the Fascist Grand Council passed Dino Grandi's motion to return command of the armed forces to the King.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.1] King Victor Emmanuel III dismissed Mussolini on 25 July 1943 and had him arrested; Marshal Pietro Badoglio formed a new government.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.2] Italy's armistice with the Allies was announced on 8 September 1943, and German forces took control of much of the north and centre.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.3] German paratroopers and SS commandos, among them Otto Skorzeny, rescued Mussolini from a mountain hotel at Gran Sasso on 12 September 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.4] Mussolini headed the Italian Social Republic, known as the Salò Republic after its seat on Lake Garda, a German puppet state that executed his son-in-law Ciano and other Grand Council members at Verona in January 1944.
[hist-eur3-s1.7.5] Mussolini and his mistress Clara Petacci were shot by partisans near Lake Como on 28 April 1945, and their bodies were displayed in Piazzale Loreto in Milan.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.b] Mussolini led Italy from 1922 to 1943 and was the founder and leader of the Fascist movement.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.0] Born in 1883 at Predappio in the Romagna region, he trained as a teacher, became a socialist journalist, and edited Avanti! from 1912.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.1] He was expelled from the Socialist Party in 1914 for supporting Italian entry into the First World War, and he served in the army and was wounded in 1917.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.2] As founder of the Fasci di Combattimento (1919) and the National Fascist Party (1921), he led the movement that took power in 1922 and ruled as dictator from 1925.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.3] He was a participant who shaped events: he organised the March on Rome from Milan, took personal responsibility for the violence in January 1925 and made the key decisions on Abyssinia, the Axis and entry into the war.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.4] Contemporaries were divided: many conservatives, the Vatican and foreign statesmen admired his suppression of socialism (Churchill praised him on a visit to Rome in 1927), while Socialists, Popolari and liberals such as Giovanni Amendola, who died after a Fascist beating in 1926, denounced his violence.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.5] Historians differ: Renzo De Felice argued that the regime enjoyed real popular consent in the years 1929-1936, while Denis Mack Smith portrayed Mussolini as an opportunist and propagandist more concerned with image than with real achievement.
[hist-eur3-s1.8.6] Many historians now see Italian Fascism as an incomplete totalitarianism, since the King, the Church, the army and big business kept independent power, which is why the King was able to dismiss him in 1943.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.b] Judgements of the regime turn on whether its stability and public works are weighed against its violence, economic failures and disastrous war.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.0] Achievement: Mussolini ended parliamentary rule and established a one-party state by 1926 with little open resistance for most of the 1920s.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.1] Limit: the corporate state existed largely on paper, as employers kept control of production and the corporations had little real power.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.2] Achievement: the Lateran Treaties of 1929 settled the Roman Question, and the Pontine Marshes scheme, the autostrade motorways and rail electrification gave the regime visible successes.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.3] Limit: the Battle for Grain did not make Italy self-sufficient in food, and the deflationary policy of Quota 90 damaged industry and jobs.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.4] Achievement: Italy gained territory in Abyssinia and Fiume, which the regime presented as the fulfilment of national destiny.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.5] Limit: the Abyssinian war was costly, the alliance with Germany drew Italy into a war it was ill-prepared to fight, and the regime collapsed in 1943 after defeats in North Africa and Sicily.
[hist-eur3-s1.9.6] Contemporary view: many Italians and foreign conservatives saw Mussolini as the saviour of order who had defeated Bolshevism; later view: his regime is judged by its violence, its alliance with Hitler, the 1938 racial laws and the defeat it brought on Italy.
