[hist-eur5-s4.1.6] Muslim Algerians who wanted full French citizenship had to give up their Islamic personal status, a condition that few chose to meet before 1954.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.0] The National Liberation Front (FLN) launched a rising on 1 November 1954 with attacks on police and military targets across Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.1] The French government sent conscripts in large numbers, reaching about 400,000 troops at the peak, and the army was given wide powers to restore order in the cities and countryside.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.2] The FLN held the Soummam Valley Congress in August 1956, which created its political leadership and confirmed the primacy of the armed struggle.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.3] The Morice Line, a barrier of wire, mines and electrified fencing along the Tunisian frontier from 1957, was built to stop arms and fighters crossing the border.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.4] The Battle of Algiers of 1957 saw French paratroopers under General Jacques Massu break the FLN's urban network in the city through arrests, interrogation and torture.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.5] The use of torture by French forces was reported in Henri Alleg's book La Question, which was banned in France in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.6] The FLN used terrorism against civilians, including bombings in Algiers, and it killed Algerians who opposed it; the French forces also killed civilians in reprisal operations.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.7] The French army moved large numbers of rural Algerians, probably more than a million, into fenced regroupement camps from 1955 and especially after 1957, as part of its counter-insurgency strategy.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.8] French military deaths numbered about 25,000; estimates of Algerian deaths range from about 300,000 to the Algerian official figure of 1.5 million, and historians still debate the figures.
[hist-eur5-s4.2.9] Support for the FLN abroad grew: Tunisia and Morocco sheltered its fighters, Egypt supplied arms, and the French bombing of the Tunisian village of Sakiet in February 1958 damaged France's reputation with the US and UN.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.b] A revolt by settlers and army officers in Algiers brought Charles de Gaulle back to power.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.0] On 13 May 1958 settlers and army officers seized government buildings in Algiers and demanded that Algeria stay French, and they formed a Committee of Public Safety.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.1] The Fourth Republic's government under Pierre Pflimlin lost the confidence of the army, President Coty asked de Gaulle to form a government, and the National Assembly invested him on 1 June 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.2] A new constitution was approved by referendum on 28 September 1958, establishing the Fifth Republic with a strong presidency.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.3] The constitution set up the French Community, which gave the colonies internal self-government, but kept foreign affairs, defence and currency with France.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.4] De Gaulle was elected president in December 1958 by an electoral college, and he took office in January 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.5] Between 1946 and 1958 the Fourth Republic had some two dozen governments, which made it hard to sustain a consistent policy on Algeria.
[hist-eur5-s4.3.6] Speaking to the crowd in Algiers on 4 June 1958 de Gaulle said 'Je vous ai compris' (I have understood you), and at Mostaganem on 6 June he cried 'Vive l'Algérie française', which led the settlers to believe he would keep Algeria French.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.0] On 16 September 1959 de Gaulle announced that Algerians would be offered self-determination, a choice between independence, integration with France and a form of association.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.1] In January 1960 settler militants staged the Barricades Week in Algiers, a revolt that de Gaulle put down with the army.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.2] The referendum of 8 January 1961 approved self-determination by about 75 per cent of votes cast overall, and by about 70 per cent in Algeria itself.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.3] In April 1961 a group of generals who opposed Algerian independence seized Algiers in the Algiers putsch, which failed after four days when conscripts and the army stayed loyal to de Gaulle.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.4] The Secret Army Organisation (OAS), formed in early 1961, carried out bombings and assassinations in Algeria and in France to stop independence, and it attacked de Gaulle himself.
[hist-eur5-s4.4.5] In a speech of 4 November 1960 de Gaulle spoke of an 'Algérie algérienne' and the prospect of an Algerian Republic, and an earlier set of talks with the FLN at Melun in June 1960 broke down.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.0] The Évian Accords were signed on 18 March 1962 after negotiations between the French government and the FLN, and a ceasefire took effect the next day.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.1] The Accords guaranteed the rights of the French minority, gave France continued use of military bases including Mers-el-Kébir for a time, and provided for French access to Saharan oil and gas.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.2] The Évian Accords were approved in a referendum in France on 8 April 1962 and in Algeria on 1 July 1962, with an overwhelming majority in favour.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.3] Algeria became independent on 5 July 1962, and Ahmed Ben Bella, after a struggle with rival nationalist leaders in the summer of 1962, became prime minister in September 1962 and president in 1963.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.4] The OAS was defeated in Algiers and in France, and its leaders were tried; General Salan, arrested in April 1962, received a life sentence, and a general amnesty in 1968 freed those still in prison.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.5] The OAS ambush of de Gaulle's car at Petit-Clamart on 22 August 1962 failed, and its organiser Jean Bastien-Thiry was executed in March 1963, the last person executed by firing squad in France for a political offence.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.6] On 26 March 1962 French troops fired on pro-French demonstrators in the rue d'Isly in Algiers, killing about 50 people, deepening the settlers' sense of betrayal.
[hist-eur5-s4.5.7] After independence Ben Bella was overthrown by his defence minister Houari Boumediene in June 1965; Algeria nationalised French oil and gas holdings in 1971, and France handed over the naval base of Mers-el-Kébir in 1968.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.0] About 800,000 settlers left Algeria in 1962, many within a few months and most for France, which had to absorb them into an already strained economy; nearly a million had gone by the mid-1960s.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.1] The harkis were Algerians who had served as auxiliaries in the French forces; the French government gave orders to prevent their organised transfer, and while about 90,000 reached France with their families, tens of thousands were killed in reprisals, with estimates ranging widely from about 30,000 to 150,000.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.2] Most harkis who reached France were placed in camps and forest hamlets; the question of recognition and compensation remained a political issue, and President Chirac paid national tribute to them in 2001 and President Macron asked their forgiveness in 2021.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.3] The departure of the settlers ended the French presence in Algerian agriculture and administration, and many farms were taken over by the new state.
[hist-eur5-s4.6.4] On 5 July 1962, the day of independence, violence against Europeans broke out in Oran, where hundreds were killed or went missing, and many settlers fled the city.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.b] De Gaulle led the Free French in the Second World War and then ended the Algerian war as president of the Fifth Republic.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.0] Born 22 November 1890 in Lille, he was a career army officer who became undersecretary for war in June 1940 and then, with his appeal of 18 June 1940 from London, leader of the Free French.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.1] He headed the provisional government from 1944 to January 1946, resigned, founded the Rally of the French People (RPF) in 1947 and withdrew from politics in the 1950s, returning to power in 1958.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.2] As a participant in the 1958 crisis he was welcomed by the settlers, who believed he would keep Algeria French, but his 1959 policy changed course.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.3] Contemporaries split sharply: the settlers and the OAS saw him as a traitor, while many in France and Algeria saw him as the leader who ended a costly war.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.4] Some historians argue that de Gaulle always intended to end French rule in Algeria and moved slowly to avoid civil war, while others argue that he drifted from integration towards independence as the war's costs, the army's attitude and international pressure became clear; he moved from 'Je vous ai compris' in 1958 to self-determination in 1959.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.5] He resigned as president in April 1969 and died on 9 November 1970.
[hist-eur5-s4.7.6] He used the Community and, after 1960, bilateral co-operation accords to keep French influence in sub-Saharan Africa, working through Jacques Foccart, his adviser on African affairs, and the Brazzaville Conference of 1944 that he had convened had promised only reform within the empire.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.0] Achievement: de Gaulle ended a war that the Fourth Republic had failed to win, and he gave Algerians the right of self-determination.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.1] Limit: the war cost many lives, caused the exodus of about a million people and divided the French army and political class.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.2] Achievement: the Fifth Republic's constitution gave France a stable government that could take the decision to withdraw.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.3] Limit: the French Community did not hold the colonies together, and most independent states of West and Equatorial Africa kept close ties with France through co-operation accords, with French troops stationed in several of them.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.4] Explanations for withdrawal: the cost and unpopularity of the Algerian war, the independence of Tunisia and Morocco in 1956, UN General Assembly debates on Algeria from 1955, American pressure and the growth of African nationalist parties.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.5] The British and French cases differed: Britain's transfers were mostly negotiated, while France fought a war in Algeria before accepting independence.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.6] Later view: the war divided the army, the political class and French society, and many judge that de Gaulle's greatest achievement was to end it without a collapse of the Republic.
[hist-eur5-s4.8.7] Contemporary view: settlers, the OAS and the generals of the 1961 putsch saw withdrawal as betrayal, while about 91 per cent of French voters approved the Évian Accords in April 1962.
