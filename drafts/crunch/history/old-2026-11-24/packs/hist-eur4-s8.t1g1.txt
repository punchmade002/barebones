[hist-eur4-s8.0.b] Communist economies were run by central planning, in which the state set production targets and allocated resources, and this model was copied across eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.0] Gosplan, the state planning committee, drew up five-year plans that set output targets for industry, agriculture and trade.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.1] Priority was given to heavy industry, steel, coal, machinery and armaments, while consumer goods and housing were neglected.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.2] Collectivisation of agriculture was carried through in the Soviet Union in the early 1930s, and was imposed on most eastern European farms after 1945, with the exception of Poland, where most farms stayed private.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.3] Comecon, founded in January 1949, coordinated trade and planning among the Soviet bloc, and trade was largely conducted in non-convertible currencies at prices set by planners.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.4] Stalin's five-year plans from 1928 built heavy industry and cities at great human cost, and collectivisation of farms in the early 1930s, together with the famine of 1932–33, killed millions.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.5] In eastern Europe, industry, banks and large estates were nationalised in 1945–48, and Six-Year or Five-Year Plans on the Soviet pattern were introduced from 1949–51, with new steel towns such as Nowa Huta in Poland and Stalinstadt in East Germany.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.6] Comecon was set up in response to the Marshall Plan; in practice it became a system of trade with the Soviet Union, which supplied cheap raw materials and energy and took manufactured goods, so the east European economies became dependent on Soviet supplies.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.7] In 1949–53 eastern Europe's trade shifted from the West to the Soviet Union, and reparations and joint companies in Hungary, Romania and East Germany took resources for the Soviet economy.
[hist-eur4-s8.0.8] Output was driven by pressing workers to exceed their norms, as in the Stakhanovite campaigns, and consumer goods were rationed in the early 1950s.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.b] Central planning produced chronic shortages of goods, poor quality and slow adoption of new technology, which undermined the economies over time.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.0] Planners set targets in gross output terms, which encouraged factories to produce heavy and low-quality goods rather than goods that consumers wanted.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.1] Shortages of consumer goods, meat, housing and cars were common, and long queues and black markets were a permanent feature of daily life.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.2] Innovation was slow because managers had little incentive to adopt new technology, and the state discouraged risk-taking.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.3] The arms race and the cost of maintaining an army and military industries absorbed a large share of national output, especially in the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.4] Soviet agriculture remained unproductive, with a large share of the labour force employed on farms, and the Soviet Union imported grain from the 1960s onwards.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.5] Prices were fixed by the state rather than by supply and demand, so shortages in shops coexisted with surpluses of unwanted goods in warehouses.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.6] Housing was built in standard blocks and was often overcrowded; in East Germany the wait for a car such as the Trabant could be many years.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.7] Environmental damage was severe, as industrial regions such as northern Bohemia, Upper Silesia and the Aral Sea basin were polluted by heavy industry and irrigation.
[hist-eur4-s8.1.8] The economies relied on extensive growth, adding labour and raw materials, and could not move to intensive growth based on technology and productivity once the supply of new workers dried up in the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.b] Several reforms tried to introduce market elements or decentralisation, but most were partial and some were reversed.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.0] Khrushchev's 1957 decentralisation of industry replaced ministries with regional economic councils, a reform that was later reversed.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.1] Alexei Kosygin's reform of September 1965 gave enterprises more control over profits and pricing, but it did not end central control of investment.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.2] Hungary's New Economic Mechanism of 1968 allowed limited market prices and private enterprise in services, and it became a model of partial reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.3] Hungary's policy under János Kádár, later called goulash communism, tolerated small private plots and enterprises and gave citizens more consumer goods than in other bloc states.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.4] Czechoslovakia's New Economic Model of the 1960s, developed by the economist Ota Šik, proposed market mechanisms that the 1968 invasion ended.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.5] Khrushchev tried to lift the production of consumer goods and housing, launched a mass housebuilding programme, and promised to overtake the USA in per capita output, but the targets were not met.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.6] The 1965 Kosygin reforms were abandoned within a few years because ministries and the party feared the loss of control, and the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 discouraged further experiment.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.7] Yugoslavia, outside Comecon after 1948, developed a system of worker self-management and market socialism, and allowed its citizens to travel and work abroad; but it ran up heavy foreign debt and suffered high inflation in the 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s8.2.8] Hungary's reforms gave managers more freedom and allowed a second economy of private work after hours, but the state kept control of the main industries and prices.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.b] Under Leonid Brezhnev from 1964 to 1982 the Soviet economy grew more slowly, and the period became known as the era of stagnation.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.0] Growth in the Soviet economy slowed in the 1970s, as the labour force grew more slowly and the rise in productivity fell.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.1] The Soviet Union earned large hard currency revenues from oil and gas exports after the price rises of 1973 and 1979, which let the leadership buy Western grain and technology and postpone reform; when oil prices fell in the mid-1980s this support vanished.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.2] The Soviet leadership avoided reform, and living standards for most people improved only slowly during the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.3] Under Brezhnev the Soviet Union reached rough nuclear parity with the USA and increased its military spending, which took a large share of national output at the cost of consumer goods and investment.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.4] The war in Afghanistan from 1979 added to the burden, and the system of rewarding party loyalty rather than performance led to corruption and an ageing leadership.
[hist-eur4-s8.3.5] Consumers experienced shortages of basic goods, and the black market, bribery and informal networks (blat) became part of ordinary life.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.b] Poland's attempts to raise living standards by borrowing from the West ended in debt and repeated waves of protest.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.0] Protests in Gdańsk and other Baltic ports in December 1970, against food price rises, led to the fall of Władysław Gomułka and his replacement by Edward Gierek.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.1] Gierek borrowed heavily from Western banks to finance imports of machinery and consumer goods, which were intended to modernise industry and win public support.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.2] The June 1976 price rises led to strikes and protests, and the government withdrew most of the increases within days.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.3] By 1980 Poland's foreign debt had become unsustainable, and the strikes of that summer at the Gdańsk shipyard led to the founding of Solidarity.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.4] Poland's Western debt rose to more than 20 billion dollars by 1980, and a large share of its export earnings went on servicing it.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.5] Price rises announced on 1 July 1980 sparked strikes that spread to Gdańsk in August; the economy had shrunk and shops were bare of meat.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.6] Gierek's policy of importing Western technology failed because the new factories produced goods that were not competitive in Western markets, and agricultural output suffered from a lack of investment.
[hist-eur4-s8.4.7] Poland's economic crisis was a cause of Solidarity, and the economic collapse in the 1980s helped to bring the communists to the Round Table talks of 1989.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.b] By the late 1980s the economic gap between the communist states and the West had widened, and the reform attempts came too late.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.0] Mikhail Gorbachev's perestroika aimed to restructure the economy, but it coincided with falling oil prices in 1986, which cut the Soviet Union's hard currency earnings.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.1] Romania under Nicolae Ceaușescu repaid its foreign debt in the late 1980s by cutting imports and imposing severe austerity, and the population suffered shortages of food, heating and electricity.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.2] East Germany was the most productive economy of the bloc, but it too depended on West German credit and trade concessions by the late 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.3] The end of the Soviet bloc's economic system left a legacy of obsolete industry, environmental damage and unemployment that the successor states had to address.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.4] Gorbachev's economic reforms included the Law on State Enterprise (1987), which gave factories more freedom, and the 1988 law on cooperatives, which allowed private business; they weakened central control without creating a functioning market.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.5] By 1989–90 the Soviet economy was suffering from shortages, a growing budget deficit, rationing in some cities and falling output.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.6] The gap with the West was visible by 1989: East German and Czechoslovak citizens could see how much better West Germans and Austrians lived on television and through travel, and living standards in the Soviet Union lagged far behind.
[hist-eur4-s8.5.7] East Germany's economy had been propped up by loans from West Germany, including a billion-mark credit in 1983, and the German monetary union of July 1990 exposed its industries to competition and led to mass closures.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.b] The bloc was not uniform, and examiners reward comparison of different states.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.0] Czechoslovakia had been the most industrialised state in the region before the war, and its living standards were the highest in the bloc in the 1950s, but it stagnated under rigid planning in the 1960s and was the home of Ota Šik's reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.1] East Germany was the most advanced economy in the bloc and the showcase of socialism, but it lost skilled workers to the West until the Wall was built in 1961.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.2] Bulgaria and Romania were still largely agricultural in 1945 and were industrialised rapidly, Romania developing heavy industry, oil and chemicals and pursuing an independent foreign policy under Ceaușescu.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.3] Ceaușescu's Romania exported food and fuel to pay its debts, rationed food and electricity in the 1980s, and planned to demolish thousands of villages in his systematisation programme.
[hist-eur4-s8.6.4] Hungary's partial reforms and Poland's private farming made those states more flexible than the Soviet Union, but their dependence on Western credit made them vulnerable when interest rates and prices rose around 1980.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.b] Communist economies achieved rapid industrialisation and social welfare, but they failed to sustain growth or satisfy consumers.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.0] Achievements: rapid industrialisation, near-universal literacy, free health care and education, and full employment, including high employment of women.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.1] Limits: chronic shortages, low quality, poor housing, environmental damage and a standard of living that fell further behind the West.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.2] Contemporary Western accounts often stressed the failure of the planned economy, while Soviet and bloc officials emphasised the achievements of industrialisation and social welfare.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.3] Later historians stress both the system's real successes in mobilising resources and the structural limits that made it impossible to sustain growth without political reform.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.4] Eastern Europe's communist states did expand education, cheap housing and health care, but their living standards fell behind West European levels by the 1970s.
[hist-eur4-s8.7.5] The final judgement of historians is that the planned economy could build basic industry, but could not adapt to the information-based economies of the 1980s.

[hist-eur4-s2.2.b] The crises before Khrushchev set the pattern of confrontation without direct war.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.0] Berlin Blockade (1948–49): the first major crisis, ended by the airlift and followed by the founding of NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.1] Korean War (1950–53): the North Korean invasion of the South in June 1950 persuaded Western governments that communism was expansionist, and led to the rearmament of NATO, the creation of a unified NATO command under Eisenhower and the push to rearm West Germany.
[hist-eur4-s2.2.2] Stalin's death on 5 March 1953 opened a more flexible phase; an armistice in Korea followed in July 1953.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.b] Berlin was the most dangerous flashpoint in Europe, because West Berlin was an enclave inside East Germany and a window to the West.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.0] On 27 November 1958 Khrushchev issued an ultimatum giving the Western powers six months to agree to a settlement that would end their occupation of West Berlin.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.1] Khrushchev's visit to the USA in September 1959, including talks with Eisenhower at Camp David, produced a brief thaw that the U-2 incident later ended.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.2] The Paris summit of May 1960 collapsed after the U-2 spy plane piloted by Francis Gary Powers was shot down over the Soviet Union on 1 May 1960.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.3] At the Vienna summit in June 1961 Khrushchev pressed the new US president John F. Kennedy on Berlin, and the crisis deepened over the summer.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.4] East German forces began building the Berlin Wall on 13 August 1961, closing the border between the two halves of the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.5] The Western powers did not use force against the Wall, accepting it as the price of avoiding war; Kennedy is reported to have said that a wall was better than a war.
[hist-eur4-s2.3.6] Between 1961 and 1989 the Wall and the border fortifications around it were guarded, and people who tried to cross were shot or arrested.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.b] The Cuban Missile Crisis was the closest the Cold War came to nuclear war, and it was resolved by a compromise that Europeans watched closely.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.0] In October 1962 US reconnaissance photographs showed Soviet medium-range missiles being installed in Cuba, within range of much of the USA.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.1] President Kennedy announced a naval quarantine of Cuba on 22 October 1962 and demanded the removal of the missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.2] For about a week the two superpowers were on the brink of war; Kennedy sent former secretary of state Dean Acheson to brief the French president de Gaulle, who gave firm support, while most European governments learned of the crisis only as it unfolded.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.3] On 28 October 1962 Khrushchev agreed to withdraw the missiles in return for a US pledge not to invade Cuba.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.4] The secret part of the deal was a US promise to remove its Jupiter missiles from Turkey, which was not made public at the time.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.5] The crisis shaped later arms control: a Moscow–Washington hotline was set up in 1963, and the Partial Test Ban Treaty followed in August 1963.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.6] Khrushchev's motives were to defend Castro's Cuba after the Bay of Pigs invasion of 1961, to offset US missiles in Turkey and Italy, and to narrow the Soviet Union's weakness in long-range missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.7] The crisis began on 16 October 1962 when Kennedy was shown U-2 photographs; Kennedy's advisers (ExComm) weighed an air strike against a naval blockade and chose the blockade, described publicly as a quarantine.
[hist-eur4-s2.4.8] Seen from Europe, the crisis showed that Berlin and Cuba were linked, and made West European leaders more aware that decisions on nuclear war would be taken in Washington and Moscow.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.b] Each superpower relied on nuclear weapons to deter attack, and limited agreements grew out of the fear that deterrence could fail.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.0] Nuclear deterrence meant that each side kept enough weapons to destroy the other after a first strike, a condition later called mutually assured destruction.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.1] The Soviet Union tested the 50-megaton Tsar Bomba on 30 October 1961, the largest nuclear explosion ever set off.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.2] The Partial Test Ban Treaty, signed in Moscow on 5 August 1963 by the USA, Britain and the Soviet Union, banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in space and under water.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.3] The Non-Proliferation Treaty of 1968 committed non-nuclear states not to acquire nuclear weapons, while the nuclear powers agreed to pursue disarmament.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.4] Western Europe depended on the US nuclear umbrella under NATO, and debates in France and Britain over independent deterrents reflected doubts about that protection.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.5] France tested its first atomic bomb in 1960 and developed its own deterrent, which it kept outside NATO's military command from 1966.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.6] The ideological dispute with Mao's China grew from the late 1950s and became open in 1960, when Soviet advisers were withdrawn from China.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.7] The Sino-Soviet split meant the Soviet Union faced two rival communist powers and could not count on Chinese support in Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.8] Albania sided with China and in 1968 became the only member state to leave the Warsaw Pact.
[hist-eur4-s2.5.9] Soviet leaders regarded the Berlin Wall as a means of stabilising the German Democratic Republic, which had lost about 2.7 million people to the West since 1945.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.b] Détente was a period of easing tension, from about 1969 to the late 1970s, in which the superpowers and European states signed agreements on arms and borders.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.0] Willy Brandt's Ostpolitik sought better relations with Eastern Europe; the Treaty of Moscow of 12 August 1970 renounced the use of force between the Federal Republic and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.1] The Four Power Agreement on Berlin of 3 September 1971 guaranteed access between West Berlin and West Germany and confirmed the Western presence in the city.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.2] The Basic Treaty of 1972 established relations between the Federal Republic and the German Democratic Republic, which led to both joining the United Nations in 1973.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.3] SALT I, signed on 26 May 1972, limited anti-ballistic missile systems and placed interim limits on strategic offensive missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.4] The Conference on Security and Co-operation in Europe produced the Helsinki Final Act on 1 August 1975, signed by 35 states including the USA, Canada and the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s2.6.5] The Final Act recognised existing borders in Europe and committed signatories to respect human rights, a commitment that later dissidents used to press their governments.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.b] Détente broke down in the late 1970s, and a new arms race in Europe led to the deployment of intermediate-range missiles in the early 1980s.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.0] On 24 December 1979 Soviet forces invaded Afghanistan, and the USA responded with a grain embargo and a boycott of the Moscow Olympics in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.1] NATO's double-track decision of 12 December 1979 agreed to deploy Pershing II and cruise missiles in Western Europe unless arms talks with the Soviet Union succeeded.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.2] The Soviet Union had deployed SS-20 intermediate-range missiles in the late 1970s, which threatened Western Europe.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.3] Large peace demonstrations took place in West Germany, Britain and the Netherlands in 1981–83 against the deployment of the new missiles.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.4] The Soviet leadership's fear of a Western first strike led to a serious misreading during the NATO exercise Able Archer 83 in November 1983.
[hist-eur4-s2.7.5] The crisis was defused by arms negotiations, and the Intermediate-Range Nuclear Forces Treaty was signed in December 1987 under Gorbachev.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.b] Examiners ask which events changed the course of the Cold War, so know the order and what each changed.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.0] Berlin Blockade, 1948–49: confirmed the division of Germany and led to NATO.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.1] Hungary, 1956: showed that the Soviet Union would use force to keep its bloc together.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.2] Berlin Wall, 1961 and Cuba, 1962: the high points of confrontation, after which both sides sought safeguards such as the hotline and the Test Ban Treaty.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.3] Prague Spring, 1968: the invasion of Czechoslovakia confirmed the Brezhnev Doctrine but did not end détente.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.4] Helsinki, 1975: the high point of détente, which gave dissidents in the East a human rights standard.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.5] Afghanistan and the Euromissiles, 1979: ended détente and began the second Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.6] Reagan's election in 1980 brought a hard line: in March 1983 he called the Soviet Union an evil empire, and announced the Strategic Defense Initiative.
[hist-eur4-s2.8.7] Gorbachev from 1985, the Reykjavik summit of October 1986 and the INF Treaty of 1987 reversed the arms race, and 1989 ended Soviet control of eastern Europe (see the notes on reform movements and the collapse of communism).
[hist-eur4-s2.9.b] Khrushchev's record is judged by contrasting his bold moves with the setbacks that ended his rule.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.0] Achievements: de-Stalinisation, the Partial Test Ban Treaty, the hotline, the Austrian State Treaty of 1955 and the space successes.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.1] Failures: the Virgin Lands harvest crises, the reversal of his administrative reforms, and the Cuban settlement, which Soviet leaders saw as a retreat.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.2] Contemporary Western views often focused on his unpredictability, while his Soviet colleagues attacked his rash 'hare-brained schemes' and his boasting.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.3] Later historians have judged him as a reformer who opened the system but never built a lasting alternative to Stalinist planning.
[hist-eur4-s2.9.4] The Berlin Wall, which his policy helped to produce, was for many observers the most lasting symbol of his era, and later assessments weigh it heavily against his reform record.

CANDIDATES hist-eur4-s4: 4. Reform movements in Eastern Europe, the collapse of the Soviet Union, and fragmentation and realignment, 1953–1992
[hist-eur4-s4.0.b] Gorbachev was the Soviet leader whose reforms opened the way to the end of communist rule in eastern Europe and to the break-up of the Soviet Union.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.0] Born in 1931 in Privolnoye in the Stavropol region, he studied law at Moscow State University, rose through the Communist Party in Stavropol, and became a full member of the Politburo in 1980.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.1] He became General Secretary of the Communist Party on 11 March 1985, and in 1988 he became chairman of the Supreme Soviet, and in 1990 the first executive president of the USSR.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.2] He pursued glasnost, which meant greater openness and criticism of the past, and perestroika, a restructuring of the economy that tried to combine market elements with planning.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.3] He was a participant who chose not to use force in eastern Europe, and he withdrew Soviet troops from Afghanistan by February 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.4] Contemporaries in the West welcomed him: Margaret Thatcher, before he came to power, judged that he was a man she could do business with, and Reagan negotiated arms agreements with him.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.5] Conservatives at home regarded him as having destroyed the Soviet system, while many Russians later blamed him for economic collapse.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.6] Historians differ on whether he intended to end communism or to save it, and most agree that his reforms had consequences he did not foresee.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.7] Gorbachev rose under the patronage of Yuri Andropov, and as General Secretary he drew on a generation of younger reformers, including Alexander Yakovlev and Eduard Shevardnadze (foreign minister from 1985).
[hist-eur4-s4.0.8] His early slogan was uskorenie, acceleration of economic growth; when it failed he moved to perestroika and, at the 27th Party Congress in February 1986, to openness.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.9] Democratisation: in 1989 the first partly free elections to the Congress of People's Deputies were held in March, and in 1990 the party gave up its constitutional monopoly of power.
[hist-eur4-s4.0.10] He received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1990 for his role in ending the Cold War.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.b] Karol Wojtyła, elected pope in 1978, supported the Polish opposition to communism and became a political figure as well as a religious leader.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.0] Born in Wadowice in 1920, he was Archbishop of Kraków and became a cardinal in 1967 before being elected pope on 16 October 1978, the first non-Italian pope since 1523.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.1] His visit to Poland from 2 to 10 June 1979 drew very large crowds, and it encouraged Poles to organise independently of the state.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.2] He met Lech Wałęsa during his 1983 visit to Poland and gave moral support to Solidarity, which the Polish authorities tried to suppress.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.3] He was shot in St Peter's Square on 13 May 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca, and he attributed his survival to divine intervention.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.4] Contemporaries in the Polish and Soviet governments saw him as a threat, and the Polish state security services monitored the Church closely.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.5] Historians disagree about how far he caused the 1989 revolutions; many see him as a moral leader who strengthened Polish civil society, while others stress the economic and political factors that made communism unsustainable.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.6] During the 1979 visit he spoke in Warsaw's Victory Square in a Mass attended by hundreds of thousands, and many Poles took his call for the renewal of their land as encouragement to demand change.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.7] Gdańsk strikers in August 1980 displayed the pope's portrait at the shipyard gate, and many Poles saw his election and visit as having given them the confidence to organise.
[hist-eur4-s4.1.8] After martial law he visited Poland in 1983 and 1987 and used sermons to defend workers' rights without calling for revolt; in 1989 he received Gorbachev in the Vatican on 1 December.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.b] Reform in eastern Europe repeatedly appeared and was suppressed, which showed the limits of Soviet tolerance.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.0] East German workers rose on 17 June 1953 against increased work norms and shortages, and Soviet tanks suppressed the uprising in East Berlin and other towns; it spread to hundreds of towns before it was crushed.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.1] Poznań workers protested in June 1956, and Władysław Gomułka returned to power in October 1956 after a confrontation with Moscow that was resolved without Soviet intervention.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.2] Poland's October 1956 settlement allowed limited autonomy for the Church and for intellectuals, but the Polish party maintained one-party rule.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.3] Czechoslovakia's Alexander Dubček became First Secretary of the Communist Party in January 1968, and he launched the Prague Spring reforms.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.4] The Soviet response in each case combined concession and threat: Poland in 1956 won a new leadership, Hungary in 1956 was crushed, and Czechoslovakia in 1968 was invaded.
[hist-eur4-s4.2.5] Poland's Gomułka ended forced collectivisation in 1956 and released Cardinal Wyszyński from internment, but by the 1960s he had reimposed tight controls.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.b] The Prague Spring of 1968 tried to create socialism with a human face, and Soviet and Warsaw Pact forces ended it by force.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.0] The Action Programme of April 1968 proposed freedom of expression, a looser press, the rehabilitation of victims of the 1950s and limits on the secret police.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.1] Dubček's government also planned economic reforms that allowed more market mechanisms, and it promised a federal settlement between Czechs and Slovaks.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.2] On 20–21 August 1968 Warsaw Pact forces from the Soviet Union, Poland, Hungary and Bulgaria invaded Czechoslovakia (East German units were mobilised but did not cross), and Dubček was arrested and taken to Moscow; Romania refused to take part.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.3] Albania withdrew from the Warsaw Pact in protest against the invasion.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.4] The Brezhnev Doctrine, set out in a Pravda article in September 1968 and in Brezhnev's speech to the Polish party congress that November, held that socialist states had a duty to intervene when socialism was threatened in another socialist state.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.5] Gustáv Husák replaced Dubček and presided over a period of normalisation, in which many reformers were removed from office and purged from the party.
[hist-eur4-s4.3.6] Charter 77, a document of Czechoslovak dissidents issued in January 1977 and led by Václav Havel, demanded that the state observe the human rights it had accepted.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.b] Solidarity was the first independent trade union in the Soviet bloc, and its rise forced the communist state to recognise an opposition.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.0] Strikes at the Lenin Shipyard in Gdańsk in August 1980 led to the Gdańsk Agreement of 31 August 1980, which recognised the right to form independent trade unions.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.1] Solidarity was led by Lech Wałęsa, an electrician at the shipyard, and at its peak in 1981 it had about ten million members.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.2] Its demands included free trade unions, the right to strike, better food supplies and access to the media.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.3] On 13 December 1981 General Wojciech Jaruzelski declared martial law, arrested Solidarity leaders and banned the union.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.4] The Polish authorities argued that martial law prevented a Soviet invasion; historians accept that Moscow had pressed for firm action but that Jaruzelski also acted to keep communist control, and Soviet archives show the Soviet leaders did not plan to send troops in.
[hist-eur4-s4.4.5] Solidarity was legalised again in April 1989 after the Round Table talks between the government and the opposition from February to April 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.b] In 1989 communist rule collapsed across eastern Europe in a series of peaceful transfers and one violent revolution, largely without Soviet intervention.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.0] In Poland, Solidarity won almost all of the seats it contested in the June 1989 elections, and in August 1989 Tadeusz Mazowiecki became prime minister of the first non-communist government in the Soviet bloc since 1948.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.1] In Hungary the government began dismantling the border fence with Austria in May 1989, allowed East Germans to cross to Austria from September 1989, and Imre Nagy was reburied as a national hero in June 1989, while in the autumn the party accepted multi-party elections.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.2] East German citizens fled through Hungary and Czechoslovakia, and mass demonstrations in Leipzig and East Berlin forced Erich Honecker's resignation on 18 October 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.3] The Berlin Wall was opened on 9 November 1989 after an announcement that was misread as permitting unrestricted crossings.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.4] In Czechoslovakia the Velvet Revolution began with a student demonstration on 17 November 1989, and Václav Havel was elected president on 29 December 1989.
[hist-eur4-s4.5.5] In Bulgaria Todor Zhivkov was removed on 10 November 1989, and in Romania Nicolae Ceaușescu was executed on 25 December 1989 after a violent uprising.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.b] Gorbachev's reforms at home and his non-intervention abroad removed the props that had kept communist states in power.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.0] Chernobyl exploded on 26 April 1986 in Soviet Ukraine, and the official response was criticised for concealment, which strengthened the case for glasnost.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.1] The INF Treaty of 8 December 1987 eliminated US and Soviet intermediate-range missiles, a landmark in arms control.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.2] Gennady Gerasimov's remark in October 1989 that the Brezhnev Doctrine had been replaced by the Sinatra Doctrine meant that Eastern European states could choose their own path.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.3] Lithuania declared independence on 11 March 1990, and other Soviet republics followed in demanding sovereignty.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.4] The Warsaw Pact was dissolved on 1 July 1991 and Comecon was dissolved in June 1991.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.5] A conservative coup against Gorbachev on 19–21 August 1991 failed after Boris Yeltsin rallied opposition in Moscow, and it accelerated the break-up of the union.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.6] The Soviet Union was dissolved on 26 December 1991, a day after Gorbachev resigned as its president.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.7] Gorbachev and Reagan met at Geneva (November 1985) and Reykjavik (October 1986), where they discussed deep cuts in nuclear weapons, and signed the INF Treaty at Washington in December 1987.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.8] Gorbachev told the United Nations in December 1988 that the Soviet Union would cut its armed forces by 500,000 men and withdraw divisions from eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.9] Boris Yeltsin was elected president of the Russian republic in June 1991 and became the main rival to Gorbachev's authority.
[hist-eur4-s4.6.10] The eight leaders who staged the coup in August 1991 included the vice-president, the head of the KGB and the defence minister; it left Gorbachev weakened and the republics moved to leave the union.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.b] The end of communism led to the reunification of Germany, the break-up of Yugoslavia and the new alignments of central Europe.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.0] The German Democratic Republic and the Federal Republic were unified on 3 October 1990, under the Two Plus Four settlement signed in September 1990.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.1] The Visegrád group of Poland, Czechoslovakia and Hungary was formed in February 1991 to coordinate their approach to integration with the West; its members later joined NATO (1999 for Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic) and the EU (2004).
[hist-eur4-s4.7.2] Slovenia and Croatia declared independence on 25 June 1991, and fighting followed in Slovenia and Croatia, while Bosnia and Herzegovina's independence led to war from 1992.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.3] Czechoslovakia divided peacefully into the Czech Republic and Slovakia on 1 January 1993, after the elections of 1992.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.4] The Baltic states of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania regained independence in 1991 and sought membership of Western institutions.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.5] Germany: the chancellor Helmut Kohl pushed for rapid unity after the Wall fell; a currency union came into force on 1 July 1990, and the Two Plus Four Treaty let a united Germany remain in NATO.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.6] Yugoslavia: after Tito's death in 1980 a federal presidency governed; from 1991 Serbia under Slobodan Milošević opposed the secession of Slovenia and Croatia, and the war in Bosnia (1992–95) was the most savage conflict in Europe since 1945.
[hist-eur4-s4.7.7] The European Community recognised Slovenia and Croatia in January 1992, and the Baltic states were admitted to the United Nations in September 1991, as Western institutions began to expand eastwards.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.b] Historians explain the collapse through economic failure, the loss of legitimacy, and the decision not to use force.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.0] Achievements of Gorbachev: arms control, the withdrawal from Afghanistan, freedom of speech and the peaceful transfer of power across eastern Europe.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.1] Failures of Gorbachev: a worsening economy, the rise of nationalism in the republics and the failure to reform the party's monopoly fast enough.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.2] Some historians stress the structural weakness of the command economy and the cost of the arms race, which the West pressed on through defence spending.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.3] Others stress the agency of opposition movements such as Solidarity and Charter 77, which kept reform alive through repression.
[hist-eur4-s4.8.4] Contemporary Western opinion often credited Reagan's firm policy, while later historians give more weight to the Soviet system's internal crisis.
