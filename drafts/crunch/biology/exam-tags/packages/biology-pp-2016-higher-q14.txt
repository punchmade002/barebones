CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio17-s3: Mutations
[bio17-s3.0.b] DNA is copied every time a cell divides, and the copying is not perfect. A mutation is what is left when a change in the DNA is not put right, and it matters because it is the raw material for new traits.
[bio17-s3.0.0] A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA: either in the base sequence of a gene, called a point or gene mutation, or in the structure of a chromosome, called a chromosomal mutation.
[bio17-s3.0.1] A mutation that changes a gene produces a new allele (a different version of that gene).
[bio17-s3.0.2] Mutations arise spontaneously, mainly as uncorrected errors in base pairing during DNA replication (the copying of DNA before a cell divides), when a wrong base is paired and nobody fixes it.
[bio17-s3.0.3] Mutagens (agents that increase the rate of mutation) include ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive substances) and ultraviolet light, which damage DNA directly.
[bio17-s3.0.4] Other mutagens are chemicals such as those in tobacco smoke, and some viruses.
[bio17-s3.0.5] Repair enzymes correct most damage, so only the errors that escape repair become permanent mutations. There is more on mutagens and cancer in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.b] Whether a mutation matters to a family depends on which cell it happens in. What it does to the organism depends on whether it changes a protein, and for evolution it matters because mutation creates new alleles.
[bio17-s3.1.0] Body-cell (somatic) mutations affect only the individual and the cells descended from the mutated cell, so they are not inherited; they can lead to cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.1] Mutations in cells that form gametes (germ-line mutations) can be passed to offspring, and are then present in every cell of the child because every cell grows from the fertilised egg.
[bio17-s3.1.2] Most mutations are neutral, either because they fall in non-coding DNA (DNA that does not code for a protein) or because they are silent, meaning the protein is not changed.
[bio17-s3.1.3] Many mutations are harmful, because they change or stop a protein.
[bio17-s3.1.4] A few mutations are beneficial in a particular environment, for example lactase persistence (being able to digest milk sugar as an adult).
[bio17-s3.1.5] Mutations giving antibiotic resistance help bacteria survive antibiotics, so they are beneficial to the bacteria in the presence of the drug.
[bio17-s3.1.6] Mutation is the only source of new alleles, and so the ultimate source of variation for natural selection to act on. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio17-s3.2.b] A point mutation is a tiny change at one spot in a gene. Because the genetic code is read in codons (groups of three bases, each coding for one amino acid), the effect depends on exactly what changes. The simplest case is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.2.0] A point mutation is a change in the nucleotide sequence at one point in a gene, usually of one or a few base pairs. This includes small deletions and insertions, not only substitutions.
[bio17-s3.2.1] It occurs when a wrong nucleotide pairs during replication and is not corrected, or when a nucleotide is added or missed out.
[bio17-s3.2.2] At the next replication the change is copied into both strands, so it becomes permanent.
[bio17-s3.2.3] In a substitution one base is replaced by another, so only one codon changes.
[bio17-s3.2.4] Silent substitution: the new codon codes for the same amino acid, because the code is degenerate (several codons can code for the same amino acid), so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.2.5] Missense substitution: the new codon codes for a different amino acid, which may or may not change the protein's shape and function.
[bio17-s3.2.6] Nonsense substitution: the codon becomes a stop codon (a signal to end translation), so the polypeptide is cut short and usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.b] Adding or losing bases is more dangerous than swapping one, because it can shift the way the whole rest of the gene is read.
[bio17-s3.3.0] Insertion or deletion of one base, or of any number of bases that is not a multiple of three, is a frameshift: every codon after the change is read differently, because the code is read in threes without gaps from the start codon.
[bio17-s3.3.1] After a frameshift many amino acids are wrong, and a stop codon often appears early or is lost, so the protein usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.2] A frameshift near the start of a gene is more damaging than one near the end, because more of the protein is changed.
[bio17-s3.3.3] Insertion or deletion of three bases adds or removes one amino acid without a frameshift, so the rest of the protein is normal, but even one missing amino acid can change the folding, as in cystic fibrosis.
[bio17-s3.4.b] To model a mutation, write the mRNA in codons, apply the change, then read each new codon with the codon table. Start with the normal mRNA AUG UUU GGC AAA UAA, which codes for methionine, phenylalanine, glycine and lysine, then stops.
[bio17-s3.4.0] Substitution, silent: GGC changed to GGA; still glycine, so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.4.1] Substitution, missense: UUU changed to UCU; serine instead of phenylalanine.
[bio17-s3.4.2] Substitution, nonsense: AAA changed to UAA, a stop codon; the chain ends after glycine.
[bio17-s3.4.3] Insertion of G after AUG: AUG GUU UGG CAA AUA A… gives methionine, valine, tryptophan, glutamine, isoleucine…; every amino acid after the start is changed and the original stop codon is lost from the reading frame.
[bio17-s3.4.4] Deletion of the first U of UUU: AUG UUG GCA AAU AA… gives methionine, leucine, alanine, asparagine…; this is a frameshift, because the groups of three have moved along.
[bio17-s3.4.5] Deletion of the whole codon UUU: AUG GGC AAA UAA gives methionine, glycine, lysine; one amino acid is missing and the rest is unchanged, so there is no frameshift.
[bio17-s3.4.6] Word analogy for revision: THE CAT ATE THE RAT; substitution THE CAT ATE THE HAT changes one word; insertion THE CXA TAT ETH ERA T gives nonsense words after the change; deletion THE ATA TET HER AT shifts every word after it.
[bio17-s3.5.b] Sickle-cell anaemia shows how a change in a single base can alter a protein and so a whole disease. It is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.5.0] Sickle-cell anaemia is a substitution in the gene for beta-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin, on chromosome 11.
[bio17-s3.5.1] One base change alters the codon for the sixth amino acid from GAG to GUG in mRNA (CTC to CAC in the DNA strand being read), so valine replaces glutamic acid.
[bio17-s3.5.2] The changed haemoglobin, haemoglobin S, molecules stick together into long fibres when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a rigid sickle shape.
[bio17-s3.5.3] Sickled cells block small blood vessels, causing pain and organ damage, and are destroyed quickly, causing anaemia.
[bio17-s3.5.4] Sickle-cell is recessive: people with two copies have the disease; people with one copy (sickle-cell trait) are usually healthy and are more resistant to malaria.
[bio17-s3.5.5] This is why the sickle-cell allele is common in parts of Africa where malaria is common, an example of natural selection.
[bio17-s3.6.b] Cystic fibrosis is the example of a point mutation that is a small deletion. It shows that losing even one amino acid can ruin a protein.
[bio17-s3.6.0] Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7, which codes for a chloride-ion channel protein in cell membranes.
[bio17-s3.6.1] The most common cystic fibrosis mutation, F508del (delta F508), is a deletion of three bases, which removes one amino acid, phenylalanine, at position 508 of the protein.
[bio17-s3.6.2] The faulty protein folds wrongly, is broken down by the cell and does not reach the membrane, giving thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas.
[bio17-s3.6.3] Cystic fibrosis is recessive, and Ireland has the highest rate of cystic fibrosis in the world.
[bio17-s3.6.4] Newer CFTR modulator drugs help the faulty protein fold and work, which shows how knowing the mutation leads to treatment.
[bio17-s3.6.5] Cystic fibrosis is one of the conditions tested for in the newborn heel-prick screening offered to every baby in Ireland. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio17-s3.7.b] A chromosomal mutation changes a large piece of a chromosome, so many genes are affected at once, unlike a point mutation. A letter model makes the four types easy to follow.
[bio17-s3.7.0] A chromosomal mutation is a change in the structure of one or more chromosomes that affects many genes at once.
[bio17-s3.7.1] It occurs when chromosomes break, for example from ionising radiation or chemicals, or from errors when chromosomes are copied or separated in meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes), and the broken pieces are lost or rejoin in the wrong place.
[bio17-s3.7.2] Large changes can be seen under the light microscope in a karyotype (a picture of a cell's chromosomes).
[bio17-s3.7.3] Model: write a chromosome as A B C • D E F G H, where the letters are genes or groups of genes and • is the centromere (the constricted point where the two halves of a chromosome are joined).
[bio17-s3.7.4] A different, non-homologous chromosome (one that is not its partner in a pair) is written M N O • P Q R.
[bio17-s3.8.b] Two of the four types change how many copies of a section there are, one by losing it and one by repeating it.
[bio17-s3.8.0] Deletion: a section breaks off and is lost, so its genes are missing: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E H, with F and G lost.
[bio17-s3.8.1] Deletion is usually harmful, because a whole set of genes is missing.
[bio17-s3.8.2] Duplication: a section is copied twice, so its genes are repeated: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E F E F G H.
[bio17-s3.8.3] Extra copies can upset the balance of gene products (the proteins the genes make); over evolutionary time, duplicated genes can mutate to take on new functions.
[bio17-s3.9.b] The other two types move genes without necessarily losing any. An inversion turns a section around in the same chromosome; a translocation moves a section to another chromosome.
[bio17-s3.9.0] Inversion: a section breaks out, turns through 180 degrees and rejoins, so its genes are in reverse order: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D G F E H.
[bio17-s3.9.1] No genes are lost, so an inversion often has no effect on the person, unless a break falls inside a gene; it can cause problems in meiosis, leading to gametes with missing or extra genes.
[bio17-s3.9.2] Translocation: a section of one chromosome breaks off and joins a different, non-homologous chromosome; in a reciprocal translocation two chromosomes swap sections.
[bio17-s3.9.3] In a reciprocal translocation, A B C • D E F G H and M N O • P Q R become A B C • D E F Q R and M N O • P G H.
[bio17-s3.9.4] If no genes are lost the person may be healthy, but a break can join two genes into a new hybrid (fusion) gene, and carriers can produce gametes with unbalanced sets of genes.
[bio17-s3.9.5] Diagram: each of the four mutations drawn as a chromosome with lettered bands and a centromere, before and after, with the translocation shown between two chromosomes of different sizes.
[bio17-s3.10.b] Each structural type has a real disorder that illustrates it.
[bio17-s3.10.0] Deletion: cri du chat syndrome (French for “cry of the cat”) is caused by deletion of part of the short arm of chromosome 5.
[bio17-s3.10.1] Babies with cri du chat have a high-pitched cry like a cat's, a small head and low birth weight, and later intellectual disability and delayed development.
[bio17-s3.10.2] Duplication: Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A is caused by duplication of a small section of chromosome 17 containing the PMP22 gene.
[bio17-s3.10.3] The extra copy disrupts the insulating myelin sheath of nerves in the limbs, causing gradual weakness of the muscles of the feet and lower legs.
[bio17-s3.10.4] Inversion: an inversion within the gene for blood-clotting factor VIII on the X chromosome breaks the gene and causes nearly half of all cases of severe haemophilia A.
[bio17-s3.10.5] By contrast, a common inversion in chromosome 9 usually has no effect, because no genes are broken. There is more on haemophilia as a sex-linked trait in Sex-linked traits.
[bio17-s3.11.b] The Philadelphia chromosome shows how swapping pieces between chromosomes can create a gene that causes cancer.
[bio17-s3.11.0] The Philadelphia chromosome is found in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), a cancer of white blood cells.
[bio17-s3.11.1] A reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, in a bone-marrow stem cell, joins part of the ABL gene from chromosome 9 to the BCR gene on chromosome 22.
[bio17-s3.11.2] This forms a BCR-ABL fusion gene on a shortened chromosome 22, the Philadelphia chromosome.
[bio17-s3.11.3] The fusion gene codes for an enzyme that is permanently switched on and drives the cell to divide uncontrollably, so it is an oncogene (a gene that causes cancer).
[bio17-s3.11.4] CML is caused by a somatic mutation and is not inherited, because it happens in a body cell and not in a gamete-forming cell.
[bio17-s3.11.5] The Philadelphia chromosome was discovered in Philadelphia, USA, in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford; Janet Rowley showed in 1973 that it arises by translocation.
[bio17-s3.11.6] The drug imatinib blocks the BCR-ABL enzyme. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.12.b] Some conditions come from having the wrong number of chromosomes, not from a change in structure. They are therefore not examples of deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.0] Some conditions are caused by a change in the number of chromosomes, not in their structure, so they are not deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.1] They usually result from non-disjunction: a homologous pair, or two copies of a chromosome, fails to separate during meiosis, so a gamete has one chromosome too many or too few.
[bio17-s3.12.2] Down syndrome: in about 95% of cases there are three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), giving 47 chromosomes, after non-disjunction, usually during egg formation.
[bio17-s3.12.3] The risk of Down syndrome rises with the mother's age.
[bio17-s3.12.4] Features of Down syndrome include a characteristic facial appearance, some degree of intellectual disability and a higher risk of heart defects.
[bio17-s3.12.5] A few per cent of Down syndrome cases are caused instead by a translocation that attaches extra chromosome-21 material to another chromosome; only those cases involve a structural change.
[bio17-s3.13.b] Sex chromosomes can also be gained or lost, and a karyotype is the tool that shows number changes and large structural changes.
[bio17-s3.13.0] Turner syndrome is a change in number with 45 chromosomes, X: one X and no second sex chromosome, female.
[bio17-s3.13.1] Klinefelter syndrome is a change in number with 47 chromosomes, XXY, male.
[bio17-s3.13.2] A karyotype is a photograph of a cell's chromosomes, taken during mitosis when they are condensed, and arranged in homologous pairs by size and shape.
[bio17-s3.13.3] A karyotype shows changes in number (for example three chromosome 21s) and large structural changes (for example the shortened chromosome 22).
[bio17-s3.14.b] The two kinds of mutation differ in size, in how they arise, and in how they are detected. Comparing them in pairs is the quickest way to answer a distinguish question.
[bio17-s3.14.0] Scale: a point mutation changes one or a few bases in one gene; a chromosomal mutation changes the arrangement or number of copies of a large section with many genes.
[bio17-s3.14.1] How they arise: point mutations mainly from mispairing during replication; chromosomal mutations from breakage and wrong rejoining of chromosomes, often during meiosis.
[bio17-s3.14.2] Detection: point mutations need DNA sequencing (reading the order of bases), because they are too small to see; large chromosomal mutations can be seen in a karyotype under the light microscope.
[bio17-s3.14.3] Examples: sickle-cell anaemia (substitution) and cystic fibrosis (three-base deletion); cri du chat (deletion), Charcot–Marie–Tooth type 1A (duplication), severe haemophilia A (inversion), and the Philadelphia chromosome in CML (translocation).

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio25-s2: Osmosis in food preservation and plant health
[bio25-s2.0.b] Food goes off because microorganisms feed on it, and microorganisms need water to live. Adding a lot of salt or sugar turns osmosis against them: it pulls water out of their cells.
[bio25-s2.0.0] Food spoils mainly because bacteria and fungi (moulds and yeasts) grow on it, digesting it and making it unsafe or unpleasant to eat. Like all cells, they need water for their enzymes to work and to grow and reproduce.
[bio25-s2.0.1] Mechanism, step 1: a high concentration of salt or sugar is added to the food.
[bio25-s2.0.2] 2. The food's water is now a concentrated (hypertonic) solution compared with the cytoplasm (the watery contents) of any microorganism on it.
[bio25-s2.0.3] 3. Water leaves the microbial cells by osmosis, moving from the more dilute cytoplasm to the more concentrated solution outside.
[bio25-s2.0.4] 4. The cells are dehydrated and plasmolysed (the cell membrane shrinks away from the cell wall; bacteria and fungi have cell walls), so their enzymes can't work and they can't grow or reproduce; many die.
[bio25-s2.0.5] The salt or sugar also binds water in the food, so less free water is available to any microbe.
[bio25-s2.0.6] Preserving by drying (e.g. dried fruit and dried fish) works by removing water directly, which has the same effect on microbes. It is related to osmosis but is not osmosis itself.
[bio25-s2.1.b] Three traditional methods preserve food with salt, sugar or acid. Salt and sugar work by osmosis, but acid works in a different way, by its low pH.
[bio25-s2.1.0] Salting: meat and fish are rubbed with dry salt or soaked in strong brine (salt solution), e.g. bacon, ham and salted fish. Salt also draws water out of the food itself by osmosis, drying it further, so even less water is left for microbes.
[bio25-s2.1.1] Sugaring: fruit is preserved in a high concentration of sugar, e.g. jam, marmalade and candied fruit; fruit is also stored in heavy syrup.
[bio25-s2.1.2] Honey keeps for a very long time because its sugar concentration is so high that microbes lose water to it instead of growing.
[bio25-s2.1.3] Pickling: vegetables kept in vinegar are preserved mainly by the low pH of the acid (ethanoic acid), which stops enzymes and microbes working, not by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.1.4] Brine pickles (salt solutions) combine osmosis with acid made by fermentation (microbes breaking down sugars in the vegetable), so both effects protect the food.
[bio25-s2.2.b] Osmotic preservation is useful but not perfect. To evaluate it, weigh what it does well against where it fails and what it does to our health.
[bio25-s2.2.0] Advantages: it is cheap, simple and traditional; it needs no refrigeration, so it was used for long journeys and winter stores; and it works against both bacteria and fungi.
[bio25-s2.2.1] Limits: some microorganisms tolerate high salt or sugar concentrations, especially moulds and yeasts, so jam can still grow mould on its surface once opened and should be refrigerated.
[bio25-s2.2.2] Preservation fails if the salt or sugar concentration is too low, because there is then not enough of a concentration difference to draw water out of microbes. For example, reduced-sugar jams spoil faster.
[bio25-s2.2.3] Osmotic preservation slows microbial growth but doesn't sterilise the food (kill all microbes). It is usually combined with other methods, such as cooking, sealing, refrigeration, smoking and acids.
[bio25-s2.2.4] Health link: salted foods add much salt to the diet, and high salt intake is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.
[bio25-s2.2.5] Sugary preserves add sugar, which is linked to tooth decay and obesity. So salting and sugaring are used less today, and refrigeration and freezing more. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio25-s2.2.6] Osmosis also affects food preparation: salting sliced vegetables, e.g. cucumber and aubergine, draws water out of them.
[bio25-s2.2.7] Limp vegetables, e.g. celery and lettuce, become crisp again when placed in cold water, because their cells take in water by osmosis and become turgid (swollen and firm with water).
[bio25-s2.3.b] Plant cells stay firm because they are filled with water taken in by osmosis, and this water pressure does several jobs for the plant.
[bio25-s2.3.0] Healthy plants keep their cells turgid (full of water and pushing outwards) by taking in water by osmosis into the vacuole (the large sap-filled space) of each cell.
[bio25-s2.3.1] The turgor pressure of cells pressing against their walls supports non-woody plants, leaves and flowers, holding them upright and spreading leaves to the light for photosynthesis.
[bio25-s2.3.2] Turgor pressure drives cell enlargement: young cells expand as water enters their vacuoles, so plant growth depends on water uptake by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.3.3] Guard cells (the pair of cells around each stoma, the tiny pore in a leaf) open the stomata when they become turgid and close them when they become flaccid (soft and limp from losing water), controlling gas exchange and water loss. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.3.4] Wilting: when water is lost from the leaves by transpiration (evaporation from the leaf) faster than the roots take it up, the cells lose water, become flaccid, and the plant wilts.
[bio25-s2.3.5] Wilting closes the stomata, so less carbon dioxide gets in and it reduces photosynthesis and growth. It is reversed if water is supplied soon enough; prolonged wilting kills the plant.
[bio25-s2.4.b] Roots rely on the soil water being more dilute than their own cells. If that balance flips, osmosis runs the wrong way and the plant loses water even though the soil is wet.
[bio25-s2.4.0] Roots take in water by osmosis because the soil water is normally more dilute than the cell sap (the solution in the vacuole) of the root cells. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.4.1] If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the contents of the root cells, water stops entering the roots and can leave them by osmosis. The root cells become plasmolysed, the plant cannot take up enough water, and it wilts and may die, even in moist soil.
[bio25-s2.4.2] Causes of salty soil water: flooding of coastal land by seawater (e.g. storm surges), and salt spread on roads in icy weather, which damages roadside trees and hedges.
[bio25-s2.4.3] Irrigation (watering crops artificially) in hot, dry regions is another cause: water evaporates and leaves salts behind in the soil (salinisation), lowering crop yields.
[bio25-s2.4.4] Fertiliser burn: applying too much fertiliser, especially concentrated fertiliser close to roots or on dry soil, makes the soil solution too concentrated, so water leaves the roots and the leaves turn brown and scorched.
[bio25-s2.4.5] Advice to avoid fertiliser burn: apply the recommended amount, spread it evenly and water it in. Salt was used in the past as a weedkiller for the same reason.
[bio25-s2.4.6] Some plants of salty habitats, such as salt marshes and seashores, survive because their root cells hold a higher solute concentration than the salty soil water, so water still enters by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.4.7] Sustainability link: soil salinisation from irrigation, and seawater flooding linked to rising sea levels and stronger storms, reduce the land available for crops. Careful irrigation and drainage reduce salinisation.

CANDIDATES bio26-s2: The nephron
[bio26-s2.0.b] Urine is made by tiny tubes called nephrons, so you need to know the parts of one in the order the fluid flows through them. Each part has its own job, and its position in the kidney is part of the answer.
[bio26-s2.0.0] A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney. It is the kidney's functional unit (the smallest part that does the kidney's job), where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one cell thick throughout.
[bio26-s2.0.1] Bowman's capsule is the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex (the outer layer of the kidney), wrapped around the glomerulus (a capillary knot). Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the capsule's inner space.
[bio26-s2.0.2] The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the first, long, coiled (convoluted) section after the capsule, in the cortex. Proximal means nearer the capsule.
[bio26-s2.0.3] The loop of Henle is a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla (the inner part of the kidney), with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
[bio26-s2.0.4] The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) is the second coiled section, back in the cortex. Distal means further from the capsule. It empties into a collecting duct.
[bio26-s2.0.5] The collecting duct is a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla, through a pyramid, and opens into the renal pelvis (the funnel that collects urine).
[bio26-s2.0.6] The syllabus does not list the collecting duct, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH. Some textbooks do not count it as part of the nephron.
[bio26-s2.1.b] A nephron works because blood is brought right up against it. The blood vessels are arranged so that pressure builds up for filtering, and so that the tubule is wrapped in a second set of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) to take back what it reabsorbs.
[bio26-s2.1.0] A branch of the renal artery (the artery that brings blood to the kidney) divides into arterioles (small arteries). The afferent arteriole (afferent means carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
[bio26-s2.1.1] The capillaries of the glomerulus rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent means carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. The afferent is the wider one, so do not say the efferent is wider.
[bio26-s2.1.2] Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus, like water backing up behind a narrowed nozzle. This high pressure drives filtration.
[bio26-s2.1.3] The glomerulus is unusual because it is a capillary network lying between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule (a small vein) as capillaries normally do.
[bio26-s2.1.4] The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries, so they must be close to the tubule.
[bio26-s2.1.5] These capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration, because the cells need ATP for active transport, and they carry away the cells' carbon dioxide.
[bio26-s2.1.6] The capillaries join into venules, then into a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
[bio26-s2.1.7] Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below; mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).
[bio26-s2.2.b] The first step in making urine is filtration. It happens in the glomerulus and capsule, where pressure forces fluid out of the blood without checking whether the body needs it.
[bio26-s2.2.0] Filtration, also called ultrafiltration or filtration under pressure, is the forcing of small molecules out of the blood by the high pressure in the glomerulus. They pass through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
[bio26-s2.2.1] Substances that pass into the filtrate (the fluid that enters the capsule) are water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea (the waste made from breaking down amino acids), vitamins and some hormones.
[bio26-s2.2.2] Substances that are not filtered and stay in the blood are red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
[bio26-s2.2.3] Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances such as glucose and amino acids are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
[bio26-s2.2.4] Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood); this is why reabsorption is essential.
[bio26-s2.2.5] Structure suits filtration: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arteriole raise the pressure, and the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous, so fluid passes through easily.
[bio26-s2.2.6] The many capillaries in each glomerulus, with about a million glomeruli in the kidney, give a large filtering surface, so a large volume of fluid can be filtered every day.
[bio26-s2.3.b] Filtration lets everything small through, so the body has to take back what it wants. Most of this reabsorption happens in the PCT, whose structure is built for the job.
[bio26-s2.3.0] Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
[bio26-s2.3.1] The PCT is the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here.
[bio26-s2.3.2] Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, which uses ATP (the cell's energy currency) to move them into the cells. Water follows by osmosis, and some urea diffuses back into the blood.
[bio26-s2.3.3] Water is always reabsorbed passively by osmosis; it is never actively pumped. Only salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.3.4] The PCT is long and coiled, which gives time and surface area for reabsorption. Its cells have microvilli (tiny finger-like projections) on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area.
[bio26-s2.3.5] The PCT cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. The tubule is closely surrounded by capillaries, and its wall is one cell thick, so the distance to the blood is short.
[bio26-s2.3.6] The carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.
[bio26-s2.4.b] After the PCT, the rest of the nephron fine-tunes the filtrate. The loop of Henle makes the medulla salty, and this lets the collecting duct take back more water.
[bio26-s2.4.0] The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty, with the highest solute concentration deep in the medulla.
[bio26-s2.4.1] The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
[bio26-s2.4.2] In the DCT more salts and water are reabsorbed, with the amounts adjusted to the body's needs.
[bio26-s2.4.3] The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption.
[bio26-s2.4.4] In the collecting duct, as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood, so the urine becomes more concentrated. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH, a hormone. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation.
[bio26-s2.4.5] What remains in the collecting duct is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s2.5.b] Comparing the composition of blood plasma, glomerular filtrate and urine shows what each part of the nephron has done. Each difference between them has one explanation.
[bio26-s2.5.0] Protein is present in plasma but absent from the filtrate and urine, because it is too large to be filtered.
[bio26-s2.5.1] Glucose and amino acids are present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration, because filtration lets them through freely. They are absent from urine, because all of them are reabsorbed in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.5.2] Urea is at the same concentration in plasma and filtrate, because it is freely filtered. It is much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
[bio26-s2.5.3] Salts are similar in plasma and filtrate. In urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
[bio26-s2.5.4] Skill: given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference. Absent from the filtrate means not filtered (too large); absent from the urine means reabsorbed; more concentrated in the urine means water was reabsorbed and the substance was not.
[bio26-s2.6.b] Following the fluid from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, each part of the nephron does one main job.
[bio26-s2.6.0] Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule: filtration under pressure.
[bio26-s2.6.1] Proximal convoluted tubule: reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and most water.
[bio26-s2.6.2] Loop of Henle: makes the medulla salty; water is reabsorbed from the descending limb.
[bio26-s2.6.3] Distal convoluted tubule: adjusts salts and water, and secretes hydrogen ions, potassium ions and some drugs.
[bio26-s2.6.4] Collecting duct: water is reabsorbed under ADH control.
[bio26-s2.6.5] Renal pelvis: receives the urine that is left.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio5-s2: Levels of organisation
[bio5-s2.0.b] Living things are organised in levels of increasing complexity. Each level is made of units of the level below working together, so a whole organism can be understood by building up from its cells.
[bio5-s2.0.0] The order is cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Learn it as a ladder: each step up combines the units of the step below.
[bio5-s2.0.1] Below the cell, cells are built of organelles (small structures inside the cell, each with a job), which are built of biomolecules (e.g. proteins, lipids). There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
[bio5-s2.0.2] Cell: the basic unit of life. In a multicellular organism (one made of many cells) cells are specialised (differentiated), so that the structure of each cell suits one job.
[bio5-s2.0.3] Tissue: a group of similar cells with the same structure, working together to carry out a particular function. Because the cells are alike, the tissue does one job well.
[bio5-s2.0.4] Organ: a structure made of several different tissues working together to carry out one or more functions. Having different tissues is what separates an organ from a tissue.
[bio5-s2.0.5] Organ system: a group of organs working together to carry out a major function, such as digesting food.
[bio5-s2.0.6] Organism: an individual living thing, whose organ systems work together to keep it alive.
[bio5-s2.0.7] Diagram: a five-step flow chart from cell to organism, with a human example at each level (muscle cell → muscle tissue → stomach → digestive system → human) and a plant example (xylem vessel → vascular tissue → leaf → shoot system → plant).
[bio5-s2.1.b] A specialised cell has a structure that suits its particular job. Cells in a multicellular organism are specialised, and each example below links a feature of the cell to its function.
[bio5-s2.1.0] Unicellular organisms (those made of a single cell), e.g. Amoeba, yeast and bacteria, carry out every life process in one cell, so that cell is not specialised for a single job.
[bio5-s2.1.1] Differentiation: in a multicellular organism, all body cells contain the same DNA, but each cell type uses (expresses) only some of its genes, so cells become specialised.
[bio5-s2.1.2] Unspecialised cells that can still become several cell types are called stem cells. There is more on their uses in Genetic Engineering.
[bio5-s2.1.3] Red blood cell: no nucleus and a biconcave disc shape (a disc dipped in on both faces), giving more room for haemoglobin (the pigment that carries oxygen) and a large surface area for carrying oxygen.
[bio5-s2.1.4] Nerve cell (neuron): long thin extensions carry impulses (electrical signals) over long distances quickly, which suits a cell that links distant parts of the body.
[bio5-s2.1.5] Sperm cell: a tail for swimming, and many mitochondria in the middle section to release the energy that powers the tail.
[bio5-s2.1.6] Root hair cell: a long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water and minerals from the soil.
[bio5-s2.1.7] Palisade cell of a leaf: packed with chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens) for photosynthesis. Guard cells change shape to open and close the stomata (the pores in the leaf surface).
[bio5-s2.2.b] Animal bodies are built from four main tissue types. Each is a group of similar cells doing one kind of job.
[bio5-s2.2.0] Epithelial tissue lines and covers surfaces, e.g. the lining of the gut and the skin surface.
[bio5-s2.2.1] Connective tissue supports and connects other tissues, e.g. bone, cartilage and blood. Blood counts as a connective tissue.
[bio5-s2.2.2] Muscle tissue contracts (shortens) to cause movement. There are three types: skeletal, smooth and cardiac (heart muscle).
[bio5-s2.2.3] Nervous tissue carries impulses, e.g. in the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
[bio5-s2.3.b] Plants also have tissue types, each a group of similar cells with one job. Together they build plant organs such as the leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.0] Dermal tissue is the epidermis, a protective outer layer covering the plant.
[bio5-s2.3.1] Vascular tissue carries materials around the plant: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries food.
[bio5-s2.3.2] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant, carrying out photosynthesis and storage, e.g. the mesophyll (the inner tissue) of a leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.3] Meristematic tissue consists of dividing cells at root and shoot tips, where growth happens, so new cells for growth are made there.
[bio5-s2.3.4] A leaf contains dermal, ground and vascular tissue, which shows that an organ is made of several tissue types. Plant organs are root, stem, leaf and flower.
[bio5-s2.4.b] Organs are made of several tissue types, and organs that cooperate form organ systems. Each system has one major function.
[bio5-s2.4.0] The stomach is an organ containing epithelial tissue lining it, smooth muscle that churns food, connective tissue and nervous tissue. Four different tissues working together make it an organ.
[bio5-s2.4.1] Other human organs include the heart, lungs, kidneys and brain.
[bio5-s2.4.2] Digestive system: breaks down and absorbs food. Circulatory system: transports materials around the body.
[bio5-s2.4.3] Breathing system: gas exchange (taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide). Nervous system: fast coordination.
[bio5-s2.4.4] Endocrine system: hormonal coordination. Musculoskeletal system: support and movement.
[bio5-s2.4.5] Urinary system: excretion (removing wastes) and water balance. Reproductive system: reproduction. Immune system: defence.
[bio5-s2.4.6] Plants have two systems. The root system anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals.
[bio5-s2.4.7] The shoot system (stem, leaves and flowers) carries out photosynthesis, transport and reproduction.
[bio5-s2.5.b] Size changes how a living thing gets what it needs. The bigger an organism, the harder it is to supply every cell, which is why large organisms need specialised systems.
[bio5-s2.5.0] As an organism gets bigger, its volume increases faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio falls.
[bio5-s2.5.1] Worked example: a 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm² and a volume of 1 cm³, a ratio of 6:1. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of 24 cm² and a volume of 8 cm³, a ratio of 3:1.
[bio5-s2.5.2] A unicellular organism has a large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane is enough to supply oxygen and food and to remove wastes.
[bio5-s2.5.3] In a large multicellular organism most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow to reach them.
[bio5-s2.5.4] Multicellular organisms therefore need specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gut lining, root hairs, leaves) to take in materials, and transport systems (blood and heart; xylem and phloem) to carry them to every cell.
[bio5-s2.5.5] They also need coordination systems (nervous and hormonal) that link all the parts and keep conditions constant (homeostasis).
[bio5-s2.5.6] Division of labour: each cell type does one job very efficiently, but no cell can survive alone, so the cells depend on each other.
[bio5-s2.6.b] Being made of many specialised cells brings benefits, but it also has costs. Comparing the two shows why both unicellular and multicellular life exist.
[bio5-s2.6.0] Advantage: a larger size, which means fewer predators and access to light or food.
[bio5-s2.6.1] Advantage: cells inside the body are protected from the environment, because they are surrounded by other cells.
[bio5-s2.6.2] Advantage: efficiency from specialisation, since each cell type does one job well.
[bio5-s2.6.3] Advantage: the organism can survive the death of individual cells, which are replaced.
[bio5-s2.6.4] Disadvantage: more energy and resources are needed to build and maintain the body.
[bio5-s2.6.5] Disadvantage: damage to one vital organ or system can kill the whole organism, because the other cells depend on it.
[bio5-s2.6.6] Disadvantage: growth to reproductive age takes longer than for unicellular organisms, which can divide within hours.

CANDIDATES bio6-s3: Classification
[bio6-s3.0.b] There are so many kinds of living thing that biologists sort them into groups, the way a library sorts books into sections, shelves and titles. The groups sit inside one another in a fixed set of levels.
[bio6-s3.0.0] Classification is the grouping of organisms according to their shared characteristics. Taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
[bio6-s3.0.1] Organisms are placed in a hierarchy of ranks (taxa, the levels of grouping), from broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
[bio6-s3.0.2] Moving down the ranks, each group has fewer members, which share more features and are more closely related. This is why two species in the same genus are far more alike than two in the same kingdom.
[bio6-s3.0.3] Worked example, human: domain Eukarya, kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Primates, family Hominidae, genus Homo, species Homo sapiens.
[bio6-s3.1.b] The species is the smallest rank, so it needs a clear definition and a universal name. Every species gets a two-part scientific name, which is called binomial nomenclature.
[bio6-s3.1.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (young that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, a mule, is infertile, so they are different species.
[bio6-s3.1.1] Limitation of the species definition: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually (e.g. bacteria), because they do not interbreed at all, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding.
[bio6-s3.1.2] Binomial nomenclature: every species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name (capital first letter) followed by the species name (lower case), printed in italics or underlined when handwritten.
[bio6-s3.1.3] Examples of binomial names: Homo sapiens (human), Escherichia coli (a gut bacterium), Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould).
[bio6-s3.1.4] The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature; his Species Plantarum (1753) used it for all plants.
[bio6-s3.1.5] Species in the same genus are closely related, which is why they share the first part of their name. The lion (Panthera leo) and tiger (Panthera tigris) share the genus Panthera.
[bio6-s3.2.b] Before sorting living things into kingdoms, biologists ask one basic question about their cells: is there a nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) or not? The answer splits cellular life into two big groups.
[bio6-s3.2.0] The first division of cellular life is by cell type. Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (the small working parts of a cell). Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, and membrane-bound organelles. There is more on this in Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
[bio6-s3.2.1] Two kingdoms are prokaryotic, Bacteria and Archaea, each forming its own domain. Four kingdoms are eukaryotic, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, all in the domain Eukarya.
[bio6-s3.2.2] The distinction became clear only once the electron microscope showed cell interiors, because the details inside prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are too small to see with a light microscope.
[bio6-s3.3.b] Both prokaryotic kingdoms are single-celled with no nucleus, so cell structure alone does not separate them. One way of telling them apart is the chemistry of their cell walls, and another is rRNA. Each is described by cell type, number of cells, cell wall, nutrition and examples.
[bio6-s3.3.0] Bacteria: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls of peptidoglycan (a mesh of sugars and amino acids). They reproduce by binary fission (one cell splits into two).
[bio6-s3.3.1] Bacterial nutrition varies. Bacteria are autotrophic (make their own food), either photosynthetic or chemosynthetic, or heterotrophic (take in food made by others), either saprophytic (feeding on dead matter) or parasitic (feeding on a living host).
[bio6-s3.3.2] Bacteria examples: Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus (used to make yoghurt) and Streptococcus.
[bio6-s3.3.3] Archaea: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls that have no peptidoglycan. This difference in cell wall is one way of telling them apart from bacteria. Many live in extreme environments.
[bio6-s3.3.4] Archaea examples: methanogens (methane-producing archaea) in cattle digestive systems, and Halobacterium in salt lakes.
[bio6-s3.4.b] The four eukaryotic kingdoms all have a nucleus. They are separated by how many cells they have, what their cell walls are made of, how they get food and how they store carbohydrate.
[bio6-s3.4.0] Protista: eukaryotic and mostly unicellular, though some are multicellular algae such as seaweeds (kelp). They have no tissues (groups of similar cells doing one job) of the kind found in plants and animals. Some are photosynthetic (algae) and some are heterotrophic.
[bio6-s3.4.1] Protista includes eukaryotes that do not belong in the other three eukaryotic kingdoms. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (causes malaria) and Phytophthora infestans (causes potato blight, which caused the Great Famine in Ireland, 1845–1849).
[bio6-s3.4.2] Fungi: eukaryotic and mostly multicellular, made of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium (a tangled mass of threads), though yeasts are unicellular. They have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise, and their cell walls are made of chitin.
[bio6-s3.4.3] Fungi are heterotrophic: they secrete enzymes onto food and absorb the digested products, living as saprophytes or parasites. They store carbohydrate as glycogen and reproduce by spores. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), mushrooms and Penicillium.
[bio6-s3.4.4] Plantae: eukaryotic and multicellular, with cell walls of cellulose and chloroplasts (where photosynthesis happens). They are autotrophic by photosynthesis, store carbohydrate as starch, and mostly do not move. Examples: mosses, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants such as the daisy and oak.
[bio6-s3.4.5] Animalia: eukaryotic and multicellular, with no cell walls. They are heterotrophic, taking in (ingesting) food, and store carbohydrate as glycogen. Most can move and have nerves and muscles. Examples: sponges, earthworms, insects, fish and mammals.
[bio6-s3.5.b] Classification by kingdom and domain only works for things made of cells. Viruses fall outside it.
[bio6-s3.5.0] Viruses are not in any kingdom or domain, because they are not cells. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio6-s3.6.b] Grouping by appearance can mislead, so modern classification also asks which organisms share a recent ancestor. This evolutionary family-tree approach is called phylogeny.
[bio6-s3.6.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms. Phylogenetic classification groups organisms by how closely related they are by descent from common ancestors, rather than by appearance alone.
[bio6-s3.6.1] A phylogenetic tree shows these relationships. Each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor, and the closer two species' branches join, the more recent their common ancestor and the more closely related they are.
[bio6-s3.6.2] Species in the same genus share a very recent common ancestor; genera in the same family share an older one. This is how the ranks of the hierarchy reflect evolutionary history.
[bio6-s3.6.3] Example: the lion and tiger (genus Panthera) are more closely related to each other than either is to the domestic cat (Felis catus), but all three are in the cat family (Felidae).
[bio6-s3.6.4] The dog (Canis familiaris, also written Canis lupus familiaris) is in a different family, Canidae, and all four animals are in the order Carnivora.
[bio6-s3.6.5] Evidence used to work out phylogeny includes shared anatomical structures with the same basic plan, e.g. the five-digit limb of mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians; embryology (the study of early development); and fossils. There is more on this in Evolution: The Origins of Life.
[bio6-s3.6.6] The most powerful evidence is comparison of DNA base sequences and protein amino-acid sequences: the fewer the differences, the more closely related the organisms, because less time has passed for differences to build up.
[bio6-s3.6.7] Appearance can mislead. Whales and dolphins look like fish but are mammals: they breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young on milk.
[bio6-s3.6.8] Diagram: a phylogenetic tree for lion, tiger, domestic cat and dog, with the nodes for genus Panthera, family Felidae and order Carnivora labelled.
[bio6-s3.7.b] Classification is not fixed. Each time scientists gained a new tool for looking at living things, the groupings were redrawn, which is why you will meet different numbers of kingdoms in the history of the subject.
[bio6-s3.7.0] Early systems had two kingdoms, plants and animals. Linnaeus, in the 18th century, classified organisms by visible features.
[bio6-s3.7.1] Microscopes revealed single-celled organisms that fitted neither kingdom. In 1866 Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista.
[bio6-s3.7.2] In 1969 Robert Whittaker proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. Monera held all the prokaryotes.
[bio6-s3.7.3] Fungi were separated from plants in Whittaker's system because they have chitin walls, no chlorophyll and absorb their food.
[bio6-s3.7.4] rRNA sequencing (reading the sequence of a molecule found in ribosomes) by Carl Woese showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups. This gave three domains (1990).
[bio6-s3.7.5] The six kingdoms used in this course followed, with Monera split into Bacteria and Archaea. There is more on this in Domains of life.
[bio6-s3.7.6] Systems change because new technology provides new evidence, e.g. the light microscope, the electron microscope, biochemistry and DNA sequencing. Classification is revised to reflect evolutionary relationships more accurately. It is a model that scientists keep testing and improving.
[bio6-s3.8.b] DNA sequencing, reading the order of bases in an organism's DNA, is now the strongest tool for classification. It is still moving organisms between groups and adding new ones.
[bio6-s3.8.0] DNA sequencing and whole-genome comparisons are now the main evidence for classification, and have moved many organisms. Phytophthora (potato blight) was long classed as a fungus because it grows as threads. Its DNA and its cellulose cell walls show that it belongs with the protists (water moulds).
[bio6-s3.8.1] DNA evidence placed the giant panda in the bear family.
[bio6-s3.8.2] Environmental DNA sequencing, which sequences all the DNA in a soil or water sample, has revealed huge numbers of microorganisms that have never been grown in a laboratory. These add new branches to the tree of life.
[bio6-s3.8.3] Asgard archaea were first reported in 2015 from DNA in sediment near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vents in the Arctic Ocean. They share many genes with eukaryotes.
[bio6-s3.8.4] Some scientists argue that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, which would mean only two primary domains. This is still debated, so classification remains a work in progress.
[bio6-s3.8.5] DNA barcoding uses a short standard gene sequence to identify species quickly, e.g. to identify species from tiny samples or to detect mislabelled food.
[bio6-s3.9.b] A dichotomous key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by answering a series of either/or questions about what you can see. The worked key below identifies six invertebrates (animals without backbones).
[bio6-s3.9.0] A dichotomous key identifies an organism by a series of steps. Each step gives a choice between two contrasting statements (a couplet) about a visible feature. Each statement leads either to a name or to the number of the next couplet.
[bio6-s3.9.1] Worked example: a key to six invertebrates.
[bio6-s3.9.2] 1. Legs present → go to 2; legs absent → go to 4.
[bio6-s3.9.3] 2. Three pairs of legs → ladybird (an insect); more than three pairs of legs → go to 3.
[bio6-s3.9.4] 3. Four pairs of legs → spider; seven pairs of legs → woodlouse.
[bio6-s3.9.5] 4. Shell present → snail; no visible shell → go to 5.
[bio6-s3.9.6] 5. Body divided into rings (segments) → earthworm; body not divided into rings → slug.
[bio6-s3.10.b] To use a key, start at couplet 1 and follow the numbers until you reach a name. Practise tracing a route for each organism so you can do it in the exam.
[bio6-s3.10.0] Using the key: an animal with eight legs goes 1 (legs present) → 2 (more than three pairs) → 3 (four pairs) → spider. Eight legs means four pairs, which is why it ends at spider.
[bio6-s3.10.1] Write the route for one more organism: the slug goes 1 → 4 → 5 → slug, because it has no legs, no visible shell and a body that is not divided into rings.
[bio6-s3.11.b] You may have to build a key of your own, or use the features of the kingdoms to place an unknown organism. Both rely on picking features that give a clear yes or no.
[bio6-s3.11.0] To construct a key, list the organisms, choose a feature that splits them into two groups, and keep splitting each group in two until each organism is on its own.
[bio6-s3.11.1] Use features that are easy to observe and do not vary between individuals or seasons, e.g. number of legs, not size or colour, because size and colour change with age and season and would give unreliable answers.
[bio6-s3.11.2] A key for n organisms needs n − 1 couplets. For example, the six-invertebrate key above has five couplets.
[bio6-s3.11.3] To classify an unknown organism to a kingdom, use the features in order. Does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a bacterium or an archaeon, separated by cell-wall chemistry or rRNA.
[bio6-s3.11.4] If it has a nucleus, ask further questions. Is it multicellular with tissues? What is its cell wall made of? Is it autotrophic or heterotrophic?
[bio6-s3.11.5] Worked example: a newly found organism is eukaryotic, is made of hyphae with chitin walls, and absorbs food from dead leaves. It is in the kingdom Fungi.
[bio6-s3.12.b] Classification is more than tidy labelling. It makes biology easier to study, communicate and apply to health, farming and conservation.
[bio6-s3.12.0] It organises the enormous diversity of life into manageable groups, making it easier to study and to find information.
[bio6-s3.12.1] Scientific names are universal and unique, which avoids the confusion of common names. The robin of Europe (Erithacus rubecula) and the robin of North America (Turdus migratorius) are different species in different genera.
[bio6-s3.12.2] It shows evolutionary relationships, so features of a new organism can be predicted from its relatives. For example, relatives of a plant that produces a useful drug may produce similar compounds.
[bio6-s3.12.3] Health: correctly identifying a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) decides the treatment. A bacterial infection may respond to an antibiotic, but a fungal or viral one will not.
[bio6-s3.12.4] Agriculture and the environment: identifying pests, invasive species and endangered species is needed for control and conservation, and for measuring biodiversity (the variety of living things in an area).

CANDIDATES bio7-s1: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
[bio7-s1.0.b] Biologists sort all cells into two types, depending on whether the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. The names themselves tell you the difference, and you need the units to talk about how big cells are.
[bio7-s1.0.0] A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments wrapped in their own membrane). Its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. “Pro” means before and “karyon” means kernel or nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.1] A eukaryotic cell has its DNA enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and it has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. “Eu” means true, so eukaryote means “true nucleus”. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.2] Cells are far too small for millimetres to be a handy unit, so their sizes are given in micrometres. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres) and 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.
[bio7-s1.1.b] Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells look very different, but both are living cells that must do the same basic jobs. So they share a core set of features.
[bio7-s1.1.0] Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves the cell, and cytoplasm, the fluid where many of the cell's reactions take place.
[bio7-s1.1.1] Both have ribosomes (the tiny structures that make proteins), and both use DNA as the genetic material, reading it with the same genetic code.
[bio7-s1.1.2] Both respire, releasing energy from food, and both use ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy) as the energy currency.
[bio7-s1.1.3] These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor, because such an unusual set of identical features is unlikely to have arisen separately.
[bio7-s1.2.b] The most important difference between the two cell types is how the DNA is stored. This affects where the DNA sits, what shape it has and what it is packed with.
[bio7-s1.2.0] Nucleus: prokaryotes have none. Their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Eukaryotes have a true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores (small channels through the envelope).
[bio7-s1.2.1] Number and shape of chromosomes: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome (a closed loop of DNA). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes (DNA with two ends).
[bio7-s1.2.2] Packaging: eukaryotic DNA is wound around histone proteins, which help to pack it. In bacteria the circular chromosome is not wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s1.2.3] Prokaryotes often also have plasmids, small extra rings of DNA separate from the main chromosome. Plasmids can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance, which is why resistance can spread between bacteria.
[bio7-s1.2.4] Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast), not in all eukaryotes, so do not say that eukaryotes have none or that all have them.
[bio7-s1.3.b] Eukaryotic cells divide their work between membrane-bound organelles, whereas prokaryotes have to do everything without them. Ribosomes are the one structure both have, but they differ in size.
[bio7-s1.3.0] Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none, so no mitochondria, no chloroplasts and no other membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.3.1] Without organelles, prokaryotes carry out respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it (folds of the membrane that increase its area).
[bio7-s1.3.2] Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. This is one more way these organelles resemble prokaryotic cells.
[bio7-s1.3.3] Ribosomes: both cell types have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). The S number is a measure of size.
[bio7-s1.3.4] Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they stop bacteria making proteins and harm bacteria but not human cells, which use 80S ribosomes in their cytoplasm.
[bio7-s1.4.b] The remaining differences are about the whole cell: how big it is, what surrounds it, and how the organism is built and reproduces.
[bio7-s1.4.0] Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so they are roughly ten times larger in length.
[bio7-s1.4.1] Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one, made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells have chitin walls, and animal cells have none.
[bio7-s1.4.2] Some prokaryotes have extra outer structures: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum, a whip-like tail, for movement. There is more on the full bacterial cell in Microorganisms.
[bio7-s1.4.3] Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular (made of a single cell) and divide by binary fission, in which one cell splits in two. Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis.
[bio7-s1.4.4] Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotes are protists, fungi, plants and animals.
[bio7-s1.5.b] How a cell looks in a micrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope) depends on the microscope, because a light microscope cannot show very small structures but an electron microscope can. Knowing what each shows lets you identify a cell.
[bio7-s1.5.0] Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure. It is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
[bio7-s1.5.1] Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm and the cell membrane as a line. Plant cells also show the cell wall, a large vacuole (a fluid-filled sac) and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.5.2] Electron micrographs show what the light microscope cannot. In prokaryotes they show the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall.
[bio7-s1.5.3] In eukaryotes, electron micrographs show the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
[bio7-s1.5.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule, flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), with the eukaryotic cell drawn much larger.
[bio7-s1.6.b] You may be given a micrograph and asked whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and to justify the answer. Work through the same checks in order.
[bio7-s1.6.0] 1. Check for a nucleus. If one is present, the cell is eukaryotic, because only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nucleus.
[bio7-s1.6.1] 2. Check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size. A cell of about 1–10 µm suggests a prokaryote, since eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm.
[bio7-s1.6.2] 3. Look for membrane-bound organelles, which only eukaryotes have.
[bio7-s1.6.3] 4. For a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts, which mean a plant cell, or their absence, which suggests an animal cell. There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
