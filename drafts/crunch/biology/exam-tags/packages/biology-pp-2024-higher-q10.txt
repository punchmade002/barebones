CANDIDATES bio13-s3: Investigation: rate of enzyme activity
[bio13-s3.0.b] An enzyme is a biological catalyst, so what we want to know in an investigation is how fast it works. That speed is called the rate, and it is always measured against time.
[bio13-s3.0.0] The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is the amount of substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on) converted into product per unit time, e.g. cm³ of oxygen produced per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.1] The rate can be measured either by how fast a product appears or by how fast the substrate disappears, whichever is easier to detect for that reaction.
[bio13-s3.0.2] Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂); the rate is measured by the oxygen released, as foam or as a volume of gas.
[bio13-s3.0.3] Amylase digests starch to maltose; the rate is measured by the time taken for the starch to disappear, tested with iodine.
[bio13-s3.0.4] Rate = amount of product ÷ time: e.g. 30 cm³ of foam in 2 minutes gives a rate of 15 cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.5] Where the time taken to reach an end-point (such as the starch disappearing) is measured, rate = 1 ÷ time: starch gone in 120 s gives a rate of 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹, often written as 1000/time to give whole numbers.
[bio13-s3.0.6] A shorter time means a faster rate, because the same change has been completed more quickly.
[bio13-s3.0.7] Measure the initial rate, at the start of the reaction (e.g. in the first one or two minutes), because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
[bio13-s3.1.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. Presenting them clearly in a table and a graph is how you show the pattern in your results.
[bio13-s3.1.0] A results table has the independent variable (the factor you change) in the first column, then the repeat readings, the mean and the rate, each column headed with its unit.
[bio13-s3.1.1] A graph has the independent variable on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis, with points plotted accurately and joined by a smooth curve or line of best fit.
[bio13-s3.2.b] All five investigations use the same small set of enzymes, substrates and equipment. Knowing what each is for makes the methods easy to follow.
[bio13-s3.2.0] Catalase is found in almost all cells that use oxygen, where it breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism. Good sources are celery, potato, liver and yeast.
[bio13-s3.2.1] Catalase extract: blend a fixed mass of chopped celery (e.g. 20 g) with distilled water (e.g. 100 cm³), then filter through muslin or filter paper. Make one batch and use it for every test, so the enzyme is the same each time.
[bio13-s3.2.2] Amylase: a commercial (e.g. fungal) amylase solution made up at a fixed concentration; starch solution (e.g. 1%) is its substrate. Saliva contains amylase but is avoided for hygiene reasons.
[bio13-s3.2.3] Equipment for measuring: 100 cm³ graduated cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes for measuring volumes, thermometer to check temperatures, stopwatch to time each run, electronic balance.
[bio13-s3.2.4] Other equipment: test tubes and boiling tubes, blender or mortar and pestle, knife and chopping board, filter funnel, spotting tile and droppers, conical flask with bung and delivery tube, trough, labels.
[bio13-s3.2.5] Water baths can be thermostatically controlled electric baths, or beakers of water warmed with kettle water or cooled with ice, checked with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.2.6] Reagents: hydrogen peroxide solution (one stock of fixed concentration), buffer solutions or buffer tablets of known pH, washing-up liquid, distilled water, starch solution, amylase solution and iodine solution.
[bio13-s3.3.b] Four ideas recur across the investigations: a buffer to hold pH steady, washing-up liquid to make the oxygen measurable in the foam methods, a waiting time so the mixture reaches the right temperature, and a control to prove the enzyme is responsible.
[bio13-s3.3.0] A buffer is a solution that keeps the pH constant even when acids or alkalis are produced or added.
[bio13-s3.3.1] A buffer at pH 7 (or at the enzyme's optimum) is added in every investigation except the pH investigation, so that pH is a controlled variable (kept the same each time).
[bio13-s3.3.2] In the foam methods, a drop of washing-up liquid traps the oxygen released by catalase as a foam, so its volume can be read in a graduated cylinder; the same amount is added every time.
[bio13-s3.3.3] Reaching temperature (equilibration): the enzyme and the substrate are placed in the same water bath, in separate containers, for about 5 minutes before they are mixed, so the reaction starts at the chosen temperature.
[bio13-s3.3.4] A control in each investigation uses enzyme that has been boiled (denatured), or distilled water in place of the enzyme, with everything else the same.
[bio13-s3.3.5] The control shows that the oxygen (or the loss of starch) is caused by the enzyme and not by the substrate breaking down on its own.
[bio13-s3.4.b] The chemicals and equipment here are mostly mild, but several can harm skin, eyes or clothes if handled carelessly.
[bio13-s3.4.0] Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent that irritates or burns the eyes and skin; wear safety glasses (and gloves), measure it with a syringe, and wash any splash off with plenty of water.
[bio13-s3.4.1] Buffers of very low or very high pH are irritants, so wear safety glasses.
[bio13-s3.4.2] Use tongs or a test-tube holder and take care with boiling water and hot water baths; keep electrical water baths and blenders away from spilt water.
[bio13-s3.4.3] Take care with knives and blender blades. If liver is used, treat it as raw meat: wear gloves, keep it off benches used for other work and wash hands afterwards.
[bio13-s3.4.4] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes, so handle it with care. Never eat or drink in the laboratory, because chemicals could be swallowed.
[bio13-s3.5.b] Here we vary how much hydrogen peroxide (the substrate) the same amount of catalase has to work on, and see how the rate of oxygen production changes.
[bio13-s3.5.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of substrate (hydrogen peroxide) concentration on the rate of catalase activity, by changing only the substrate so any change in rate must be due to it.
[bio13-s3.5.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increases the rate of oxygen production, because more substrate molecules collide with active sites per second, until all the active sites are occupied and the rate levels off.”
[bio13-s3.5.2] Independent variable: hydrogen peroxide concentration, made by diluting the stock with distilled water to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the stock (e.g. 2 cm³ stock + 8 cm³ water = 20%), always to the same total volume (e.g. 10 cm³).
[bio13-s3.5.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): volume of oxygen collected in the first minute (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.5.4] Controlled variables: volume of the same celery extract (so enzyme concentration is constant), temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), total volume of solution, timing and apparatus.
[bio13-s3.5.5] Control: the 100% hydrogen peroxide with boiled extract (or with water in place of the extract), which gives little or no oxygen; the 0% tube also shows that no oxygen forms without substrate.
[bio13-s3.6.b] The oxygen given off is collected in an upturned cylinder of water, so its volume can be read directly.
[bio13-s3.6.0] 1. Fill a graduated cylinder with water and turn it upside down in a trough of water, with no air at the top.
[bio13-s3.6.1] 2. Put 10 cm³ of the first hydrogen peroxide dilution and 2 cm³ of pH 7 buffer in a conical flask (or boiling tube) in the water bath, with 2 cm³ of celery extract in a separate tube beside it, for about 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.6.2] 3. Place the end of the delivery tube under the mouth of the upturned cylinder.
[bio13-s3.6.3] 4. Add the extract to the flask, immediately put in the bung with the delivery tube, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.6.4] 5. Read the volume of oxygen collected in the cylinder after 1 minute (or every 30 seconds for 3 minutes).
[bio13-s3.6.5] 6. Rinse the flask and repeat for each concentration. Do each concentration three times and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.6.6] Alternatives: a gas syringe connected to the flask measures the oxygen volume more accurately than collection over water; or the foam method used in the pH investigation can be used.
[bio13-s3.6.7] Another alternative: filter-paper discs soaked in catalase extract are dropped into each concentration and the time for each disc to rise to the surface (buoyed by oxygen bubbles) is timed, with rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.6.8] Diagram: a conical flask in a water bath, with a bung and delivery tube leading under an upturned graduated cylinder full of water in a trough. Label hydrogen peroxide + buffer + catalase extract, water bath, thermometer, delivery tube, oxygen collecting, trough of water.
[bio13-s3.7.b] The results follow a typical curve: a steep rise, then a plateau. Each part has its own explanation, and the plateau is caused by saturation of the active sites, not by the enzyme being denatured or used up.
[bio13-s3.7.0] Expected results: no oxygen at 0%; at low concentrations the rate rises steeply, roughly in proportion to concentration; at high concentrations it rises less and levels off into a plateau. The boiled-enzyme control gives little or no oxygen.
[bio13-s3.7.1] The rise is explained by collisions: more substrate molecules in the same volume means more collisions with active sites (the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds) per second, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and more product is made per minute.
[bio13-s3.7.2] The plateau is explained by saturation: at high substrate concentration every active site is occupied at any moment, so the enzyme (saturated) works at its maximum rate, and adding more substrate cannot increase the rate.
[bio13-s3.7.3] At the plateau the amount of enzyme is the limiting factor (the thing holding the rate back); adding more enzyme would raise the rate again.
[bio13-s3.7.4] Within the range used in a school investigation the plateau may not be reached if the enzyme is in excess.
[bio13-s3.7.5] Conclusion: state the trend shown by the mean rates, whether it supports the hypothesis, and the concentration above which the rate stopped increasing, if one was found.
[bio13-s3.7.6] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against substrate concentration (x-axis); a curve rising from the origin, steep at first and then levelling off, with the plateau labelled all active sites occupied (saturated).
[bio13-s3.8.b] Now the substrate stays the same and the amount of enzyme changes. Amylase digests starch, and the end-point is found with iodine, which turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown when starch is absent.
[bio13-s3.8.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of enzyme (amylase) concentration on the rate at which starch is digested.
[bio13-s3.8.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of amylase increases the rate of starch digestion, because more active sites are available to bind starch molecules, as long as starch is in excess.”
[bio13-s3.8.2] Independent variable: amylase concentration, made by diluting the stock amylase solution with distilled water to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the stock, always to the same volume.
[bio13-s3.8.3] Dependent variable: time taken for the starch to disappear (the iodine test no longer turns blue-black), converted to rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.8.4] Controlled variables: volume and concentration of starch solution, volume of amylase solution, temperature (water bath at e.g. 37 °C), pH (buffer at pH 7), volume of iodine in each well, and the sampling interval.
[bio13-s3.8.5] Control: starch solution with distilled water (0% amylase) or with boiled amylase in place of the amylase; it stays blue-black with iodine for the whole investigation, showing that the starch does not disappear without active enzyme.
[bio13-s3.9.b] Drops of the reacting mixture are tested with iodine at regular intervals until the blue-black colour no longer appears.
[bio13-s3.9.0] 1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile (a white tile with small hollows), so each sample can be tested as soon as it is taken.
[bio13-s3.9.1] 2. Put 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of pH 7 buffer into a test tube, and 2 cm³ of the first amylase dilution into another, then stand both in the water bath for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.9.2] 3. Pour the amylase into the starch, mix, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.9.3] 4. Every 30 seconds, take one drop of the mixture with a clean dropper and add it to the next well of iodine.
[bio13-s3.9.4] 5. Note the time at which a drop first leaves the iodine yellow-brown (no blue-black): this is the end-point, when all the starch has been digested.
[bio13-s3.9.5] 6. Repeat for each amylase concentration, three times each, so that a mean can be calculated and a one-off error does not mislead you; then calculate the mean time and the rate.
[bio13-s3.9.6] Alternative: with catalase, vary the volume of celery extract (e.g. 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 cm³, each made up to the same total volume with distilled water) or the number of equal-sized celery discs, and measure the foam or oxygen volume in a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.9.7] Diagram: a spotting tile with a row of wells, the first wells blue-black and the later wells yellow-brown, labelled with sampling times; and the starch–amylase tube in a water bath with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.10.b] With more enzyme, the starch disappears sooner. Unlike the substrate investigation, the graph is a straight line, as long as there is plenty of substrate.
[bio13-s3.10.0] Expected results: the higher the amylase concentration, the shorter the time for the starch to disappear, so the faster the rate; the 0% and boiled-enzyme controls never reach the end-point.
[bio13-s3.10.1] A graph of rate against enzyme concentration is a straight line through the origin: doubling the enzyme roughly doubles the rate.
[bio13-s3.10.2] Explanation: more enzyme molecules means more active sites, so more substrate molecules can be bound and converted at the same time, and more product is made per unit time.
[bio13-s3.10.3] The straight line continues only while substrate is in excess (more than the enzyme can use). If the enzyme concentration becomes very high, substrate becomes the limiting factor and the line levels off.
[bio13-s3.10.4] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against enzyme concentration (x-axis); a straight line rising from the origin, labelled substrate in excess.
[bio13-s3.11.b] Each enzyme works best at a particular pH. Here, buffers set the pH, and the volume of foam produced by catalase shows how active it is at each one.
[bio13-s3.11.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.11.1] Hypothesis: “Catalase has an optimum pH at which its rate is highest, and the rate decreases at pH values above and below it, because the shape of the active site is changed.”
[bio13-s3.11.2] Independent variable: pH, set with buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10), which hold each pH steady so it does not drift as the reaction proceeds.
[bio13-s3.11.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam produced in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.11.4] Controlled variables: temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract (or mass of celery), volume of buffer, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.11.5] Control: at each pH (or at pH 7), boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.12.b] The foam is built up in a graduated cylinder, so its height can be read at the end of a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.12.0] 1. Into a 100 cm³ graduated cylinder put 10 cm³ of pH 4 buffer, 2 cm³ of celery extract (or a fixed mass of chopped celery) and one drop of washing-up liquid.
[bio13-s3.12.1] 2. Stand the cylinder in the 25 °C water bath, with a boiling tube of hydrogen peroxide beside it, for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.12.2] 3. Note the starting volume of liquid in the cylinder, so the foam volume can be found by subtraction.
[bio13-s3.12.3] 4. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide with a syringe and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.12.4] 5. After 2 minutes, read the volume at the top of the foam, at eye level; the foam volume is this reading minus the starting volume.
[bio13-s3.12.5] 6. Wash the cylinder and repeat with each buffer, three times each, and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.12.6] Diagram: a graduated cylinder standing in a water bath with a thermometer, containing buffer, celery extract and washing-up liquid at the bottom and a column of foam above, with a syringe adding hydrogen peroxide. Label each part and the foam height being read.
[bio13-s3.13.b] The rate rises to a peak at one pH and falls on both sides. The explanation lies in how pH affects the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s3.13.0] Expected results: the most foam forms at around pH 7 (the optimum for celery and liver catalase), with less in more acidic and more alkaline buffers and very little at the extremes. The control gives no foam.
[bio13-s3.13.1] The optimum pH is the pH at which the active site has the shape and charges that best fit the substrate.
[bio13-s3.13.2] A higher or lower hydrogen ion concentration, in more acidic or more alkaline conditions, changes the charges in the active site and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
[bio13-s3.13.3] Small changes from the optimum are reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme permanently.
[bio13-s3.13.4] Each enzyme's optimum suits the place where it works: pepsin in the acid stomach has an optimum of about pH 2; salivary amylase in the mouth about pH 7; trypsin in the alkaline small intestine about pH 8.
[bio13-s3.13.5] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against pH (x-axis); a peaked, bell-shaped curve with its peak labelled optimum pH at about 7 for catalase; optionally a second curve peaking at about pH 2, labelled pepsin.
[bio13-s3.14.b] Temperature has two opposite effects on an enzyme: it speeds up collisions, but too much heat destroys the enzyme. This investigation measures catalase activity across a range of water-bath temperatures.
[bio13-s3.14.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.14.1] Hypothesis: “As temperature increases, the rate of catalase activity increases up to an optimum temperature, because molecules move faster and collide more often; above the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme is denatured.”
[bio13-s3.14.2] Independent variable: temperature, set with water baths at e.g. 0 °C (ice water), 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, each checked with a thermometer because baths drift from their set temperature.
[bio13-s3.14.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.14.4] Controlled variables: pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.14.5] Control: boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract at each temperature, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.15.b] The temperature method reuses the foam method from the pH investigation, with the buffer fixed and the water bath changed.
[bio13-s3.15.0] Method: follow the pH investigation method, but with pH 7 buffer in every cylinder, and with each cylinder (buffer, extract and washing-up liquid) and its tube of hydrogen peroxide left in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes before the peroxide is added.
[bio13-s3.15.1] Check the bath temperature with the thermometer during the 2 minutes, because it can drift; repeat three times at each temperature and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.15.2] The same method works with amylase and starch, timing the starch's disappearance at each temperature, as in the enzyme concentration investigation.
[bio13-s3.15.3] Diagram: the pH set-up, with a row of water baths at different labelled temperatures.
[bio13-s3.16.b] The curve rises slowly to a peak and then drops steeply. The rise and the fall have different causes.
[bio13-s3.16.0] Expected results: little foam at 0 °C; the rate increases as temperature rises, to a maximum at the optimum temperature; above the optimum the rate falls steeply, with little or no activity at the highest temperatures tested.
[bio13-s3.16.1] The graph is not symmetrical: a gradual rise and a steep fall. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 °C, body temperature.
[bio13-s3.16.2] Explanation of the rise: as temperature increases, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), move faster and collide more often, and more collisions have enough energy to react, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second.
[bio13-s3.16.3] Over the lower part of the range, the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (an approximate pattern, not an exact law).
[bio13-s3.16.4] Explanation of the fall: above the optimum, the enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured.
[bio13-s3.16.5] As temperature rises further, more enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls even though collisions are more frequent.
[bio13-s3.16.6] At low temperature the enzyme is inactive, not denatured: the molecules move too slowly for many collisions, but the active site keeps its shape, so activity returns when the enzyme is warmed (shown in the heat denaturation investigation).
[bio13-s3.16.7] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against temperature in °C (x-axis); a curve rising gradually to a peak labelled optimum temperature, then falling steeply to zero, with the falling part labelled enzyme denatured.
[bio13-s3.17.b] Heat can do more than slow an enzyme: it can destroy its shape for good. This investigation shows that boiled catalase stays inactive even after it is cooled.
[bio13-s3.17.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of heat denaturation on the activity of catalase.
[bio13-s3.17.1] Denaturation is the permanent loss of an enzyme's shape, and so of its active site and its activity, caused by high temperature or extreme pH.
[bio13-s3.17.2] Hypothesis: “Catalase that has been boiled will not break down hydrogen peroxide, even when cooled, because its active site has been permanently changed.”
[bio13-s3.17.3] Independent variable: whether the extract has been boiled (heat-treated) or not. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³).
[bio13-s3.17.4] Controlled variables: both extracts tested at the same temperature (e.g. 25 °C) and pH (pH 7 buffer), with the same volumes of extract, buffer and hydrogen peroxide, the same drop of washing-up liquid and the same time.
[bio13-s3.17.5] Control: the unboiled extract, which shows how much foam active catalase produces under the same conditions, so the boiled result has something to be compared with.
[bio13-s3.18.b] Half the extract is boiled and half is not; both are then tested at the same temperature, so any difference is due to boiling alone.
[bio13-s3.18.0] 1. Divide the celery extract between two labelled boiling tubes, A and B.
[bio13-s3.18.1] 2. Place tube A in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes; leave tube B at room temperature.
[bio13-s3.18.2] 3. Cool tube A, then stand both tubes in the 25 °C water bath for 5 minutes, so the boiled extract is tested at the same temperature as the unboiled.
[bio13-s3.18.3] 4. Set up two graduated cylinders, each with 10 cm³ of pH 7 buffer, a drop of washing-up liquid and 2 cm³ of extract A or B.
[bio13-s3.18.4] 5. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide to each and read the foam after 2 minutes. Repeat three times.
[bio13-s3.18.5] Expected results: the boiled extract gives little or no foam; the unboiled extract gives a large column of foam. Any small amount of foam from the boiled extract comes from hydrogen peroxide breaking down slowly on its own, or from catalase that was not fully denatured.
[bio13-s3.18.6] Conclusion: boiling denatured the catalase; its activity did not return on cooling, so the change is permanent. This supports the hypothesis and explains why boiled enzyme is used as the control in the other investigations.
[bio13-s3.18.7] Extension: extract kept in ice water for 10 minutes and then warmed to 25 °C gives as much foam as unchilled extract, showing that cooling makes an enzyme inactive without denaturing it, whereas heating denatures it.
[bio13-s3.18.8] The same investigation works with amylase: boiled amylase added to starch leaves the iodine test blue-black at every sampling time, while unboiled amylase digests the starch so the iodine stays yellow-brown.
[bio13-s3.19.b] Secondary data are results that someone else collected. They let you check and extend your own conclusions, and the specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data.
[bio13-s3.19.0] Secondary data are data collected by someone else: results pooled from other groups in the class, published tables, textbooks or research papers, or online databases.
[bio13-s3.19.1] Uses: compare your optimum with published optima (the optimum pH of digestive enzymes and the optimum temperature of human enzymes given in the pH and temperature investigations).
[bio13-s3.19.2] Uses: check whether your trend agrees with other groups' (reproducibility), and extend a conclusion beyond the range you could test.
[bio13-s3.19.3] Secondary data on enzymes from extreme environments support the link between structure and conditions: Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, works best at about 72 °C and is not denatured at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s3.19.4] When using secondary data, record its source, check that it was measured under comparable conditions (same enzyme source, substrate, pH or temperature), and judge whether the source is reliable. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio13-s3.20.b] Every method has weaknesses. Knowing where the error comes from tells you how to reduce it.
[bio13-s3.20.0] Foam volume is imprecise: bubbles burst, the top of the foam is uneven, and foam may stick to the cylinder walls. Read at eye level at exactly the same time each run; collecting the oxygen in a gas syringe is more accurate.
[bio13-s3.20.1] Gas is lost in the time between adding the enzyme and inserting the bung, or through leaks; some oxygen dissolves in the water when it is collected over water.
[bio13-s3.20.2] Water-bath temperatures drift, especially in beakers of hot water or ice; check with a thermometer throughout or use a thermostatically controlled bath.
[bio13-s3.20.3] The reaction mixture may not reach the bath temperature if equilibration is too short, so allow the full time.
[bio13-s3.21.b] Some errors come from the enzyme and reagents themselves, or from judging a colour change. Repeating and averaging makes results more reliable.
[bio13-s3.21.0] Enzyme activity varies between celery samples and falls as an extract stands, so make one batch, keep it cool and use it quickly for all the tests; a fresh batch for a repeat can give different values.
[bio13-s3.21.1] Hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down on standing, especially in light, so its concentration may fall during the session: use one fresh stock kept in a dark bottle.
[bio13-s3.21.2] The iodine end-point is a judgement of colour, and sampling every 30 seconds means the time is only known to within 30 seconds; sample more often near the end-point, compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter.
[bio13-s3.21.3] Measure volumes with syringes or graduated pipettes rather than by eye, and use a clean syringe or dropper for each solution to avoid contamination.
[bio13-s3.21.4] Repeat each value three times, calculate the mean, and identify and investigate any anomalous result; pool class data to check reproducibility.
[bio13-s3.21.5] Use at least five values of the independent variable so the shape of the graph (peak or plateau) can be seen.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio5-s1: Characteristics of living things
[bio5-s1.0.b] Biology is the study of living things, called organisms. No single feature separates living from non-living, so the specification lists eleven characteristics: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis. Together they define life.
[bio5-s1.0.0] An organism shows all eleven characteristics at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once. A dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but it is still alive.
[bio5-s1.0.1] Some non-living things show one or two characteristics. A crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells.
[bio5-s1.0.2] A fire seems to 'use oxygen', 'release energy' and 'spread', but it has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions, so it is not alive.
[bio5-s1.0.3] Something is alive only if it shows the full set, not just one or two features. Viruses are the main borderline case. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio5-s1.1.b] Living things are not random mixtures of chemicals: they are built in an ordered way, and the basic building block is the cell. Two of the eleven characteristics, organisation and cellular basis, describe this.
[bio5-s1.1.0] Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms (those made of many cells), cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs.
[bio5-s1.1.1] Even a single cell has organised parts called organelles (small structures inside a cell, each with its own job). There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio5-s1.1.2] Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life, so it is the basic unit of structure and function.
[bio5-s1.1.3] Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process. Multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells, each adapted to one job.
[bio5-s1.1.4] All cells share a cell membrane, cytoplasm (the jelly-like fluid inside the cell), ribosomes (which make proteins) and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
[bio5-s1.1.5] All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division. New cells are never made from non-living material.
[bio5-s1.1.6] These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century. Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
[bio5-s1.2.b] Every organism needs a supply of food to stay alive and to build and repair its body. Nutrition is how it gets that food, and organisms fall into two groups depending on whether they make it or take it in.
[bio5-s1.2.0] Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
[bio5-s1.2.1] Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances (ones not made by living things, such as carbon dioxide and water). Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio5-s1.2.2] A few bacteria are chemosynthetic: instead of light, they use energy from chemical reactions to make their food, so they can live where there is no sunlight.
[bio5-s1.2.3] Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals.
[bio5-s1.2.4] Fungi and many bacteria are also heterotrophs: they absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio5-s1.3.b] Food is only useful once its stored energy can be released in a form cells can use. Respiration does this inside every living cell, and it is easily confused with breathing.
[bio5-s1.3.0] Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food, mainly glucose, inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy), which powers the cell's activities.
[bio5-s1.3.1] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
[bio5-s1.3.2] Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio5-s1.3.3] In human muscle, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid. In yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[bio5-s1.3.4] Respiration is not the same as breathing. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange), while respiration is a chemical process in cells.
[bio5-s1.3.5] Plants respire all the time, day and night, because every living cell needs energy continuously. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio5-s1.4.b] Living cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Metabolism is the name for all of them, and they fall into two types: those that build up and those that break down.
[bio5-s1.4.0] Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction).
[bio5-s1.4.1] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose made from carbon dioxide and water) and protein synthesis from amino acids.
[bio5-s1.4.2] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, and the digestion of starch to maltose.
[bio5-s1.4.3] In short, anabolic means building up and using energy, while catabolic means breaking down and releasing energy. There is more on this in Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life.
[bio5-s1.5.b] Chemical reactions in cells make some products that the body cannot use. Excretion removes them before they cause harm.
[bio5-s1.5.0] Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism, which are substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed. If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
[bio5-s1.5.1] Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs. Urea, made in the liver from excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in urine. Excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
[bio5-s1.5.2] Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata (the tiny pores in leaves). Some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
[bio5-s1.5.3] Excretion is not egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.b] Cells work properly only when the conditions around them stay within narrow limits, even if the outside world changes. Homeostasis is the control that keeps them steady.
[bio5-s1.6.0] Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, such as body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance and pH, despite changes outside or inside the organism.
[bio5-s1.6.1] It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH, so a large change would slow or stop the reactions of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.2] For example, human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C, and insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. There is more on the mechanism, negative feedback, in Homeostasis.
[bio5-s1.7.b] Organisms must cope with a changing environment and must also produce new individuals, because every organism eventually dies. Response and reproduction cover these two needs.
[bio5-s1.7.0] Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
[bio5-s1.7.1] Animals respond quickly through the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio5-s1.7.2] Plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure, e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short, which reduces water loss. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio5-s1.7.3] Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
[bio5-s1.7.4] Asexual reproduction: one parent and no gametes (sex cells). The offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria and budding in yeast.
[bio5-s1.7.5] Sexual reproduction: two gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote (the first cell of the new organism). The offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans and flowering plants.
[bio5-s1.8.b] Organisms get bigger as they develop, and they pass their features on to the next generation. Growth and heredity describe these two processes.
[bio5-s1.8.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism, the building reactions of metabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
[bio5-s1.8.1] Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips. Most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
[bio5-s1.8.2] Heredity: the passing of genetic information, carried by genes made of DNA, from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. 'Like produces like', e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
[bio5-s1.8.3] Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits (its characteristics). Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance.
[bio5-s1.9.b] Here is the full set in one place for quick revision. An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life.
[bio5-s1.9.0] Organisation: highly ordered structure, from cells up to organ systems.
[bio5-s1.9.1] Response: detecting a stimulus and reacting to it.
[bio5-s1.9.2] Respiration: releasing energy from food inside cells.
[bio5-s1.9.3] Nutrition: obtaining food for energy and raw materials.
[bio5-s1.9.4] Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.9.5] Reproduction: producing new individuals of the same species.
[bio5-s1.10.b] These are the remaining five characteristics, covering growth, chemistry, stability, inheritance and cells.
[bio5-s1.10.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass.
[bio5-s1.10.1] Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism.
[bio5-s1.10.2] Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment relatively constant.
[bio5-s1.10.3] Heredity: passing genetic information from parents to offspring.
[bio5-s1.10.4] Cellular basis: made of cells, which arise only from pre-existing cells.

CANDIDATES bio8-s7: Investigation: food tests
[bio8-s7.0.b] Food tests are a fair, planned investigation, not just a set of recipes. Before testing anything you decide what you are trying to find out, what you predict, and what you will change, measure and keep the same.
[bio8-s7.0.0] Aim: to find out which nutrients (starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid) are present in a range of food samples. The investigation is qualitative, which means you record whether a nutrient is present or absent, from a colour change or other observation, not how much of it there is.
[bio8-s7.0.1] Hypothesis, e.g.: “Potato contains starch but not lipid; milk contains reducing sugar (lactose), protein and lipid.” Students predict the result for each food from what the food is, so the results can later support or contradict the prediction.
[bio8-s7.0.2] Independent variable (the one you change): the food sample tested. Dependent variable (the one you observe): the result of each test, the colour or observation, recorded as present or absent.
[bio8-s7.0.3] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the volume of food solution and of reagent, the concentration of the reagents, the water-bath temperature and heating time for Benedict's, and clean apparatus for every test to avoid carry-over from one sample to the next.
[bio8-s7.0.4] Every test needs two controls. A negative control uses distilled water in place of the food and shows the colour when the nutrient is absent.
[bio8-s7.0.5] A positive control uses a known pure sample, such as starch solution, glucose solution, egg-white (albumen) solution or vegetable oil, and shows the colour when the nutrient is present.
[bio8-s7.0.6] Results for the foods are judged against these controls, so you can tell a real colour change from the reagent's starting colour, and know the test itself is working.
[bio8-s7.1.b] Choose foods that you expect to be rich in each nutrient, and gather the equipment and reagents (the chemicals that react with a nutrient to give a visible result) before you start.
[bio8-s7.1.0] Example food samples: potato, bread, rice or flour (starch); glucose, honey, grapes, fruit juice and milk (reducing sugar); egg white, milk, meat or cheese (protein); butter, vegetable oil, nuts, crisps and cheese (lipid); and table sugar, which gives a negative Benedict's result.
[bio8-s7.1.1] Equipment: test tubes and rack, test-tube holder, droppers, graduated pipettes or syringes (to measure equal volumes), spotting tile, mortar and pestle or knife and chopping board, beaker of distilled water, filter funnel and filter paper.
[bio8-s7.1.2] Also needed: a hot water bath (a beaker of near-boiling water from a kettle, or an electric water bath) with a thermometer, brown paper, labels, safety glasses and gloves.
[bio8-s7.1.3] Reagents: iodine solution (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide), Benedict's solution, biuret reagent (or sodium hydroxide solution and dilute copper sulfate solution) and Sudan III stain.
[bio8-s7.2.b] Most tests work on a liquid, so solid foods must first be turned into a food solution. This preparation is common to all the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.0] 1. Crush or grind a solid food with a little distilled water using a mortar and pestle. Crushing breaks open the cells so the nutrients can mix into the water.
[bio8-s7.2.1] 2. Add more water, stir, and filter or let the solid settle, then use the liquid food solution for the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.2] 3. Test liquid foods such as milk and juice directly, or dilute them.
[bio8-s7.2.3] 4. Label each tube with the food and the test, so the results cannot be mixed up.
[bio8-s7.3.b] Starch is the carbohydrate plants store, and iodine solution is the quickest way to detect it. It needs no heating and gives a result at once.
[bio8-s7.3.0] Method, step 1: place a few drops of food solution, or a small piece of solid food, on a white spotting tile, or place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. The white tile makes the colour easy to see.
[bio8-s7.3.1] Step 2: add a few drops of iodine solution. Step 3: observe the colour at once. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.3.2] Step 4: repeat with water as the negative control and starch solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.3.3] Result: iodine solution is yellow-brown (orange-brown) at the start. It turns blue-black if starch is present and stays yellow-brown if starch is absent.
[bio8-s7.4.b] Some sugars can react chemically with Benedict's solution and others cannot. This test detects the ones that can, and it is the one food test that needs heat.
[bio8-s7.4.0] A reducing sugar is one that can reduce (give electrons to) the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution. Glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose and lactose are reducing sugars; sucrose is not.
[bio8-s7.4.1] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add an equal volume, 2 cm³, of Benedict's solution and mix; the mixture is blue.
[bio8-s7.4.2] Step 3: place the tube in a hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) for about 5 minutes. Never heat the tube directly in a Bunsen flame, because the liquid can spurt out.
[bio8-s7.4.3] Step 4: observe the colour. Step 5: run water as the negative control and glucose solution as the positive control, in the same water bath at the same time so the conditions are identical.
[bio8-s7.4.4] Result: if a reducing sugar is present, the blue solution turns green, then yellow, then orange, and finally a brick-red precipitate (solid particles) forms. It stays blue if no reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.5] The reducing sugar reduces blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, the brick-red precipitate. The colour reached gives a rough idea of how much sugar is present: green for a little, brick red for a lot. There is more on this in Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively.
[bio8-s7.4.6] Fehling's test is an alternative: equal volumes of Fehling's solutions A and B are mixed with the sample and heated in a water bath. A blue mixture turns to a brick-red precipitate if a reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.7] Extension: sucrose stays blue. To show it is made of reducing sugars, boil a sucrose solution with a few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid to hydrolyse it to glucose and fructose. Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate until fizzing stops, then do the Benedict's test: it now turns brick red.
[bio8-s7.5.b] Biuret detects the bonds that join amino acids together in a protein. Like iodine, it works at room temperature with no heating.
[bio8-s7.5.0] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add 2 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution and mix (or add biuret reagent, which already contains it).
[bio8-s7.5.1] Step 3: add a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution, one drop at a time, shaking gently. Step 4: leave for a few minutes and observe. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.5.2] Step 5: run water as the negative control and egg-white solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.5.3] Result: the blue colour turns purple (violet or lilac) if protein is present, and stays blue if protein is absent. The copper ions react with the peptide bonds (the links between amino acids) in the protein, so the test detects peptide bonds, not single amino acids.
[bio8-s7.6.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do not dissolve in water, and two simple tests use this. They are alternatives, so you can use either one.
[bio8-s7.6.0] Grease-spot (brown paper) test, method: rub a small amount of food on a piece of brown paper, or place a drop of liquid food on it. Place a drop of water on another part of the paper as a control.
[bio8-s7.6.1] Leave the paper to dry, or warm it gently away from flames, then hold it up to the light.
[bio8-s7.6.2] Grease-spot result: a translucent (see-through), greasy spot that does not disappear when the paper dries shows that lipid is present. The water spot also looks translucent at first but disappears as it dries, which is why the paper must dry before the result is judged.
[bio8-s7.6.3] Sudan III test, method: place 2 cm³ of food solution, or a little crushed food in 2 cm³ of water, in a test tube. Add a few drops of Sudan III, a red dye that dissolves in lipid but not in water.
[bio8-s7.6.4] Shake gently and leave to stand for a minute. Run water as the negative control and vegetable oil in water as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.6.5] Sudan III result: if lipid is present, a red-stained oily layer forms on top of the water, because lipid is less dense than water and does not mix with it. With no lipid, the dye spreads through the water or settles as red specks, with no red oily layer.
[bio8-s7.7.b] Several reagents here are corrosive, harmful or flammable, and the Benedict's test uses near-boiling water. Safe practice is part of any investigation.
[bio8-s7.7.0] Wear safety glasses throughout. Sodium hydroxide (biuret test) is corrosive and especially harmful to the eyes. Benedict's and copper sulfate solutions are harmful if swallowed or splashed. Wash any splash off the skin with plenty of water.
[bio8-s7.7.1] Heat Benedict's tests only in a water bath, never directly over a flame, because the liquid can boil suddenly and spurt out of the tube. Use a test-tube holder, point the tube away from people, and take care with near-boiling water.
[bio8-s7.7.2] Sudan III stain is usually dissolved in ethanol, an alcohol that is highly flammable, so keep it away from flames. Do not warm brown paper near a flame.
[bio8-s7.7.3] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes; wear gloves if possible.
[bio8-s7.7.4] Never taste or eat food in the laboratory. Be aware of food allergies (e.g. to nuts) among the class. Clean up spills and wash hands after the practical.
[bio8-s7.8.b] Your observations are the primary data (results you collected yourself). A clear table lets you compare every food with the controls and draw a conclusion from the evidence.
[bio8-s7.8.0] Record primary data in a results table: one row per food and one column per test, recording the colour observed and whether the nutrient is present or absent (or + or −). Include the control rows.
[bio8-s7.8.1] Expected results: potato shows starch; glucose, honey and grapes show reducing sugar; table sugar is Benedict's negative, as sucrose is non-reducing; egg white shows protein; vegetable oil and butter show lipid; milk shows reducing sugar, protein and lipid; distilled water is negative for all tests.
[bio8-s7.8.2] Conclusion: state which nutrients each food contains, supported by the results compared with the controls, and say whether this supports the hypothesis. The tests show presence only, not the amount.
[bio8-s7.9.b] Food tests are simple, so small mistakes easily give a wrong result. Knowing the common errors, and how to reduce them, makes the conclusion more reliable.
[bio8-s7.9.0] Colour judgements are subjective, so different people may see different shades. Compare each tube with the controls against a white background, and have two people check the result.
[bio8-s7.9.1] Coloured foods, such as dark fruit juice, can mask a colour change. Dilute or filter them, or use a control of the food without the reagent.
[bio8-s7.9.2] Contamination: reusing a dropper or test tube can carry a nutrient from one sample to another, giving a false positive.
[bio8-s7.9.3] A very small amount of nutrient may not give a visible change, which is a false negative. Some nutrients are inside cells, so the food must be well crushed.
[bio8-s7.9.4] Benedict's detects reducing sugars only, so a food rich in sucrose can give a negative result even though it contains sugar.
[bio8-s7.9.5] Repeat each test, e.g. three times, to check that the results are repeatable.
[bio8-s7.9.6] Diagram: a test tube of food solution with Benedict's solution standing in a beaker of hot water, with a thermometer, labelled.
