PART (b)(ii)1 (4 marks) subsections: bio29-s3
MODEL ANSWER: Location: the SA node is in the wall of the right atrium, near where the superior vena cava enters. Role: it is the pacemaker. It produces electrical impulses at regular intervals, which spread over the atria so they contract, and it sets the rate of the heartbeat.

PART (b)(ii)2 (4 marks) subsections: bio29-s3
MODEL ANSWER: Location: the AV node is at the base of the right atrium, near the septum (between the atria and ventricles). Role: it receives the impulse from the atria, delays it briefly, then passes it down the septum along conducting fibres so the ventricles contract.

PART (b)(iii) (2 marks) subsections: bio29-s3
MODEL ANSWER: The force (pressure) the blood exerts on the walls of the arteries. It is measured as two values: the higher pressure when the ventricles contract and the lower pressure when the heart relaxes between beats.

PART (c)(i)1 (1 marks) subsections: bio19-s3
MODEL ANSWER: Dendrites receive impulses and carry them towards the cell body.

PART (c)(i)2 (1 marks) subsections: bio19-s3
MODEL ANSWER: The axon carries impulses away from the cell body (to the next neuron or the effector).

PART (c)(i)3 (1 marks) subsections: bio19-s3
MODEL ANSWER: The cell body makes the neurotransmitter (and the proteins the neuron needs).

PART (c)(ii) (18 marks) subsections: bio19-s4, bio19-s5
MODEL ANSWER: 1. The impulse arrives at the synaptic knob of the presynaptic neuron. 2. Calcium ions enter the knob, which makes the vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. 3. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft. 4. It binds to specific receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, which opens ion channels, and a new impulse starts in the next (postsynaptic) neuron. 5. An enzyme in the cleft then breaks down the neurotransmitter (or it is taken back into the presynaptic neuron by reuptake), so the signal switches off.

PART (c)(iii) (3 marks) subsections: bio19-s3
MODEL ANSWER: The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer around the axon. It speeds up the impulse, which jumps between the gaps in the sheath (nodes of Ranvier).

CANDIDATES bio19-s3: Neurons
[bio19-s3.0.b] Your body has to pass information quickly between receptors (cells that detect changes), the brain and spinal cord, and muscles and glands. Neurons are the cells that do this job, and there are three types, each with its own role.
[bio19-s3.0.0] A neuron, or nerve cell, is the basic unit of the nervous system; it carries electrochemical nerve impulses (tiny electrical-and-chemical signals that travel along the cell).
[bio19-s3.0.1] Neurons are long and thin, so a single cell can connect distant parts of the body, for example the skin of the foot and the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.0.2] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect a stimulus) to the CNS (the central nervous system: brain and spinal cord).
[bio19-s3.0.3] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands, which carry out the response).
[bio19-s3.0.4] Interneurons, also called relay or connector neurons, carry impulses between neurons within the CNS, so they link the incoming and outgoing pathways.
[bio19-s3.1.b] Every neuron is built from the same three main regions: a cell body, branched dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that sends them on. The shape of each region suits its job.
[bio19-s3.1.0] The cell body contains the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm and organelles, which keeps the cell alive.
[bio19-s3.1.1] The cell body also makes the neurotransmitter (the chemical that passes the signal to the next cell) and the proteins the neuron needs.
[bio19-s3.1.2] Dendrites are short, branched extensions that receive impulses from other neurons or receptors and carry them towards the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.3] Branching gives dendrites a large surface, so one neuron can receive signals from many others.
[bio19-s3.1.4] The axon is a long single fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.5] The axon's length lets impulses reach distant effectors, for example a muscle in the foot.
[bio19-s3.2.b] Many axons are wrapped in a fatty coat that makes impulses travel faster. Fast conduction matters because the body must respond quickly, for example when you pull your hand from something hot.
[bio19-s3.2.0] The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer around many axons, so the impulse does not leak away along the way.
[bio19-s3.2.1] It speeds up the impulse, which jumps between the small gaps in the sheath called nodes of Ranvier.
[bio19-s3.2.2] Schwann cells are cells that wrap around the axon in the PNS (the peripheral nervous system: the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord), their membrane forming the myelin sheath.
[bio19-s3.3.b] At its far end an axon has to hand the impulse on to the next cell, whether another neuron, a muscle or a gland. The axon terminals are built for this hand-over.
[bio19-s3.3.0] The axon ends in swollen synaptic knobs, also called axon terminals.
[bio19-s3.3.1] Neurotransmitter vesicles are tiny sacs in the synaptic knobs that store neurotransmitter, so a supply is ready to be released.
[bio19-s3.3.2] The neurotransmitter is released to pass the impulse to the next cell. There is more on this in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s3.4.b] A motor neuron carries the command from the CNS out to an effector. Its structure is arranged to collect many inputs inside the CNS and then send one signal a long way out.
[bio19-s3.4.0] A motor neuron has its cell body at one end, inside the CNS, with many dendrites.
[bio19-s3.4.1] Its many dendrites receive input from many interneurons, so one motor neuron can combine signals from many sources.
[bio19-s3.4.2] It has one long axon, which ends in axon terminals on a muscle or gland, so the axon reaches the effector wherever it lies in the body.
[bio19-s3.5.b] A sensory neuron brings information in from a receptor to the CNS. It has a different layout from a motor neuron, with its cell body off to one side.
[bio19-s3.5.0] A sensory neuron has a long fibre, the dendron, which carries impulses from the receptor to the cell body. A dendron is not the same as a dendrite, which is short and branched.
[bio19-s3.5.1] Its cell body sits to one side of the fibre, in a ganglion (a cluster of cell bodies) outside the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.2] A shorter axon carries the impulse on into the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.3] The long dendron reaches from a receptor, for example in the skin of the foot, to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.5.4] The cell body lies off the main pathway, so it does not slow the impulse.
[bio19-s3.6.b] Between the sensory and motor neurons sits a third type that links them. Interneurons make the CNS a network rather than a set of separate wires.
[bio19-s3.6.0] Interneurons are short, with many branched connections, so each can link with many other neurons.
[bio19-s3.6.1] They are found only in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.6.2] They connect sensory neurons to motor neurons and link many neurons together.
[bio19-s3.7.b] You are often asked to label a neuron diagram or to say which type is shown. Practise drawing both types so that you can place every part and show the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.7.0] Diagram: a motor neuron and a sensory neuron, labelling cell body, nucleus, dendrites, dendron (sensory), axon, myelin sheath, Schwann cell, node of Ranvier, axon terminals, neurotransmitter vesicles, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.8.b] The importance of myelin shows when it is damaged. Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which this happens.
[bio19-s3.8.0] Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease (the immune system attacks the body's own tissue) in which the body's immune system damages the myelin sheath in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.8.1] Damaged myelin slows or blocks impulses, because the insulation that lets them jump between the nodes of Ranvier is lost.
[bio19-s3.8.2] Symptoms include problems with movement, balance and vision.

CANDIDATES bio19-s4: Neurotransmission
[bio19-s4.0.b] A nerve impulse (a signal carried by nerve cells, called neurons) rarely travels through just one neuron. It passes along a chain of neurons from receptor to effector, and the signal changes form along the way.
[bio19-s4.0.0] The chain runs receptor (detects the stimulus), sensory neuron (carries the signal towards the central nervous system), interneuron or interneurons (link neurons inside the central nervous system), motor neuron (carries the signal outwards), effector (the muscle or gland that responds).
[bio19-s4.0.1] Within a single neuron the impulse is electrical: a wave of electrical change along the membrane, caused by ions (charged particles) moving in and out of the neuron.
[bio19-s4.0.2] Between neurons the impulse is passed on chemically, across a synapse (the junction between two neurons). So the signal is electrical inside each neuron and chemical in the gaps between them.
[bio19-s4.1.b] The neurons in a chain do not physically touch, so something must carry the signal from one to the next. That job is done at the synapse, using a chemical messenger.
[bio19-s4.1.0] A synapse is the junction between two neurons, or between a neuron and an effector such as a muscle fibre (a single muscle cell).
[bio19-s4.1.1] The two cells do not touch; a tiny gap called the synaptic cleft separates them, which is why the electrical impulse cannot simply carry on across.
[bio19-s4.1.2] The neuron before the cleft is the presynaptic neuron; the one after it is the postsynaptic neuron. Pre means before and post means after.
[bio19-s4.1.3] The presynaptic axon (the long fibre of the neuron) ends in a swollen synaptic knob containing mitochondria, which supply energy, and vesicles (small membrane sacs) of neurotransmitter.
[bio19-s4.1.4] A neurotransmitter is a chemical released by a neuron that carries the impulse across a synapse.
[bio19-s4.1.5] Examples of neurotransmitters are acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine and serotonin.
[bio19-s4.2.b] A labelled synapse diagram is a common exam task, and it also shows you where each part sits relative to the cleft.
[bio19-s4.2.0] Diagram: synapse labelling presynaptic neuron, synaptic knob, mitochondria, vesicles, neurotransmitter molecules, synaptic cleft, receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, postsynaptic neuron, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s4.3.b] Crossing the cleft is a sequence of steps in which the electrical impulse is converted into a chemical signal and then back into an electrical one. Learn the steps in order.
[bio19-s4.3.0] 1. The impulse arrives at the synaptic knob.
[bio19-s4.3.1] 2. This causes calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to move into the knob.
[bio19-s4.3.2] 3. The calcium ions trigger vesicles to move to the presynaptic membrane, fuse with it and release neurotransmitter into the cleft.
[bio19-s4.3.3] 4. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft. The gap is tiny, so this is quick.
[bio19-s4.3.4] 5. It binds to specific receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane; each receptor fits only its own neurotransmitter, like a key in a lock.
[bio19-s4.3.5] 6. Binding opens ion channels in the postsynaptic membrane, ions move in, and a new impulse starts in the postsynaptic neuron if enough neurotransmitter binds.
[bio19-s4.3.6] Mitochondria in the knob supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) for making and releasing neurotransmitter.
[bio19-s4.4.b] Synapses are not just gaps to cross. Because the signal is chemical, they control how signals travel and which ones get through.
[bio19-s4.4.0] Impulses cross in one direction only, because only the presynaptic side has vesicles and only the postsynaptic side has receptors. The neurotransmitter therefore cannot send a signal backwards.
[bio19-s4.4.1] A weak stimulus may release too little neurotransmitter to start a new impulse, so synapses filter out unimportant signals.
[bio19-s4.4.2] One neuron can synapse with many others, so impulses can be spread to many cells or combined from many cells.
[bio19-s4.4.3] Many drugs act at synapses, since the chemical step can be altered. There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.

CANDIDATES bio19-s5: Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption
[bio19-s5.0.b] A synapse is the junction where one neuron passes a signal to the next across a tiny gap, the synaptic cleft. The signal crosses the gap as a chemical, a neurotransmitter, and the release of that chemical is switched on by ions.
[bio19-s5.0.0] Release is triggered by ions: calcium ions entering the synaptic knob (the swollen tip of the presynaptic neuron, the neuron sending the signal) are the trigger for the neurotransmitter to be let out.
[bio19-s5.0.1] Release lasts only a short time, so each impulse produces a brief burst of neurotransmitter rather than a steady flow.
[bio19-s5.0.2] This brief burst passes the impulse to the next neuron, and because it is short, each impulse arriving at the knob gives its own separate signal. There is more on how the release happens in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s5.1.b] Once the neurotransmitter has done its job of passing on the impulse, it cannot be left in the cleft. An enzyme (a biological catalyst) in the cleft inactivates it.
[bio19-s5.1.0] After it has passed on the impulse, the neurotransmitter is inactivated by an enzyme in the synaptic cleft, so its message stops being delivered.
[bio19-s5.1.1] Example: the enzyme acetylcholinesterase breaks the neurotransmitter acetylcholine down into choline and ethanoic acid (acetate).
[bio19-s5.2.b] Removing the neurotransmitter is not tidiness. It is what lets a synapse carry a series of separate signals.
[bio19-s5.2.0] If the neurotransmitter stayed in the cleft, it would keep binding to the receptors on the postsynaptic neuron (the neuron receiving the signal), so that neuron would keep firing.
[bio19-s5.2.1] The signal could not be switched off, so repeated signals could not be told apart: one long continuous signal would replace a series of separate ones.
[bio19-s5.3.b] Neurons do not waste neurotransmitter. The presynaptic neuron takes back either the breakdown products or the intact neurotransmitter and reuses them.
[bio19-s5.3.0] The breakdown products are reabsorbed by the presynaptic neuron and used to make new neurotransmitter; for example, choline from the breakdown of acetylcholine is reabsorbed.
[bio19-s5.3.1] Serotonin and dopamine are mainly reabsorbed whole by transporter proteins (proteins in the membrane that carry a substance across it) in the presynaptic membrane, a process called reuptake. This is why they are cleared mainly by reuptake rather than by an enzyme.
[bio19-s5.4.b] Putting the pieces together, a synapse works as a loop. The same neurotransmitter is released, removed and then remade, ready for the next impulse.
[bio19-s5.4.0] 1. An impulse arrives at the synaptic knob, and calcium ions enter the knob.
[bio19-s5.4.1] 2. Vesicles (small membrane-bound sacs) release neurotransmitter into the cleft.
[bio19-s5.4.2] 3. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft.
[bio19-s5.4.3] 4. It binds to receptors (proteins that the neurotransmitter fits) on the postsynaptic membrane.
[bio19-s5.4.4] 5. A new impulse starts in the postsynaptic neuron.
[bio19-s5.4.5] 6. An enzyme breaks the neurotransmitter down, or it is taken back by reuptake.
[bio19-s5.4.6] 7. The presynaptic neuron reabsorbs the breakdown products or the neurotransmitter, and new neurotransmitter is made and stored in vesicles, ready for the next impulse.
[bio19-s5.4.7] Diagram: a synapse showing the presynaptic knob, vesicles, cleft and postsynaptic membrane with receptors. Label: enzyme in the cleft, reuptake arrows into the presynaptic knob.
[bio19-s5.5.b] Because the synapse depends on several steps working together, a fault at any step changes how signals pass. The result can be a disorder.
[bio19-s5.5.0] Disruption can come from too little or too much neurotransmitter, faulty receptors, or blocked breakdown or reuptake.
[bio19-s5.5.1] Disruption can cause short-term (acute) or long-term (chronic) disorders, depending on whether the fault is brief or lasting.
[bio19-s5.6.b] A quick, severe disorder shows how much depends on removing neurotransmitter. A poison that stops the removal step affects the synapses between nerves and muscles within a short time.
[bio19-s5.6.0] Nerve agents and organophosphate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine.
[bio19-s5.6.1] Acetylcholine then builds up at synapses with muscles, because it is no longer broken down, causing continuous muscle contraction and paralysis.
[bio19-s5.6.2] In severe cases death follows from failure of the breathing muscles.
[bio19-s5.7.b] Long-term disorders involve a lasting change in the neurons or the signalling that a neurotransmitter carries. Each example below involves a different neurotransmitter.
[bio19-s5.7.0] In Alzheimer's disease, neurons that use acetylcholine are lost, contributing to memory loss.
[bio19-s5.7.1] Depression is associated with altered signalling by serotonin and noradrenaline. Its causes are complex, so it should not be described as simply caused by low serotonin or as a chemical imbalance.
[bio19-s5.7.2] In Parkinson's disease, dopamine-producing neurons in the brain die, causing tremor, stiffness and slow movement.
[bio19-s5.8.b] A drug inhibits the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter, so the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft longer and its effect is enhanced.
[bio19-s5.8.0] Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, e.g. donepezil, are used to treat the symptoms of Alzheimer's disease: acetylcholine is broken down more slowly, so the remaining acetylcholine-using neurons are stimulated for longer.
[bio19-s5.8.1] Monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors are antidepressants that slow the breakdown of serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine, so these neurotransmitters stay in the cleft longer.
[bio19-s5.9.b] A drug blocks the transporter that reabsorbs a neurotransmitter, so more neurotransmitter stays in the cleft for longer.
[bio19-s5.9.0] Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), e.g. fluoxetine, are used to treat depression: with serotonin reabsorption blocked, serotonin acts on the postsynaptic neuron for longer.
[bio19-s5.9.1] Neither strategy cures the disorder; both treat symptoms by enhancing the remaining signalling, which means they work with the neurons that are still functioning.
[bio19-s5.10.b] Psychoactive drugs and painkillers work at synapses in similar ways to the treatments above. They block removal, copy a neurotransmitter, or block receptors.
[bio19-s5.10.0] A psychoactive drug alters brain function, affecting mood, perception or behaviour.
[bio19-s5.10.1] Cocaine blocks the reuptake of dopamine, so dopamine builds up in the cleft, producing euphoria. There is more on its effect on mood and addiction in Dopamine and endorphins.
[bio19-s5.10.2] Nicotine mimics acetylcholine, binding to and activating some of its receptors.
[bio19-s5.10.3] Opioid painkillers such as morphine bind to the same receptors as endorphins (the body's own pain-reducing neurotransmitters), mimicking them and reducing the sensation of pain.
[bio19-s5.10.4] A drug may instead block receptors, preventing transmission: curare blocks acetylcholine receptors on muscles, so acetylcholine cannot act on them, causing paralysis.
[bio19-s5.11.b] Every type of disruption ends in one of two outcomes for impulse travel: transmission is reduced or stopped, or it is prolonged or enhanced.
[bio19-s5.11.0] Blocked release or blocked receptors: less or no neurotransmitter acts, so transmission is reduced or stops.
[bio19-s5.11.1] Blocked breakdown or blocked reuptake: the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft, so transmission is prolonged or enhanced.
[bio19-s5.11.2] Mimics: a drug that activates receptors makes transmission occur without the natural neurotransmitter.

CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.

CANDIDATES bio28-s1: Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism
[bio28-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a steady supply of oxygen and must get rid of the carbon dioxide it makes. The breathing system is the body's way of swapping these gases with the air.
[bio28-s1.0.0] Every cell respires aerobically (releases energy from food using oxygen), so every cell uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s1.0.1] The breathing system exchanges gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid, the fluid that bathes the cells): oxygen is taken into the blood, and carbon dioxide and some water vapour are removed.
[bio28-s1.0.2] Breathing, or ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. It is not respiration, which is the chemical release of energy inside cells, and gas exchange is not respiration either.
[bio28-s1.0.3] A large animal such as a human cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin, because its surface area is too small for its volume and the distances to its inner cells are too great.
[bio28-s1.0.4] So it needs a specialised exchange surface, the alveoli (tiny air sacs in the lungs), and a transport system, the blood, to carry gases to and from every cell.
[bio28-s1.1.b] The breathing tract is the pathway air follows from outside to the lungs. The first parts clean, warm and moisten the air and keep food out of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.0] Nose (nasal cavity): hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and microorganisms. The lining has a rich blood supply that warms the air, and the mucus moistens it, so the delicate alveoli do not dry out or chill.
[bio28-s1.1.1] Breathing through the mouth skips this filtering, warming and moistening, which is why nose breathing is better for the lungs.
[bio28-s1.1.2] Pharynx (throat): the passage behind the nose and mouth that is shared by air and food.
[bio28-s1.1.3] Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage (firm, flexible tissue) that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink pass into the oesophagus (the food tube) instead of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.4] Choking happens when food enters the trachea instead.
[bio28-s1.1.5] Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea. It contains the vocal cords, which vibrate as air passes over them, producing sound.
[bio28-s1.2.b] Below the larynx the airway divides again and again, like an upside-down tree, until it reaches the alveoli where gases actually cross into the blood.
[bio28-s1.2.0] Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation.
[bio28-s1.2.1] The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
[bio28-s1.2.2] Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium (a surface layer of cells carrying tiny hair-like cilia) with mucus-secreting goblet cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms.
[bio28-s1.2.3] The cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the body's first line of defence against infection.
[bio28-s1.2.4] Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one to each lung. They are also supported by cartilage.
[bio28-s1.2.5] Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow.
[bio28-s1.2.6] The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels). The alveoli are the site of gas exchange.
[bio28-s1.3.b] The lungs have no muscle of their own, so they depend on the chest wall and on the muscles around it. The pleural membranes are what link the lungs to the chest wall.
[bio28-s1.3.0] The two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (ribs, sternum and vertebrae) and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm.
[bio28-s1.3.1] Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move.
[bio28-s1.3.2] The fluid also makes the lungs cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
[bio28-s1.3.3] Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax.
[bio28-s1.3.4] Intercostal muscles: two sets, external and internal, lying between the ribs, which move the rib cage.

[bio28-s1.4.1] When the thorax volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs follow the chest wall because they have no muscle of their own.
[bio28-s1.4.2] Inhalation (inspiration) is an active process, which means it uses energy. 1. The brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles.
[bio28-s1.4.3] 2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards.
[bio28-s1.4.4] 3. The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and sternum upwards and outwards.
[bio28-s1.4.5] 4. The volume of the thorax increases.
[bio28-s1.4.6] 5. The lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.4.7] 6. Air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP (the cell's energy currency), so inhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.5.b] Breathing out at rest needs no muscle contraction at all: the body simply springs back. During exercise, however, extra muscles make exhalation active.
[bio28-s1.5.0] Exhalation (expiration) at rest is a passive process, which means it uses no energy. 1. The impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax.
[bio28-s1.5.1] 2. The diaphragm returns to its dome shape, pushed up by the abdominal organs, and the ribs move down and in under their own weight.
[bio28-s1.5.2] 3. The stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils, like a stretched elastic band springing back.
[bio28-s1.5.3] 4. The volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.5.4] 5. Air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used.
[bio28-s1.5.5] Forced exhalation, as in exercise, coughing, blowing or speaking loudly: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up.
[bio28-s1.5.6] This expels air faster and more completely, and because muscles contract, forced exhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.6.b] Putting the two halves of the breathing cycle side by side shows that every change in one is reversed in the other.
[bio28-s1.6.0] Diaphragm: it contracts and flattens in inhalation, but relaxes and domes upwards in exhalation at rest.
[bio28-s1.6.1] External intercostal muscles: they contract in inhalation but relax in exhalation. The ribs move up and out in inhalation, and down and in in exhalation.
[bio28-s1.6.2] Thorax volume increases in inhalation and decreases in exhalation. Lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure in inhalation and rises above it in exhalation, so air moves in, then out.
[bio28-s1.6.3] Inhalation is active, whereas exhalation at rest is passive.
[bio28-s1.6.4] The bell-jar model shows the volume and pressure principle: pulling the rubber sheet (the diaphragm) down inflates the balloons (the lungs). There is more on this in Models.
[bio28-s1.6.5] Diagram: two side-view sketches of the thorax, one at inhalation and one at exhalation, showing the diaphragm (flat or domed) and the ribs (raised or lowered), with arrows for rib movement and air flow. Label the diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, lungs and trachea.
[bio28-s1.7.b] Breathing continues while you sleep because the brain controls it automatically. The signal that adjusts it is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood.
[bio28-s1.7.0] Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain. It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
[bio28-s1.7.1] We can change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum (the thinking part of the brain), for example by holding our breath, speaking or singing. The automatic control takes over again afterwards.
[bio28-s1.7.2] The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises, as during exercise when muscles respire faster, the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.3] This removes more CO₂ and takes in more oxygen. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.4] An adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath, the tidal volume.
[bio28-s1.7.5] During hard exercise both the rate and the depth (the volume of air per breath) increase greatly.
[bio28-s1.8.b] Gas exchange is a simple physical process: oxygen and carbon dioxide each move down their own concentration gradient between the air in the alveolus and the blood in the capillary beside it.
[bio28-s1.8.0] Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used, so it is not active transport.
[bio28-s1.8.1] Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration than the deoxygenated blood arriving from the body in the pulmonary artery.
[bio28-s1.8.2] Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma (the liquid part of the blood), and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio28-s1.8.3] Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than the air in the alveoli. CO₂, carried mostly in the plasma, diffuses out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
[bio28-s1.8.4] The oxygenated blood leaves the lungs in the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio28-s1.8.5] The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air.
[bio28-s1.8.6] Blood flow also keeps the gradients steep, because it constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.

[bio28-s1.9.2] Moist lining: gases dissolve in the moisture before they diffuse across the wall.
[bio28-s1.9.3] Dense capillary network: every alveolus is surrounded by capillaries with a continuous blood flow, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio28-s1.9.4] Narrow capillaries: they are just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, pressed against the wall. This shortens the distance further and slows the blood, giving time for exchange.
[bio28-s1.9.5] Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation, which helps push air out.

[bio28-s1.10.0] Inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with a variable amount of water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.1] Exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. It is saturated with water vapour and is warmer than inhaled air.
[bio28-s1.10.2] Nitrogen is not used by the body, so its proportion is the same in both.
[bio28-s1.10.3] Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths work in CPR (the emergency procedure that keeps a casualty's blood supplied with oxygen).
[bio28-s1.10.4] Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing that exhaled air contains more CO₂.

[bio28-s1.11.0] Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production, so mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing (smoker's cough) and frequent chest infections.
[bio28-s1.11.1] Emphysema, mainly caused by smoking: if alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces, the surface area for gas exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless. This shows why the large surface area of the alveoli matters.
[bio28-s1.11.2] Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the amount of oxygen the blood can carry. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio28-s1.11.3] Asthma: the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts, and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that less air reaches the alveoli. Inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle.

CANDIDATES bio29-s1: Investigation: the heart
[bio29-s1.0.b] The heart is a muscular pump that never stops, and its structure is built around one job: moving blood. It is divided into four chambers, two upper and two lower, split into a left and a right side by a central wall.
[bio29-s1.0.0] Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax (the chest) between the two lungs and behind the sternum (breastbone), with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left.
[bio29-s1.0.1] The heart is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers. The fluid reduces friction as the heart beats, and the membrane prevents the heart from over-stretching.
[bio29-s1.0.2] Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
[bio29-s1.0.3] Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries (the vessels that carry blood away from the heart), so their walls are thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.0.4] The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance. The right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels).
[bio29-s1.0.5] Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.
[bio29-s1.0.6] In other words, the heart is two separate pumps side by side: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.b] Blood has to arrive at the heart and leave it by separate routes. Veins bring blood in to the atria, and arteries carry it out from the ventricles.
[bio29-s1.1.0] Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it towards it; this is not about oxygen, which is why one artery and one set of veins break the usual pattern below.
[bio29-s1.1.1] The venae cavae bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium: the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms, and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body.
[bio29-s1.1.2] The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
[bio29-s1.1.3] Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs.
[bio29-s1.1.4] The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.5] The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself with blood. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.2.b] Blood pressure inside the heart changes sharply as the muscle squeezes, and blood would simply flow back where it came from without help. Valves are flaps of tissue that keep blood flowing in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.2.0] Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. A reminder is tRIcuspid on the RIght.
[bio29-s1.2.1] AV valves let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
[bio29-s1.2.2] The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (heart strings), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
[bio29-s1.2.3] Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets.
[bio29-s1.2.4] Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
[bio29-s1.3.b] The heart wall is made of a special muscle found nowhere else in the body. Its features suit it to beating without rest for a whole lifetime.
[bio29-s1.3.0] Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped) and branched, and are joined end to end by intercalated discs.
[bio29-s1.3.1] Intercalated discs let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell, so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit.
[bio29-s1.3.2] Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue).
[bio29-s1.3.3] Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. There is more on the pacemaker that starts each beat in Heartbeat.

[bio29-s1.8.5] Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.
[bio29-s1.9.b] The conclusion links each structure you found to its function, and states whether each hypothesis was supported by the evidence.
[bio29-s1.9.0] The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
[bio29-s1.9.1] Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
[bio29-s1.9.2] The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
[bio29-s1.9.3] The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
[bio29-s1.9.4] Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls. There is more on this in Blood vessels.
[bio29-s1.9.5] For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.
[bio29-s1.10.b] A dissected animal heart is not a living human heart, and the measurements are rough. Knowing the limits shows how far the conclusions can be trusted and how to improve them.
[bio29-s1.10.0] Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
[bio29-s1.10.1] Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
[bio29-s1.10.2] Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
[bio29-s1.10.3] A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
[bio29-s1.10.4] One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio29-s3: Heartbeat
[bio29-s3.0.b] The heart does not wait for instructions to beat. A small patch of muscle in its own wall sets the pace and sends a wave of electrical signals that makes the chambers contract in the right order.
[bio29-s3.0.0] A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles (the two lower chambers) into the arteries.
[bio29-s3.0.1] An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
[bio29-s3.0.2] Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid.
[bio29-s3.0.3] The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium (an upper chamber), near where the superior vena cava (the large vein from the upper body) enters.
[bio29-s3.0.4] The SA node produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
[bio29-s3.1.b] The impulse from the SA node does not reach every part at once. It travels in a set route with a built-in pause, which makes the atria contract first and the ventricles second.
[bio29-s3.1.0] 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.1] 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles, so the ventricles cannot contract too early.
[bio29-s3.1.2] 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum (the wall between the two sides of the heart). The AV node delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.3] 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex (the pointed lower tip of the heart), and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
[bio29-s3.1.4] 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery, the two arteries leaving the heart. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.
[bio29-s3.1.5] Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.
[bio29-s3.2.b] The SA node sets a basic rhythm, but the body's needs change from moment to moment. Nerves and a hormone adjust the rate the SA node sets, so the heart can speed up or slow down.
[bio29-s3.2.0] The rate is changed to match the body's needs. It rises during exercise, because the muscles then need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂, which the faster-flowing blood delivers and carries away.
[bio29-s3.2.1] Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata (part of the brainstem) sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down.
[bio29-s3.2.2] The medulla responds to information from receptors, which detect changes in the body. For example, a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate, and a rise in blood pressure slows it.
[bio29-s3.2.3] Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction.
[bio29-s3.2.4] Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
[bio29-s3.2.5] Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart.
[bio29-s3.2.6] The artificial pacemaker detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.
[bio29-s3.3.b] Each time the ventricles contract, they force a surge of blood into the arteries. You can feel this surge as the pulse, which gives an easy way to measure heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.0] The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.1] The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe.
[bio29-s3.3.2] Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries (the tiny vessels between arteries and veins) before the blood reaches them.
[bio29-s3.3.3] Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes, so the reading is a resting one.
[bio29-s3.3.4] 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
[bio29-s3.3.5] 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
[bio29-s3.3.6] 4. Repeat three times and take the mean, which reduces the effect of any one miscount.
[bio29-s3.3.7] If asked what the pulse is, say it is a wave in the artery wall, not blood flowing past your finger.
[bio29-s3.4.b] Exercise makes the muscles need more oxygen, so the heart speeds up. Measuring the pulse before and after exercise shows this, and how quickly it recovers shows how fit someone is.
[bio29-s3.4.0] This is a standard class investigation with primary data (data you collect yourself). Measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value.
[bio29-s3.4.1] The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people.
[bio29-s3.4.2] Fitter people also tend to have a lower resting pulse, because each beat pumps more blood.
[bio29-s3.4.3] Independent variable (the one you change): activity level (rest, or after exercise). Dependent variable (the one you measure): pulse rate in beats per minute.
[bio29-s3.4.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.
[bio29-s3.5.b] Blood only flows round the body because the ventricles push it with force. That force, pressing on the walls of the vessels, is blood pressure, and when it stays too high it harms the body.
[bio29-s3.5.0] Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.5.1] Pressure is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles (small arteries) and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae.
[bio29-s3.5.2] Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
[bio29-s3.5.3] Pressure is given as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.4] Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.5] Hypertension usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
[bio29-s3.5.6] Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet, being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.5.7] High blood pressure is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.
[bio29-s3.6.b] The heart works non-stop, so its muscle needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose. It cannot get these from the blood inside its chambers, so it has a separate circulation of its own.
[bio29-s3.6.0] The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
[bio29-s3.6.1] The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve (the valve at the exit of the left ventricle). They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle.
[bio29-s3.6.2] These small arteries supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration (energy release using oxygen) that keeps it working continuously.
[bio29-s3.6.3] Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.
[bio29-s3.6.4] Diagram: a front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins. Label each.
[bio29-s3.7.b] The coronary arteries are narrow, so damage to them has serious effects. When they narrow or block, the heart muscle is starved of oxygen.
[bio29-s3.7.0] Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
[bio29-s3.7.1] If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction.
[bio29-s3.7.2] Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after a heart attack can break down the clot and restore blood flow to the starved muscle, so they limit the damage (see 'Industrial enzymes').
[bio29-s3.7.3] Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol, high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.7.4] Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight, which each remove a risk factor.
[bio29-s3.7.5] Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter (a thin tube) is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open.
[bio29-s3.7.6] Alternatively, the narrowed artery can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.

CANDIDATES bio29-s4: Blood
[bio29-s4.0.b] Blood is a tissue, like muscle or bone, but a liquid one. It is made of cells and cell fragments carried in a fluid, and each part has its own job.
[bio29-s4.0.0] Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
[bio29-s4.0.1] If blood, with a chemical added to stop it clotting, is spun in a centrifuge (a machine that spins samples so the heaviest parts sink), it separates into about 55% plasma, a straw-coloured liquid on top, and about 45% cells.
[bio29-s4.0.2] The cells form a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
[bio29-s4.0.3] Numbers in a healthy adult: red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. Red cells therefore greatly outnumber white cells, by several hundred to one.
[bio29-s4.0.4] All blood cells and platelets are made in the red bone marrow, found in spongy bone such as the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones. They come from stem cells (unspecialised cells that can become other cell types) that divide by mitosis.
[bio29-s4.0.5] Functions of blood: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature and water balance).
[bio29-s4.0.6] Diagram: a stained blood smear under a light microscope. Draw many red blood cells (pale centres, no nucleus), one or two white blood cells (larger, with a stained nucleus: a phagocyte with a lobed nucleus and a lymphocyte with a large round nucleus) and small platelets. Label each.
[bio29-s4.0.7] Diagram: draw a centrifuged tube labelled plasma, white cells and platelets layer, and red cells layer, with percentages.
[bio29-s4.1.b] Plasma is the liquid part of blood, and everything else in blood is carried in it or by it. Because it is mostly water, it can dissolve and transport a huge range of substances around the body.
[bio29-s4.1.0] Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it. Water is a good solvent, so many substances dissolve in it, and it also carries heat.
[bio29-s4.1.1] Dissolved substances transported in plasma: nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals); wastes (urea, from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; and salts, such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio29-s4.1.2] Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻). They form in red blood cells, by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻).
[bio29-s4.1.3] A small amount of carbon dioxide is simply dissolved in plasma, and some is carried by haemoglobin. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s4.1.4] Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors, for clotting; antibodies, for defence against pathogens; and albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
[bio29-s4.1.5] Plasma distributes heat from active organs, such as working muscles, around the body. Its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers (chemicals that resist changes in acidity) keep the blood pH near 7.4.
[bio29-s4.2.b] Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body. Almost every feature of their structure helps them do that one job well.
[bio29-s4.2.0] Red blood cells, or erythrocytes, are biconcave discs (round and thinner in the centre than at the edge), about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
[bio29-s4.2.1] The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface.
[bio29-s4.2.2] There is no nucleus, and no mitochondria, in the mature cell, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. The cell is also packed with haemoglobin, so each cell can carry a lot of oxygen.
[bio29-s4.2.3] The cell is small and flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. This shortens the distance oxygen must diffuse.
[bio29-s4.2.4] Because they have no mitochondria, red blood cells respire anaerobically (without oxygen), so they do not use up the oxygen they carry.
[bio29-s4.2.5] Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron, so each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is bright red.
[bio29-s4.2.6] In respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. The reaction is reversible. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio29-s4.3.b] Red blood cells wear out and must be constantly replaced. Problems with their number or with haemoglobin affect how much oxygen reaches the tissues.
[bio29-s4.3.0] Red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days. With no nucleus they cannot repair themselves, so they wear out.
[bio29-s4.3.1] Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen. The iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments, which are excreted in bile.
[bio29-s4.3.2] The kidney hormone EPO stimulates the production of red blood cells. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio29-s4.3.3] Anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin. A common cause is iron deficiency, because iron is needed to make haem.
[bio29-s4.3.4] Carbon monoxide, in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances, combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried and it can kill.
[bio29-s4.3.5] Sickle-cell anaemia is a further disorder of red blood cells, named here only.
[bio29-s4.4.b] White blood cells are the body's defenders against infection. They differ from red cells in several ways, which makes a good comparison to learn.
[bio29-s4.4.0] White blood cells, or leucocytes, defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, are larger and colourless (they have no haemoglobin), and are far fewer in number.
[bio29-s4.4.1] They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues, where infections often are.
[bio29-s4.4.2] There are two broad groups: phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells.
[bio29-s4.4.3] The number of white cells rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections. Leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells.
[bio29-s4.5.b] Platelets are not whole cells but fragments, and they are the first responders when a blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.5.0] Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus. They break off from large cells in the red bone marrow.
[bio29-s4.5.1] Platelets live about 7 to 10 days and start the clotting of blood.
[bio29-s4.5.2] Clotting matters because it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so that pathogens cannot enter. It is part of the first line of defence against infection.
[bio29-s4.6.b] Clotting is a chain of events in which one change triggers the next, ending in a mesh of protein threads that plugs the wound. Learn the stages in order.
[bio29-s4.6.0] 1. A blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.6.1] 2. Platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug.
[bio29-s4.6.2] 3. The platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors.
[bio29-s4.6.3] 4. A series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver with the help of vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin.
[bio29-s4.6.4] 5. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin.
[bio29-s4.6.5] 6. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot.
[bio29-s4.6.6] 7. At the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs. Calcium and vitamin K are both needed for clotting. Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing.
[bio29-s4.6.7] An unwanted clot inside a vessel, a thrombosis, can block an artery and cause a heart attack or stroke. Diagram: a flow chart of damage, platelets, clotting factors (with Ca²⁺ and vitamin K), prothrombin, thrombin, fibrinogen, fibrin mesh and clot. Add fibrin threads trapping red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.7.b] Your ABO blood group depends on molecules on the surface of your red blood cells. The plasma then carries antibodies matched to whichever molecules your own cells lack.
[bio29-s4.7.0] A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person's red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response. In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
[bio29-s4.7.1] The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen or antigens that a person's own red cells lack. This stops the body attacking its own cells.
[bio29-s4.7.2] Group A has antigen A on the red cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.3] Group AB has both antigens and no antibodies. Group O has no antigens and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.4] Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody, for example A cells meeting anti-A, the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps.
[bio29-s4.7.5] In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed, or burst. This can cause kidney failure and death.
[bio29-s4.7.6] Diagram: four red blood cells, one for each group, showing the antigens on the membrane (A, B, both, none), with the antibodies in the plasma written beside each. Add a sketch of agglutination, with antibodies linking red cells into a clump.
[bio29-s4.8.b] Transfusion works only if the donor's red cells do not meet an antibody in the recipient's plasma that matches their antigens. The rule follows from the ABO antigens and antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.0] What matters is the donor's red-cell antigens and the recipient's antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen against which their own plasma has antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.1] Group O red cells carry no antigens, so they can be given to anyone, and group O is called the universal donor. This applies to red cells: group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B.
[bio29-s4.8.2] Group AB people have no antibodies, so they can receive red cells of any ABO group, and group AB is the universal recipient. In practice, hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
[bio29-s4.8.3] Compatibility: group O can receive O only; group A can receive A or O; group B can receive B or O; group AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
[bio29-s4.8.4] Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and with anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only means group A; clumping with anti-B only means group B; clumping with both means AB; clumping with neither means O.
[bio29-s4.8.5] A third test, with anti-D, tests the Rhesus group.
[bio29-s4.9.b] The Rhesus factor is a second red-cell antigen system, on top of ABO. Unlike the ABO antibodies, the Rhesus antibody has to be learned by the immune system first.
[bio29-s4.9.0] The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+), and those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−).
[bio29-s4.9.1] A person's full blood group combines both systems, for example O negative or A positive.
[bio29-s4.9.2] Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present. A Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood, for example through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy.
[bio29-s4.9.3] A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
[bio29-s4.10.b] A Rhesus negative mother carrying a Rhesus positive baby can become sensitised to her baby's blood, and that may endanger a later baby. The problem is called haemolytic disease of the newborn, and it can be prevented.
[bio29-s4.10.0] 1. A Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby, inherited from a Rh+ father.
[bio29-s4.10.1] 2. Normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth, or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury, some fetal red cells enter the mother's blood.
[bio29-s4.10.2] 3. Her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells, and she is now sensitised. The first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made.
[bio29-s4.10.3] 4. In a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.10.4] 5. The baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage or death.
[bio29-s4.10.5] Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies, anti-D immunoglobulin, during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby, or after a miscarriage or injury.
[bio29-s4.10.6] These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells. The injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks.
[bio29-s4.10.7] This is artificial passive immunity (antibodies given ready-made, not made by the body). Every pregnant woman's blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason.
[bio29-s4.11.b] Knowing a person's blood group saves lives in several ways, from transfusions to pregnancy care. Each use below comes from knowing the groups.
[bio29-s4.11.0] Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient's ABO and Rhesus groups are tested, and the donor blood is cross-matched, meaning it is mixed with the patient's plasma to check that no clumping occurs.
[bio29-s4.11.1] This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and allows transfusions in surgery, after accidents, in childbirth, for anaemia and in cancer treatment.
[bio29-s4.11.2] Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
[bio29-s4.11.3] Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
[bio29-s4.11.4] Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks. Donors of all groups are needed, because blood can be stored only for a limited time.
[bio29-s4.11.5] In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service, and donated blood is screened for infections.
[bio29-s4.11.6] Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group and tissue type to reduce the chance of rejection.
[bio29-s4.11.7] Nature of Science: before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, which made safe transfusion possible.
[bio29-s4.11.8] In 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after the rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.
