CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio14-s2: Investigation: rate of photosynthesis
[bio14-s2.0.b] Before measuring anything, you need to know what "rate" means and which conditions can speed photosynthesis up or hold it back. This theory is what every hypothesis in the investigation rests on.
[bio14-s2.0.0] The rate of photosynthesis is the amount of product made, or reactant used, per unit time. It is usually measured as the rate of oxygen production, in bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute, but it can also be measured as the rate of CO₂ uptake.
[bio14-s2.0.1] Oxygen is a good thing to measure because it is a product: it comes from the splitting of water in the light-dependent stage (the first stage, which needs light).
[bio14-s2.0.2] Light intensity: light supplies the energy for the light-dependent stage, so more light means more ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and hydrogen are made per minute and the rate rises, until another factor holds it back.
[bio14-s2.0.3] Carbon dioxide concentration: CO₂ is the raw material of the light-independent stage (the second stage, which builds glucose), so more CO₂ raises the rate, until another factor holds it back.
[bio14-s2.0.4] Temperature: the light-independent stage is controlled by enzymes. Raising the temperature increases the rate, because molecules move faster and collide more often with the enzymes' active sites (the part of the enzyme where the reaction happens), up to an optimum.
[bio14-s2.0.5] Above the optimum the rate falls quickly, because the enzymes are denatured (their shape is permanently changed, so they stop working). There is more on this in Enzymes as catalysts.
[bio14-s2.0.6] A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply, which holds back the rate of a process even when the other factors are increased. At any moment the rate is limited by whichever of light intensity, CO₂ concentration or temperature is furthest from its best value.
[bio14-s2.0.7] Raising a limiting factor increases the rate. Raising a factor that is not limiting has no effect, because something else is still holding the rate back.
[bio14-s2.1.b] The bubbles you count are not the whole story. A plant is also respiring, and respiration uses oxygen, so what you measure is a net result.
[bio14-s2.1.0] A plant respires all the time, using some of the oxygen it makes, so the oxygen given off is photosynthesis minus respiration. This is called the net (apparent) rate.
[bio14-s2.1.1] In very dim light the two processes are equal and no oxygen is given off, because all the oxygen made is used up. This is the compensation point.
[bio14-s2.1.2] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, so the plant takes in oxygen for respiration.
[bio14-s2.2.b] A good investigation changes one thing and measures one thing. Planning starts with a clear aim, a prediction for each factor, and a plant that lets you see the result.
[bio14-s2.2.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of light intensity (or CO₂ concentration, or temperature) on the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed. Write one investigation per factor, changing only that factor, so you know which one caused any change.
[bio14-s2.2.1] Hypothesis for light: as light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases until it levels off. This rests on the theory that light drives the light-dependent stage until something else becomes limiting.
[bio14-s2.2.2] Hypothesis for CO₂: as CO₂ concentration increases, the rate increases until it levels off. This rests on CO₂ being the raw material of the light-independent stage.
[bio14-s2.2.3] Hypothesis for temperature: the rate increases with temperature up to an optimum and then decreases, because the enzymes of the light-independent stage work faster until they are denatured.
[bio14-s2.2.4] Elodea (Canadian pondweed) is the standard school plant. It is aquatic, so the oxygen it gives off appears as visible bubbles from the cut stem, which can be counted or collected. It is also easy to obtain, grows quickly and photosynthesises rapidly.
[bio14-s2.3.b] Next you decide what to change, what to measure and what to hold steady, and gather the apparatus. The variables below are for the light-intensity investigation.
[bio14-s2.3.0] The independent variable (the one you change) is light intensity, set by the lamp's distance from the plant or measured with a light meter in lux.
[bio14-s2.3.1] The dependent variable (the one you measure) is the rate of oxygen production, as bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute.
[bio14-s2.3.2] Controlled variables (kept the same, so they cannot affect the result): temperature (water bath), CO₂ concentration (same sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration), the same pondweed (species, length, leaves), the same lamp and bulb, the adjustment time and the counting period.
[bio14-s2.3.3] For the other factors, swap the independent variable for CO₂ concentration or temperature, and keep light intensity constant.
[bio14-s2.3.4] Control: an identical set-up kept in darkness (lamp off, tube covered with foil), or a tube with no plant, gives no bubbles. This shows that the bubbles come from photosynthesis by the plant in the light, not from gas leaving the solution.
[bio14-s2.3.5] Equipment: fresh Elodea, scalpel or scissors, boiling tube or test tube, paper clip to weigh the pondweed down, beaker as a water bath (or an electric water bath), thermometer, bench lamp (an LED bulb gives out less heat), metre stick, stopwatch, and a light meter (optional).
[bio14-s2.3.6] Chemicals: sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) powder, or solutions of known concentration, which supplies the plant with CO₂, in water that has been boiled and cooled.
[bio14-s2.3.7] For measuring volume: a funnel and graduated tube, or a capillary tube attached to a syringe, called a photosynthometer. A glowing splint is optional, for testing the gas.
[bio14-s2.4.b] This basic set-up is used for all three investigations. Only the factor you change differs, so learn it once and adapt it.
[bio14-s2.4.0] 1. Fill a boiling tube with water containing a set concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate. It dissolves to supply CO₂ to the plant, so that CO₂ is not limiting (except in the carbon dioxide investigation).
[bio14-s2.4.1] 2. Cut a healthy shoot of pondweed about 5 to 8 cm long, cutting the stem cleanly and slantwise under water.
[bio14-s2.4.2] 3. Place it in the tube upside down, with the cut end at the top, weighed down with a paper clip.
[bio14-s2.4.3] 4. Stand the tube in a beaker of water at a constant temperature, for example 25 °C, checked with a thermometer, so the temperature stays steady.
[bio14-s2.4.4] 5. Carry out the investigation in a darkened room, so that the lamp is the only light source.
[bio14-s2.4.5] Counting: let the plant adjust to each new condition for about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles leaving the cut end in one minute. Repeat the count three times at each setting and calculate the mean.
[bio14-s2.4.6] Collecting (more accurate): place an inverted funnel over the pondweed in a beaker, with a water-filled graduated tube over its stem, and record the gas collected in a set time, for example 5 minutes. Alternatively, draw the gas up a capillary tube with a syringe and measure the bubble's length.
[bio14-s2.4.7] Volume per unit time is more accurate than counting, because bubbles vary in size. Optional gas test: it relights a glowing splint, so it is rich in oxygen, but not pure oxygen, since dissolved nitrogen and carbon dioxide also come out.
[bio14-s2.5.b] Here the independent variable is light intensity, which you change by moving the lamp. The challenge is changing the light without also changing the temperature.
[bio14-s2.5.0] 1. Set up as in the basic set-up.
[bio14-s2.5.1] 2. Place the lamp 10 cm from the pondweed, measured with a metre stick from the bulb to the plant.
[bio14-s2.5.2] 3. Allow 5 minutes to adjust, then count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.5.3] 4. Move the lamp to 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, and repeat.
[bio14-s2.5.4] 5. Check the water temperature at each distance and keep it constant, by adding cold or warm water to the bath.
[bio14-s2.5.5] A lamp close to the plant also heats it, which would change two variables at once. Use an LED bulb, or place a beaker or flat-sided tank of water between the lamp and the plant as a heat shield, and check the thermometer.
[bio14-s2.5.6] Moving the lamp further away lowers the light intensity as the inverse square of the distance (light intensity ∝ 1/d²): doubling the distance gives a quarter of the light intensity. It does not fall in simple proportion.
[bio14-s2.5.7] So plot the rate against 1/d² to show the relationship with light intensity, or measure the light intensity at each position with a light meter.
[bio14-s2.6.b] Here the independent variable is CO₂ concentration, which you change by using sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of different strengths. Light and temperature are held constant.
[bio14-s2.6.0] 1. Make up a series of sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of increasing concentration, for example 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1%, using water that has been boiled and cooled. Boiling removes dissolved CO₂, so the 0% tube has very little.
[bio14-s2.6.1] 2. Keep the lamp at a fixed distance close to the plant, for example 10 cm, and keep the temperature constant, so that CO₂ is the only thing that changes.
[bio14-s2.6.2] 3. Place the same pondweed in each solution in turn, allow 5 minutes to adjust, and count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.6.3] 4. Calculate the mean for each concentration.
[bio14-s2.7.b] Here the independent variable is temperature, changed with water baths. Light and CO₂ are held constant.
[bio14-s2.7.0] 1. Set up as in the basic set-up, with a fixed lamp distance and a fixed sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration.
[bio14-s2.7.1] 2. Place the tube in a water bath at a range of temperatures, for example 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C, using ice or warm water or electric water baths, and check each with a thermometer.
[bio14-s2.7.2] 3. Let the tube reach the bath temperature, about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.7.3] 4. If the first pondweed stops producing bubbles, use a fresh piece for the higher temperatures, and note that you have done so.
[bio14-s2.7.4] Warm water holds less dissolved gas, so some bubbles at high temperatures can come from gas leaving the solution. The darkness control at each temperature checks this.
[bio14-s2.8.b] This investigation mixes electricity, water, heat and sharp tools, so each hazard needs its own precaution.
[bio14-s2.8.0] Electricity and water: keep the lamp, its cable and plug away from the water bath, dry your hands before touching switches, and do not move a lamp while it is on and hot.
[bio14-s2.8.1] Lamp bulbs, other than LEDs, get very hot and can burn, so let them cool before moving them.
[bio14-s2.8.2] Take care with the scalpel: cut on a tile, away from the body. Take care with hot water baths and glassware.
[bio14-s2.8.3] Wash your hands after handling pondweed and pond water.
[bio14-s2.8.4] Elodea canadensis is a non-native invasive species (one that spreads and harms native wildlife) in Ireland. Never put leftover pondweed or its water into ponds, lakes, rivers or drains; dispose of it in compost or general waste.
[bio14-s2.9.b] The results table and graph turn counts into a relationship you can explain. For each factor the shape of the graph tells you what is limiting the rate.
[bio14-s2.9.0] Record the primary data (the results you collect yourself) in a table: the independent variable (distance in cm and 1/d², concentration in %, or temperature in °C), the three counts and the mean number of bubbles per minute.
[bio14-s2.9.1] Plot the mean rate on the y-axis against the independent variable on the x-axis, with units, and join the points with a smooth curve or straight lines.
[bio14-s2.9.2] Light intensity: as the lamp moves closer, the rate increases, so it falls as distance increases. At high intensity the curve levels off in a plateau, because another factor, CO₂ concentration or temperature, has become limiting.
[bio14-s2.9.3] CO₂ concentration: the rate increases with CO₂ concentration and then levels off, because light intensity or temperature becomes limiting. At 0% (boiled water) there are few or no bubbles.
[bio14-s2.9.4] Temperature: the rate rises up to an optimum, then falls steeply above it, as the enzymes of the light-independent stage are denatured. Bubbling may stop at the highest temperatures. The curve is not symmetrical: the fall is steeper than the rise.
[bio14-s2.9.5] Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data and whether it supports the hypothesis. On the levelling-off part of the graph, name the factor that is now limiting.
[bio14-s2.10.b] Examiners often ask you to draw the apparatus or sketch the expected graphs. Learn the labels for each.
[bio14-s2.10.0] Diagram: the set-up. Labels: lamp, metre stick, heat shield, beaker water bath, thermometer, boiling tube of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, upside-down pondweed with bubbles at the cut end (or the funnel and graduated collecting tube).
[bio14-s2.10.1] Diagram: two sketch graphs. (a) Rate against light intensity, rising then levelling off, plus a higher curve labelled higher CO₂ concentration; label the rise light limiting and the plateau CO₂ or temperature limiting. (b) Rate against temperature, rising to an optimum then falling steeply.
[bio14-s2.11.b] Your own results are only one source. You can also use other people's data to support a conclusion, and apply the idea of limiting factors to growing crops.
[bio14-s2.11.0] Secondary data are results collected by others: other groups' class results, textbook or published graphs, or data-logger results from an oxygen or CO₂ sensor. Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility (whether others get the same result).
[bio14-s2.11.1] Interpreting a graph of rate against light intensity at two CO₂ concentrations (or two temperatures): where the two curves overlap, light is the limiting factor, because raising CO₂ makes no difference.
[bio14-s2.11.2] Where the two curves separate and each levels off, CO₂ (or temperature) is limiting, because raising it raises the plateau.
[bio14-s2.11.3] Applying limiting factors: commercial glasshouse growers raise the yield of crops such as tomatoes and lettuce by supplying extra light (artificial lighting), heat and CO₂. Burning paraffin or propane gives both heat and CO₂, or CO₂ gas can be added.
[bio14-s2.11.4] Each factor is kept just high enough that it is not limiting. Raising a factor that is not limiting wastes fuel and money and adds to emissions.
[bio14-s2.12.b] No school investigation is perfect. Knowing where the errors come from tells you how to reduce them.
[bio14-s2.12.0] Bubbles vary in size and can be missed or merge when the rate is fast, so counting is imprecise. Collecting the volume of gas is more accurate.
[bio14-s2.12.1] Other light in the room, such as daylight or other groups' lamps, changes the light intensity, so work in a darkened room.
[bio14-s2.12.2] Temperature can drift, especially with a hot bulb close to the plant, so check it and adjust it.
[bio14-s2.12.3] The gas is not pure oxygen, and some oxygen dissolves in the water instead of forming bubbles, so the bubbles underestimate the true rate. The measured rate is also a net rate, after respiration.
[bio14-s2.12.4] The carbon dioxide from sodium hydrogencarbonate is used up over time, and the plant's response may change, so use fresh solution, a fresh plant for each run if needed, and repeat the whole series.
[bio14-s2.12.5] Allowing too little time to adjust after each change gives rates that still reflect the previous setting.
[bio14-s2.12.6] Repeat each reading at least three times and calculate a mean, so a single odd count has less effect. Compare your results with other groups' results.

CANDIDATES bio14-s3: The light-dependent reactions
[bio14-s3.0.b] The light-dependent reactions start when light hits a leaf. Before any chemistry can happen, the plant has to catch that light energy and channel it to one special chlorophyll molecule, which uses it to fire off an electron.
[bio14-s3.0.0] Light travels as tiny packets of energy called photons. A pigment is a molecule that absorbs light of particular colours (wavelengths).
[bio14-s3.0.1] The thylakoid membranes hold several pigments: chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and yellow and orange pigments called carotenoids. Each absorbs slightly different colours, so having several widens the range of light the plant can use.
[bio14-s3.0.2] The pigment molecules sit in clusters of hundreds, called light-harvesting or antenna complexes, around a reaction centre. The cluster works like a satellite dish, collecting light over a wide area and focusing it on one point.
[bio14-s3.0.3] When any pigment in the cluster absorbs a photon, the energy is passed from one pigment molecule to the next until it reaches the reaction centre, a special chlorophyll a molecule.
[bio14-s3.0.4] At the reaction centre, the energy raises (excites) one of the chlorophyll's electrons to a high energy level. This energised electron leaves the chlorophyll and is taken up by an electron acceptor, the first carrier of an electron transport chain.
[bio14-s3.0.5] Because an electron carries a negative charge, losing one leaves the reaction-centre chlorophyll positively charged. It must get an electron back before it can fire off another one, and in the non-cyclic pathway it takes that electron from water.
[bio14-s3.1.b] The energised electron now has to go somewhere useful. In the non-cyclic pathway, it travels one way, from water to NADP⁺, and along the way its energy is used to make the two things the second stage of photosynthesis needs: ATP and NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.0] 1. Light energy reaches the reaction centre, and energised electrons leave the chlorophyll.
[bio14-s3.1.1] 2. The electrons are passed along an electron transport chain, a series of electron carriers in the thylakoid membrane. At each hand-over they lose a little energy, like a ball bouncing down a staircase, and this released energy is used to make ATP from ADP and phosphate (Pi).
[bio14-s3.1.2] 3. At the end of the chain, the electrons, now at lower energy, are passed to NADP⁺, a hydrogen carrier. Each NADP⁺ takes two electrons and one proton (H⁺) to form NADPH: NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.3] 4. The chlorophyll is still positively charged, so it strongly attracts electrons and pulls them from water molecules. This splits the water, a step called photolysis (splitting by light, because the energy for it ultimately came from light): 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂.
[bio14-s3.1.4] 5. The electrons from water replace the ones the chlorophyll lost, so it is neutral again and ready to absorb more energy.
[bio14-s3.1.5] The protons (H⁺) from photolysis are used to form NADPH, and they also build up inside the thylakoid, where this crowd of protons is used to power ATP-making. There is more on this in The chloroplast's internal structure.
[bio14-s3.1.6] The oxygen from photolysis is a by-product. It is used in the cell's respiration or released through the stomata.
[bio14-s3.1.7] It is called non-cyclic because the electrons go one way only, from water through the chlorophyll and the chain to NADP⁺, and never return to where they started. The electrons aren't used up; they end up on NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.8] Products of the non-cyclic pathway: ATP, NADPH and oxygen.
[bio14-s3.2.b] Sometimes the energised electrons take a shortcut. In the cyclic pathway they loop back to the chlorophyll they came from, making ATP on the way but nothing else.
[bio14-s3.2.0] In the cyclic pathway, some energised electrons leaving the reaction-centre chlorophyll pass along carriers of the electron transport chain and then return to the same chlorophyll molecule, instead of going on to NADP⁺.
[bio14-s3.2.1] As they pass along the carriers, they give up their extra energy, which is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio14-s3.2.2] Because the electrons come back, the chlorophyll is neutral again without needing electrons from water. So water is not split, no oxygen is released and no NADPH is made.
[bio14-s3.2.3] Products of the cyclic pathway: ATP only.
[bio14-s3.2.4] Why the plant needs it: the light-independent reactions use more ATP than NADPH, 3 ATP for every 2 NADPH for each CO₂ molecule fixed (built into sugar) in the light-independent reactions, the second stage that makes glucose. The cyclic pathway tops up the extra ATP.
[bio14-s3.2.5] It also runs when NADP⁺ is in short supply because most of it is already loaded as NADPH. The energised electrons then still have somewhere to go.
[bio14-s3.3.b] Exams ask you to tell the two pathways apart. The differences all come back to one thing: where the electrons end up.
[bio14-s3.3.0] Route of the electrons: non-cyclic goes one way, from water to NADP⁺; cyclic goes from the chlorophyll round the carriers and back to the same chlorophyll.
[bio14-s3.3.1] Final electron acceptor: NADP⁺ in the non-cyclic pathway; the chlorophyll molecule itself in the cyclic pathway.
[bio14-s3.3.2] Water: split by photolysis in the non-cyclic pathway, to replace the lost electrons; not needed in the cyclic pathway, because the electrons come back.
[bio14-s3.3.3] Products: non-cyclic makes ATP, NADPH and oxygen; cyclic makes ATP only.
[bio14-s3.3.4] What they share: both start with light energy reaching the reaction centre and an energised electron leaving the chlorophyll, both pass electrons along carriers to make ATP, and both happen on the thylakoid membranes.
[bio14-s3.3.5] Older Irish textbooks and past papers call the cyclic pathway pathway 1 and the non-cyclic pathway pathway 2, and describe NADP⁺ first picking up two electrons (becoming NADP⁻) and then a proton. Recognise these names, but learn the single step to NADPH given above.
[bio14-s3.3.6] Diagram: one energy diagram with chlorophyll at the bottom receiving light from pigment molecules; the electron rising to an acceptor; a downhill chain (ATP made) ending at NADP⁺ → NADPH; water entering below the chlorophyll; a loop back to the chlorophyll for the cyclic pathway (ATP made).
[bio14-s3.4.b] Put together, the light-dependent reactions turn light into a form of energy the rest of the chloroplast can use, and hand it on to the second stage.
[bio14-s3.4.0] Location: the thylakoid membranes (grana) of the chloroplast, because that is where the pigments and electron carriers are held.
[bio14-s3.4.1] Inputs: light energy, water, ADP + Pi and NADP⁺.
[bio14-s3.4.2] Outputs: ATP and NADPH, which pass into the stroma for the light-independent reactions, and oxygen, released as a by-product.
[bio14-s3.4.3] Energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in ATP and in the high-energy electrons carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s3.4.4] The two products have different jobs: ATP carries energy, and NADPH carries electrons and hydrogen to reduce carbon dioxide to glucose. After use, they return as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ to be reloaded. There is more on this in The light-independent reactions.

CANDIDATES bio14-s4: The light-independent reactions
[bio14-s4.0.b] The second stage of photosynthesis is where glucose is actually built. It uses the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage as its energy and hydrogen supply, and in return sends the empty carriers back.
[bio14-s4.0.0] Location: the stroma (the fluid that fills the chloroplast), which contains the enzymes for these reactions. The reactions need no light directly, but they depend on the ATP and NADPH made in the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoids.
[bio14-s4.0.1] 1. Carbon dioxide diffuses into the stroma, from the air spaces of the leaf through the cell and the chloroplast's membranes. It is taken up, or fixed, by combining with a carbon dioxide-accepting compound already present in the stroma.
[bio14-s4.0.2] 2. NADPH and ATP from the light-dependent reactions enter the reactions, because they supply the hydrogen and the energy that CO₂ needs to become glucose.
[bio14-s4.0.3] 3. Protons and electrons, that is hydrogen, are transferred from NADPH to the carbon compounds made from CO₂. This adds hydrogen to the carbon, which is reduction, turning CO₂ into carbohydrate. NADPH becomes NADP⁺.
[bio14-s4.0.4] 4. The energy to achieve this conversion comes from ATP, which is broken down to ADP + Pi (phosphate).
[bio14-s4.0.5] 5. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the product. Six molecules of CO₂ are fixed for each molecule of glucose, because each supplies one of its six carbon atoms. The glucose is then used, or converted to sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s4.0.6] 6. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return to the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes, where they are reloaded to ATP and NADPH and can be re-used.
[bio14-s4.0.7] The reactions form a cycle, the Calvin cycle. Most of the carbon compounds made regenerate the CO₂-accepting compound, so the cycle can keep fixing CO₂, and the rest is built into glucose.
[bio14-s4.1.b] Once you know the inputs and outputs, you can explain why this stage responds to temperature and CO₂ and why it stops soon after dark, even though it uses no light directly.
[bio14-s4.1.0] Summary in words: carbon dioxide + hydrogen (from NADPH) + energy (from ATP) → glucose, with NADP⁺ and ADP + Pi released, so the carriers are free to be reloaded.
[bio14-s4.1.1] Every step is catalysed by enzymes in the stroma, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, and falls when the enzymes are denatured. This is why temperature affects the rate of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s4.1.2] CO₂ is the raw material of this stage, so its supply directly limits the rate of glucose production.
[bio14-s4.1.3] In darkness the light-dependent reactions stop, so ATP and NADPH run out within a short time. The light-independent reactions then stop too, even though they do not use light directly.
[bio14-s4.2.b] Photosynthesis is best understood as two linked stages joined by recycled carrier molecules. Modelling it means showing what goes in and out of each stage and what the carriers move between them.
[bio14-s4.2.0] Stage 1, the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes (the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast): light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll, splits water and drives electron transfer. This produces O₂, which is released, and loads the transfer molecules: ADP + Pi → ATP and NADP⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.1] Stage 2, the light-independent reactions in the stroma: the transfer molecules are unloaded, which is why this stage needs stage 1. NADPH gives its protons and electrons to CO₂, and ATP gives the energy, producing glucose. NADPH → NADP⁺ and ATP → ADP + Pi.
[bio14-s4.2.2] The transfer molecules link the two stages. They carry energy (ATP) and electrons and hydrogen (NADPH) from stage 1 to stage 2, and return empty, as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺, from stage 2 to stage 1.
[bio14-s4.2.3] Because the transfer molecules are recycled, the chloroplast needs only a small supply of each.
[bio14-s4.2.4] Tracing the atoms: the oxygen gas comes from water (stage 1). The carbon and the oxygen in glucose come from CO₂ (stage 2). The hydrogen in glucose comes from water, carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.5] Diagram: thylakoid (stage 1) beside stroma (stage 2). Stage 1: in light, H₂O; out O₂. Stage 2: in CO₂; out glucose. Two loops between them: ATP across and ADP + Pi back, NADPH across and NADP⁺ back, each arrow labelled energy, or protons and electrons.

CANDIDATES bio16-s1: The cell cycle
[bio16-s1.0.b] Before you can follow the cell cycle you need to know what is being copied and shared out: the chromosomes. How many a cell has, and how they are arranged, decides whether it is diploid or haploid.
[bio16-s1.0.0] A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (the units of inheritance). There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio16-s1.0.1] Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin (DNA with its proteins). Before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio16-s1.0.2] Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent; the alleles (versions of a gene) on them may differ. Human body cells contain 23 such pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.3] Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, so that its chromosomes lie in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46. The 46 counts chromosomes, not pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.4] Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23. Haploid means one complete set, not half a chromosome.
[bio16-s1.0.5] When two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.
[bio16-s1.0.6] Haploid cells are made by meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number; mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s1.0.7] Each species has its own chromosome number: human 2n = 46, the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8, the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.
[bio16-s1.1.b] Cells don't just divide and stop: each one lives through a repeating sequence of events called the cell cycle. It shows how a cell prepares for division and then carries it out.
[bio16-s1.1.0] The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
[bio16-s1.1.1] It has three main activities, in this order. First, growth: the cell makes new proteins and organelles (the working parts of a cell) and gets bigger.
[bio16-s1.1.2] Second, DNA synthesis: every chromosome is copied, so the cell has two copies of its genetic information to hand out.
[bio16-s1.1.3] Third, division: the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells.
[bio16-s1.1.4] The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, about 90% of the cycle, so division is short.
[bio16-s1.1.5] In many human cells grown in the laboratory, one cycle takes about 24 hours.
[bio16-s1.1.6] Interphase is not a resting stage. The cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
[bio16-s1.1.7] One cycle, including division, produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s1.2.b] Not every cell divides at the same pace, and no cell divides without being told it is safe to. Whether a cell cycles depends on its job and on control checks built into the cycle.
[bio16-s1.2.0] Some cells leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, for example most mature nerve cells. Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide.
[bio16-s1.2.1] Other cells divide often throughout life, for example cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems, regions where growth happens).
[bio16-s1.2.2] The cycle is controlled. Proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on.
[bio16-s1.2.3] Signals from other cells (growth factors, chemical messages) tell a cell whether to divide. There is more on how loss of this control leads to cancer in Cancer.
[bio16-s1.2.4] Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle. Large sector: interphase (cell growth and DNA replication). Small sector: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis). Arrow for direction. Higher Level: also label G1, S, G2 and the four stages of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.3.b] Interphase is long and busy, and it has three parts: G1, S and G2. Together they prepare the cell so that division can be quick and accurate.
[bio16-s1.3.0] G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including the enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job.
[bio16-s1.3.1] S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere (the narrow point where they are held together).
[bio16-s1.3.2] G2 (second growth phase): further growth and more organelles, for example mitochondria. The proteins of the spindle (the fibres that later pull chromosomes apart) are made, and ATP is stored for division.
[bio16-s1.3.3] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), for example mature nerve cells.
[bio16-s1.3.4] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.
[bio16-s1.4.b] Mitosis is the division of the nucleus. It runs as one smooth process, but it is split into four stages to make it easier to describe, and each stage has a characteristic look and job.
[bio16-s1.4.0] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process, divided for convenience into four stages in this order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. It is followed by cytokinesis, which is division of the cytoplasm and not a stage of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.4.1] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear membrane breaks down, and spindle fibres (protein tubes called microtubules) begin to form.
[bio16-s1.4.2] In animal cells, two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them. Plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
[bio16-s1.4.3] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
[bio16-s1.4.4] Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first, which gives a V shape.
[bio16-s1.4.5] Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome, so each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio16-s1.4.6] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
[bio16-s1.4.7] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell, 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, shaded to show the two parents). Labels: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow (end of telophase).
[bio16-s1.5.b] You can tell which stage a cell is in just by looking at how its chromosomes are arranged under the microscope. Counting dividing cells then tells you how fast a tissue is dividing.
[bio16-s1.5.0] In stained cells from a growing root tip (for example onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase, seen as a nucleus with no visible chromosomes, because interphase is the longest part of the cycle.
[bio16-s1.5.1] Dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes: condensed and scattered for prophase; in a line across the middle for metaphase.
[bio16-s1.5.2] Two groups moving apart is anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, with new nuclei forming, is telophase.
[bio16-s1.5.3] The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing. For example, 24 dividing cells out of 200 gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%.
[bio16-s1.5.4] A high mitotic index shows a tissue that is dividing rapidly, such as a root tip or a tumour.
[bio16-s1.6.b] After the nucleus has divided, the cell still has to split in two. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and plant and animal cells do it differently because plant cells have a rigid wall.
[bio16-s1.6.0] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.1] In animal cells the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
[bio16-s1.6.2] In plant cells the rigid cell wall can't pinch in. Instead, vesicles (small membrane sacs) from the Golgi apparatus collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate.
[bio16-s1.6.3] The cell plate grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.4] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

CANDIDATES bio16-s3: DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle
[bio16-s3.0.b] Before a cell divides, it has to copy its DNA, the molecule that carries its genetic instructions. This copying is called DNA replication, and it is what lets one cell become two with the same instructions.
[bio16-s3.0.0] DNA replication is the process by which a DNA molecule makes an exact copy of itself, giving two identical DNA molecules.
[bio16-s3.0.1] In eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus) it happens in the nucleus during interphase, the growing phase before the cell divides. It is the DNA-synthesis part of the cell cycle, the repeating sequence of events in a dividing cell.
[bio16-s3.0.2] Every chromosome in the nucleus is copied once, and only once, per cell cycle, so the DNA is doubled exactly one time before each division.
[bio16-s3.0.3] Replication is needed because a cell that divides must give each daughter cell a complete and identical set of genetic instructions. Without replication each daughter cell would receive only half the DNA.
[bio16-s3.1.b] A DNA molecule is a double helix (a twisted ladder) made of two strands held together by pairs of bases. Replication works by pulling the two strands apart and using each one as a pattern for a new partner strand. Follow the steps in order.
[bio16-s3.1.0] 1. Enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions) unwind the double helix and separate, or unzip, the two strands by breaking the weak hydrogen bonds between the base pairs. The strong bonds of each sugar–phosphate backbone are not broken, so each strand stays intact.
[bio16-s3.1.1] 2. Each separated single strand acts as a template (pattern) for building a new strand, because its exposed bases decide which nucleotides can attach.
[bio16-s3.1.2] 3. Free DNA nucleotides in the nucleus line up against the exposed bases of each template strand by complementary base pairing: A pairs with T, and C pairs with G.
[bio16-s3.1.3] 4. Enzymes join the new nucleotides to each other to form a new sugar–phosphate backbone, making a complementary strand; hydrogen bonds form between the new and old bases.
[bio16-s3.1.4] 5. Each new DNA molecule winds back into a double helix. The result is two DNA molecules, each made of one original (parent) strand and one new strand.
[bio16-s3.1.5] This is why replication is described as semi-conservative: half of each new molecule is conserved from the parent. There is more on the evidence for this in Modelling DNA replication and information flow.
[bio16-s3.2.b] Replication has to be accurate, because any error is passed on to every daughter cell. Fixed base pairing and checking enzymes are what keep the copies exact.
[bio16-s3.2.0] Because base pairing is fixed, each new strand is an exact complement of its template, so both new molecules have the same base sequence as the original.
[bio16-s3.2.1] For example, a section of DNA with one strand ATTGCA and the other strand TAACGT separates: ATTGCA is a template for a new TAACGT, and TAACGT is a template for a new ATTGCA, giving two molecules identical to the original.
[bio16-s3.2.2] Enzymes make replication fast and accurate; they also check the new strands and correct most mistakes. A rare uncorrected mistake changes the base sequence, which is a mutation (a change in the DNA).
[bio16-s3.2.3] Diagram: a short section of DNA (about four base pairs) as a ladder: parent molecule, strands separating with free nucleotides pairing, two daughter molecules each with one old strand (dark) and one new strand (light). Label parent strand, new strand, hydrogen bonds broken, free nucleotide, complementary base pair, enzyme.
[bio16-s3.3.b] Replication copies the DNA, but copying alone does not make two cells. Mitosis (the division of the nucleus) and cytokinesis (the division of the rest of the cell) then share the copies out, so each new cell ends up with a full set.
[bio16-s3.3.0] Replication in interphase doubles the DNA. Afterwards each chromosome consists of two identical copies joined together, so the cell temporarily has twice the normal amount of DNA. A human cell still has 46 chromosomes, but each one is double.
[bio16-s3.3.1] Mitosis then separates the two copies of every chromosome, sending one copy to each end of the cell, so each daughter nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome: 46 chromosomes in each human daughter cell, with the same number and the same genes as the parent.
[bio16-s3.3.2] Cytokinesis then divides the cell, so the amount of DNA in each daughter cell returns to the amount in the parent cell before replication. Each daughter cell can then grow and repeat the cycle.
[bio16-s3.3.3] Replication and mitosis together keep the genetic information the same in every cell of a multicellular organism, and pass identical copies to offspring in asexual reproduction. There is more on these roles in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s3.3.4] Order matters: replication must be complete before mitosis begins, which the cell checks, and it happens only once per cycle. Otherwise daughter cells would get missing or extra DNA. When this control fails, cells can be damaged or become cancerous.
[bio16-s3.3.5] Diagram: a graph of DNA per cell (y-axis, arbitrary units) against time (x-axis) over two cell cycles. DNA is steady at one level in early interphase, rises to double during replication, stays double until the end of mitosis, then drops back at cytokinesis. Label interphase, DNA replication, mitosis, cytokinesis.

CANDIDATES bio9-s1: ATP
[bio9-s1.0.b] A transfer molecule carries energy, or electrons and hydrogen, from the reaction that releases them to the reaction that uses them.
[bio9-s1.0.0] A transfer molecule is not used up: after handing on its load it returns to be recharged, like a rechargeable battery, so a cell needs only a small amount of each one, recycled continuously.
[bio9-s1.0.1] ATP is the main energy-transfer molecule in all living cells, from bacteria to humans, which is why it is called the energy currency of the cell: it is the common 'money' every reaction accepts.
[bio9-s1.0.2] Metabolism is all the chemical reactions in a cell. These reactions fall into two types, catabolic and anabolic.
[bio9-s1.0.3] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, which breaks down food such as glucose.
[bio9-s1.0.4] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. protein synthesis and photosynthesis.
[bio9-s1.0.5] ATP links the two: catabolic reactions make ATP, and anabolic reactions and other cell work use it, so energy released in one place can be spent in another. There is more on this in Biomolecules and metabolism.
[bio9-s1.1.b] ATP is a small molecule with three parts, and its name tells you what they are. Knowing the parts matters because the exam asks you to name them and draw them.
[bio9-s1.1.0] ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate. It is made of three parts: the nitrogenous base adenine, the five-carbon sugar ribose, and a chain of three phosphate groups attached to the ribose.
[bio9-s1.1.1] Adenine joined to ribose is called adenosine; 'tri' means three phosphates. So adenosine triphosphate is adenosine with three phosphates attached.
[bio9-s1.1.2] ATP is a modified nucleotide, built to the same plan of base, sugar and phosphate as an RNA nucleotide (a building block of RNA). There is more on this in Nucleotides, DNA and RNA.
[bio9-s1.1.3] ADP (adenosine diphosphate) has only two phosphate groups, one fewer than ATP.
[bio9-s1.1.4] Pi (inorganic phosphate) is a single free phosphate group, not attached to any larger molecule.
[bio9-s1.1.5] Diagram: ATP drawn as a box labelled adenine, joined to a pentagon labelled ribose, joined to a chain of three circles labelled phosphate, with the bond to the third (end) phosphate marked as the one broken to release energy; a second sketch showing ADP + Pi.
[bio9-s1.2.b] A cell gets energy from ATP by removing its end phosphate. The reaction is simple, but its features explain why ATP is such a useful energy source.
[bio9-s1.2.0] Energy is released when the end phosphate is removed: ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi + energy. So the products of ATP breakdown are ADP, Pi and energy.
[bio9-s1.2.1] The reaction uses water to split the molecule, so it is a hydrolysis reaction. It is catalysed (sped up) by an enzyme called ATPase.
[bio9-s1.2.2] The bond to the end phosphate is often called a high-energy bond: it is easily broken, and the reaction releases about 30 kJ per mole of ATP (30.5 kJ/mol) under standard conditions.
[bio9-s1.2.3] The energy is released in one quick step, in a small packet that suits a single reaction and is available right where the cell needs it.
[bio9-s1.2.4] Often the released phosphate is attached to another molecule, such as a carrier protein or a reactant (a molecule taking part in a reaction). This makes it more reactive or changes its shape so that it can do work.
[bio9-s1.3.b] ATP is not used up for good. Once its end phosphate has been removed, the cell puts a phosphate back on to ADP, which needs an input of energy.
[bio9-s1.3.0] ATP is remade by adding a phosphate group back on to ADP: ADP + Pi + energy → ATP + H₂O.
[bio9-s1.3.1] Adding phosphate is called phosphorylation. It is catalysed by enzymes, mainly ATP synthase, the enzyme that makes most of the cell's ATP.
[bio9-s1.3.2] In all living cells, the energy to make ATP comes from respiration, the breakdown of food such as glucose. Some ATP is made in the cytoplasm and most in the mitochondria. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio9-s1.3.3] In plants and algae, ATP is also made in the light-dependent stage of photosynthesis in the chloroplasts, using light energy. This ATP is used inside the chloroplast to make glucose.
[bio9-s1.4.b] Breaking down ATP and remaking it are two halves of one loop. Because the loop runs all the time, a cell can keep spending energy without running out of ATP.
[bio9-s1.4.0] ATP is continuously broken down to ADP + Pi to release energy for cell work, and ADP + Pi are continuously rebuilt into ATP using energy from respiration (or light). This loop is the ATP/ADP cycle.
[bio9-s1.4.1] Cells hold only a small store of ATP at any time. Each molecule is recycled many times, so ATP must be made constantly while a cell is alive.
[bio9-s1.4.2] When respiration stops, for example after a long time without oxygen or in the presence of a poison that blocks respiration, ATP runs out within minutes and cell processes fail.
[bio9-s1.4.3] Diagram: the ATP/ADP cycle as a circle, with ATP at the top and ADP + Pi at the bottom; one arrow down labelled energy released for cell work: muscle contraction, active transport, synthesis; one arrow up labelled energy in from respiration (and light in photosynthesis).
[bio9-s1.5.b] Almost every energy-demanding job in a cell runs on ATP. The main uses are movement, moving substances across membranes and building molecules.
[bio9-s1.5.0] Muscle contraction: ATP provides the energy for protein filaments in muscle fibres to slide past each other, shortening the muscle. Very active muscle cells therefore contain many mitochondria, to make plenty of ATP.
[bio9-s1.5.1] Active transport: carrier proteins in the cell membrane use energy from ATP to pump substances against their concentration gradient (from low to high concentration), e.g. root hair cells taking in mineral ions from dilute soil water.
[bio9-s1.5.2] The sodium–potassium pump in nerve cells uses ATP to restore the ion balance after an impulse. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio9-s1.5.3] Synthesis (anabolic reactions): ATP is needed to join amino acids into proteins at ribosomes, to make DNA when it is copied before cell division, and to make glycogen and starch from glucose.
[bio9-s1.5.4] The light-independent stage of photosynthesis uses ATP to build glucose from carbon dioxide.
[bio9-s1.5.5] Other uses: cell division (moving chromosomes), movement of cilia and flagella (hair-like and whip-like projections), and the light produced by fireflies (bioluminescence), where the enzyme luciferase uses ATP.
[bio9-s1.6.b] Glucose holds a lot of energy, yet cells do not run reactions straight from it. They first use its energy to make ATP, and this is the better arrangement for several reasons.
[bio9-s1.6.0] Breaking down one glucose molecule releases far more energy than any single reaction needs, and it does so over many steps.
[bio9-s1.6.1] ATP releases a small, manageable amount, matched to one reaction, in a single step, so less energy is wasted as heat.
[bio9-s1.6.2] ATP is available instantly where it is needed and is easily recycled. One type of molecule can supply energy to every kind of reaction in the cell.
[bio9-s1.6.3] ATP is a short-term energy carrier, not a store. Long-term energy is stored in starch (plants), glycogen (animals and fungi) and fats and oils, which respiration uses to make ATP when needed.
[bio9-s1.7.b] Fireflies make light using ATP, and this has been put to practical use. Because the light needs ATP, it can reveal whether living material is present.
[bio9-s1.7.0] Because every living cell contains ATP, hygiene tests in food factories and hospitals swab a surface and add luciferase, the firefly enzyme that gives off light when ATP is present.
[bio9-s1.7.1] Light given off shows that ATP is present, and therefore that living or recently living material, such as bacteria or food residue, is on the surface.
