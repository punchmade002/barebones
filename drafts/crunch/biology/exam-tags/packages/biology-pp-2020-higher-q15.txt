PART (b)(iv) (9 marks) subsections: bio22-s4
MODEL ANSWER: 1. Attachment: proteins on the virus coat bind to specific receptor proteins on the host cell membrane.
2. Entry: the virus's nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) enters the host cell (the coat breaks down and releases it).
3. Replication: the viral genes take over the cell, and the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP and raw materials are used to copy the viral nucleic acid and make viral proteins (coat proteins).
4. Assembly: new nucleic acid and coat proteins come together to form many new virus particles.
5. Release: the new viruses are released, by the cell bursting (lysis) or by budding out through the membrane, and they go on to infect other cells.

MODEL ANSWER: Cohesion-tension (Dixon and Joly): Water evaporates from the leaf mesophyll cells and leaves through the stomata (transpiration), which pulls on the water in the xylem and puts the water column under tension (transpiration pull). Cohesion: water molecules stick to each other by hydrogen bonds, so the column does not break and the whole column is pulled up as one. Adhesion: water molecules also stick to the walls of the narrow xylem vessels, which helps hold the column up against gravity.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio16-s5: Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer
[bio16-s5.0.b] Cancer is rarely down to one thing. The ideas of risk and carcinogens set out here are the ones the later blocks on infections, environment and genes all use.
[bio16-s5.0.0] Most cancers have no single cause. They result from several mutations (changes in DNA) building up in one cell, so anything that damages DNA, makes cells divide more often or weakens the body's defences raises the risk.
[bio16-s5.0.1] A factor that increases the chance of developing a disease is a risk factor.
[bio16-s5.0.2] A carcinogen is any agent that can cause cancer. Most carcinogens are mutagens: they damage DNA, causing mutations in proto-oncogenes (genes that promote cell division) or tumour suppressor genes (genes that restrain it).
[bio16-s5.0.3] The three kinds of cause in the specification are infectious agents, environmental factors (including lifestyle) and genetic susceptibility (an inherited tendency). They often act together in the same person.
[bio16-s5.0.4] Risk is a probability, not a certainty. A risk factor makes cancer more likely, but not everyone exposed develops cancer, and some people with no known risk factor do.
[bio16-s5.1.b] Cancer itself is not infectious, but some infections raise the risk of it. For each agent, learn the route: the agent, how it pushes cells towards cancer, and which cancer results.
[bio16-s5.1.0] Human papillomavirus (HPV) is a virus spread by sexual contact. Some high-risk types infect cells of the cervix (neck of the womb). The virus's DNA enters the cells and its proteins switch off the cell's tumour suppressor proteins, including p53, so infected cells divide without control.
[bio16-s5.1.1] Persistent HPV infection causes most cervical cancers, and two types, HPV 16 and HPV 18, cause about 70% of them. HPV also causes some cancers of the throat, anus, penis and vulva.
[bio16-s5.1.2] Most HPV infections are cleared by the immune system and cause no harm, so infection raises the risk of cancer but does not mean cancer. There is more on HPV as a virus in Viruses.
[bio16-s5.1.3] Hepatitis B and hepatitis C viruses infect liver cells. Long-term infection causes repeated liver damage and inflammation, and the repeated cell division needed to repair the liver gives more chances for mutations. They cause liver cancer.
[bio16-s5.1.4] Helicobacter pylori is a bacterium that lives in the stomach lining, causing long-term inflammation and ulcers. Long-term infection raises the risk of stomach cancer. It can be cleared with antibiotics.
[bio16-s5.1.5] Epstein–Barr virus, the virus that causes glandular fever, is linked to some lymphomas (cancers of lymph tissue), e.g. Burkitt's lymphoma, and to cancer of the nose and throat (nasopharyngeal cancer).
[bio16-s5.1.6] Infections raise cancer risk in three ways. Some viruses insert their own genes, whose proteins interfere with control of the cell cycle (HPV). Long-term inflammation and repeated repair division increase the chance of mutation (hepatitis viruses, H. pylori).
[bio16-s5.1.7] Third, infections that weaken the immune system let abnormal cells escape destruction. Remember that cancer itself is not infectious: it cannot be caught from another person.
[bio16-s5.2.b] Tobacco is the largest single avoidable cause of cancer. It shows clearly how a chemical in the environment can cause mutations.
[bio16-s5.2.0] Tobacco smoke contains many carcinogens. Tar contains benzo[a]pyrene, a chemical that binds to DNA and causes mutations, including in TP53, the gene for the tumour suppressor protein p53.
[bio16-s5.2.1] Smoking is the main cause of lung cancer. It also causes cancers of the mouth, throat, larynx, oesophagus, pancreas, bladder, kidney and cervix.
[bio16-s5.2.2] Second-hand (passive) smoke, breathed in by people near a smoker, also causes lung cancer.
[bio16-s5.2.3] The risk of smoking-related cancer rises with the number of cigarettes and the number of years of smoking, which is called a dose–response relationship (more exposure, more risk). The risk falls after stopping.
[bio16-s5.3.b] Some radiation carries enough energy to damage DNA directly. The two types to know are ultraviolet (UV) light and ionising radiation, and they are different.
[bio16-s5.3.0] Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun and from sunbeds is absorbed by DNA in skin cells. It makes neighbouring thymine bases on a strand bond together (thymine dimers), which distorts the DNA and causes mutations if not repaired.
[bio16-s5.3.1] UV causes skin cancers, including melanoma, the most dangerous type. Skin cancer is the most common cancer in Ireland. People with fair skin that burns easily are at greatest risk, and sunburn, especially in childhood, raises the risk.
[bio16-s5.3.2] Ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays and radioactive substances) has enough energy to break DNA strands.
[bio16-s5.3.3] Radon is a natural radioactive gas that comes from uranium in rocks and soil and seeps into buildings. It is the second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking. Parts of Ireland have high radon levels, so homes can be tested and fitted with a radon sump or barrier.
[bio16-s5.3.4] After the Chernobyl nuclear accident in 1986, released radioactive iodine caused a large increase in thyroid cancer in children in the surrounding area. Medical X-rays use the lowest effective dose for this reason.
[bio16-s5.4.b] Chemicals at work, what we drink and eat, body weight and simply getting older all change cancer risk. Each works by damaging DNA or by making cells divide more.
[bio16-s5.4.0] Asbestos fibres, once used in building insulation, lodge in the lungs and cause mesothelioma (a cancer of the lining of the lungs) and lung cancer. Smokers exposed to asbestos have a far greater risk than the two risks added together, because the factors multiply.
[bio16-s5.4.1] Alcohol is broken down in the body to ethanal (acetaldehyde), which damages DNA. Drinking raises the risk of cancers of the mouth, throat, oesophagus, liver, bowel and breast.
[bio16-s5.4.2] The risk from alcohol rises with the amount drunk, and there is no completely safe level for cancer risk.
[bio16-s5.4.3] Processed meat (e.g. bacon, ham and sausages) was classified by the World Health Organization's cancer agency, IARC, in 2015 as a cause of bowel cancer, and red meat as probably causing it. A diet high in fibre, from wholegrains, fruit and vegetables, lowers the risk of bowel cancer.
[bio16-s5.4.4] Obesity raises the risk of several cancers, e.g. bowel, breast after the menopause, womb, kidney and oesophagus, partly because fat tissue releases hormones and growth signals that stimulate cell division. Regular physical activity lowers the risk of bowel and breast cancer.
[bio16-s5.4.5] Age is the strongest single risk factor. Most cancers are diagnosed in older people, because mutations build up over a lifetime.
[bio16-s5.5.b] Some people start life with a head start towards cancer because they inherit a faulty gene. This is a minority of cases, but it shows clearly how mutations lead to cancer.
[bio16-s5.5.0] Most mutations that cause cancer arise during a person's life in body cells (acquired, or somatic, mutations) and are not inherited. Only about 5–10% of cancers are strongly linked to an inherited faulty gene.
[bio16-s5.5.1] A person who inherits one faulty copy of a tumour suppressor gene has it in every cell, so only one more mutation, in the other copy, is needed in a cell to lose that brake.
[bio16-s5.5.2] This is why inherited cancers often appear at a younger age, in more than one family member, and sometimes in both of a pair of organs, e.g. both breasts.
[bio16-s5.5.3] BRCA1 and BRCA2 are tumour suppressor genes whose proteins repair damaged DNA. An inherited faulty copy greatly increases the lifetime risk of breast and ovarian cancer in women, and raises the risk of prostate and breast cancer in men.
[bio16-s5.5.4] Either parent can pass on a faulty BRCA gene, with a 50% chance for each child, which is a dominant pattern of inheritance (one faulty copy is enough to raise risk).
[bio16-s5.5.5] Lynch syndrome is caused by inherited faulty copies of genes that repair copying errors in DNA. It greatly raises the risk of bowel cancer and of cancer of the womb.
[bio16-s5.5.6] People with a strong family history can be tested for these faulty genes. Those who carry one can have more frequent or earlier screening, and some choose surgery to reduce risk, e.g. removal of the breasts or ovaries.
[bio16-s5.5.7] The actress Angelina Jolie publicly described her choice of a preventive double mastectomy in 2013, after testing positive for a faulty BRCA1 gene. Her account greatly increased the number of women seeking testing. There is more on genetic testing in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio16-s5.6.b] Evaluating means weighing how strong the evidence is and what can be done about each factor. Each point below is a short argument you can use.
[bio16-s5.6.0] Avoidable or unavoidable: smoking, alcohol, diet, body weight, sun and sunbed exposure, radon and some infections can be changed or prevented, but age and inherited genes cannot.
[bio16-s5.6.1] The World Health Organization estimates that between 30% and 50% of cancers could be prevented by avoiding risk factors and using existing prevention strategies.
[bio16-s5.6.2] Size of effect: tobacco is the largest single avoidable cause of cancer. A strong effect, consistent across many studies, with a dose–response relationship and a known mechanism, is strong evidence of cause.
[bio16-s5.6.3] The link between smoking and lung cancer was first shown by Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill, in a study of lung-cancer patients in Britain published in 1950.
[bio16-s5.6.4] Factors interact: a person's risk depends on several factors together. Smoking and asbestos multiply each other's effects, and alcohol and smoking together raise the risk of mouth and throat cancer more than either alone.
[bio16-s5.6.5] An inherited BRCA mutation raises risk, but lifestyle still matters. So it is hard to say what caused any one person's cancer.
[bio16-s5.6.6] Time lag: cancers usually appear many years or decades after exposure, e.g. mesothelioma after asbestos and lung cancer after smoking. This makes causes harder to identify and means the benefits of prevention appear only years later.
[bio16-s5.6.7] Individual or public action: individuals can change their behaviour, but laws and public-health measures often have larger effects. Arguments against heavy regulation include personal freedom and cost; arguments for it include protecting others, e.g. from second-hand smoke, and reducing health-care costs.
[bio16-s5.7.b] Knowing a factor is linked to cancer is not the same as knowing it causes cancer. The kind of evidence behind a claim decides how far to trust it.
[bio16-s5.7.0] Much of the evidence is correlational. Most evidence about diet and lifestyle comes from observing large populations, which shows links but must rule out other explanations, e.g. people who eat more vegetables may also smoke less.
[bio16-s5.7.1] Laboratory and animal studies can show a mechanism, but results in animals don't always apply to humans. Media reports of single studies are often unreliable.
[bio16-s5.8.b] Laws and free programmes can lower cancer risk across a whole population, which is why public action is often more effective than asking individuals to change.
[bio16-s5.8.0] In 2004 Ireland became the first country in the world to ban smoking in all enclosed workplaces, including pubs and restaurants. This protects workers and customers from second-hand smoke.
[bio16-s5.8.1] Sunbed use by under-18s is banned in Ireland, which limits exposure to UV radiation during the years when sunburn does most harm.
[bio16-s5.8.2] Vaccination and screening programmes are free, so cost does not stop people taking part.
[bio16-s5.9.b] Prevention is better than treatment. Vaccines remove infections that cause cancer, and lifestyle changes remove the avoidable risk factors met earlier.
[bio16-s5.9.0] The HPV vaccine protects against infection by the HPV types that cause most cervical cancers, and some other cancers. It works best if given before any exposure to the virus, so it is offered free in Ireland to girls and boys in first year of secondary school.
[bio16-s5.9.1] A large Swedish study published in 2020 found that girls vaccinated before age 17 had close to 90% lower risk of invasive cervical cancer than unvaccinated women.
[bio16-s5.9.2] The HPV vaccine does not protect against every HPV type, so cervical screening is still needed. There is more on how vaccines work in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio16-s5.9.3] The hepatitis B vaccine is given to babies in Ireland as part of the 6-in-1 vaccine. By preventing hepatitis B infection it prevents many cases of liver cancer.
[bio16-s5.9.4] There is no vaccine against hepatitis C, but hepatitis C can now be cured with antiviral drugs.
[bio16-s5.9.5] Lifestyle changes that lower risk: don't smoke, and avoid second-hand smoke; keep a healthy body weight; be physically active; eat plenty of wholegrains, fibre, fruit and vegetables, and limit processed and red meat; limit alcohol.
[bio16-s5.9.6] Also protect skin from the sun with shade, clothing, a hat and sunscreen, and don't use sunbeds; test the home for radon; take part in vaccination and screening.
[bio16-s5.10.b] Finding cancer early gives the best chance of cure. Early detection includes screening of healthy people, self-checks, and diagnosis once something is suspected.
[bio16-s5.10.0] A cancer found early is usually small and has not spread (no metastasis), so it can often be removed completely and treatment is much more likely to succeed. Screening can also find pre-cancerous changes, which can be treated before cancer develops.
[bio16-s5.10.1] Screening means testing healthy people with no symptoms. Ireland's free national programmes are BreastCheck, which offers mammograms (X-rays of the breast) to women aged 50–69 every two years.
[bio16-s5.10.2] CervicalCheck screens women and people with a cervix aged 25–65. The sample is first tested for high-risk HPV, and only HPV-positive samples are examined under the microscope for abnormal cells.
[bio16-s5.10.3] BowelScreen is a free home test kit that detects hidden traces of blood in a stool sample, followed by a colonoscopy (a camera examination of the bowel) if positive.
[bio16-s5.10.4] Self-examination and symptoms: check breasts and testicles for lumps, and check moles using the ABCDE rule: Asymmetry, irregular Border, more than one Colour, Diameter more than 6 mm, and Evolving or changing.
[bio16-s5.10.5] Go to a doctor about warning signs such as an unexplained lump, unexplained weight loss, a persistent cough, or blood in the urine or stool.
[bio16-s5.10.6] Diagnosis: a biopsy, a small sample of tissue, is examined under the microscope for cancer cells. Scans (X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound) show where tumours are and whether they have spread. Blood tests can detect some cancers or the substances they release.
[bio16-s5.10.7] Limits of screening: no test is perfect. A false positive causes worry and unnecessary further tests; a false negative gives false reassurance; and some screening finds slow-growing cancers that would never have caused harm (overdiagnosis). So screening is offered to the age groups where the benefits are greatest.
[bio16-s5.11.b] Treatment depends on the type and spread of the cancer. For each treatment, learn how it works, its advantage and its disadvantage.
[bio16-s5.11.0] Surgery removes the tumour with a margin of surrounding normal tissue, and sometimes nearby lymph nodes. It is most effective for a solid tumour that has not spread; it is not possible for leukaemias (cancers of the blood) or widespread cancer.
[bio16-s5.11.1] Radiotherapy aims beams of ionising radiation at the tumour, damaging the DNA of the cancer cells so that they die. Rapidly dividing cells with faulty repair are especially vulnerable. Side effects come from damage to nearby normal tissue, e.g. sore skin and tiredness.
[bio16-s5.11.2] Chemotherapy uses drugs carried in the blood throughout the body to kill dividing cells, usually by damaging DNA or stopping DNA replication or mitosis. Unlike surgery, it can reach cancer cells that have spread.
[bio16-s5.11.3] Two chemotherapy drugs act on mitosis. Vincristine, from the Madagascar periwinkle plant, stops the spindle (the fibres that pull chromosomes apart) forming; paclitaxel (Taxol, first obtained from the bark of the Pacific yew tree) stops the spindle breaking down. Either way, chromosomes can't be separated.
[bio16-s5.11.4] Because chemotherapy also kills normal rapidly dividing cells, its side effects include hair loss (hair follicles), nausea and mouth ulcers (gut lining), and anaemia and infections (bone marrow). It is given in cycles to let normal tissues recover.
[bio16-s5.11.5] Hormone therapy is used for cancers that need a hormone to grow. Many breast cancers have oestrogen receptors, and the drug tamoxifen blocks the receptors, so oestrogen can't stimulate the cancer cells to divide.
[bio16-s5.11.6] A bone-marrow (stem cell) transplant is used for some leukaemias. After high-dose treatment destroys the diseased bone marrow, healthy blood-forming stem cells from a matched donor, or the patient's own cells collected earlier, are given to rebuild it.
[bio16-s5.12.b] Newer treatments and tests aim to hit cancer more precisely and find it sooner. For each, know what it is, what it offers and its limits.
[bio16-s5.12.0] Targeted therapy uses drugs designed to act on the protein made by a particular mutated gene in the cancer, so they harm normal cells less. Targeted drugs work only for cancers with that target, and cancers can become resistant.
[bio16-s5.12.1] Imatinib (Glivec) blocks the abnormal protein made by the BCR-ABL gene in chronic myeloid leukaemia. This gene is formed by the Philadelphia translocation, and the drug has turned a fatal leukaemia into one most patients live with for many years.
[bio16-s5.12.2] Trastuzumab (Herceptin) is an antibody that attaches to the HER2 receptor on breast cancer cells that have too many of these receptors.
[bio16-s5.12.3] Immunotherapy helps the patient's own immune system destroy cancer cells. Checkpoint-inhibitor drugs release the brakes that cancer cells use to switch off T lymphocytes (immune cells that kill abnormal cells). The 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine went to James Allison and Tasuku Honjo for this work.
[bio16-s5.12.4] Checkpoint inhibitors have produced long-lasting responses in some people with advanced melanoma and lung cancer. In CAR-T cell therapy, a patient's T cells are genetically modified to recognise their cancer cells, multiplied and returned to the body; it is used for some leukaemias and lymphomas.
[bio16-s5.12.5] Immunotherapy works in only some patients, can cause the immune system to attack healthy organs, and is very expensive.
[bio16-s5.12.6] Personalised (precision) medicine: sequencing the DNA of a patient's tumour shows which genes are mutated, so doctors can choose the drug most likely to work for that tumour rather than for the organ in which it started.
[bio16-s5.12.7] Personalised mRNA cancer vaccines, made to match the mutated proteins of one patient's tumour, train the immune system to attack it. They are in clinical trials, e.g. for melanoma, and are not yet routine.
[bio16-s5.13.b] Biopsies normally need a tissue sample taken by a procedure. A newer approach uses only a blood sample.
[bio16-s5.13.0] A liquid biopsy is a blood test that detects small fragments of DNA released by tumours into the blood, so no tissue needs to be removed.
[bio16-s5.13.1] This may allow cancers to be found earlier, and relapse (the cancer coming back) to be detected, without surgery. The tests are still being evaluated for accuracy.

CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio20-s1.10.5] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; oestrogen; female secondary sexual characteristics and thickening of the endometrium.
[bio20-s1.10.6] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; progesterone; maintains the endometrium and pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio20-s2: Hormonal manipulation
[bio20-s2.0.b] Hormones are chemical messengers that control growth and body functions. Because they act in tiny amounts, people can change how an organism works by adding hormones or blocking them. This is called hormonal manipulation, and it is used in sport, health and agriculture.
[bio20-s2.0.0] Hormonal manipulation is the deliberate use of natural or synthetic (man-made) hormones, or of drugs that block hormones, to change how an organism grows or functions.
[bio20-s2.0.1] It can work by adding a hormone the body lacks or by supplying extra, or by blocking a hormone's release or action, so the body's own control system is deliberately altered.
[bio20-s2.0.2] The syllabus asks you to recognise the impact of these manipulations on organisms, so for each use you need the benefit and the cost.
[bio20-s2.1.b] Some athletes take hormones to gain a physical edge. Two are misused: anabolic steroids, which build muscle, and EPO, which boosts the blood's oxygen supply. Both are dangerous and banned.
[bio20-s2.1.0] Anabolic steroids are synthetic derivatives of testosterone (the male sex hormone). They increase muscle mass and strength and speed up recovery from training, which is why athletes misuse them.
[bio20-s2.1.1] Anabolic steroids cause liver damage, increase the risk of heart disease, and cause acne and mood changes including aggression.
[bio20-s2.1.2] In males, anabolic steroids reduce sperm production, cause infertility and cause shrinking of the testes.
[bio20-s2.1.3] In females, anabolic steroids cause male characteristics to develop, because the drug is a form of a male hormone.
[bio20-s2.1.4] EPO (erythropoietin) is a hormone made by the kidneys that stimulates red blood cell production.
[bio20-s2.1.5] Athletes misuse EPO to increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, because red blood cells carry oxygen, and so their endurance.
[bio20-s2.1.6] Extra red cells thicken the blood, which increases the risk of clots, heart attack and stroke.
[bio20-s2.1.7] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) bans these substances in competitive sport and tests athletes through urine and blood samples. Their use is unfair to other competitors.
[bio20-s2.2.b] Doctors use hormones to replace ones the body no longer makes enough of, to control fertility and to relieve symptoms of the menopause such as hot flushes. Each treatment fixes a specific hormone problem.
[bio20-s2.2.0] Insulin injections treat type 1 diabetes, in which the pancreas makes little or no insulin, so blood glucose would otherwise rise too high.
[bio20-s2.2.1] Most insulin used today is human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria (bacteria given the human insulin gene). There is more on this in Genetic Engineering.
[bio20-s2.2.2] The contraceptive pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone, or progesterone alone. These are female sex hormones.
[bio20-s2.2.3] The pill inhibits (reduces) the release of FSH and LH, the pituitary hormones that make an egg mature and be released, so no egg matures and ovulation (release of an egg) is prevented.
[bio20-s2.2.4] Fertility treatment uses hormones to stimulate the ovaries to release eggs, which helps women who do not ovulate normally to become pregnant.
[bio20-s2.2.5] Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) supplies oestrogen, often with progesterone, after the menopause (when the ovaries stop producing these hormones) to relieve symptoms such as hot flushes and to reduce bone loss.
[bio20-s2.2.6] For some women, HRT slightly increases the risk of some cancers and of blood clots, so it carries a risk as well as a benefit.
[bio20-s2.2.7] Thyroxine tablets are given to people whose thyroid gland produces too little thyroxine, replacing the missing hormone.
[bio20-s2.3.b] Farmers use hormones to control breeding, boost yields and manage plants. Animal uses raise welfare and food-safety concerns, while plant hormones are used as weedkillers, rooting aids and ripening agents.
[bio20-s2.3.0] Progesterone, given as a vaginal sponge or implant, synchronises the breeding cycles of cattle and sheep, so a herd can be bred and give birth at the same time.
[bio20-s2.3.1] Growth-promoting hormones increase meat production in cattle.
[bio20-s2.3.2] Bovine somatotropin (BST), a growth hormone from cattle, increases milk yield in cattle.
[bio20-s2.3.3] Growth-promoting hormones and BST are banned in the EU, including Ireland, because of concerns about animal welfare and hormone residues (traces left behind) in food. BST is permitted in the USA.
[bio20-s2.3.4] Synthetic auxins such as 2,4-D are selective weedkillers (they kill some plants but not others). They kill broad-leaved weeds in cereal crops by making them grow uncontrollably.
[bio20-s2.3.5] Auxin rooting powder makes cuttings (pieces cut from a plant) form roots, so new plants can be grown from them.
[bio20-s2.3.6] Ethene gas is used to ripen fruit such as bananas in storage after transport, so fruit can be shipped unripe and ripened when needed.
[bio20-s2.4.b] Hormonal manipulation brings real gains but also real harms. To evaluate it, weigh the benefits against the costs for each use.
[bio20-s2.4.0] Benefits: better health, treatment of disease (such as insulin for type 1 diabetes), increased food production and convenience (such as synchronised breeding and ripening on demand).
[bio20-s2.4.1] Costs: side effects (such as those of anabolic steroids, EPO and HRT), an unfair advantage in sport, animal welfare concerns, hormone residues in food and environmental effects of weedkillers.

CANDIDATES bio21-s1: Structures and systems for response
[bio21-s1.0.b] Every living thing has to cope with a changing world, from a predator appearing to a drop in water supply. Detecting a change and reacting to it is called response, and it is the starting point for every system in this subsection.
[bio21-s1.0.0] Response is a characteristic of all living things: organisms detect stimuli (changes in their internal or external environment) and react to them in ways that help them survive.
[bio21-s1.0.1] The general pathway is stimulus → receptor (detects it) → coordinator (processes the information) → effector (a muscle or gland) → response.
[bio21-s1.0.2] In other words, a receptor notices the change, a coordinator decides what to do, and an effector carries it out, so the body reacts in a useful way rather than at random.
[bio21-s1.1.b] The nervous system is the body's fast communication network. It links the places that detect changes to the places that act on them.
[bio21-s1.1.0] Receptors in the sense organs (such as the skin, eye and ear) detect stimuli. Here sense organs are named only as receptors, with no structure needed.
[bio21-s1.1.1] Neurons (nerve cells) carry fast electrical impulses from the receptors to the brain and spinal cord, which act as the coordinator.
[bio21-s1.1.2] The brain and spinal cord send impulses along neurons to muscles and glands, which act as effectors, so the body can respond.
[bio21-s1.1.3] Nervous responses are fast and short-lived, because electrical impulses travel quickly and stop as soon as the stimulus ends. There is more on this in The Nervous System.
[bio21-s1.1.4] Diagram: a nervous pathway from receptor to coordinator to effector. Label the sense organ, sensory neuron, brain and spinal cord, motor neuron and muscle or gland.
[bio21-s1.2.b] The hormonal system is a second way of coordinating a response. Instead of electrical impulses along neurons, it uses chemical messengers carried in the blood.
[bio21-s1.2.0] Glands release hormones (chemical messengers) directly into the blood, rather than along neurons.
[bio21-s1.2.1] Hormones travel in the blood to the target organs, the organs that have the means to respond to them.
[bio21-s1.2.2] Hormonal responses are slower and longer-lasting than nervous responses, because a chemical has to be carried round the body in the blood and takes time to be cleared away.
[bio21-s1.2.3] Examples: adrenaline, released in danger, and insulin, released after a meal. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio21-s1.2.4] Diagram: a gland releasing a hormone into a blood vessel, with an arrow to a target organ. Label the gland, hormone, blood vessel and target organ.
[bio21-s1.3.b] Detecting a stimulus and deciding what to do achieves nothing unless the body moves. The musculoskeletal system, made of muscles and bones, does the moving.
[bio21-s1.3.0] Muscles are the effectors of most nervous responses, so most nerve impulses to effectors end at a muscle.
[bio21-s1.3.1] Muscles contract (shorten) and pull on bones at joints, the places where bones meet, to move the body.
[bio21-s1.3.2] Example: withdrawing a hand from heat, carried out by muscles pulling on the bones of the arm. There is more on this in The Musculoskeletal System.
[bio21-s1.3.3] Diagram: a muscle attached to a bone across a joint. Label the bone, the joint and the muscle.
[bio21-s1.4.b] Some stimuli are not changes in temperature or danger from outside but invaders inside the body. The immune system is the response system that deals with them.
[bio21-s1.4.0] The immune system responds to pathogens (disease-causing organisms) and other foreign antigens (molecules the body recognises as not its own).
[bio21-s1.4.1] White blood cells respond in two ways: they engulf bacteria, or they produce antibodies (proteins made against a specific foreign antigen). There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio21-s1.5.b] Responses rarely use just one system. A single danger can involve the nervous, hormonal and musculoskeletal systems one after another.
[bio21-s1.5.0] In danger, the nervous system stimulates the adrenal glands (hormone-releasing glands) to release adrenaline, so a nerve signal triggers a hormone response.
[bio21-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the muscles for action, so the body is ready to act.
[bio21-s1.5.2] The musculoskeletal system then carries out the movement, as the effector.
[bio21-s1.6.b] Plants have no nervous system or muscles. They respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and through their structure.
[bio21-s1.6.0] Because plants cannot run away from danger, their protection depends on features built into their structure and on chemicals they make.
[bio21-s1.7.b] An anatomical adaptation is a feature of a plant's structure that protects it. These features work against grazing animals, insects and pathogens (disease-causing organisms).
[bio21-s1.7.0] Thorns, spines and prickles deter grazing animals, since they make the plant painful to eat: holly leaves have spines, brambles have prickles and cacti have spines.
[bio21-s1.7.1] A thick waxy cuticle (a waterproof layer on the surface) and bark form barriers to pathogens and to water loss, so microbes find it hard to get in and water stays inside.
[bio21-s1.7.2] Stinging hairs inject irritants into anything that touches them, as in nettles.
[bio21-s1.7.3] Hairy leaves deter insects.
[bio21-s1.7.4] Diagram: a labelled leaf showing the waxy cuticle and a spiny leaf margin, such as holly.
[bio21-s1.8.b] Plants also protect themselves by making chemicals. Some make a plant unpleasant or poisonous to eat, and others fight infection.
[bio21-s1.8.0] Toxic or bad-tasting chemicals deter herbivores (plant-eating animals): nicotine in tobacco, caffeine in tea and coffee plants, and tannins in oak leaves.
[bio21-s1.8.1] Nettle stings contain histamine and formic acid, which is why the stinging hairs of nettles act as both an anatomical and a chemical defence.
[bio21-s1.8.2] Some plants produce antimicrobial chemicals when infected, which limit the spread of a pathogen.
[bio21-s1.9.b] Plants also react to changes in their surroundings. Some of these responses are slow and some are quick.
[bio21-s1.9.0] Guard cells (the pair of cells around each stoma, or leaf pore) close the stomata when water is short, reducing water loss. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio21-s1.9.1] The sensitive plant, Mimosa pudica, folds its leaves rapidly when touched, a fast response by a plant.
[bio21-s1.10.b] Putting the two side by side shows why plants rely on protective adaptations: plants cannot move away from danger, as humans can, and their responses are generally slower.
[bio21-s1.10.0] Speed: human responses can be fast, through nerves, whereas plant responses are generally slower.
[bio21-s1.10.1] Mechanism: humans use nerves and hormones, whereas plants use chemicals and their structure.
[bio21-s1.10.2] Protection: plants cannot move away from danger, so their protective adaptations matter more.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio22-s4: Viruses: replication
[bio22-s4.0.b] A virus is not a cell, so it lacks the machinery that every living cell uses to build new copies of itself. This is why a virus can only reproduce by taking over a host cell (the living cell it infects).
[bio22-s4.0.0] A virus has no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), almost no enzymes and no energy supply, so it can't make anything by itself.
[bio22-s4.0.1] It reproduces by delivering its nucleic acid (its DNA or RNA) into a host cell. The host cell then makes new virus particles using its own ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier), nucleotides (the building blocks of nucleic acids) and amino acids (the building blocks of proteins).
[bio22-s4.1.b] Every virus follows the same basic cycle of six stages, which ends with new viruses leaving the cell. The first three get the virus's nucleic acid inside the right kind of cell and free to act.
[bio22-s4.1.0] Stage 1, attachment: proteins on the capsid (the protein coat) or the envelope (the outer membrane some viruses have) bind to specific receptor proteins on the host cell membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.1] HIV binds to the CD4 receptor on helper T cells, and the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds to the ACE2 receptor on cells lining the airways. Receptor binding decides which cells a virus can infect, because only cells with the matching receptor can be attached to.
[bio22-s4.1.2] Stage 2, entry: a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria) injects only its nucleic acid through the bacterial wall, leaving the empty coat outside.
[bio22-s4.1.3] Animal viruses enter whole, either by the host membrane folding in around them (endocytosis) or, for enveloped viruses, by the envelope fusing with the host membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.4] Stage 3, uncoating: inside the cell, the capsid breaks down and releases the viral nucleic acid, so its genes can be read.
[bio22-s4.2.b] Once the viral nucleic acid is free inside the cell, the virus uses the cell's machinery to make its parts, put them together and escape. The new viruses then repeat the cycle in other cells.
[bio22-s4.2.0] Stage 4, replication and synthesis: the viral genes take over the cell. Viral mRNA (the message copied from a gene) is made, and the host's ribosomes translate it into viral proteins, which are capsid proteins and viral enzymes.
[bio22-s4.2.1] In stage 4 the viral nucleic acid is copied many times, and host-cell activities are often shut down so that the cell's resources go into making viruses.
[bio22-s4.2.2] Stage 5, assembly: new nucleic acid and capsid proteins come together to form many new virus particles, often hundreds per cell. Viruses are assembled from parts, not grown or divided.
[bio22-s4.2.3] Stage 6, release: either the cell bursts (lysis), releasing all the viruses at once and killing the cell, or, for enveloped viruses, each new virus pushes out through the membrane and takes a piece of it as its envelope (budding), so the cell can survive for a time.
[bio22-s4.2.4] The new viruses infect neighbouring cells, and the cycle repeats.
[bio22-s4.2.5] Diagram: the lytic cycle of a bacteriophage in five or six sketches in a circle: phage attached by tail fibres; DNA injected; phage DNA copied and proteins made; new phages assembled; cell bursting. Label phage, bacterial cell wall, phage DNA, bacterial chromosome, new phages.
[bio22-s4.2.6] Diagram: an enveloped animal virus, such as influenza, entering a cell by endocytosis or fusion and leaving by budding, with the host membrane forming the new envelope.
[bio22-s4.3.b] A virus does not always burst its host straight away. Some bacteriophages (and some human viruses) can instead hide inside the host for a time, so there are two possible routes after infection.
[bio22-s4.3.0] Lytic cycle: the virus replicates immediately, following the stages above, and the host cell bursts. Example: bacteriophage T4 infecting the bacterium E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.1] Lysogenic cycle: some viruses insert their DNA into the host cell's chromosome, where it stays inactive and is copied every time the cell divides, so all daughter cells carry it.
[bio22-s4.3.2] In bacteria the inserted DNA is called a prophage. Example: bacteriophage lambda in E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.3] A trigger, such as stress like UV light, can cut the viral DNA out of the chromosome and start the lytic cycle.
[bio22-s4.3.4] Latency in human viruses: some viruses hide in cells in an inactive state for years and reactivate later.
[bio22-s4.3.5] Herpes simplex virus stays in nerve cells after the first infection and reactivates to cause repeated cold sores.
[bio22-s4.3.6] Varicella-zoster virus causes chickenpox, stays in nerve cells, and can reactivate decades later as shingles.
[bio22-s4.3.7] A latent virus is hidden from the immune system and from antiviral drugs, because it is not making viral proteins or new viruses that they could target.
[bio22-s4.4.b] Viruses cause disease in several different ways, from simply destroying cells to turning the body's own defences against it. Each mode of action follows from what a virus does to the cells it infects.
[bio22-s4.4.0] Killing cells: lysis destroys infected cells. Influenza destroys cells lining the airways, causing a sore throat and cough, and the cold virus damages cells of the nose and throat.
[bio22-s4.4.1] Disrupting cell function: the virus diverts the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and energy to making viruses, so the cell stops its normal work.
[bio22-s4.4.2] Damaging the immune system: HIV infects and destroys helper T cells, the white blood cells that coordinate the immune response.
[bio22-s4.4.3] As helper T cell numbers fall over years, the immune system can no longer coordinate its responses, and the person develops AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome).
[bio22-s4.4.4] People with AIDS die of opportunistic infections and cancers, which a healthy immune system would control but which take hold when the immune system is weakened.
[bio22-s4.4.5] Causing cancer: some viruses insert genes that interfere with the control of cell division, e.g. human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio22-s4.4.6] Triggering harmful immune responses: much of the fever, aches and tissue damage in viral illness is caused by the body's own immune response (inflammation) to infected cells.
[bio22-s4.5.b] Viruses are grouped by the type of nucleic acid they carry, and this affects where they replicate, which enzymes they need and how fast they change. Comparing the two groups is a likely exam question.
[bio22-s4.5.0] Genetic material: a DNA virus has DNA as its genetic material; an RNA virus has RNA.
[bio22-s4.5.1] DNA viruses include herpes simplex and varicella-zoster (chickenpox) viruses, human papillomavirus (HPV), adenoviruses, the smallpox virus, and bacteriophages T4 and lambda.
[bio22-s4.5.2] RNA viruses include influenza, measles, mumps, polio, rhinoviruses (the common cold), SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Ebola and tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s4.5.3] Where they replicate: most DNA viruses enter the host nucleus and use the host's own enzymes, such as DNA polymerase and RNA polymerase, to copy their DNA and make mRNA.
[bio22-s4.5.4] Poxviruses such as the smallpox virus are an exception: they replicate in the cytoplasm with their own enzymes.
[bio22-s4.5.5] Most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm and must carry or code for their own enzyme, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, to copy RNA from RNA, because host cells have no enzyme that does this.
[bio22-s4.5.6] Mutation rate: the enzyme that copies RNA has no proofreading (no checking for mistakes), so RNA viruses make many more copying errors and mutate much faster than DNA viruses, whose DNA is copied by host polymerases that proofread.
[bio22-s4.5.7] RNA viruses such as influenza and HIV change their surface antigens quickly, so new strains escape existing immunity. This is why flu vaccines are updated every year, and drug resistance appears quickly.
[bio22-s4.6.b] Once inside the cell, an RNA virus's genes still have to be turned into proteins. RNA viruses do this in one of two ways, depending on the virus.
[bio22-s4.6.0] In some RNA viruses, such as poliovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the RNA acts directly as mRNA when it enters the cell and is translated at once by the host's ribosomes.
[bio22-s4.6.1] In others, such as influenza and measles, the RNA must first be copied into a complementary strand that acts as mRNA.
[bio22-s4.6.2] DNA is a more stable molecule than RNA, which fits with DNA viruses mutating more slowly.
[bio22-s4.6.3] Mutation as a factor in disease emergence is covered further in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s4.7.b] Retroviruses break the usual rule that information flows from DNA to RNA. HIV is the key example, and its cycle has a few extra steps compared with the general cycle.
[bio22-s4.7.0] A retrovirus is an RNA virus that makes a DNA copy of its RNA inside the host cell, using its own enzyme, reverse transcriptase, which reverses the usual direction of DNA to RNA. HIV is an example.
[bio22-s4.7.1] HIV replication, step 1: its envelope protein binds the CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
[bio22-s4.7.2] Step 2: the envelope fuses with the cell membrane, and the capsid enters.
[bio22-s4.7.3] Step 3: reverse transcriptase makes double-stranded DNA from the viral RNA.
[bio22-s4.7.4] Step 4: a second viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this DNA into the host's chromosome, where it is called a provirus and can stay hidden for years.
[bio22-s4.7.5] Step 5: when the cell is activated, the provirus is transcribed into viral mRNA and new viral RNA, and viral proteins are made.
[bio22-s4.7.6] Step 6: new viruses assemble and bud from the cell, taking an envelope of host membrane.
[bio22-s4.7.7] Reverse transcriptase makes many errors, so HIV mutates very quickly, which is one reason there is still no effective HIV vaccine.
[bio22-s4.7.8] Diagram: an HIV particle (envelope with surface proteins, capsid, two RNA strands and reverse transcriptase), and a flow of the six steps into and out of a helper T cell. Label receptor, viral RNA, viral DNA, host chromosome (provirus) and budding virus.
[bio22-s4.8.b] Antibiotics don't work on viruses, so viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs that interrupt one stage of the replication cycle. Each drug targets a step that depends on a viral protein.
[bio22-s4.8.0] Antiviral drugs block a step in the cycle that uses a viral protein, so the host cell isn't harmed.
[bio22-s4.8.1] Aciclovir blocks the DNA polymerase of herpes viruses, which treats cold sores and shingles.
[bio22-s4.8.2] Oseltamivir blocks neuraminidase, the influenza enzyme that releases new viruses from the cell surface.
[bio22-s4.8.3] HIV drugs include reverse transcriptase inhibitors, of which zidovudine (AZT), approved in 1987, was the first.
[bio22-s4.8.4] HIV drugs also include protease inhibitors, which stop new viral proteins being cut into their working form.
[bio22-s4.8.5] HIV is treated with a combination of several antiretroviral drugs at once (antiretroviral therapy).
[bio22-s4.8.6] Antiretroviral therapy keeps the virus at very low levels and lets the immune system recover, but it doesn't cure the infection, because the provirus remains in the host DNA.
[bio22-s4.8.7] Using several drugs together makes resistance much less likely, since the virus would need several mutations at once.
[bio22-s4.9.b] The syllabus asks you to model how viruses replicate within cells. A model turns the six stages into something you can draw, handle and use to make predictions, while leaving out some detail.
[bio22-s4.9.0] To model the cycle, first list the stages in order: attachment, entry, uncoating, replication and synthesis, assembly and release. Then draw each as a labelled sketch or box, showing the host-cell part used at that stage (receptor, membrane, ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides).
[bio22-s4.9.1] Join the stages with arrows into a cycle, because the new viruses go on to infect other cells.
[bio22-s4.9.2] Mark how a DNA virus, an RNA virus and a retrovirus differ at stage 4.
[bio22-s4.9.3] Mark where an antiviral drug would act.
[bio22-s4.9.4] A physical model can use card or modelling clay to represent viruses and cells, to show attachment and budding.
[bio22-s4.9.5] The model shows the order of events, the dependence on host machinery, and why one infected cell releases many viruses. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s4.9.6] The model simplifies the timing (a cycle can take minutes to hours), the numbers released, the molecular steps, and the differences between viruses.
[bio22-s4.9.7] Worked prediction: if each infected cell releases 100 viruses and each of those infects a new cell, two cycles from one virus give 100 × 100 = 10,000 viruses. This is an illustrative calculation, not a figure for a real virus.
[bio22-s4.9.8] Diagram: a flow chart of the cycle, with stages as labelled boxes joined by arrows into a loop, and the host-cell part used at each stage marked on it.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio30-s2: Transport of water and minerals
[bio30-s2.0.b] Water moves in one continuous flow, from the soil through the plant and out into the air. This flow is the transpiration stream, and its first part carries water from the soil into the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.0] Overview: water moves from the soil into the root hairs (tiny outgrowths of root cells that give a large surface for absorption), across the root cortex (the layer of cells between the surface and the centre), into the xylem (the water-carrying tubes), up the stem and into the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.1] It then leaves the leaves as water vapour through the stomata (pores in the leaf surface), completing the continuous flow called the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.0.2] Stage 1, into the root hair by osmosis: soil water is a dilute solution with a higher water concentration, while the cell sap in the root hair's vacuole is more concentrated, with a lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.3] Water therefore passes in through the selectively permeable cell membrane, moving from higher to lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.4] Stage 2, across the cortex: water moves from cell to cell by osmosis, each cell inwards being slightly more concentrated than the one before, so the water keeps flowing inwards. It also seeps along the cellulose cell walls and through the spaces between cells.
[bio30-s2.0.5] Water then passes through the endodermis (the ring of cells around the centre of the root) into the xylem at the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.6] Stage 3, up the xylem: water travels up the root and stem xylem as continuous columns, which carry it into the veins of the leaves. The force that moves it is the transpiration pull, which comes from evaporation in the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.7] At Higher Level, root pressure and the cohesion–tension model give a fuller account of how water rises, in their own blocks below.
[bio30-s2.1.b] Once water reaches the leaf it has to get out again, and the way it leaves is what pulls the whole column up from the roots. This part of the route also shows that the plant itself does no work to lift the water.
[bio30-s2.1.0] Stage 4, into the leaf cells: water leaves the xylem in the veins and passes by osmosis into the mesophyll cells (the cells in the middle layer of the leaf), keeping them turgid (firm and full of water) and supplying a small amount for photosynthesis.
[bio30-s2.1.1] Stage 5, out of the leaf: water evaporates from the moist walls of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, which become saturated with water vapour. The vapour then diffuses out through the open stomata into the drier air outside, from a higher to a lower concentration of water vapour.
[bio30-s2.1.2] Transpiration pull: as water evaporates from the leaves, more water is drawn out of the xylem to replace it, so the whole column of water in the xylem is pulled up from the roots, like drinking through a straw.
[bio30-s2.1.3] The energy for the transpiration pull comes from the sun's heat, which causes evaporation; the plant uses none of its own ATP (its cell energy currency) to move water up, so the pull is a passive process.
[bio30-s2.1.4] Diagram: an outline of a whole plant with arrows showing the route soil water, root hair, cortex, root xylem, stem xylem, leaf vein, mesophyll cell, air space, stoma, air; label osmosis (root hair, cortex), evaporation (mesophyll cell walls) and diffusion (stoma).
[bio30-s2.2.b] Transpiration is the process that drives the water flow described above. It brings real benefits, but it is also a cost the plant cannot avoid, so you need to be able to give both sides.
[bio30-s2.2.0] Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the parts of a plant above the ground, mainly through the stomata of the leaves. Small amounts are lost through the cuticle (the waxy surface layer) and the lenticels (small pores in woody stems).
[bio30-s2.2.1] Most of the water a plant absorbs is lost by transpiration; only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis or kept in the cells. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio30-s2.2.2] Transpiration benefits the plant by pulling water and dissolved minerals up from the roots to the leaves, the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.2.3] It keeps the leaf cells supplied with water, so they stay turgid and the leaves stay firm and spread out to the light. Evaporation also cools the leaves in strong sunlight, because evaporating water carries heat away.
[bio30-s2.2.4] Transpiration is unavoidable. The stomata must be open to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis, and water vapour escapes through the same pores.
[bio30-s2.2.5] If water is lost faster than the roots can take it in, the cells lose turgor (they go limp) and the plant wilts. Prolonged wilting stops photosynthesis and can kill the plant, so the plant balances gas exchange against water loss.
[bio30-s2.2.6] Conditions that increase transpiration are bright light, high temperature, wind, low humidity, a large leaf area and a thin cuticle. Each is explained and investigated in Investigation: rate of transpiration.
[bio30-s2.3.b] A stoma is a pore that lets CO₂ in and water vapour out, and the plant can open or close it. The guard cells on either side of the pore do this by changing how much water they hold.
[bio30-s2.3.0] Each stoma is a pore between two guard cells. The wall of a guard cell next to the pore, the inner wall, is thicker and less stretchy than the outer wall, and this difference is what makes the cell bend when it swells.
[bio30-s2.3.1] Opening: in the light, guard cells actively take in potassium ions (K⁺) from neighbouring epidermal cells (the ordinary cells of the leaf surface), using ATP. This raises the solute concentration in the guard cells, so water enters them by osmosis from the surrounding cells.
[bio30-s2.3.2] The guard cells swell and become turgid. Because the thick inner wall stretches less than the thin outer wall, the cells bend outwards and curve apart, and the pore opens.
[bio30-s2.3.3] Closing: potassium ions leave the guard cells, water follows by osmosis, the guard cells become flaccid (limp) and straighten, and the pore closes.
[bio30-s2.3.4] Diagram: a pair of guard cells drawn twice, once open (turgid, curved apart, pore open) and once closed (flaccid, straight, pore closed); label guard cell, thick inner wall, thin outer wall, chloroplast, nucleus and stoma (pore); add arrows for K⁺ and water entering when the stoma opens.
[bio30-s2.4.b] The plant does not open its stomata at random. It opens them when CO₂ is needed and closes them when water must be saved, and it places them where least water is lost.
[bio30-s2.4.0] Stomata are usually open in daylight, when CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis, and when the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is low. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s2.4.1] Stomata close at night, when there is no photosynthesis and so no need for CO₂, and in very hot, dry conditions. They also close when CO₂ concentration inside the leaf is high.
[bio30-s2.4.2] When water is short and the plant begins to wilt, a chemical signal from the roots closes the stomata, saving water even though photosynthesis slows.
[bio30-s2.4.3] Stomata are mostly on the lower surface of a broad leaf, where it is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates from them. Leaves floating on water, such as the water lily, have them on the upper surface instead, because the lower surface touches the water.
[bio30-s2.5.b] Because transpiration cannot be stopped, plants survive by limiting it. Their leaves and habits are adapted to lose less water, most of all in dry, windy or cold places.
[bio30-s2.5.0] Leaf features that reduce water loss include a waxy, waterproof cuticle, thicker on the leaves of plants in dry or windy places; most stomata on the lower, shaded surface; and stomata that close at night and in drought.
[bio30-s2.5.1] Deciduous trees (trees that drop their leaves each year) shed their leaves in autumn, so they do not lose water in winter, when cold soil water is hard to absorb.
[bio30-s2.5.2] Evergreen conifers have needle-shaped leaves with a small surface area and a thick cuticle, which means little water evaporates even though the leaves stay on all year.
[bio30-s2.5.3] Plants of dry habitats (xerophytes) have extra adaptations. Marram grass of sand dunes rolls its leaves into a tube, with the stomata on the inside surface, trapping humid air. Cacti have leaves reduced to spines and store water in a swollen green stem.
[bio30-s2.6.b] Water is not the only thing roots take in. They also absorb dissolved minerals from the soil, but minerals enter by a different process from water, and that process costs the plant energy.
[bio30-s2.6.0] Plants need minerals such as nitrate, phosphate, potassium, magnesium and calcium. They are taken up from the soil water as dissolved ions, for example nitrate (NO₃⁻), potassium (K⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺).
[bio30-s2.6.1] Mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside the root hair cells than in the soil water, so they must be pumped in against their concentration gradient by carrier proteins in the cell membrane, using ATP from respiration. This is active transport.
[bio30-s2.6.2] Where the soil concentration is higher than in the cell, some ions also enter by diffusion.
[bio30-s2.6.3] Because active transport needs ATP, roots need oxygen for aerobic respiration. In waterlogged soil the air spaces fill with water, the roots get too little oxygen, mineral uptake falls and the plant grows poorly.
[bio30-s2.6.4] This is one reason soil must be well drained, and why roots have air spaces in the cortex.
[bio30-s2.6.5] The ions move across the cortex to the xylem and are carried up, dissolved in the water of the transpiration stream, to the leaves, growing tips and other parts, where they are taken into cells. The faster the transpiration, the faster minerals reach the leaves.
[bio30-s2.6.6] Pumping mineral ions into the root also makes the root cells more concentrated than the soil water, which keeps water entering by osmosis.
[bio30-s2.7.b] Transpiration pulls water up from above, but roots can also push it up from below. This push is called root pressure, and it is an active process that needs living root cells.
[bio30-s2.7.0] Root pressure is a pressure in the root xylem that pushes water a short distance up the stem from below.
[bio30-s2.7.1] Living root cells actively pump mineral ions into the xylem, using ATP. This makes the xylem sap more concentrated than the surrounding cells, so water passes into the xylem by osmosis, building up a pressure that pushes the water upwards.
[bio30-s2.7.2] Evidence: when a stem is cut off near ground level, sap oozes from the cut stump for some time, pushed out by the roots. Grapevines pruned in spring bleed sap in this way.
[bio30-s2.7.3] Guttation: on cool, humid nights or early mornings, when transpiration is very slow, drops of water are forced out through special pores at the tips and edges of leaves, for example on grass and strawberry leaves.
[bio30-s2.7.4] Guttation drops are not dew, because they appear at the leaf edges, not all over the surface.
[bio30-s2.7.5] Root pressure needs living, respiring root cells. It stops if the roots are killed, cooled or deprived of oxygen.
[bio30-s2.7.6] That it stops in these conditions shows that root pressure is an active process.
[bio30-s2.7.7] Root pressure is most significant at night and in spring, before the leaves have opened and when transpiration is low, and it can refill xylem columns that contain air bubbles.
[bio30-s2.7.8] Root pressure is far too weak to raise water to the top of a tall tree, and some plants show little or none, so it is not the main mechanism.
[bio30-s2.8.b] Root pressure is too weak to lift water to the top of a tall tree, so something else must do it. The cohesion–tension model explains how a pulling force from the leaves, and water's tendency to stick together, raises an unbroken column of water.
[bio30-s2.8.0] The cohesion–tension model explains how water rises to the tops of the tallest trees. It was proposed in 1894 by two Irish scientists at Trinity College Dublin, the botanist Henry Dixon and the physicist John Joly.
[bio30-s2.8.1] 1. Transpiration: water evaporates from the walls of the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata.
[bio30-s2.8.2] 2. The mesophyll cells replace the lost water by osmosis from the cells deeper in the leaf, and these take water from the xylem in the veins.
[bio30-s2.8.3] 3. Tension: removing water from the top of the xylem puts the column of water in the xylem under tension, a pulling force or negative pressure, which is passed all the way down the stem to the roots. This is the transpiration pull.
[bio30-s2.8.4] 4. Cohesion: water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, so they stick together and the column does not break. Each molecule pulled up drags the one behind it, so the whole unbroken column moves up as one.
[bio30-s2.8.5] 5. Adhesion: water molecules are also attracted to the walls of the narrow xylem vessels. This helps hold the column up against gravity and stops it pulling away from the walls.
[bio30-s2.8.6] 6. At the bottom, water is drawn into the root xylem from the cortex, and into the root hairs from the soil, to replace it.
[bio30-s2.8.7] The process is passive, so the plant spends no ATP on it. The lignified (stiffened with lignin) walls of the xylem stop the vessels collapsing inwards under the tension.
[bio30-s2.9.b] A model is only convincing if observations support it. Two observations show that the water in the xylem really is under tension, and one other factor is too weak to matter.
[bio30-s2.9.0] The trunk of a tree becomes very slightly narrower during the day, when transpiration is fastest, as the tension pulls the xylem walls inwards.
[bio30-s2.9.1] When a xylem vessel under tension is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out. If air enters the xylem the column breaks and water flow in that vessel stops.
[bio30-s2.9.2] This is why the stems of cut flowers should be cut again under water before being placed in a vase.
[bio30-s2.9.3] Capillarity, the rise of water up narrow tubes by adhesion, raises water only a short way and is a minor factor.
[bio30-s2.10.b] The two mechanisms work together to move water, but they differ in direction, energy source, strength and timing. Comparing them in pairs is the clearest way to keep them apart.
[bio30-s2.10.0] Root pressure pushes water from below; cohesion–tension pulls it from above.
[bio30-s2.10.1] Root pressure is active, using ATP in the root cells; cohesion–tension is passive, powered by the sun's energy, which evaporates the water.
[bio30-s2.10.2] Root pressure raises water only a short distance; cohesion–tension is the main mechanism and can lift water to the top of the tallest trees.
[bio30-s2.10.3] Root pressure is most important at night and in spring, when transpiration is low; cohesion–tension works when the stomata are open and transpiration is fast, mainly by day.

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.
