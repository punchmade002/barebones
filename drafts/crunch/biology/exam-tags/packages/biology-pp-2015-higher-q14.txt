CANDIDATES bio11-s3: Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance
[bio11-s3.0.b] Most of your genes sit on the chromosomes inside the nucleus of each cell, and nuclear inheritance is how those genes pass from parents to offspring. It follows a tidy, predictable route from the parent's nucleus to the child's.
[bio11-s3.0.0] Nuclear inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried on the chromosomes in the nucleus. Nearly all of an organism's genes are nuclear; in humans, about 20,000 protein-coding genes.
[bio11-s3.0.1] 1. The DNA in the nucleus is copied before division, so each new cell can receive a full set of genetic information.
[bio11-s3.0.2] 2. Meiosis (the division that makes gametes, the sex cells) separates each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent). Each gamete therefore receives one chromosome of every pair, and so one allele (version) of each gene.
[bio11-s3.0.3] 3. At fertilisation the nuclei of a male and a female gamete fuse, so the zygote (the first cell of the new individual) receives one set of chromosomes from each parent.
[bio11-s3.0.4] 4. Mitosis (ordinary cell division) then passes the full set of chromosomes to every body cell as the zygote grows.
[bio11-s3.0.5] Both parents contribute equally: the offspring inherits one allele of each autosomal gene (a gene on a non-sex chromosome) from its mother and one from its father. So for nuclear genes it does not matter which parent carries an allele.
[bio11-s3.0.6] For example, a tall male crossed with a short female gives the same results as a short male crossed with a tall female (reciprocal crosses). The exception is genes on the sex chromosomes.
[bio11-s3.0.7] Each gamete gets only one allele of each pair, chosen at random, so nuclear genes are inherited in the predictable ratios described by Mendel's laws, and new combinations of alleles arise in every generation. There is more on this in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s3.1.b] The nucleus is not the only place in a cell that holds DNA. Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry a little of their own, and genes in this DNA are inherited by a different route from nuclear genes.
[bio11-s3.1.0] Non-nuclear (also called extranuclear or cytoplasmic) inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried in DNA outside the nucleus. This DNA is in mitochondria, which are found in all eukaryotes, and in chloroplasts, which are found in plants and algae only.
[bio11-s3.1.1] Each mitochondrion and chloroplast contains several copies of its own small, circular DNA molecule, like the DNA of a bacterium. It is not wound around histones (the proteins that nuclear DNA is wrapped around). Each organelle also has its own small (70S) ribosomes.
[bio11-s3.1.2] A cell contains many such organelles, so it has hundreds to thousands of copies of the organelle DNA, compared with two copies of each nuclear gene.
[bio11-s3.1.3] Organelle genes code for some of the proteins the organelle needs: for mitochondria, some proteins of aerobic respiration (releasing energy from food using oxygen); for chloroplasts, some proteins of photosynthesis. They also code for the organelle's own rRNA and tRNA (the RNA parts of its ribosomes and protein-making machinery).
[bio11-s3.1.4] Most proteins in these organelles are coded by nuclear genes and imported, so the organelle depends on both its own genes and the nuclear genes.
[bio11-s3.1.5] Mitochondria and chloroplasts are thought to be descended from bacteria that came to live inside an ancestral cell, which is why they have their own DNA. Evidence includes their circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membrane and division by splitting in two.
[bio11-s3.1.6] New mitochondria and chloroplasts come only from the division of existing ones; the cell does not make them from scratch. This is why the organelle DNA is passed on only along with the cytoplasm that holds the organelles.
[bio11-s3.2.b] Chromosomes are moved around by a precise mechanism, but organelles are not. This difference, together with the way the egg and sperm differ in size, explains why non-nuclear genes usually come from the mother only.
[bio11-s3.2.0] Organelles lie in the cytoplasm (the cell contents outside the nucleus), and they are shared out between daughter cells at random when the cytoplasm divides, not by the spindle (the fibres that pull chromosomes apart) as chromosomes are.
[bio11-s3.2.1] Their genes therefore do not segregate (separate into different gametes) in meiosis, and do not follow Mendel's laws or Mendelian ratios.
[bio11-s3.2.2] In humans and most animals, the egg contributes nearly all the cytoplasm of the zygote, including its mitochondria. The sperm contributes almost only its nucleus; the few sperm mitochondria, in its midpiece, that enter the egg are destroyed.
[bio11-s3.2.3] So mitochondrial genes are inherited from the mother only. In most flowering plants, chloroplasts (and mitochondria) are likewise inherited through the egg cell, from the female parent.
[bio11-s3.2.4] Consequence 1: all the offspring of a mother share her mitochondrial DNA, whatever the father.
[bio11-s3.2.5] Consequence 2: reciprocal crosses give different results, with the offspring resembling the female parent for the trait, unlike nuclear genes where reciprocal crosses give the same results.
[bio11-s3.2.6] Consequence 3: mitochondrial DNA does not mix between two parents, so it changes only by mutation and passes down the female line almost unchanged.
[bio11-s3.3.b] An early experiment on plants showed non-nuclear inheritance in action. It is a worked example of offspring copying the female parent rather than following Mendel's ratios.
[bio11-s3.3.0] In 1909 Carl Correns studied the four-o'clock plant (Mirabilis jalapa), which has branches bearing green, white or variegated (green-and-white) leaves. The white areas lack working chloroplasts, so they cannot photosynthesise.
[bio11-s3.3.1] Seeds from flowers on green branches gave green seedlings, and seeds from flowers on white branches gave white seedlings, which soon died.
[bio11-s3.3.2] Seeds from variegated branches gave a mixture of seedlings, whatever branch the pollen came from.
[bio11-s3.3.3] The offspring took the leaf type of the female parent. Leaf colour was inherited through the chloroplasts in the egg's cytoplasm, which made this one of the first demonstrations of non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio11-s3.4.b] Human mitochondrial DNA is tiny, but because of maternal inheritance it has medical and scientific uses. Faults in it cause disease, and its unmixed female line makes it a useful record.
[bio11-s3.4.0] Human mitochondrial DNA is about 16,600 base pairs long and carries 37 genes. By comparison, there are about 20,000 protein-coding genes on nuclear chromosomes of up to hundreds of millions of base pairs each.
[bio11-s3.4.1] Mutations in mitochondrial DNA can cause diseases that mostly affect tissues with high energy demands, such as muscle, nerves, brain and eyes, because they reduce aerobic respiration, the process that supplies those tissues' energy. Example: Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), which causes loss of central vision, usually in young adults.
[bio11-s3.4.2] An affected mother can pass a mitochondrial disease to all her children, sons and daughters, because every child gets its mitochondria from her egg. An affected father passes it to none, because his mitochondria are not passed on. Sons inherit their mother's mitochondria too, but only daughters pass them on.
[bio11-s3.4.3] Severity varies: a cell may contain a mixture of normal and mutant mitochondria, and the proportion passed to each egg varies, so children of the same mother can be affected to different degrees.
[bio11-s3.4.4] Mitochondrial donation (“three-parent IVF”) replaces a mother's faulty mitochondria with healthy mitochondria from a donor egg, keeping the nuclear DNA of both parents. In 2015 the UK became the first country to allow it by law.
[bio11-s3.4.5] In mitochondrial donation the child's nuclear DNA comes from its two parents and its small amount of mitochondrial DNA from the donor. There is more on the ethics in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio11-s3.4.6] Because mitochondrial DNA passes down the female line without mixing and changes only by mutation, it is used to trace maternal ancestry and human migration and evolution.
[bio11-s3.4.7] It is also used to identify remains when nuclear DNA is too damaged, since there are many copies per cell and it survives better.
[bio11-s3.5.b] Putting the two systems side by side shows how a difference in where the DNA is kept leads to differences in how it is passed on. Each point below pairs nuclear inheritance with non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio11-s3.5.0] Location: nuclear DNA is in chromosomes in the nucleus; non-nuclear DNA is in mitochondria and chloroplasts in the cytoplasm.
[bio11-s3.5.1] Form: nuclear DNA is long, linear molecules wound around histones; non-nuclear DNA is small, circular molecules without histones.
[bio11-s3.5.2] Amount: most of the genes are nuclear (about 20,000 protein-coding genes in humans); few are non-nuclear (37 in human mitochondria), coding mainly for the organelle's own proteins and RNA.
[bio11-s3.5.3] Copies: two copies of each autosomal gene in a diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes); hundreds to thousands of copies of organelle DNA per cell.
[bio11-s3.5.4] Parents contributing: both parents equally for nuclear genes; usually the mother only for non-nuclear genes (maternal inheritance).
[bio11-s3.5.5] Distribution to gametes and daughter cells: chromosomes are separated precisely by the spindle in meiosis and mitosis; organelles are shared out randomly when the cytoplasm divides.
[bio11-s3.5.6] Patterns: nuclear inheritance follows Mendel's laws with predictable ratios, and reciprocal crosses give the same results; non-nuclear inheritance gives no Mendelian ratios, and reciprocal crosses give different results.
[bio11-s3.5.7] Variation between generations: nuclear inheritance gives new combinations of alleles from both parents each generation; non-nuclear DNA does not mix, and changes only by mutation.
[bio11-s3.6.b] Despite their differences, the two kinds of DNA are the same kind of molecule working in the same way. A comparison answer should say what they share as well as how they differ.
[bio11-s3.6.0] Both are DNA, using the same genetic code (with minor differences in mitochondria) and the same base pairing, so the same rules read and copy them.
[bio11-s3.6.1] Both are copied before cell division, so that each daughter cell or organelle receives genetic information.
[bio11-s3.6.2] Both can mutate and so cause inherited disease, such as LHON for mitochondrial DNA.
[bio11-s3.6.3] Diagram: sperm and egg fusing. Egg large, nucleus plus many mitochondria in cytoplasm; sperm nucleus in head, few mitochondria in midpiece. Arrows: nuclear DNA from both parents, mitochondria from egg only. Labels: nucleus (nuclear DNA, both parents), mitochondria (mitochondrial DNA, mother).

CANDIDATES bio19-s1: CNS and PNS
[bio19-s1.0.b] The nervous system detects stimuli (changes in the surroundings or inside the body) and coordinates responses by carrying nerve impulses. To do this it is split into two divisions: one that makes decisions and one that carries messages.
[bio19-s1.0.0] The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord, the control centre of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.1] The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is all the nerves outside the CNS, so it reaches every part of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.2] The CNS receives information, processes it and sends out instructions, which is why it is the part that decides what to do.
[bio19-s1.0.3] The PNS carries impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body, so it is the communication line that links the CNS to receptors and effectors.
[bio19-s1.0.4] A neuron is a nerve cell; a nerve is a bundle of many neuron fibres wrapped in connective tissue, so one nerve carries many separate signals at once.
[bio19-s1.0.5] A nerve impulse is an electrochemical signal travelling along a neuron, which is how information moves around the body.
[bio19-s1.1.b] Information has to travel into the CNS and instructions have to travel out again. The PNS does both jobs using two kinds of neuron, and its fibres run through the body bundled in nerves.
[bio19-s1.1.0] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect stimuli) to the CNS, so the CNS learns what is happening.
[bio19-s1.1.1] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors, which are muscles and glands, so the CNS's instructions produce a response such as a movement or a secretion.
[bio19-s1.1.2] Nerve fibres of the PNS run through the body inside nerves, which carry them to almost every tissue.
[bio19-s1.1.3] Humans have 31 pairs of spinal nerves, which leave the spinal cord between the vertebrae (the bones of the backbone).
[bio19-s1.1.4] Each spinal nerve joins the cord by two roots, short branches that attach it to the spinal cord, so sensory and motor fibres enter and leave by separate routes.
[bio19-s1.1.5] The dorsal (back) root carries sensory fibres into the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.1.6] The ventral (front) root carries motor fibres out of the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.b] A neuron's cell body (the part containing the nucleus) can sit in different places depending on the type of neuron. Knowing where each type lies tells you which part of the nervous system it belongs to.
[bio19-s1.2.0] Sensory neuron cell bodies lie in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on the dorsal root just outside the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.1] Motor neuron cell bodies lie in the grey matter of the CNS, inside the cord, and their fibres leave through the ventral root.
[bio19-s1.2.2] Interneurons (neurons that link one neuron to another) lie entirely within the CNS, so they never leave the brain or spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.3.b] The spinal cord runs from the base of the brain down the back, inside the vertebral column, which protects it. It is the main route between the brain and the rest of the body.
[bio19-s1.3.0] The spinal cord relays impulses between the body and the brain, so sensory information travels up to the brain and instructions travel back down.
[bio19-s1.3.1] It carries out spinal reflexes, which are fast automatic responses, such as the withdrawal reflex when you touch something painful. There is more on this in The reflex action.
[bio19-s1.4.b] Cut straight across (a transverse section), the spinal cord shows a clear pattern of grey and white tissue around a central canal. The two tissues differ because they are made of different parts of neurons.
[bio19-s1.4.0] A central canal, containing cerebrospinal fluid (a watery fluid that cushions the CNS), lies at the centre of the cord.
[bio19-s1.4.1] Around the canal is an H- or butterfly-shaped core of grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.2] White matter surrounds the grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.3] Grey matter is made of cell bodies and unmyelinated interneurons (interneurons without a fatty myelin sheath), which is why it looks grey.
[bio19-s1.4.4] White matter is made of myelinated axons (long fibres coated in myelin); it is white because myelin is fatty.
[bio19-s1.4.5] In the brain the arrangement is reversed: the cerebral cortex is grey matter on the outside, with white matter beneath.
[bio19-s1.4.6] Diagram: transverse section of the spinal cord labelling central canal, grey matter, white matter, dorsal root, dorsal root ganglion, ventral root and spinal nerve.
[bio19-s1.5.b] The brain and spinal cord control the whole body, so they need strong protection. The CNS is protected by bone, membranes and fluid.
[bio19-s1.5.0] The brain is protected by the skull (cranium), a rigid bony case.
[bio19-s1.5.1] The spinal cord is protected by the vertebrae, which form a bony tunnel around it.
[bio19-s1.5.2] Three membranes, the meninges, surround the brain and spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.5.3] Cerebrospinal fluid lies between the inner meninges and fills the brain's cavities and the central canal.
[bio19-s1.5.4] Cerebrospinal fluid cushions the CNS against shock, so a knock to the head or back is absorbed, and it supplies the CNS with nutrients.
[bio19-s1.5.5] Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, usually caused by bacterial or viral infection.
[bio19-s1.6.b] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and the part that handles most of its higher functions, such as thought and voluntary movement.
[bio19-s1.6.0] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain, divided into left and right cerebral hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum.
[bio19-s1.6.1] The cerebral cortex, its outer layer, is grey matter deeply folded to increase its surface area, and so the number of neurons.
[bio19-s1.6.2] Each cerebral hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body, so the left hemisphere controls the right side and vice versa.
[bio19-s1.6.3] The cerebrum controls voluntary movement (movement you choose to make) and interprets the senses.
[bio19-s1.6.4] It also governs memory, thought, language, intelligence and personality.
[bio19-s1.7.b] Smooth, accurate movement needs a part of the brain dedicated to coordinating it. That is the cerebellum.
[bio19-s1.7.0] The cerebellum lies at the back of the brain, below the cerebrum, and also has a folded surface.
[bio19-s1.7.1] It coordinates muscular movement and controls balance and posture.
[bio19-s1.8.b] Some vital actions must carry on without you thinking about them, even during sleep. The medulla oblongata controls these.
[bio19-s1.8.0] The medulla oblongata lies at the base of the brain, where it joins the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.8.1] It controls involuntary actions (those that happen without conscious control), including breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure.
[bio19-s1.8.2] It controls reflexes such as swallowing, coughing and sneezing.
[bio19-s1.9.b] The hypothalamus is a small region that keeps conditions in the body steady and connects the nervous system to the hormone system.
[bio19-s1.9.0] The hypothalamus lies below the cerebrum and above the pituitary gland, to which it is connected.
[bio19-s1.9.1] It controls homeostasis (keeping the internal environment steady): body temperature, water balance, hunger and thirst. There is more on this in Homeostasis.
[bio19-s1.9.2] It links the nervous and hormonal systems by controlling the pituitary gland, so nerve signals can lead to hormone release.
[bio19-s1.10.b] You may be asked to label the brain, so know where each part sits relative to the others.
[bio19-s1.10.0] Diagram: vertical section of the brain labelling cerebrum, cerebral cortex, corpus callosum, cerebellum, medulla oblongata, hypothalamus and pituitary gland, with the spinal cord continuing below.

CANDIDATES bio19-s2: The reflex action
[bio19-s2.0.b] Every reflex, and every nervous response, is built from the same three parts: something that triggers it, something that detects it and something that acts. A reflex begins with a stimulus that a receptor detects and ends with a response from an effector.
[bio19-s2.0.0] A stimulus is a change in the internal or external environment that can be detected, such as heat on the skin or an object approaching the eye.
[bio19-s2.0.1] A receptor detects the stimulus. It is a cell or nerve ending that is sensitive to that particular type of change, and it starts the impulse.
[bio19-s2.0.2] An effector, a muscle or a gland, carries out the response. A muscle responds by contracting; a gland responds by secreting.
[bio19-s2.1.b] Some situations need a response so quickly that there is no time to think. A reflex action is the body's built-in solution: a response that is fixed and does not wait for a decision.
[bio19-s2.1.0] A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, happening without conscious thought. Involuntary means you do not choose it or control it.
[bio19-s2.1.1] Reflexes protect the body from harm, for example pulling a hand from a hot object or blinking when something approaches the eye, so damage is limited before you even notice.
[bio19-s2.1.2] Reflexes also maintain posture and normal body functions; coughing and sneezing clear the airways, for example.
[bio19-s2.1.3] Reflexes are inborn, so they are not learned. You are born able to do them.
[bio19-s2.1.4] The same stimulus always produces the same response, which is what makes a reflex automatic and predictable.
[bio19-s2.2.b] A reflex works because the nerve pathway involved is short and fixed. The reflex arc is the pathway an impulse (the electrical signal carried by nerve cells) follows in a reflex action.
[bio19-s2.2.0] The pathway runs receptor, sensory neuron, interneuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord, motor neuron, effector. The sensory neuron carries the impulse in, the motor neuron carries it out, and the interneuron connects them.
[bio19-s2.2.1] The sensory neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root (the back root of the spinal nerve); its cell body lies in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on that root.
[bio19-s2.2.2] The motor neuron leaves the spinal cord through the ventral root (the front root), carrying the impulse out to the effector.
[bio19-s2.2.3] Diagram: reflex arc drawn through a transverse section of the spinal cord, labelling receptor in the skin, sensory neuron, dorsal root ganglion, interneuron, grey matter, white matter, motor neuron, ventral root and effector muscle, with arrows showing the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s2.3.b] Touching a hot object shows the whole reflex arc at work. Follow the impulse from the finger to the muscle that pulls the hand away.
[bio19-s2.3.0] 1. Pain and heat receptors in the skin of the finger are stimulated by the hot object.
[bio19-s2.3.1] 2. An impulse travels along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.3.2] 3. It passes across a synapse (the tiny gap between two neurons) to an interneuron in the grey matter.
[bio19-s2.3.3] 4. It passes across a synapse to a motor neuron.
[bio19-s2.3.4] 5. The motor neuron carries the impulse to the biceps, the effector.
[bio19-s2.3.5] 6. The biceps contracts and the hand is pulled away from the object.
[bio19-s2.4.b] A reflex is faster than a voluntary action because of the route it takes. The brain is not left out, though: it is told what happened a moment later.
[bio19-s2.4.0] The impulse takes a short route through the spinal cord, crossing few synapses. Each synapse adds a small delay, so fewer synapses means a faster response.
[bio19-s2.4.1] It does not wait for a decision by the brain, which is why reflexes are rapid.
[bio19-s2.4.2] Interneurons also carry impulses up the spinal cord to the brain, so pain is felt just after the hand has moved.
[bio19-s2.4.3] The brain can sometimes override a reflex, for example when a person holds on to a hot plate to avoid dropping it.
[bio19-s2.5.b] The knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is the simplest reflex arc, because it leaves out the interneuron. It is a useful contrast with the withdrawal reflex.
[bio19-s2.5.0] Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches the thigh muscle, which is the stimulus.
[bio19-s2.5.1] The sensory neuron synapses directly with the motor neuron in the spinal cord, with no interneuron, so the pathway has only one synapse.
[bio19-s2.5.2] The leg kicks. Doctors use this reflex to test the nervous system, because a normal response shows the reflex pathway is working.
[bio19-s2.6.b] Not every reflex passes through the spinal cord. Some are controlled by the brain instead, but they are still rapid, automatic and involuntary.
[bio19-s2.6.0] Some reflexes are controlled by the brain rather than the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.6.1] Examples are blinking, coughing, sneezing and the pupil narrowing in bright light.

CANDIDATES bio22-s2: Innate and adaptive immunity
[bio22-s2.0.b] Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection by pathogens (disease-causing organisms such as bacteria and viruses). The immune system does this by telling self from non-self, and it has two parts that work at different speeds.
[bio22-s2.0.0] The immune system consists of physical and chemical barriers, white blood cells (made from stem cells in the bone marrow), the lymphatic system (lymph nodes, spleen and thymus) and defence proteins in the blood, such as antibodies and complement.
[bio22-s2.0.1] The body's own cells carry their own marker molecules on their surface, which the immune system normally ignores, so foreign cells and molecules, which lack these markers, are attacked.
[bio22-s2.0.2] Innate immunity, also called non-specific immunity, is present from birth, acts against any pathogen in the same way and acts within minutes to hours.
[bio22-s2.0.3] Adaptive immunity, also called acquired or specific immunity, develops during life after exposure to a particular pathogen and acts against that pathogen only.
[bio22-s2.0.4] Adaptive immunity takes days to develop the first time, but it remembers the pathogen, which is why a later infection is dealt with much faster.
[bio22-s2.0.5] Innate defences are found in all animals and in plants; adaptive immunity, with antibodies and lymphocytes (the white blood cells that run it), is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.1.b] The best way to deal with a pathogen is to stop it getting in. Skin and the linings of the body's openings form physical barriers that block or trap microorganisms before they can cause infection.
[bio22-s2.1.0] Skin is a tough outer layer of dead, keratin-filled cells (keratin is a tough protein) that pathogens can't easily cross.
[bio22-s2.1.1] Oil (sebum) from skin glands makes the surface slightly acidic, which slows bacterial growth.
[bio22-s2.1.2] Breaks in the skin are sealed by blood clotting, which stops blood loss and blocks pathogen entry.
[bio22-s2.1.3] Mucous membranes line the breathing, digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts. Their sticky mucus traps microorganisms and dust, so these don't reach the cells beneath.
[bio22-s2.1.4] In the trachea and bronchi (the main airways to the lungs), cilia (tiny hair-like projections) beat the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.
[bio22-s2.1.5] Coughing and sneezing are reflexes (automatic responses) that expel irritants, so trapped microorganisms are blown out of the airways.
[bio22-s2.2.b] Some barriers work by chemistry rather than by physically blocking entry. Acids and enzymes kill or inhibit microorganisms, and harmless resident microorganisms add a living defence of their own.
[bio22-s2.2.0] Hydrochloric acid in the stomach, at about pH 2, kills most microorganisms swallowed with food.
[bio22-s2.2.1] Lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus, breaks down bacterial cell walls, so the bacteria burst or die.
[bio22-s2.2.2] The acidic conditions in the vagina inhibit many pathogens.
[bio22-s2.2.3] Harmless microorganisms living on the skin and in the gut and vagina, the normal microbiota, compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, which leaves pathogens less room to establish an infection. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s2.3.b] If a pathogen gets past the barriers, the innate response moves on to cells that destroy it. Phagocytes find the invader, swallow it and digest it in a process called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.0] Phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens, and the process is called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.1] Neutrophils are the most numerous white blood cells and the first to arrive at an infection. Macrophages, formed from monocytes (another type of white blood cell), are larger and longer-lived.
[bio22-s2.3.2] 1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals released by pathogens and damaged cells.
[bio22-s2.3.3] 2. It binds the pathogen.
[bio22-s2.3.4] 3. It surrounds the pathogen with extensions of its membrane and takes it into a vesicle (a small membrane-bound sac inside the cell).
[bio22-s2.3.5] 4. Digestive enzymes are released into the vesicle and break the pathogen down.
[bio22-s2.3.6] 5. The harmless products are absorbed or released.
[bio22-s2.3.7] Diagram: the stages of phagocytosis in four sketches: a phagocyte approaching a bacterium; membrane extensions surrounding it; the bacterium enclosed in a vesicle; the bacterium being digested. Label phagocyte, nucleus, bacterium, vesicle and digestive enzymes.
[bio22-s2.4.b] Inflammation and fever are whole-body or whole-area responses that bring more defences to an infection and make conditions harder for the pathogen. The signs you notice when you are ill are the immune system at work.
[bio22-s2.4.0] Inflammation: damaged cells and mast cells (cells in tissues that release signals when damaged) release histamine and other chemicals, which widen local blood vessels and make capillaries leakier.
[bio22-s2.4.1] More blood flow brings phagocytes and defence proteins to the site, and fluid leaks into the tissue, giving redness, heat, swelling and pain at the site.
[bio22-s2.4.2] Pus is a mixture of dead phagocytes, pathogens and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.4.3] Fever: chemicals released by white blood cells reset the temperature set point in the hypothalamus (the brain region that controls body temperature) upwards, raising body temperature. There is more on this in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio22-s2.4.4] A higher temperature slows the reproduction of many pathogens and speeds up the reactions of the immune system, so fever helps the body fight infection.
[bio22-s2.5.b] Besides phagocytes, the innate response uses proteins in the blood and released by infected cells. Plants have no adaptive immunity, so their innate defences have to do all the work.
[bio22-s2.5.0] Complement, a group of blood proteins, coats pathogens so that phagocytes engulf them more easily, and it can punch holes in bacterial membranes, bursting them.
[bio22-s2.5.1] Interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that make neighbouring cells resist virus replication, so the infection is slowed before it spreads.
[bio22-s2.5.2] Natural killer cells also act in the innate response, in addition to phagocytes and defence proteins.
[bio22-s2.5.3] Plants have innate defences only: physical barriers (cuticle, bark and cell walls), protective chemicals, and the death of infected cells to wall off a pathogen. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio22-s2.6.b] Adaptive immunity depends on recognising a particular pathogen. The molecules it recognises are called antigens, and the immune system's ability to tell them apart from the body's own molecules is what makes the response specific.
[bio22-s2.6.0] An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen or on a toxin it releases, that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s2.6.1] Antigens trigger a specific immune response, including antibody production.
[bio22-s2.6.2] Each pathogen carries several antigens, so more than one kind of antibody can be made against it. The body's own cell markers are self antigens, which are normally tolerated, so the body doesn't attack itself.
[bio22-s2.7.b] An antibody is the body's protein that matches one antigen. Its Y shape and the shape of its tips explain why each antibody works against only one antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.0] An antibody, also called an immunoglobulin, is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells, which are derived from B lymphocytes. It binds to one specific antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.1] An antibody has four polypeptide chains (chains of amino acids), two long heavy chains and two short light chains, held together by disulfide bonds.
[bio22-s2.7.2] The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ between antibodies and form two identical antigen-binding sites. The rest of the molecule is the constant region, which is the same in all antibodies of a class.
[bio22-s2.7.3] Specificity: the shape of the antigen-binding site is complementary to the shape of one antigen, so each antibody binds only that antigen, like a key in a lock. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio22-s2.7.4] Diagram: an antibody as a Y shape, labelled heavy chain, light chain, variable region, antigen-binding site (two), constant region and disulfide bonds, with an antigen fitting one binding site.
[bio22-s2.8.b] Once antibodies have bound to an antigen, they can stop the pathogen in four different ways. The Y shape matters again here, because the two arms bind the antigen while the stem is recognised by other defences.
[bio22-s2.8.0] Agglutination: because each antibody has two binding sites, antibodies link pathogens together into clumps that can't spread and are easily engulfed.
[bio22-s2.8.1] Neutralisation: antibodies cover the parts of viruses that attach to cells, or bind to toxins (antitoxins), so that they can't act.
[bio22-s2.8.2] Opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens, marking them for phagocytes, which have receptors for the antibody's constant region.
[bio22-s2.8.3] Complement activation: antibodies activate complement (the blood proteins of the innate response), which bursts bacterial cells.
[bio22-s2.9.b] The body can't know in advance which pathogen it will meet, so it keeps a huge variety of B cells ready. Only the ones that fit the invader are chosen to multiply, a process called clonal selection.
[bio22-s2.9.0] Lymphocytes are the white blood cells of adaptive immunity, and there are two main kinds.
[bio22-s2.9.1] B lymphocytes (B cells) produce antibodies. This is the antibody-mediated or humoral response, acting on pathogens and toxins in blood and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.9.2] T lymphocytes (T cells) act directly on infected body cells in the cell-mediated response (Higher Level).
[bio22-s2.9.3] Clonal selection: the body has millions of different B cells, each with one type of antibody fixed in its membrane as a receptor, made before any infection.
[bio22-s2.9.4] When a pathogen enters, only the B cells whose receptors fit its antigen bind to it. These are selected and, with chemical signals from helper T cells, divide rapidly by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells.
[bio22-s2.9.5] The clone differentiates (becomes specialised) into plasma cells and memory B cells.
[bio22-s2.9.6] Plasma cells secrete large amounts of the matching antibody into the blood, thousands of molecules per second, for a few days, and then die.
[bio22-s2.9.7] Memory cells stay in the body for years, ready to respond to the same antigen.
[bio22-s2.10.b] The first time you meet a pathogen, the response is slow. A second meeting is much faster, because memory cells are already there. This difference is the reason you rarely get the same infection twice.
[bio22-s2.10.0] Primary response, on first exposure: there is a delay of several days, often about a week, while matching B cells are selected and cloned. Antibody levels rise slowly and stay fairly low.
[bio22-s2.10.1] During the primary response the person may become ill, because the pathogen multiplies before enough antibodies are made.
[bio22-s2.10.2] Secondary response, on later exposure to the same antigen: memory cells recognise it at once and quickly produce many plasma cells, so antibodies appear within a few days, reach a much higher level and last longer.
[bio22-s2.10.3] The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear, so the person is immune. This is the basis of vaccination. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s2.10.4] Diagram: a graph of antibody concentration (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis), with a first exposure giving a small, slow primary peak and a second exposure giving a faster, much taller, longer secondary peak. Label both exposures, the lag phase, and the primary and secondary responses.
[bio22-s2.11.b] Antibodies work in blood and tissue fluid, but some pathogens hide inside body cells. The cell-mediated response uses T cells to find and deal with those infected cells, and it begins with the cell showing that it is infected.
[bio22-s2.11.0] The cell-mediated response is the part of adaptive immunity carried out by T lymphocytes acting directly on cells, not by antibodies.
[bio22-s2.11.1] It is needed because antibodies can't reach pathogens inside body cells, such as viruses and some bacteria, for example the TB bacterium, which lives inside macrophages.
[bio22-s2.11.2] It also destroys cancer cells and cells of transplanted organs, which is why transplant patients take drugs that suppress the immune system.
[bio22-s2.11.3] Antigen presentation: an infected body cell displays fragments of the pathogen's antigens on its surface, held in its self-marker proteins (MHC proteins).
[bio22-s2.11.4] Macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells that have engulfed a pathogen display its antigens in the same way.
[bio22-s2.11.5] T cells can't recognise free antigens, so this presentation is how they detect infection.
[bio22-s2.12.b] Helper T cells act as the managers of adaptive immunity. Once one is activated, it signals to the other cells that need to act, so it links the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.12.0] Activation of helper T cells: a helper T cell whose receptor fits the displayed antigen binds to the antigen-presenting cell and is activated. It divides to form a clone of helper T cells, as B cells do.
[bio22-s2.12.1] Helper T cells coordinate the response by releasing chemical signals called cytokines.
[bio22-s2.12.2] Cytokines stimulate the matching killer T cells to divide.
[bio22-s2.12.3] Cytokines also stimulate the matching B cells to divide into plasma cells and memory cells, because B cells need this help to respond fully.
[bio22-s2.12.4] Cytokines also attract and activate macrophages, which increases phagocytosis at the infection.
[bio22-s2.12.5] Helper T cells therefore link the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.13.b] After helper T cells give the signal, the killer T cells destroy infected cells, the B cells add antibodies, and suppressor T cells end the response. Memory cells are left behind for next time.
[bio22-s2.13.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) of the clone bind to body cells displaying the antigen and kill them, destroying the pathogens inside before they can multiply. There is more on this in White blood cells.
[bio22-s2.13.1] When the infection is cleared, suppressor T cells switch off the response by stopping the activity of killer T cells and B cells, so that healthy tissue isn't damaged.
[bio22-s2.13.2] Memory T cells remain, so a second infection triggers a faster, larger cell-mediated response.
[bio22-s2.13.3] Role of B lymphocytes: B cells act as antigen-presenting cells, taking in the antigen they bind and displaying it to helper T cells.
[bio22-s2.13.4] The antibodies released by the plasma cells of B cells mark infected cells and pathogens for destruction.
[bio22-s2.13.5] Diagram: draw a flow diagram in this order: pathogen; antigen presented by macrophage or infected cell; helper T cell activated and cloned; cytokines; killer T cells cloned, infected cells killed; B cells cloned into plasma cells, antibodies; memory T and B cells formed; suppressor T cells end response.
[bio22-s2.14.b] The two systems differ in speed, specificity and memory, but they are not separate. A comparison of the two is a standard exam question, so learn each as a pair.
[bio22-s2.14.0] Presence: innate immunity is present from birth, while adaptive immunity develops after exposure to a pathogen.
[bio22-s2.14.1] Specificity: innate immunity is non-specific, giving the same response to every pathogen, while adaptive immunity is specific, with each response against one antigen.
[bio22-s2.14.2] Memory: innate immunity gives the same response each time, while adaptive immunity has memory, so a second response is faster and stronger.
[bio22-s2.14.3] Speed: innate immunity acts within minutes to hours, while adaptive immunity takes days on first exposure.
[bio22-s2.14.4] Mechanisms: innate immunity uses barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages), inflammation, fever, complement, interferons and natural killer cells; adaptive immunity uses B and T lymphocytes and antibodies.
[bio22-s2.14.5] Distribution: innate defences are found in all animals and in plants, while adaptive immunity is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.14.6] The two systems work together: macrophages of the innate system present antigens to start the adaptive response, and antibodies of the adaptive system make phagocytosis and complement more effective.

CANDIDATES bio22-s5: White blood cells
[bio22-s5.0.b] The immune system is run by several different kinds of white blood cell, and they all start from the same place. Knowing where each type comes from and matures explains how the body learns to attack invaders but not itself.
[bio22-s5.0.0] White blood cells (leucocytes) are the cells of the immune system. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, which holds the genes they need to make the proteins for their defensive jobs.
[bio22-s5.0.1] All white blood cells develop from stem cells (cells that can divide and become different cell types) in the red bone marrow, the soft tissue inside some bones.
[bio22-s5.0.2] They travel in the blood and lymph (the fluid that drains from tissues), and many leave the capillaries to enter the tissues, because that is where pathogens (disease-causing organisms) are found.
[bio22-s5.0.3] There are two broad groups. Phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages) engulf pathogens, taking them into the cell.
[bio22-s5.0.4] The second group is the lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells). These are small cells with a large round nucleus filling most of the cell and little cytoplasm.
[bio22-s5.0.5] B lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow. T lymphocytes leave the marrow and mature in the thymus, a gland in the chest behind the breastbone (T for thymus).
[bio22-s5.0.6] During maturation, lymphocytes that would attack the body's own cells are removed, so mature lymphocytes respond only to non-self antigens (molecules on a pathogen's surface that the immune system recognises as foreign).
[bio22-s5.1.b] Monocytes are the white blood cells that travel in the blood and then settle in tissues as macrophages. Macrophages are the body's general-purpose cleaners and also the link between the fast, general response and the specific one.
[bio22-s5.1.0] Monocytes are the largest white blood cells, with a large kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus, which makes them easy to tell from lymphocytes under the microscope.
[bio22-s5.1.1] They circulate in the blood for a few days, then move into tissues and develop into macrophages, which can live for months.
[bio22-s5.1.2] Macrophages carry out phagocytosis (engulfing and digesting) of pathogens, dead cells and debris. There is more on the stages in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.1.3] Macrophages are antigen-presenting cells: they display fragments of engulfed pathogens on their surface to activate helper T cells, so they link innate immunity (the fast, non-specific response) and adaptive immunity (the slower, specific one).
[bio22-s5.1.4] Macrophages release chemicals that cause inflammation and fever and attract other white blood cells to the site of infection.
[bio22-s5.1.5] Diagram: a monocyte (large cell, kidney-shaped nucleus) beside a lymphocyte (small cell, large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm). Label nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane.
[bio22-s5.2.b] Natural killer (NK) cells are a fast first line of attack against body cells that have gone wrong, either because a virus is hiding inside them or because they have become cancerous. They check cells for a missing marker rather than for a particular pathogen.
[bio22-s5.2.0] Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes of the innate system. They are not specific to one antigen and need no earlier exposure, so they act within hours of an infection, before B and T cells have responded.
[bio22-s5.2.1] Healthy body cells display normal self-marker (MHC) proteins on their surface, which tell the immune system the cell belongs to the body.
[bio22-s5.2.2] Many virus-infected cells and cancer cells display fewer of these markers, or display stress molecules instead. NK cells kill cells showing this missing self pattern.
[bio22-s5.2.3] NK cells also kill cells coated with antibodies, the proteins that bind to antigens.
[bio22-s5.2.4] NK cells release perforin, a protein that makes pores in the target cell's membrane, and enzymes called granzymes that enter through the pores and trigger the cell to self-destruct (apoptosis, programmed cell death).
[bio22-s5.2.5] The dying cell breaks into fragments that macrophages engulf, so the virus inside is destroyed without spreading.
[bio22-s5.2.6] NK cells also release chemicals that activate macrophages, which helps clear the fragments.
[bio22-s5.3.b] B lymphocytes are the cells behind antibodies. Each B cell is committed to one antibody shape, so the body needs a huge range of B cells to cover many different antigens.
[bio22-s5.3.0] B lymphocytes carry antibody molecules in their membrane as receptors, which let them bind a matching antigen. Each B cell and its descendants make only one antibody shape.
[bio22-s5.3.1] B lymphocytes are responsible for antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity, meaning protection by antibodies carried in body fluids. Clonal selection, plasma cells and memory B cells are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.3.2] Plasma cells, the antibody-secreting form of B cells, are larger than resting B cells, with extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum (a network of membranes studded with ribosomes) to make and secrete large amounts of antibody protein.
[bio22-s5.3.3] Antibodies act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, in blood, tissue fluid and mucus, not on pathogens hidden inside cells, because antibodies cannot get into body cells.
[bio22-s5.4.b] T lymphocytes are the cells that control and carry out cell-mediated immunity, the response that deals with infected body cells. Helper T cells are the coordinators that most of the adaptive response depends on.
[bio22-s5.4.0] All T cells have T-cell receptors on their surface that recognise one antigen, but only when it is displayed on a cell's surface (presented), never as a free molecule.
[bio22-s5.4.1] T cells don't make antibodies. That is the job of plasma cells, which come from B cells.
[bio22-s5.4.2] Helper T cells carry the surface marker CD4.
[bio22-s5.4.3] Helper T cells are the coordinators of the adaptive response, signalling with cytokines (chemical messengers between immune cells). Which cells they activate, and in what order, is taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.4.4] Almost every adaptive response depends on helper T cells, so HIV, which infects and destroys CD4 helper T cells, leaves the body unable to fight many infections.
[bio22-s5.5.b] Killer T cells deal with threats that antibodies cannot reach, because the pathogen is hidden inside a body cell. They destroy the whole infected cell.
[bio22-s5.5.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) carry the surface marker CD8.
[bio22-s5.5.1] Killer T cells bind to body cells displaying their matching antigen, such as virus-infected cells, cancer cells and cells of transplanted tissue.
[bio22-s5.5.2] They kill these cells by releasing perforin and granzymes, which triggers apoptosis (the cell's programmed self-destruction).
[bio22-s5.5.3] Killer T cells use the same killing method as NK cells, but only against cells showing their one specific antigen, whereas NK cells look for missing self-markers.
[bio22-s5.5.4] Each killer T cell can kill many target cells in turn.
[bio22-s5.6.b] An immune response needs brakes, and it needs a record of past infections. Suppressor T cells provide the brakes, and memory T cells provide the record that makes a second infection easier to fight.
[bio22-s5.6.0] Suppressor T cells, now usually called regulatory T cells, are the brakes of the immune system.
[bio22-s5.6.1] Suppressor T cells end a response once an infection is cleared, so the body is not damaged by an immune response that carries on. The details are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.6.2] They also suppress lymphocytes that react to the body's own antigens, helping to prevent autoimmune disease (the body attacking its own tissues) and allergy.
[bio22-s5.6.3] Memory T cells form from the clone of activated T cells (the identical cells that divide from one activated T cell) and remain in the blood and lymph tissue for years or decades.
[bio22-s5.6.4] On re-exposure to the same antigen, memory T cells quickly produce new helper and killer T cells, giving a faster, stronger cell-mediated response. Memory B cells do the same for antibodies.
[bio22-s5.6.5] Memory cells are why vaccination works: the vaccine exposes you to the antigen safely, so memory cells are ready if the real pathogen arrives.
[bio22-s5.7.b] The outcome asks you to compare the roles of different white blood cells. The first comparison is between the fast, general innate cells and the slower, specific adaptive cells, and then between the ways each one destroys pathogens.
[bio22-s5.7.0] Innate versus adaptive: monocytes, macrophages and NK cells are part of innate immunity, which is non-specific, acts quickly and has no memory.
[bio22-s5.7.1] B and T lymphocytes are adaptive: they are specific to one antigen, slower on first exposure, and they form memory cells.
[bio22-s5.7.2] Macrophages engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis. NK and killer T cells kill infected body cells by perforin and apoptosis.
[bio22-s5.7.3] B cells, as plasma cells, secrete antibodies. Helper T cells kill nothing themselves but signal to other cells.
[bio22-s5.7.4] Suppressor T cells regulate the response. Memory cells kill nothing but enable a rapid future response.
[bio22-s5.8.b] Cells also differ in what they attack and in how they recognise it. This explains why different cells are needed to cover both free pathogens and pathogens hidden inside body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.0] Where the target is: antibodies from B cells act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, because antibodies cannot enter body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.1] Killer T and NK cells act on pathogens inside body cells by killing the cell. Macrophages engulf free pathogens.
[bio22-s5.8.2] Recognition: B cells recognise free antigens with membrane antibodies, while T cells recognise only antigens presented on cell surfaces.
[bio22-s5.8.3] NK cells recognise missing or abnormal self-markers, not a specific antigen.
[bio22-s5.8.4] Macrophages recognise general features of pathogens and antibody-coated particles, so they need no specific receptor for each pathogen.
[bio22-s5.9.b] This list puts each cell side by side: whether it is innate or adaptive, whether it is specific, its main role, and where it matures. Use it to compare cells quickly.
[bio22-s5.9.0] Monocyte or macrophage: innate; non-specific; main role is phagocytosis and antigen presentation; matures in the bone marrow, then in tissues.
[bio22-s5.9.1] NK cell: innate; non-specific; kills infected and cancer cells; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.2] B lymphocyte: adaptive; specific; main role is antibodies (via plasma cells) and memory; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.3] Helper T cell: adaptive; specific; activates B cells, killer T cells and macrophages (CD4); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.4] Killer T cell: adaptive; specific; kills infected, cancer and transplant cells (CD8); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.5] Suppressor T cell: adaptive; ends responses and prevents attack on self; matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.6] Memory B and T cells: adaptive; specific; long-lived, giving a fast secondary response.
[bio22-s5.10.b] In a real infection the cells act as a team in sequence. Influenza, a virus that infects the cells lining the airways, shows how the innate and adaptive cells combine.
[bio22-s5.10.0] First, infected cells release interferons (chemical signals that warn neighbouring cells of a virus) and NK cells kill some of them within hours. Macrophages engulf viruses and present antigens, and helper T cells are activated.
[bio22-s5.10.1] Next, killer T cells destroy infected cells while plasma cells release antibodies that neutralise free viruses, so both hidden and free viruses are dealt with.
[bio22-s5.10.2] Finally, suppressor T cells end the response and memory cells remain. Because flu antigens change, memory cells may not recognise next year's strain.

CANDIDATES bio23-s1: The male reproductive system
[bio23-s1.0.b] The male reproductive system has two jobs: making sperm and a hormone, and getting the sperm to the female. Knowing the route sperm take makes each organ's role easy to place.
[bio23-s1.0.0] The male reproductive system produces sperm, the male gametes (sex cells), and the hormone testosterone, and delivers sperm into the female's vagina during sexual intercourse, so that a sperm can fertilise an egg.
[bio23-s1.0.1] Sperm pass in order: testis → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra (where fluids from the seminal vesicles and prostate are added) → out through the penis. Sperm are made in the testis, so that is the starting point.
[bio23-s1.0.2] Semen is sperm suspended in seminal fluid. Seminal fluid, made by the seminal vesicles and the prostate, gives the sperm a liquid to swim in and supplies an energy source.
[bio23-s1.0.3] Seminal fluid is slightly alkaline overall, which neutralises the acidity of the vagina and of any urine left in the urethra, because acid damages sperm.
[bio23-s1.0.4] Sperm make up only a small part of the volume of semen, because most of it is seminal fluid.
[bio23-s1.1.b] Sperm cannot be made at normal body temperature, so the testes hang outside the body. The scrotum is the sac that holds them and keeps them at the right temperature.
[bio23-s1.1.0] Scrotum: a sac of skin and muscle hanging outside the abdomen that holds the two testes. Muscles in it pull the testes closer to the body when it is cold, to keep them warm, and relax to let them hang lower when it is warm.
[bio23-s1.1.1] The scrotum keeps the testes about 2–3 °C below core body temperature (37 °C), which sperm production needs; this is why the testes are outside the body.
[bio23-s1.2.b] The testes are where sperm and testosterone are made. Inside, two different tissues do these two jobs.
[bio23-s1.2.0] Testes (singular testis): the two oval male gonads, which are the primary sex organs (the organs that make the gametes).
[bio23-s1.2.1] Each testis is packed with many long, tightly coiled seminiferous tubules, whose walls produce sperm by meiosis (cell division that halves the chromosome number), continuously from puberty.
[bio23-s1.2.2] The coiling packs a very long length of tubule, and so a large sperm-producing surface, into a small organ, because a long tube can fit in little space when coiled.
[bio23-s1.2.3] Between the seminiferous tubules are interstitial cells, which secrete testosterone into the blood; the testes are therefore also endocrine glands (glands that release hormones into the blood, so the hormone reaches the whole body).
[bio23-s1.2.4] Testosterone has roles in puberty, sperm production and the secondary sexual characteristics (the body changes that develop at puberty). There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s1.2.5] The testes form inside the abdomen of the foetus and normally descend into the scrotum before birth, which puts them in the cooler position sperm production needs.
[bio23-s1.2.6] A testis that has not descended cannot produce sperm normally, which is a known cause of male infertility.
[bio23-s1.3.b] Once made, sperm travel through a series of tubes. Each one has a different job: storage, transport and exit from the body.
[bio23-s1.3.0] Epididymis: a single, very long, tightly coiled tube lying against the back of each testis.
[bio23-s1.3.1] Sperm from the seminiferous tubules pass into the epididymis, where they are stored and mature, gaining the ability to swim. The coiling gives a long tube for storage in a small space.
[bio23-s1.3.2] Vas deferens (sperm duct; plural vasa deferentia): a muscular tube carrying sperm from the epididymis up into the abdomen and around the bladder, to join the urethra.
[bio23-s1.3.3] During ejaculation the smooth muscle of the vas deferens contracts in waves (peristalsis) to push sperm along, which is why the tube has a thick muscular wall.
[bio23-s1.3.4] The vas deferens is the tube that is cut and tied in a vasectomy, the male sterilisation operation, so that sperm can no longer reach the urethra.
[bio23-s1.3.5] Urethra: the tube running from the bladder through the length of the penis to the outside, so it is the final exit for both urine and semen.
[bio23-s1.3.6] In males the urethra carries both urine and semen, but never at the same time: during ejaculation a ring of muscle (sphincter) at the base of the bladder closes, so urine cannot mix with semen.
[bio23-s1.3.7] In females the urethra carries only urine and opens separately from the vagina. There is more on this in The female reproductive system.
[bio23-s1.4.b] Sperm need a liquid to swim in and an energy source. Two glands add fluids that supply these, so that sperm can swim and reach the egg.
[bio23-s1.4.0] Seminal vesicles: two glands behind the bladder whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
[bio23-s1.4.1] The seminal vesicles secrete a thick, alkaline fluid rich in the sugar fructose; the sperm's mitochondria respire fructose to release energy for swimming.
[bio23-s1.4.2] This fluid makes up most of the volume of semen, so the seminal vesicles supply the bulk of the liquid sperm swim in.
[bio23-s1.4.3] Prostate gland: a single gland, about the size of a walnut, just below the bladder, surrounding the start of the urethra.
[bio23-s1.4.4] The prostate secretes a thin, milky fluid containing enzymes that keep the semen liquid after ejaculation, so the sperm can swim free.
[bio23-s1.4.5] The secretions of the seminal vesicles and the prostate together form seminal fluid, which is why seminal fluid as a whole is slightly alkaline.
[bio23-s1.4.6] Because the prostate surrounds the urethra, an enlarged prostate, common in older men, can press on the urethra and make urination difficult.
[bio23-s1.5.b] The penis delivers semen into the vagina and also lets urine out. Its tip and the skin covering it have their own roles.
[bio23-s1.5.0] Penis: the male organ that places semen in the vagina during intercourse, and through which urine and semen leave the body via the urethra.
[bio23-s1.5.1] The penis contains spongy erectile tissue. During sexual arousal its blood vessels widen and the spaces fill with blood under pressure, so the penis becomes larger, firm and erect, which allows it to enter the vagina.
[bio23-s1.5.2] Glans: the rounded, sensitive tip of the penis, with many nerve endings; stimulation of these leads to ejaculation.
[bio23-s1.5.3] The urethra opens at the tip of the glans, so semen and urine leave the body there.
[bio23-s1.5.4] Foreskin: a fold of loose skin that covers and protects the glans when the penis is not erect, and pulls back (retracts) during an erection.
[bio23-s1.5.5] The foreskin is removed in circumcision, an operation that leaves the glans uncovered.
[bio23-s1.5.6] Ejaculation: at the peak of sexual stimulation, waves of muscle contraction in the epididymis, vasa deferentia, glands and urethra expel semen from the penis.
[bio23-s1.5.7] The semen from one ejaculation (a few millilitres) contains many millions of sperm, so many sperm are available to reach the egg.
[bio23-s1.6.b] Exams ask you to draw and label this system, so it helps to know where each part sits in relation to the bladder.
[bio23-s1.6.0] Diagram: side view (longitudinal section) with the bladder for orientation. Label testis, scrotum, epididymis, vas deferens (sperm duct), seminal vesicle, prostate gland, urethra, penis, glans, foreskin, bladder. Draw the vas deferens running up from the epididymis and looping up and over the ureter behind the bladder to join the urethra just below the bladder.
[bio23-s1.7.b] Each point gives the part, where it is, what it does and how its structure suits that job. They run in the order of the sperm pathway.
[bio23-s1.7.0] Scrotum – holds the testes outside the abdomen – keeps them 2–3 °C below body temperature – muscles pull the testes closer to the body in cold conditions.
[bio23-s1.7.1] Testis – the sperm-producing organ, inside the scrotum – makes sperm in the coiled seminiferous tubules and testosterone in the interstitial cells – very long coiled tubules give a large surface in a small organ.
[bio23-s1.7.2] Epididymis – on the back of each testis – stores and matures sperm so they can swim – a single very long, coiled tube that stores a lot in a small space.
[bio23-s1.7.3] Vas deferens – from the epididymis, up into the abdomen and around the bladder to the urethra – carries sperm along the route – a muscular tube whose walls contract in peristalsis.
[bio23-s1.8.b] The pathway continues with the glands that add fluid, then the urethra and the penis, which delivers the semen.
[bio23-s1.8.0] Seminal vesicle – behind the bladder – adds a thick, alkaline, fructose-rich fluid that gives energy and makes up most of the semen – a gland whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
[bio23-s1.8.1] Prostate – below the bladder, around the urethra – adds an enzyme-containing fluid that keeps semen liquid – a gland that surrounds the start of the urethra.
[bio23-s1.8.2] Urethra – from the bladder through the penis – carries semen and urine, never together – a sphincter closes the bladder during ejaculation.
[bio23-s1.8.3] Penis – outside the body – places semen in the vagina – erectile tissue fills with blood so it becomes firm enough to enter the vagina.
[bio23-s1.8.4] Glans – tip of the penis – detects stimulation and leads to ejaculation – many nerve endings in its sensitive tip, and the urethra opens there.
[bio23-s1.8.5] Foreskin – fold of skin over the glans – protects the glans and pulls back during an erection – loose skin that retracts.

CANDIDATES bio23-s3: Gametes and fertilisation
[bio23-s3.0.b] Gametes are the cells that carry genes from one generation to the next, and they are made by a special kind of cell division. Knowing where this division happens, and which cells do it, is the starting point for sperm and egg production.
[bio23-s3.0.0] A gamete is a haploid sex cell, meaning it has only one chromosome of each pair. In humans the haploid number is 23 (n = 23).
[bio23-s3.0.1] Sperm and eggs are made by meiosis (the cell division that halves the chromosome number), so each gamete carries one chromosome of each pair, and each gamete is genetically different from the others. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio23-s3.0.2] At fertilisation two haploid gametes fuse to give a diploid zygote (the first cell of a new individual, with both chromosomes of each pair) with 46 chromosomes (2n = 46).
[bio23-s3.0.3] Meiosis happens only in the gonads (the organs that make gametes): in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes) for sperm, and in the ovaries for eggs.
[bio23-s3.0.4] The cells that undergo meiosis are diploid. Every other cell in the body divides by mitosis, which keeps the chromosome number the same.
[bio23-s3.1.b] Unlike eggs, sperm are made continuously, in huge numbers, from puberty onwards. The process happens in the testes and finishes in a storage tube beside them.
[bio23-s3.1.0] Sperm production begins at puberty and then continues every day throughout a man's life, though it declines in old age.
[bio23-s3.1.1] Many millions of sperm are made each day in the walls of the seminiferous tubules, from diploid cells that first multiply by mitosis (which keeps a supply of cells available) and then divide by meiosis.
[bio23-s3.1.2] Each cell that completes meiosis gives four sperm, all of equal size.
[bio23-s3.1.3] Half the sperm carry an X chromosome and half carry a Y, because the dividing cell has one X and one Y. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio23-s3.1.4] The new sperm move into the epididymis (a coiled tube on the back of each testis), where they are stored and mature, becoming able to swim.
[bio23-s3.1.5] It takes about two to three months for a sperm to be produced and matured, so a change in lifestyle or health affects sperm only after some weeks.
[bio23-s3.1.6] Production needs the lower temperature of the scrotum (the sac that holds the testes outside the body) and the hormones FSH and testosterone.
[bio23-s3.2.b] Egg production runs on a very different timetable from sperm production. A girl's eggs begin forming before she is born, and she never makes more.
[bio23-s3.2.0] Egg production starts before birth: while a girl is a foetus (an unborn baby), her ovaries form all the immature eggs she will ever have, each inside a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac of cells that nourishes the egg).
[bio23-s3.2.1] Each of these eggs begins meiosis but stops part-way.
[bio23-s3.2.2] A baby girl is born with about one to two million immature eggs. Most degenerate (break down), and only about 400–500 are ever released in her life.
[bio23-s3.2.3] No new eggs are made after birth, which is why the supply is fixed.
[bio23-s3.2.4] From puberty, usually one follicle each month develops fully and releases its egg at ovulation (the release of an egg from the ovary). The monthly cycle is dealt with in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s3.2.5] Meiosis continues around ovulation but is completed only if a sperm enters the egg, so an egg that is never fertilised never finishes meiosis.
[bio23-s3.3.b] Meiosis in the ovary does not split the cell evenly, and the eggs it makes are as old as the woman herself. Both facts matter for the early embryo and for fertility.
[bio23-s3.3.0] Division in egg production is unequal: almost all the cytoplasm goes to one cell, the egg, and the other products are tiny cells called polar bodies, which degenerate.
[bio23-s3.3.1] So one cell undergoing meiosis gives one egg, not four.
[bio23-s3.3.2] This unequal split gives the egg a large store of cytoplasm, food reserves and organelles (the small working structures inside a cell) to support the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.3.3] Because the eggs are as old as the woman, they age with her. In older women more eggs have errors in chromosome separation, which is why the risk of conditions such as Down syndrome rises with the mother's age. There is more on this in Mutations.
[bio23-s3.3.4] Fertility also falls with age because of these egg changes.
[bio23-s3.4.b] A sperm has one job, to carry the father's haploid nucleus to the egg. Every part of its structure helps it do that, so it is small, streamlined and powered for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.0] A sperm is a very small, mobile cell, about 0.05 mm long.
[bio23-s3.4.1] The head contains the haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes) and, at its tip, the acrosome, a cap containing enzymes that digest a path through the layers around the egg.
[bio23-s3.4.2] The middle piece is packed with mitochondria that release energy (ATP) by aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen) to power the tail.
[bio23-s3.4.3] The tail is a flagellum (a whip-like tail) that beats to make the sperm swim.
[bio23-s3.4.4] A sperm has very little cytoplasm, which keeps it small and streamlined for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.5] Diagram: a sperm cell. Label the head, acrosome, nucleus, middle piece, mitochondria and tail.
[bio23-s3.5.b] The egg has a different job from the sperm. It cannot move, so its structure is built to supply and protect the new life once a sperm arrives.
[bio23-s3.5.0] The egg (ovum) is one of the largest cells in the body, about 0.1 mm across, just visible to the naked eye. It cannot move by itself.
[bio23-s3.5.1] It contains a haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes, always including an X), a cell membrane, and a large volume of cytoplasm holding food reserves and many mitochondria, which feed and power the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.5.2] Around the egg is a thick, clear, jelly-like coat called the zona pellucida, and outside that an outer layer of follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.5.3] Diagram: an egg, drawn much larger than the sperm. Label the nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, zona pellucida (jelly coat) and follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.6.b] Sperm and eggs are both haploid gametes, but their differences follow from their jobs: the sperm travels and delivers, and the egg stores food and waits. Compare them in pairs, one feature at a time.
[bio23-s3.6.0] Size: sperm are tiny, about 0.05 mm long, while the egg is far larger, about 0.1 mm across, with a far greater volume.
[bio23-s3.6.1] Movement: the sperm swims with its tail, but the egg cannot swim and is moved along the fallopian tube by cilia (tiny hair-like structures lining the tube).
[bio23-s3.6.2] Numbers: there are millions of sperm per ejaculation, but usually one egg a month.
[bio23-s3.6.3] Cytoplasm and food: sperm have almost none, but the egg is a large store.
[bio23-s3.6.4] Sex chromosome: a sperm carries an X or a Y, but the egg always carries an X.
[bio23-s3.6.5] Production: sperm are made continuously from puberty, four per meiosis, while eggs begin to form before birth, one per meiosis.
[bio23-s3.7.b] Fertilisation needs sperm and egg to meet in the right place at the right time. Only a tiny fraction of sperm survive the journey, and the timing is tight because both gametes live for a limited time.
[bio23-s3.7.0] During intercourse semen (the fluid containing the sperm) is ejaculated into the top of the vagina, near the cervix (the neck of the uterus).
[bio23-s3.7.1] Sperm swim through the cervical mucus, which is easiest to pass around ovulation when it is thin, then through the uterus and into the fallopian tubes, helped by contractions of the uterus.
[bio23-s3.7.2] Only a few hundred of the millions of sperm reach the egg. Most die in the acidic vagina, are blocked by mucus, or go into the tube with no egg.
[bio23-s3.7.3] Sperm can survive up to about five days in the female tract.
[bio23-s3.7.4] An egg survives only about 12–24 hours after ovulation.
[bio23-s3.7.5] So intercourse in the five days before ovulation, or on the day of ovulation, can lead to fertilisation, because live sperm and a live egg are then present together.
[bio23-s3.8.b] Fertilisation is more than a sperm touching an egg. It is the fusion of two nuclei, and it happens in a set order that stops more than one sperm getting in.
[bio23-s3.8.0] Fertilisation is the fusion of the haploid nucleus of a sperm with the haploid nucleus of an egg to form a diploid zygote.
[bio23-s3.8.1] In humans it normally takes place in the upper part of a fallopian tube, within a day of ovulation.
[bio23-s3.8.2] 1. Sperm reach the egg and push between the follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.8.3] 2. The acrosome of a sperm releases its enzymes, which digest a path through the zona pellucida. This is called the acrosome reaction.
[bio23-s3.8.4] 3. The membrane of one sperm fuses with the egg's membrane, and the sperm's nucleus enters the egg. The tail is left outside, so the sperm's mitochondria stay out.
[bio23-s3.8.5] 4. At once the egg's outer layer changes and the zona pellucida hardens, so no other sperm can enter. This ensures the zygote receives only one set of chromosomes from the father.
[bio23-s3.8.6] 5. The egg completes its meiosis.
[bio23-s3.8.7] 6. The sperm nucleus and egg nucleus fuse, giving a zygote with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent.
[bio23-s3.9.b] Fertilisation settles several things at once: the child's sex, where its mitochondria come from, and the start of a new genetically unique individual.
[bio23-s3.9.0] At fertilisation the sex of the child is decided by whether the sperm carries an X or a Y, because the egg always carries an X. The zygote receives its mitochondria from the egg only. There is more on this in Sex determination and in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio23-s3.9.1] Fertilisation restores the diploid number, which would otherwise halve in every generation.
[bio23-s3.9.2] It combines genetic material from two parents, so the offspring is genetically unique. Together with meiosis, this is a source of variation.
[bio23-s3.9.3] Fertilisation starts the development of the embryo. The zygote begins to divide by mitosis as it moves down the tube. There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s3.9.4] Diagram: a sequence showing a sperm entering an egg (the acrosome digesting the zona pellucida), the sperm nucleus inside the egg, and the two nuclei fusing to form the zygote. Label the sperm nucleus, egg nucleus, zona pellucida, zygote, n and 2n.
[bio23-s3.10.b] Twins can arise in two different ways, and the difference explains why some twins are genetically identical and others are not.
[bio23-s3.10.0] Identical twins come from one zygote that splits into two embryos early in development, so they have the same genes.
[bio23-s3.10.1] Non-identical (fraternal) twins come from two eggs released at the same time and fertilised by two different sperm, so they are no more alike genetically than other brothers and sisters.

CANDIDATES bio23-s8: Fertility control and infertility
[bio23-s8.0.b] Fertility is the ability to produce offspring, but it is not available all the time in everyone. Males and females differ because sperm production runs continuously while egg release happens once per cycle.
[bio23-s8.0.0] A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.0.1] Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life, declining slowly in old age, and every ejaculation (release of semen) contains millions of sperm. This is why a man is fertile on any day.
[bio23-s8.0.2] Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle (ovulation), so fertility comes in a short window once each cycle rather than every day.
[bio23-s8.0.3] The egg survives only about 12–24 hours after release, while sperm can survive up to about five days in the female body.
[bio23-s8.0.4] So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation, when sperm already present can wait for the egg, and the day of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.0.5] In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because the day of ovulation varies from woman to woman and cycle to cycle, the window cannot be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
[bio23-s8.1.b] A woman's fertility is not the same at every age. It follows the state of her eggs, which she is born with and which do not renew.
[bio23-s8.1.0] Female fertility is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline.
[bio23-s8.1.1] Female fertility ends at the menopause, the time of life when ovulation and periods stop for good. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s8.2.b] Contraception means stopping a pregnancy from starting. Every method blocks one step on the route from egg release to implantation, and methods differ in how reliable they are and in what else they protect against.
[bio23-s8.2.0] Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.2.1] Methods work in one of three ways: by preventing ovulation, by preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or by preventing implantation, the embedding of the embryo in the lining of the uterus.
[bio23-s8.2.2] Methods are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
[bio23-s8.2.3] Effectiveness with perfect use: the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective.
[bio23-s8.2.4] With typical, real-life use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time, namely natural methods, condoms and the pill, fail more often, because people forget or make mistakes.
[bio23-s8.2.5] Long-acting methods, namely the implant, coils and sterilisation, stay above 99% effective with typical use, because there is nothing to remember.
[bio23-s8.2.6] Only condoms, male and female, protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), because they stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.2.7] People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.b] Natural methods use no drugs or devices. They rely on behaviour: avoiding intercourse altogether, or avoiding it on the days when pregnancy is possible.
[bio23-s8.3.0] Abstinence, not having intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.1] Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, which they find by combining several signs.
[bio23-s8.3.2] Sign 1, the calendar (rhythm) method: recording cycle lengths over time to estimate when ovulation happens.
[bio23-s8.3.3] Sign 2, body temperature: measured each morning before getting up, it rises by a small amount, a few tenths of a degree Celsius, just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of the hormone progesterone.
[bio23-s8.3.4] Sign 3, cervical mucus (the fluid made by the cervix, the neck of the uterus): it becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation.
[bio23-s8.3.5] Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used, giving a warning that ovulation is near. There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.4.b] Natural methods appeal to some people but are demanding to use and fail more often than other methods in real life. Knowing both sides lets you discuss them fairly.
[bio23-s8.4.0] Advantages of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and they are acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception, for example the teaching of the Catholic Church, which accepts only natural methods.
[bio23-s8.4.1] Limits of natural methods: they need training, daily records and regular cycles, and are much less reliable with typical use.
[bio23-s8.4.2] Natural methods are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, because cycles are then irregular. They also give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.4.3] Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation.
[bio23-s8.4.4] Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception, because ovulation can return without warning.
[bio23-s8.5.b] Barrier methods put a physical block between sperm and egg. Of the methods used during intercourse, only the condoms in this group protect against STIs, because they also stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.5.0] Male condom: a thin latex, or non-latex, sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm cannot enter the vagina.
[bio23-s8.5.1] The male condom also blocks the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV.
[bio23-s8.5.2] The male condom is cheap, widely available and has no hormones; it can tear or slip, and must be put on before any contact.
[bio23-s8.5.3] Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; it also protects against STIs.
[bio23-s8.5.4] Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide (a sperm-killing chemical) and left in for several hours afterwards; it does not protect well against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.b] The copper coil is a device rather than a barrier, but it is grouped with mechanical methods because it is a physical object placed in the body. It works without hormones.
[bio23-s8.6.0] Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse.
[bio23-s8.6.1] Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and it changes the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) so an embryo is unlikely to implant.
[bio23-s8.6.2] The copper coil lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility.
[bio23-s8.6.3] The copper coil can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days.
[bio23-s8.6.4] The copper coil can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.5] It is grouped as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical methods.
[bio23-s8.7.b] Sterilisation is a permanent operation that blocks the tubes gametes travel along. In men this is vasectomy, which stops sperm joining the semen without changing anything else about the man.
[bio23-s8.7.0] Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens (the tube carrying sperm from the testis) is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes. There is more on this in The male reproductive system.
[bio23-s8.7.1] After a vasectomy the man still ejaculates semen, because the fluids come from glands that are untouched; his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected.
[bio23-s8.7.2] The sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.7.3] A vasectomy is not effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
[bio23-s8.8.b] Female sterilisation works on the same principle as vasectomy: the tubes are blocked so egg and sperm cannot meet. Unlike vasectomy, it works straight away.
[bio23-s8.8.0] Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube, the tube that carries the egg from the ovary to the uterus, is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm cannot meet.
[bio23-s8.8.1] After tubal ligation ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before, because the egg is reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.8.2] Tubal ligation is effective at once.
[bio23-s8.9.b] Sterilisation suits some people very well and others badly. The key issue is that it is meant to be permanent.
[bio23-s8.9.0] Sterilisation is very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember, but it should be treated as irreversible: reversal is difficult and often fails.
[bio23-s8.9.1] Sterilisation carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.9.2] It suits people who are sure they want no more children.
[bio23-s8.9.3] Diagram: outline of the male system with the cut in each vas deferens marked, and of the female system with the cut or clip in each fallopian tube marked; label the vas deferens, testis, fallopian tube, ovary, uterus and “cut and tied”.
[bio23-s8.10.b] Chemical methods use hormones or sperm-killing chemicals. The pill is the best known: it is taken by mouth every day and uses synthetic versions of the body's own reproductive hormones.
[bio23-s8.10.0] The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation; the detail of how is in Hormonal manipulation. It also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass.
[bio23-s8.10.1] The combined pill is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed (a bleed like a period) happens.
[bio23-s8.10.2] The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who cannot take oestrogen.
[bio23-s8.11.b] Some hormonal methods release their hormone slowly over months or years, so the user has nothing to remember daily. This is why the implant and the coils, which are long-acting, stay above 99% effective in real life.
[bio23-s8.11.0] Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously.
[bio23-s8.11.1] The implant is a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years.
[bio23-s8.11.2] The injection is given every few months.
[bio23-s8.11.3] The hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS) is placed in the uterus and lasts several years; it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.
[bio23-s8.11.4] The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
[bio23-s8.12.b] Some chemical methods are used after the event or alongside another method rather than as a regular choice.
[bio23-s8.12.0] Emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, work mainly by delaying ovulation, so there is no egg for sperm to meet; one type works within 3 days and another within 5 days.
[bio23-s8.12.1] The copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method. Emergency pills are not for regular use.
[bio23-s8.12.2] Spermicides are chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
[bio23-s8.13.b] Hormonal methods are very effective and have extra health benefits, but they act on the whole body, so they also carry side effects and risks.
[bio23-s8.13.0] Hormonal methods are very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful.
[bio23-s8.13.1] The combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb).
[bio23-s8.13.2] Hormonal methods can cause side effects, for example headaches, mood changes and irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods.
[bio23-s8.13.3] The combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it is not prescribed to everyone.
[bio23-s8.13.4] Hormonal methods give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.13.5] The pill must be taken correctly every day; vomiting or some medicines can stop it working.
[bio23-s8.14.b] Some infections spread mainly through sexual contact, and some of them damage the reproductive system. This links contraception to infertility.
[bio23-s8.14.0] An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact.
[bio23-s8.14.1] Bacterial STIs are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are treated with antibiotics.
[bio23-s8.14.2] Viral STIs are HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B, which can be controlled but is not usually cured. There is more on pathogens in Pathogens.
[bio23-s8.14.3] Chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes, causing pelvic inflammatory disease, which scars and blocks the tubes.
[bio23-s8.14.4] Blocked tubes from pelvic inflammatory disease are a major preventable cause of female infertility, because the egg and sperm can no longer meet, and they raise the risk of ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy that starts outside the uterus).
[bio23-s8.14.5] In men chlamydia and gonorrhoea can inflame the epididymis, the coiled tube on the testis where sperm mature and are stored.
[bio23-s8.15.b] Because many STIs cause no symptoms and can damage fertility, prevention combines protection during sex, testing and treatment, and vaccination.
[bio23-s8.15.0] Prevention: correct use of condoms every time, and fewer sexual partners.
[bio23-s8.15.1] Prevention: regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners breaks the chain of transmission, because many infected people have no symptoms and would otherwise pass the infection on without knowing.
[bio23-s8.15.2] Prevention: vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B. There is more on HPV in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio23-s8.15.3] For HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP) lower the risk of infection.
[bio23-s8.15.4] Treatment of people with HIV reduces their virus to levels at which it is not passed on sexually.
[bio23-s8.15.5] Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics (the drugs no longer kill it), which makes prevention more important.
[bio23-s8.16.b] Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) lets a woman store her eggs while they are still young and healthy, to be used later. It uses a series of steps similar to the start of IVF.
[bio23-s8.16.0] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once, rather than the usual one.
[bio23-s8.16.1] 2. The eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation.
[bio23-s8.16.2] 3. The eggs are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, do not form, and they are stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
[bio23-s8.16.3] 4. Later the eggs are thawed and fertilised by IVF (fertilisation in a dish) or by ICSI (one sperm injected into an egg).
[bio23-s8.17.b] Egg freezing preserves fertility but cannot promise a baby. Who uses it, and why it is not a guarantee, are the points to know.
[bio23-s8.17.0] Uses: before cancer treatment, because chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries, or by women who wish to delay having children.
[bio23-s8.17.1] Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.17.2] Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen, since younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better.
[bio23-s8.17.3] Freezing gives no guarantee of a baby, and not every egg survives thawing.
[bio23-s8.17.4] The process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.
[bio23-s8.18.b] Infertility is common, and its causes can lie with the man, the woman, both, or neither. Finding the cause decides which treatment is suitable.
[bio23-s8.18.0] Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
[bio23-s8.18.1] The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives.
[bio23-s8.18.2] Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither, which is called unexplained infertility.
[bio23-s8.19.b] Male infertility usually comes down to too few sperm, sperm that cannot do their job, or sperm that cannot get out. Lifestyle can also reduce sperm production.
[bio23-s8.19.0] Low sperm count (too few sperm in the semen), and sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped, for example with a faulty tail, reduce the chance that one reaches the egg.
[bio23-s8.19.1] A blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens, for example after infection or vasectomy, stops sperm leaving the testes.
[bio23-s8.19.2] Undescended testes (testes that have not moved down into the scrotum) and low testosterone or low pituitary hormones are also male causes.
[bio23-s8.19.3] Lifestyle and environmental factors reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids and overheating of the testes. There is more on steroids in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s8.19.4] Male infertility is diagnosed by semen analysis, which tests sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope.
[bio23-s8.20.b] Female infertility arises when no egg is released, when egg and sperm cannot meet, or when the uterus cannot support an embryo.
[bio23-s8.20.0] Failure to ovulate, caused by a hormone imbalance, for example polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight, means no egg is released.
[bio23-s8.20.1] Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, for example after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI, stop egg and sperm meeting.
[bio23-s8.20.2] Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries.
[bio23-s8.20.3] Female causes also include problems of the uterus or cervical mucus, and age, because egg number and quality fall.
[bio23-s8.20.4] Female infertility is diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.
[bio23-s8.21.b] If the problem is that a woman does not release eggs regularly, the treatment is to make her ovaries release them. This is a first step before more complex techniques.
[bio23-s8.21.0] Stimulating ovulation is for women who do not ovulate regularly, for example because of PCOS.
[bio23-s8.21.1] Fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs.
[bio23-s8.21.2] The woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles (the fluid-filled sacs in the ovary that each contain an egg) develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation.
[bio23-s8.21.3] Risk: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely.
[bio23-s8.21.4] Risk: the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious.
[bio23-s8.21.5] Lifestyle change, for example weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.
[bio23-s8.22.b] Fertility drugs work by acting on the same hormone system that controls the menstrual cycle, either by boosting the brain's signals to the ovaries or by supplying them directly.
[bio23-s8.22.0] Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation. There is more on feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.22.1] Injected fertility drugs contain FSH, often with LH, and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop.
[bio23-s8.22.2] A final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge.
[bio23-s8.22.3] The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.
[bio23-s8.23.b] Artificial insemination helps when the sperm have trouble reaching the egg. It puts sperm into the uterus by hand, so more of them start closer to the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.0] Artificial insemination, or intrauterine insemination (IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.23.1] This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes, where fertilisation happens.
[bio23-s8.23.2] It is used for a mildly low sperm count or poor motility (swimming), cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm.
[bio23-s8.23.3] The woman's fallopian tubes must be open, because the sperm still have to travel along them to meet the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.4] The same technique is widely used in farming, for example to breed cattle from selected bulls.
[bio23-s8.24.b] IVF takes fertilisation out of the body. Eggs and sperm are brought together in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is then placed in the uterus.
[bio23-s8.24.0] IVF means “in glass”, that is, fertilisation outside the body.
[bio23-s8.24.1] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs.
[bio23-s8.24.2] 2. The eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound.
[bio23-s8.24.3] 3. The eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place.
[bio23-s8.24.4] 4. The embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days, up to the blastocyst stage (a ball of cells ready to implant). There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.24.5] 5. Usually one, sometimes two, embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube.
[bio23-s8.24.6] 6. A pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted.
[bio23-s8.24.7] Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts.
[bio23-s8.25.b] IVF is used when simpler treatments will not work or are unsuitable. It was developed in the twentieth century and is now widely used.
[bio23-s8.25.0] IVF is used for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (checking embryos for a genetic condition before transfer). There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s8.25.1] History: the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe.
[bio23-s8.25.2] Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010.
[bio23-s8.25.3] Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.
[bio23-s8.26.b] ICSI is a version of IVF designed for severe male infertility. Instead of leaving sperm to fertilise the egg in the dish, the laboratory places one sperm inside the egg.
[bio23-s8.26.0] ICSI is the same as IVF except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope.
[bio23-s8.26.1] Because the sperm does not have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility.
[bio23-s8.26.2] ICSI is used for very low sperm count, sperm that cannot swim or cannot penetrate the egg, and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF.
[bio23-s8.26.3] ICSI is also used when sperm are taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle, for example after a vasectomy or a blockage.
[bio23-s8.26.4] ICSI is also used with frozen eggs, which are thawed before the sperm is injected.
[bio23-s8.27.b] A flow chart shows IVF and ICSI together, because they share every step except how the egg is fertilised.
[bio23-s8.27.0] Diagram: a flow chart of IVF with ICSI as a branch: stimulate ovaries → collect eggs → (IVF: mix eggs and sperm in a dish | ICSI: inject one sperm into each egg) → grow embryos for 2–5 days → transfer an embryo to the uterus → pregnancy test.
[bio23-s8.28.b] Discussing contraception means weighing benefits against drawbacks. The benefits range from the individual to the whole planet.
[bio23-s8.28.0] Contraception lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and to space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies.
[bio23-s8.28.1] It prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers.
[bio23-s8.28.2] Condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit.
[bio23-s8.28.3] Hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods, and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer.
[bio23-s8.28.4] At a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats. This is a sustainability link; there is more on this in The cross-cutting themes.
[bio23-s8.29.b] Contraception also has drawbacks, some medical, some personal, and one environmental.
[bio23-s8.29.0] Hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots.
[bio23-s8.29.1] No method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one.
[bio23-s8.29.2] User-dependent methods often fail in real life, because they must be used correctly every time.
[bio23-s8.29.3] Sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted.
[bio23-s8.29.4] Some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds.
[bio23-s8.29.5] Synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.
[bio23-s8.30.b] Treatments for infertility bring benefits that are both personal and medical. Each argument rests on the biology of the technique.
[bio23-s8.30.0] It allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress.
[bio23-s8.30.1] ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers.
[bio23-s8.30.2] Egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.30.3] Preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on.
[bio23-s8.30.4] Donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children.
[bio23-s8.30.5] Research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
[bio23-s8.31.b] Infertility treatments also raise health, cost and ethical concerns. Some are risks to mother and baby, others are questions of fairness and identity.
[bio23-s8.31.0] Stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother; this is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured.
[bio23-s8.31.1] Ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous.
[bio23-s8.31.2] Success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who cannot pay.
[bio23-s8.31.3] ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility, for example some changes on the Y chromosome, can be passed to a son.
[bio23-s8.31.4] Spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s8.31.5] Children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity.
[bio23-s8.31.6] Treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk, for example a higher chance of chromosome conditions.
[bio23-s8.32.b] Egg freezing gives women more control over timing but is not a guarantee. Weigh the benefits against the limits.
[bio23-s8.32.0] For: it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.32.1] For: it gives women more choice over when to have children.
[bio23-s8.32.2] Against: success is uncertain, and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages.
[bio23-s8.32.3] Against: it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs.
[bio23-s8.32.4] Against: it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.
