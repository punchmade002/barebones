CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio22-s6: Emergence and spread of infectious disease
[bio22-s6.0.b] Disease specialists use precise words for how common a disease is and how fast it is changing. You need these terms to describe any outbreak, and they appear throughout the rest of this subsection.
[bio22-s6.0.0] An emerging infectious disease is one that has newly appeared in a population, or one that was known but is rapidly increasing in incidence (the number of new cases) or in geographic range.
[bio22-s6.0.1] A re-emerging disease is one that had declined and is increasing again, e.g. measles where vaccination rates fall, or TB with drug-resistant strains.
[bio22-s6.0.2] Endemic means always present at a fairly steady level in a region, so the number of cases is predictable from year to year.
[bio22-s6.0.3] An epidemic, or outbreak, is a sudden increase in cases above the expected level in a region.
[bio22-s6.0.4] A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across several continents or worldwide, e.g. COVID-19, which the World Health Organization declared a pandemic on 11 March 2020.
[bio22-s6.1.b] Some terms describe where a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) lives between outbreaks and who can pass it on. These explain why some diseases are so hard to get rid of.
[bio22-s6.1.0] A zoonosis is an infectious disease that passes from animals to humans, e.g. avian influenza or rabies.
[bio22-s6.1.1] Most emerging human diseases are zoonoses, often from wild animals, because people have little immunity to pathogens they have never met. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.1.2] A reservoir is a host population in which a pathogen persists and from which it infects others, e.g. badgers for bovine TB in Ireland, or wild birds for avian influenza.
[bio22-s6.1.3] A carrier is an infected individual who shows no symptoms but can pass on the pathogen, so nobody knows to avoid them.
[bio22-s6.2.b] A pathogen can only cause an outbreak if it can get from one host to the next. There are several routes, and each has typical diseases that use it, in both animals and plants.
[bio22-s6.2.0] Airborne droplets and aerosols (tiny particles hanging in the air) from coughs, sneezes and breath spread influenza, TB, measles and COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.2.1] Wind-borne spores spread ash dieback, cereal rusts and potato blight in plants, because spores are light enough to be carried between fields.
[bio22-s6.2.2] Direct contact, touch and skin to skin, spreads ringworm and athlete's foot.
[bio22-s6.2.3] Indirect contact via contaminated objects and surfaces (fomites), hands, tools and clothing spreads disease. In plants, contact with hands and tools spreads tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s6.2.4] Contaminated food and water, the faecal–oral route (pathogens in faeces reach the mouth), spreads cholera, Salmonella food poisoning and amoebic dysentery.
[bio22-s6.2.5] Body fluids spread HIV and hepatitis B through sexual contact, blood (shared needles and unscreened transfusions) and mother to baby.
[bio22-s6.2.6] Vectors are animals that carry pathogens between hosts: mosquitoes (malaria), ticks (Lyme disease), tsetse flies (sleeping sickness), elm bark beetles (Dutch elm disease), aphids (many plant viruses, e.g. barley yellow dwarf virus) and biting midges (bluetongue virus of sheep and cattle).
[bio22-s6.2.7] In plants, rain splash, soil, infected seed, tubers and cuttings also spread disease. Vegetative propagation (growing new plants from parts of old ones) passes viruses and blight to the next crop, and grafting does too.
[bio22-s6.3.b] Diseases spread when infected organisms, or the things they touch, move. Over short distances this happens through everyday contact, and over a whole country it follows the trade and travel routes of animals and people.
[bio22-s6.3.0] Local spread occurs within a household, school, hospital or farm, between neighbouring fields by wind and rain splash, and between neighbouring farms by animals straying, shared equipment, vehicles and people's boots.
[bio22-s6.3.1] Regional spread occurs when farm animals move through marts (livestock markets) and between farms, when plants and animals are traded within a country or the EU, when wild animals move seasonally, and when people commute and holiday.
[bio22-s6.3.2] In the 2001 foot-and-mouth disease epidemic in Britain, infected sheep were moved through markets across the country before the disease was detected, spreading it to many farms.
[bio22-s6.3.3] Control of that epidemic needed a ban on animal movement and the culling of millions of animals, which shows how costly it is once movement has carried a disease widely.
[bio22-s6.4.b] Modern travel and trade mean a pathogen no longer stays where it started. A disease can reach another continent before anyone knows it is there.
[bio22-s6.4.0] Air travel can carry an infected person to any continent within a day, faster than many diseases take to show symptoms, so travellers can carry infection without knowing it.
[bio22-s6.4.1] COVID-19 reached every continent within months of its first identification in Wuhan, China, in December 2019.
[bio22-s6.4.2] International trade moves pathogens and vectors in plants, wood, soil, live animals and goods. Ash dieback spread through Europe partly in imported young ash plants and was found in Ireland in 2012.
[bio22-s6.4.3] The second, more aggressive epidemic of Dutch elm disease reached Britain from the late 1960s in imported elm logs.
[bio22-s6.4.4] The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus), a vector of several viruses, has spread to many countries in used tyres and plants.
[bio22-s6.4.5] Migrating wild birds carry avian influenza between continents, because infected birds fly long distances without any border controls.
[bio22-s6.5.b] Trade in live animals can move a disease into places where the local species have never met it. Amphibians show how serious this can be.
[bio22-s6.5.0] The pet and wildlife trade spread chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) around the world in the trade in amphibians.
[bio22-s6.5.1] Chytrid is linked to the decline of hundreds of amphibian species and the extinction of some.
[bio22-s6.6.b] Emergence and spread depend on several interrelated factors, and the first is persistence. The longer a host carries a pathogen in a way that can pass on, the more chances it has to infect others.
[bio22-s6.6.0] The longer a host is infectious, the more others it can infect.
[bio22-s6.6.1] Persistence includes long infectious periods, carriers without symptoms, latent infections (the pathogen is present but inactive) that reactivate, and reservoir hosts.
[bio22-s6.6.2] HIV may cause no symptoms for years while the person can still pass it on.
[bio22-s6.6.3] Herpes viruses stay latent in nerve cells for life, so the person is never rid of the virus. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.6.4] TB bacteria can stay dormant (inactive) in the lungs for years and become active later.
[bio22-s6.7.b] Persistence shows up in each kind of host, from a single healthy-looking person to wildlife and crops.
[bio22-s6.7.0] Mary Mallon, known as Typhoid Mary, was born in Co. Tyrone in 1869 and worked as a cook in New York. She carried the typhoid bacterium, Salmonella Typhi, without symptoms and infected many of the families she cooked for.
[bio22-s6.7.1] Health authorities isolated her for most of the rest of her life, because as a carrier she could not be identified by illness.
[bio22-s6.7.2] Bovine TB persists in Irish cattle partly because badgers act as a wildlife reservoir that reinfects herds, so clearing the disease from cattle alone is not enough.
[bio22-s6.7.3] Plant viruses persist in perennial plants (which live for years), weeds and tubers, so infected seed potatoes carry viruses into the next year's crop.
[bio22-s6.7.4] Phytophthora infestans, the cause of potato blight, survives the winter in infected tubers left in the ground or in dumps.
[bio22-s6.8.b] A mutation is a random change in a pathogen's genetic material. Pathogens that reproduce quickly produce huge numbers of changed copies, so some will spread better than the original.
[bio22-s6.8.0] Pathogens with short generation times and large populations produce many mutants, because each new copy is another chance for a change.
[bio22-s6.8.1] RNA viruses mutate fastest because their copying enzyme doesn't proofread (check for copying errors). There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.8.2] Antigenic drift: small mutations gradually change a virus's surface antigens (the molecules the immune system recognises), so memory cells and antibodies from earlier infections or vaccines no longer fit well.
[bio22-s6.8.3] Antigenic drift is why influenza returns every winter and why flu vaccines are updated each year.
[bio22-s6.8.4] Mutations likewise produced new SARS-CoV-2 variants, such as Delta and Omicron, that spread faster or partly escaped immunity, causing new waves of COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.9.b] Mutations can also change what a pathogen is able to do, giving it a new host or defeating a crop's defences.
[bio22-s6.9.0] Antigenic shift (influenza): if two different flu viruses infect the same cell, e.g. in a pig, their RNA segments can mix to form a new strain with surface antigens that no human population has met.
[bio22-s6.9.1] Antigenic shift has caused flu pandemics, e.g. the 2009 H1N1 (swine flu) pandemic, because with no existing immunity the new strain spreads easily.
[bio22-s6.9.2] Mutations can let a pathogen infect a new host species (spillover, e.g. a bird virus gaining the ability to infect mammals), become more transmissible, or overcome resistance bred into crops.
[bio22-s6.9.3] The Ug99 strain of wheat stem rust, identified in Uganda in 1999, overcame resistance genes used in most of the world's wheat varieties.
[bio22-s6.10.b] Resistance means a pathogen or pest survives a drug or chemical that used to kill it. It arises by natural selection (survival of the individuals best suited to their conditions), which happens in four steps. There is more on this in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
[bio22-s6.10.0] 1. Random mutations, or genes passed between bacteria on plasmids (small rings of DNA), make a few individuals resistant.
[bio22-s6.10.1] 2. The antibiotic or chemical kills the susceptible individuals.
[bio22-s6.10.2] 3. The resistant survivors reproduce with less competition.
[bio22-s6.10.3] 4. The next generation is mostly resistant. The chemical doesn't cause the mutation; it selects it.
[bio22-s6.11.b] Resistance arises by natural selection when a drug or chemical kills susceptible individuals, and some practices make it spread faster. The examples cover people, crops and livestock.
[bio22-s6.11.0] Overuse and misuse of antibiotics speed up resistance: use for viral infections (which antibiotics cannot treat), not finishing courses, and use in farm animals.
[bio22-s6.11.1] Poor infection control in hospitals and heavy repeated use of one pesticide also speed up resistance.
[bio22-s6.11.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many antibiotics and spreads in hospitals.
[bio22-s6.11.3] Multidrug-resistant TB, malaria parasites resistant to some antimalarial drugs, and mosquitoes resistant to insecticides, which weakens malaria control.
[bio22-s6.11.4] Fungi that cause crop diseases are resistant to some fungicides, and parasitic worms of sheep and cattle are resistant to some anthelmintics (drugs that kill worms).
[bio22-s6.11.5] Resistant pathogens persist longer in hosts and spread further, linking factors 1 and 3.
[bio22-s6.12.b] A pathogen needs susceptible hosts, meaning individuals with no immunity to it. How many of these there are in a population strongly affects how far and how fast an outbreak goes.
[bio22-s6.12.0] A pathogen spreads only if it can reach susceptible (non-immune) hosts. The higher the proportion of immune individuals, the fewer susceptible hosts there are and the slower the spread. This is herd immunity. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s6.12.1] A pathogen new to a population meets no immunity at all, so it spreads fast and can be very severe, e.g. COVID-19 in 2020, before vaccines.
[bio22-s6.12.2] Smallpox and measles, brought by Europeans to the Americas from the late 1400s, killed very large numbers of Indigenous people, who had never been exposed to them.
[bio22-s6.12.3] Immunity falls when vaccination rates drop, as when measles outbreaks return.
[bio22-s6.12.4] Immunity also falls when protection from vaccination or infection wanes over time, and where many people have weakened immune systems, e.g. from untreated HIV or malnutrition, or the very young and very old.
[bio22-s6.13.b] Plants have no antibody-based immunity like animals, but the same principle applies. What matters is whether any individuals in the crop can resist the pathogen.
[bio22-s6.13.0] In plants, the equivalent is genetic uniformity. A monoculture (a crop of one genetically identical variety) has no resistant individuals, so a pathogen that infects one plant can infect them all.
[bio22-s6.13.1] In Ireland in the 1840s, most of the poor depended on one potato variety, the Lumper, grown from tubers (genetically identical clones) and susceptible to blight.
[bio22-s6.13.2] When Phytophthora infestans arrived in 1845, crops failed across the country in successive years. About one million people died and over a million emigrated in the Great Famine.
[bio22-s6.14.b] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more other hosts they meet, so the further a pathogen travels. Mobility applies to people, livestock, wildlife and vectors.
[bio22-s6.14.0] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more contacts each has and the further the pathogen spreads. The routes are those in local, regional and global spread above.
[bio22-s6.14.1] Crowding in cities, refugee camps, festivals and mass gatherings, travel and migration, and the movement of livestock all increase spread.
[bio22-s6.14.2] Migration of wild animals, such as birds, also moves pathogens into new areas.
[bio22-s6.14.3] Vector mobility: climate warming lets vectors survive further north and for longer each year, carrying disease into new regions.
[bio22-s6.14.4] Bluetongue, a midge-borne virus of sheep and cattle, spread into northern Europe from 2006.
[bio22-s6.14.5] Reducing mobility reduces spread: animal movement bans in foot-and-mouth outbreaks, travel restrictions and quarantine in human epidemics, and import controls on plants. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.15.b] To compare outbreaks and predict whether one will grow, scientists use a single number, R₀. It measures how contagious a pathogen is when nobody has immunity.
[bio22-s6.15.0] The basic reproduction number, R₀ ('R nought'), is the average number of new (secondary) infections caused by one infected individual in a population where everyone is susceptible, with no immunity and no control measures.
[bio22-s6.15.1] R₀ greater than 1: each case causes more than one new case on average, so numbers grow and an epidemic can occur.
[bio22-s6.15.2] R₀ equal to 1: numbers stay steady, which is endemic.
[bio22-s6.15.3] R₀ less than 1: each case causes fewer than one new case, so the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.16.b] Each infected person passes the pathogen to R₀ others, so cases multiply by R₀ every generation of infection (one round of people infecting the next). Working through numbers shows why R₀ above or below 1 matters.
[bio22-s6.16.0] Growth example: with R₀ = 2, one case leads to 2, then 4, then 8, then 16 new cases per generation of infection. After 10 generations, one case has led to 2¹⁰ = 1,024 new cases in that generation.
[bio22-s6.16.1] Decline example: with R₀ = 0.5, 100 cases give 50, then 25, then about 12, and the outbreak fades.
[bio22-s6.16.2] Diagram: a branching tree from one case, giving 2, then 4, then 8 cases (R₀ = 2), beside a single chain with one case per generation (R₀ = 1). Label each generation.
[bio22-s6.17.b] R₀ is not a fixed property of a pathogen. It combines features of the pathogen, the host and the environment, which are the factors already covered.
[bio22-s6.17.0] R₀ depends on how long a host is infectious (persistence), so a longer infectious period raises R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.1] R₀ depends on how many contacts an infected host has per day (mobility and crowding), so more contacts raise R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.2] R₀ depends on the chance that a contact passes on the infection (the transmission route, and mutations that make a pathogen more transmissible).
[bio22-s6.17.3] So R₀ is not fixed for a disease: it differs between populations and settings.
[bio22-s6.18.b] In real outbreaks some people are already immune or control measures are in place. The effective reproduction number measures spread under those real conditions.
[bio22-s6.18.0] The effective reproduction number, R (sometimes written Rt), is the average number of new infections per case at a given time, in a population where some people are immune or control measures are in place.
[bio22-s6.18.1] Approximately, R = R₀ × the proportion still susceptible. For example, R₀ = 3 with half the population immune gives R = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5.
[bio22-s6.18.2] Control aims to bring R below 1, so that each case infects fewer than one other person and the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.19.b] If R₀ tells us how contagious a disease is, we can work out how many people must be immune to stop it spreading. The more contagious the disease, the higher that proportion.
[bio22-s6.19.0] Herd-immunity threshold: to stop sustained spread, the proportion immune must be at least 1 − 1/R₀.
[bio22-s6.19.1] Examples: R₀ = 2 needs 1 − ½ = 50% immune; R₀ = 4 needs 1 − ¼ = 75% immune; R₀ = 10 needs 90% immune.
[bio22-s6.19.2] Measles is one of the most contagious diseases, with R₀ usually quoted as 12–18, so about 95% of people must be immune.
[bio22-s6.19.3] The World Health Organization therefore recommends at least 95% coverage with two doses of measles vaccine.
[bio22-s6.20.b] R₀ comes from a simple model, and a model is only as good as its assumptions. Real outbreaks differ in important ways.
[bio22-s6.20.0] The model's assumptions are that everyone is equally likely to meet everyone else and that R₀ stays constant. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s6.20.1] Real populations are clustered, behaviour changes during outbreaks and new variants change R₀, so estimates are updated as data come in.
[bio22-s6.21.b] The six factors do not work one at a time. Real outbreaks happen when several combine, and other changes in how we live bring pathogens and new hosts into contact.
[bio22-s6.21.0] The factors act together, not one at a time. A mutation that makes a virus more transmissible raises R₀, and a population with low immunity and high mobility then lets it spread widely.
[bio22-s6.21.1] Long persistence in carriers lets a pathogen reach new areas undetected, and resistance to drugs lets infections last longer, raising R₀ further.
[bio22-s6.21.2] Plant example: a genetically uniform crop (low immunity), a pathogen spread by wind and trade (mobility) and survival in tubers (persistence) together explain the speed of the potato famine.
[bio22-s6.21.3] Other drivers increase contact between pathogens and new hosts: clearing forests and expanding farms into wild areas (more contact with wild animals), intensive farming of large numbers of genetically similar animals kept close together, climate change (vectors' ranges), poverty, poor sanitation, and conflict, which weakens health systems.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio30-s2: Transport of water and minerals
[bio30-s2.0.b] Water moves in one continuous flow, from the soil through the plant and out into the air. This flow is the transpiration stream, and its first part carries water from the soil into the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.0] Overview: water moves from the soil into the root hairs (tiny outgrowths of root cells that give a large surface for absorption), across the root cortex (the layer of cells between the surface and the centre), into the xylem (the water-carrying tubes), up the stem and into the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.1] It then leaves the leaves as water vapour through the stomata (pores in the leaf surface), completing the continuous flow called the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.0.2] Stage 1, into the root hair by osmosis: soil water is a dilute solution with a higher water concentration, while the cell sap in the root hair's vacuole is more concentrated, with a lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.3] Water therefore passes in through the selectively permeable cell membrane, moving from higher to lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.4] Stage 2, across the cortex: water moves from cell to cell by osmosis, each cell inwards being slightly more concentrated than the one before, so the water keeps flowing inwards. It also seeps along the cellulose cell walls and through the spaces between cells.
[bio30-s2.0.5] Water then passes through the endodermis (the ring of cells around the centre of the root) into the xylem at the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.6] Stage 3, up the xylem: water travels up the root and stem xylem as continuous columns, which carry it into the veins of the leaves. The force that moves it is the transpiration pull, which comes from evaporation in the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.7] At Higher Level, root pressure and the cohesion–tension model give a fuller account of how water rises, in their own blocks below.
[bio30-s2.1.b] Once water reaches the leaf it has to get out again, and the way it leaves is what pulls the whole column up from the roots. This part of the route also shows that the plant itself does no work to lift the water.
[bio30-s2.1.0] Stage 4, into the leaf cells: water leaves the xylem in the veins and passes by osmosis into the mesophyll cells (the cells in the middle layer of the leaf), keeping them turgid (firm and full of water) and supplying a small amount for photosynthesis.
[bio30-s2.1.1] Stage 5, out of the leaf: water evaporates from the moist walls of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, which become saturated with water vapour. The vapour then diffuses out through the open stomata into the drier air outside, from a higher to a lower concentration of water vapour.
[bio30-s2.1.2] Transpiration pull: as water evaporates from the leaves, more water is drawn out of the xylem to replace it, so the whole column of water in the xylem is pulled up from the roots, like drinking through a straw.
[bio30-s2.1.3] The energy for the transpiration pull comes from the sun's heat, which causes evaporation; the plant uses none of its own ATP (its cell energy currency) to move water up, so the pull is a passive process.
[bio30-s2.1.4] Diagram: an outline of a whole plant with arrows showing the route soil water, root hair, cortex, root xylem, stem xylem, leaf vein, mesophyll cell, air space, stoma, air; label osmosis (root hair, cortex), evaporation (mesophyll cell walls) and diffusion (stoma).
[bio30-s2.2.b] Transpiration is the process that drives the water flow described above. It brings real benefits, but it is also a cost the plant cannot avoid, so you need to be able to give both sides.
[bio30-s2.2.0] Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the parts of a plant above the ground, mainly through the stomata of the leaves. Small amounts are lost through the cuticle (the waxy surface layer) and the lenticels (small pores in woody stems).
[bio30-s2.2.1] Most of the water a plant absorbs is lost by transpiration; only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis or kept in the cells. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio30-s2.2.2] Transpiration benefits the plant by pulling water and dissolved minerals up from the roots to the leaves, the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.2.3] It keeps the leaf cells supplied with water, so they stay turgid and the leaves stay firm and spread out to the light. Evaporation also cools the leaves in strong sunlight, because evaporating water carries heat away.
[bio30-s2.2.4] Transpiration is unavoidable. The stomata must be open to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis, and water vapour escapes through the same pores.
[bio30-s2.2.5] If water is lost faster than the roots can take it in, the cells lose turgor (they go limp) and the plant wilts. Prolonged wilting stops photosynthesis and can kill the plant, so the plant balances gas exchange against water loss.
[bio30-s2.2.6] Conditions that increase transpiration are bright light, high temperature, wind, low humidity, a large leaf area and a thin cuticle. Each is explained and investigated in Investigation: rate of transpiration.
[bio30-s2.3.b] A stoma is a pore that lets CO₂ in and water vapour out, and the plant can open or close it. The guard cells on either side of the pore do this by changing how much water they hold.
[bio30-s2.3.0] Each stoma is a pore between two guard cells. The wall of a guard cell next to the pore, the inner wall, is thicker and less stretchy than the outer wall, and this difference is what makes the cell bend when it swells.
[bio30-s2.3.1] Opening: in the light, guard cells actively take in potassium ions (K⁺) from neighbouring epidermal cells (the ordinary cells of the leaf surface), using ATP. This raises the solute concentration in the guard cells, so water enters them by osmosis from the surrounding cells.
[bio30-s2.3.2] The guard cells swell and become turgid. Because the thick inner wall stretches less than the thin outer wall, the cells bend outwards and curve apart, and the pore opens.
[bio30-s2.3.3] Closing: potassium ions leave the guard cells, water follows by osmosis, the guard cells become flaccid (limp) and straighten, and the pore closes.
[bio30-s2.3.4] Diagram: a pair of guard cells drawn twice, once open (turgid, curved apart, pore open) and once closed (flaccid, straight, pore closed); label guard cell, thick inner wall, thin outer wall, chloroplast, nucleus and stoma (pore); add arrows for K⁺ and water entering when the stoma opens.
[bio30-s2.4.b] The plant does not open its stomata at random. It opens them when CO₂ is needed and closes them when water must be saved, and it places them where least water is lost.
[bio30-s2.4.0] Stomata are usually open in daylight, when CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis, and when the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is low. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s2.4.1] Stomata close at night, when there is no photosynthesis and so no need for CO₂, and in very hot, dry conditions. They also close when CO₂ concentration inside the leaf is high.
[bio30-s2.4.2] When water is short and the plant begins to wilt, a chemical signal from the roots closes the stomata, saving water even though photosynthesis slows.
[bio30-s2.4.3] Stomata are mostly on the lower surface of a broad leaf, where it is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates from them. Leaves floating on water, such as the water lily, have them on the upper surface instead, because the lower surface touches the water.
[bio30-s2.5.b] Because transpiration cannot be stopped, plants survive by limiting it. Their leaves and habits are adapted to lose less water, most of all in dry, windy or cold places.
[bio30-s2.5.0] Leaf features that reduce water loss include a waxy, waterproof cuticle, thicker on the leaves of plants in dry or windy places; most stomata on the lower, shaded surface; and stomata that close at night and in drought.
[bio30-s2.5.1] Deciduous trees (trees that drop their leaves each year) shed their leaves in autumn, so they do not lose water in winter, when cold soil water is hard to absorb.
[bio30-s2.5.2] Evergreen conifers have needle-shaped leaves with a small surface area and a thick cuticle, which means little water evaporates even though the leaves stay on all year.
[bio30-s2.5.3] Plants of dry habitats (xerophytes) have extra adaptations. Marram grass of sand dunes rolls its leaves into a tube, with the stomata on the inside surface, trapping humid air. Cacti have leaves reduced to spines and store water in a swollen green stem.
[bio30-s2.6.b] Water is not the only thing roots take in. They also absorb dissolved minerals from the soil, but minerals enter by a different process from water, and that process costs the plant energy.
[bio30-s2.6.0] Plants need minerals such as nitrate, phosphate, potassium, magnesium and calcium. They are taken up from the soil water as dissolved ions, for example nitrate (NO₃⁻), potassium (K⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺).
[bio30-s2.6.1] Mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside the root hair cells than in the soil water, so they must be pumped in against their concentration gradient by carrier proteins in the cell membrane, using ATP from respiration. This is active transport.
[bio30-s2.6.2] Where the soil concentration is higher than in the cell, some ions also enter by diffusion.
[bio30-s2.6.3] Because active transport needs ATP, roots need oxygen for aerobic respiration. In waterlogged soil the air spaces fill with water, the roots get too little oxygen, mineral uptake falls and the plant grows poorly.
[bio30-s2.6.4] This is one reason soil must be well drained, and why roots have air spaces in the cortex.
[bio30-s2.6.5] The ions move across the cortex to the xylem and are carried up, dissolved in the water of the transpiration stream, to the leaves, growing tips and other parts, where they are taken into cells. The faster the transpiration, the faster minerals reach the leaves.
[bio30-s2.6.6] Pumping mineral ions into the root also makes the root cells more concentrated than the soil water, which keeps water entering by osmosis.
[bio30-s2.7.b] Transpiration pulls water up from above, but roots can also push it up from below. This push is called root pressure, and it is an active process that needs living root cells.
[bio30-s2.7.0] Root pressure is a pressure in the root xylem that pushes water a short distance up the stem from below.
[bio30-s2.7.1] Living root cells actively pump mineral ions into the xylem, using ATP. This makes the xylem sap more concentrated than the surrounding cells, so water passes into the xylem by osmosis, building up a pressure that pushes the water upwards.
[bio30-s2.7.2] Evidence: when a stem is cut off near ground level, sap oozes from the cut stump for some time, pushed out by the roots. Grapevines pruned in spring bleed sap in this way.
[bio30-s2.7.3] Guttation: on cool, humid nights or early mornings, when transpiration is very slow, drops of water are forced out through special pores at the tips and edges of leaves, for example on grass and strawberry leaves.
[bio30-s2.7.4] Guttation drops are not dew, because they appear at the leaf edges, not all over the surface.
[bio30-s2.7.5] Root pressure needs living, respiring root cells. It stops if the roots are killed, cooled or deprived of oxygen.
[bio30-s2.7.6] That it stops in these conditions shows that root pressure is an active process.
[bio30-s2.7.7] Root pressure is most significant at night and in spring, before the leaves have opened and when transpiration is low, and it can refill xylem columns that contain air bubbles.
[bio30-s2.7.8] Root pressure is far too weak to raise water to the top of a tall tree, and some plants show little or none, so it is not the main mechanism.
[bio30-s2.8.b] Root pressure is too weak to lift water to the top of a tall tree, so something else must do it. The cohesion–tension model explains how a pulling force from the leaves, and water's tendency to stick together, raises an unbroken column of water.
[bio30-s2.8.0] The cohesion–tension model explains how water rises to the tops of the tallest trees. It was proposed in 1894 by two Irish scientists at Trinity College Dublin, the botanist Henry Dixon and the physicist John Joly.
[bio30-s2.8.1] 1. Transpiration: water evaporates from the walls of the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata.
[bio30-s2.8.2] 2. The mesophyll cells replace the lost water by osmosis from the cells deeper in the leaf, and these take water from the xylem in the veins.
[bio30-s2.8.3] 3. Tension: removing water from the top of the xylem puts the column of water in the xylem under tension, a pulling force or negative pressure, which is passed all the way down the stem to the roots. This is the transpiration pull.
[bio30-s2.8.4] 4. Cohesion: water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, so they stick together and the column does not break. Each molecule pulled up drags the one behind it, so the whole unbroken column moves up as one.
[bio30-s2.8.5] 5. Adhesion: water molecules are also attracted to the walls of the narrow xylem vessels. This helps hold the column up against gravity and stops it pulling away from the walls.
[bio30-s2.8.6] 6. At the bottom, water is drawn into the root xylem from the cortex, and into the root hairs from the soil, to replace it.
[bio30-s2.8.7] The process is passive, so the plant spends no ATP on it. The lignified (stiffened with lignin) walls of the xylem stop the vessels collapsing inwards under the tension.
[bio30-s2.9.b] A model is only convincing if observations support it. Two observations show that the water in the xylem really is under tension, and one other factor is too weak to matter.
[bio30-s2.9.0] The trunk of a tree becomes very slightly narrower during the day, when transpiration is fastest, as the tension pulls the xylem walls inwards.
[bio30-s2.9.1] When a xylem vessel under tension is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out. If air enters the xylem the column breaks and water flow in that vessel stops.
[bio30-s2.9.2] This is why the stems of cut flowers should be cut again under water before being placed in a vase.
[bio30-s2.9.3] Capillarity, the rise of water up narrow tubes by adhesion, raises water only a short way and is a minor factor.
[bio30-s2.10.b] The two mechanisms work together to move water, but they differ in direction, energy source, strength and timing. Comparing them in pairs is the clearest way to keep them apart.
[bio30-s2.10.0] Root pressure pushes water from below; cohesion–tension pulls it from above.
[bio30-s2.10.1] Root pressure is active, using ATP in the root cells; cohesion–tension is passive, powered by the sun's energy, which evaporates the water.
[bio30-s2.10.2] Root pressure raises water only a short distance; cohesion–tension is the main mechanism and can lift water to the top of the tallest trees.
[bio30-s2.10.3] Root pressure is most important at night and in spring, when transpiration is low; cohesion–tension works when the stomata are open and transpiration is fast, mainly by day.

CANDIDATES bio30-s3: Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products
[bio30-s3.0.b] Photosynthesis needs a steady supply of carbon dioxide (CO₂), the raw material the plant builds sugar from. A leaf cell has two places to get it from: the air around the plant and its own respiring cells.
[bio30-s3.0.0] A photosynthesising leaf gets CO₂ from two sources: the air outside, entering through the stomata (the tiny pores in the leaf surface), and the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio30-s3.0.1] The air contains only about 0.04% CO₂, so a leaf must exchange large volumes of air to get enough. This is why it has many stomata and a large internal surface.
[bio30-s3.0.2] Every living plant cell respires all the time, day and night, releasing CO₂ from its mitochondria (the organelles where respiration takes place). Plants do not respire only at night.
[bio30-s3.0.3] In the light, the CO₂ released by a mesophyll cell's own mitochondria, and by neighbouring cells, can be used directly by its chloroplasts without ever leaving the leaf. Mesophyll cells are the photosynthesising cells inside the leaf.
[bio30-s3.0.4] Aquatic plants take in CO₂ dissolved in the water around them.
[bio30-s3.1.b] CO₂ has to get from the outside air into the chloroplasts (the green organelles where photosynthesis happens), and it does so in a series of steps with no pump involved. The steps where it moves (2, 3 and 5) are diffusion down a concentration gradient, meaning from where there is more CO₂ to where there is less, and step 4 is the CO₂ dissolving in water.
[bio30-s3.1.0] 1. Photosynthesis uses CO₂ in the chloroplasts, so the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf falls below that of the outside air, setting up a concentration gradient.
[bio30-s3.1.1] 2. CO₂ diffuses in through the open stomata, from the higher concentration outside to the lower concentration inside.
[bio30-s3.1.2] 3. It diffuses through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll (the loosely packed layer of cells in the lower leaf) to the mesophyll cells.
[bio30-s3.1.3] 4. It dissolves in the thin film of water on the moist cell walls, because gases can only enter cells in solution.
[bio30-s3.1.4] 5. It diffuses across the cell wall, cell membrane and cytoplasm into the chloroplasts, where it is used in the light-independent stage (the part of photosynthesis that builds sugar from CO₂). The gradient is kept up because CO₂ is used as fast as it arrives.
[bio30-s3.1.5] Diagram: a transverse section of part of a leaf, with arrows for CO₂ diffusing in through a stoma, through the air spaces and into a palisade cell's chloroplast, and for oxygen and water vapour diffusing out; plus a short arrow from a mitochondrion to a chloroplast, labelled CO₂ from respiration.
[bio30-s3.2.b] Because CO₂ moves only by diffusion, a leaf is built to make that diffusion fast and short. Stomata also act as the gates that decide when CO₂ can enter.
[bio30-s3.2.0] CO₂ moves through the plant by diffusion only, through the air spaces and across moist cell surfaces. It is not carried in the xylem or phloem (the plant's water and food-carrying tubes), and no energy is used to move it.
[bio30-s3.2.1] A thin leaf (so the distance to travel is short), a large surface area, many stomata, a network of air spaces and moist mesophyll cell walls with a large total surface all speed up the diffusion of CO₂.
[bio30-s3.2.2] Stomata control the entry of CO₂: they open in the light, when CO₂ is needed. At Higher Level, the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf also helps control whether they open or close. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s3.3.b] Photosynthesis and respiration both happen in a leaf, and they swap the same gases in opposite directions. Which one wins depends on how much light there is, so the net gas exchange changes through the day.
[bio30-s3.3.0] In bright light, photosynthesis is faster than respiration. The leaf uses more CO₂ than its cells produce, so there is a net uptake of CO₂ through the stomata and a net release of oxygen.
[bio30-s3.3.1] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, only respiration. The plant takes in oxygen and gives out CO₂, through stomata that are mostly closed, through the cuticle (the waxy surface layer) in small amounts, and through lenticels (small pores in woody stems).
[bio30-s3.3.2] At dawn and dusk, in dim light, the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, a point called the compensation point. All the CO₂ produced by respiration is used in photosynthesis, so there is no net exchange of CO₂ or oxygen with the air.
[bio30-s3.3.3] Non-green parts, such as roots, woody stems and the inside of fruits and tubers, only respire because they do not photosynthesise. They take in oxygen and release CO₂ by diffusion: roots to the air spaces in the soil, and woody stems through lenticels.
[bio30-s3.3.4] The oxygen made in photosynthesis is a photosynthetic product too. It is used in the plant's own respiration, and the excess diffuses out through the stomata.
[bio30-s3.4.b] The sugar made in a leaf is needed all over the plant, in places that cannot photosynthesise. Translocation is how it gets there, carried in the phloem by a flow that goes from where the sugar is made to where it is used.
[bio30-s3.4.0] Translocation is the transport of dissolved food made in photosynthesis, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, through the phloem sieve tubes (the living tubes of the phloem) from one part of the plant to another.
[bio30-s3.4.1] Sucrose is the form in which sugar is carried, not glucose. Starch is never transported; it is stored, and must be converted to sucrose before it can move.
[bio30-s3.4.2] Amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are made in the leaves from sugars and the nitrates brought up in the xylem. They are carried in the phloem to growing regions, where they are used to make proteins.
[bio30-s3.4.3] A source is a part of the plant where sucrose is made or released into the phloem. This is mainly the photosynthesising leaves, and also storage organs when their stores are being used.
[bio30-s3.4.4] A sink is a part where sucrose is used or stored. Sinks include growing shoot and root tips, which use it in respiration and to make cellulose for new cell walls, and developing flowers, fruits and seeds.
[bio30-s3.4.5] Other sinks are the roots and storage organs, where sucrose is converted to starch, as in potato tubers in summer.
[bio30-s3.5.b] Because sources and sinks are in different places and can swap roles, phloem flow is not fixed in one direction. It is also driven by a living, energy-using process, which sets it apart from water movement in the xylem.
[bio30-s3.5.0] Because sinks can be above or below the leaves, phloem transport goes both up and down the plant, at different times in different sieve tubes.
[bio30-s3.5.1] A part can change role. A potato tuber is a sink while it fills with starch in summer, and a source the next spring, when its starch is converted back to sucrose and carried to the growing new shoots.
[bio30-s3.5.2] Translocation needs energy: at the source, companion cells (the cells beside the sieve tubes) use ATP to load sucrose actively into the sieve tubes. So it happens only in living phloem and stops if the phloem is killed, for example by heat.
[bio30-s3.5.3] There is more on this in Plant structures for transport.
[bio30-s3.6.b] Once sucrose is in the sieve tubes, something must push the sap along. The pressure-flow model explains this as a pressure difference between the source and the sink, set up by moving sucrose in and out and by water following it by osmosis (water moving into a more concentrated solution).
[bio30-s3.6.0] 1. Loading sucrose at the source makes the sap in the sieve tubes there very concentrated.
[bio30-s3.6.1] 2. Water enters the sieve tubes from the nearby xylem by osmosis, because the concentrated sap draws it in, raising the pressure.
[bio30-s3.6.2] 3. At the sink, sucrose is removed from the sieve tubes into the cells that use or store it, so water leaves and the pressure there is lower.
[bio30-s3.6.3] 4. The sap flows along the sieve tubes, through the sieve plates (the perforated walls between sieve tube cells), from the high-pressure source to the low-pressure sink, carrying the dissolved sucrose and amino acids with it.
[bio30-s3.6.4] Diagram: a plant outline with a leaf labelled source, and a root tip, a fruit and a storage organ labelled sink, with phloem arrows for sucrose moving up and down. Beside it, a sieve tube between source and sink cells: sucrose loaded, water entering by osmosis, flow, sucrose unloaded.
[bio30-s3.7.b] We can't watch sucrose moving inside a sieve tube, so the idea that the phloem carries food rests on experiments and observations. Three pieces of evidence all point to the phloem rather than the xylem.
[bio30-s3.7.0] Ringing, or girdling: removing a complete ring of bark from a woody stem removes the phloem, which lies just inside the bark, but leaves the xylem, the wood, intact.
[bio30-s3.7.1] The bark just above the ring swells as sugars build up there, and the tissues below the ring, including the roots, are starved of food. If the roots run out of food, the whole tree dies.
[bio30-s3.7.2] The leaves above the ring do not wilt at first, because the xylem still carries water up.
[bio30-s3.7.3] Animals that strip a ring of bark from trees, such as deer, rabbits and grey squirrels, can kill them in this way. Foresters protect young trees with tree guards.
[bio30-s3.7.4] Aphids are small insects that feed by pushing their needle-like mouthparts into a single sieve tube. The sap that flows into them is rich in sucrose and amino acids.
[bio30-s3.7.5] If an aphid's body is cut away, the sap keeps oozing out of its mouthparts, which shows that the sap is under pressure in the sieve tube.
[bio30-s3.7.6] Radioactive tracers: if a leaf is given CO₂ containing radioactive carbon (¹⁴CO₂), the radioactive carbon soon appears in sucrose, first in the phloem of the leaf, then in the phloem of the stem and in the sinks, and not in the xylem.
[bio30-s3.8.b] Plants move three different kinds of material by three different mechanisms. Comparing them side by side shows where students most often mix them up.
[bio30-s3.8.0] What moves: water and minerals move in the xylem; sucrose and amino acids move in the phloem; CO₂ and oxygen move by diffusion through air spaces, in neither tissue.
[bio30-s3.8.1] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only; phloem moves food up or down, from source to sink; CO₂ moves down its concentration gradient, into the leaf by day and out of it at night.
[bio30-s3.8.2] Driving force: for water, evaporation from the leaves (the transpiration pull), helped at Higher Level by root pressure; for sucrose, active loading at the source and the pressure difference between source and sink; for CO₂, diffusion down a concentration gradient.
[bio30-s3.8.3] Energy used by the plant: none to lift water, which is driven by the sun's energy; ATP for loading sucrose and for absorbing minerals; none for CO₂ diffusion.

CANDIDATES bio30-s4: CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata
[bio30-s4.0.b] Stomata are the tiny pores in a leaf's surface through which CO₂ enters and water vapour escapes. Their width is not fixed: the plant adjusts it in response to several signals, and the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is one of them.
[bio30-s4.0.0] The concentration of CO₂ in the air spaces inside the leaf (the gaps between the cells) is one of the factors that controls the width of the stomatal pores, along with light and water supply.
[bio30-s4.0.1] A low concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf causes the stomata to open, or to open wider, so that more CO₂ can diffuse in.
[bio30-s4.0.2] A high concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf causes the stomata to close, or partly close, so less gas passes through the pore.
[bio30-s4.0.3] Each stoma is surrounded by two guard cells, which change shape to open or close the pore. Guard cells respond to the CO₂ concentration around them, so the response is local to each stoma.
[bio30-s4.0.4] Low CO₂ leads to potassium ions (K⁺) being taken into the guard cells, water entering by osmosis and the guard cells becoming turgid (swollen and firm), so the pore opens.
[bio30-s4.0.5] High CO₂ leads to K⁺ leaving the guard cells, water following by osmosis and the guard cells becoming flaccid (limp), so the pore closes.
[bio30-s4.0.6] The response is to the concentration inside the leaf, which depends on the balance between photosynthesis, which uses CO₂, and respiration, which releases it. It is not simply the concentration in the outside air.
[bio30-s4.1.b] The CO₂ response is useful because the CO₂ level inside a leaf changes with the balance between photosynthesis and respiration, so the stomata open when CO₂ is needed and close when it is not.
[bio30-s4.1.0] In the light, photosynthesis uses CO₂ faster than respiration produces it, so the CO₂ concentration in the air spaces falls. The low CO₂ opens the stomata, letting in more CO₂ for photosynthesis exactly when it is needed.
[bio30-s4.1.1] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, but respiration continues, so CO₂ builds up in the air spaces. The high CO₂ closes the stomata.
[bio30-s4.1.2] The plant does not need CO₂ in the dark, so closing the stomata saves water that would otherwise be lost by transpiration (evaporation of water vapour through the open stomata).
[bio30-s4.1.3] This is a negative feedback loop (a change sets off a response that reverses the change). When internal CO₂ is low, the stomata open and CO₂ enters, so its concentration rises; as it rises, the stomata begin to close.
[bio30-s4.1.4] CO₂ concentration therefore helps match the opening of the stomata to the leaf's demand for CO₂, keeping the balance between taking in CO₂ and losing water. There is more on negative feedback in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio30-s4.1.5] The CO₂ response works together with the other controls. Light opens the stomata directly as well as by lowering CO₂ through photosynthesis.
[bio30-s4.1.6] Shortage of water closes the stomata even when CO₂ is low, because preventing wilting takes priority over photosynthesis. So on a hot, dry afternoon the stomata may close although the leaf needs CO₂, and photosynthesis slows.
[bio30-s4.1.7] Experimental evidence: if a leaf in the light is given air with extra CO₂, its stomata close partly. If a leaf in the dark is given CO₂-free air, its stomata open partly. This shows that CO₂ acts on the stomata on its own, separately from light.
[bio30-s4.2.b] Stomata are the main route by which a leaf loses water, so anything that changes their width also changes the rate of transpiration. This links CO₂ to water loss, and to wider questions about farming and climate.
[bio30-s4.2.0] Because CO₂ controls the width of the stomata, it affects transpiration. Raising the CO₂ concentration around a leaf closes the stomata partly and reduces the rate of transpiration; lowering it opens them and increases the rate.
[bio30-s4.2.1] This is why CO₂ concentration is one of the factors in Investigation: rate of transpiration.
[bio30-s4.2.2] Sustainability link: the CO₂ concentration of the atmosphere is rising, mainly from burning fossil fuels. Plants grown in raised CO₂ tend to open their stomata less, so they lose less water per unit of sugar made.
[bio30-s4.2.3] Leaves of the same species collected over the last two centuries and kept in herbarium collections (preserved plant specimens) show fewer stomata per unit area as CO₂ has risen.
[bio30-s4.2.4] Technology link: glasshouse growers add CO₂ to raise photosynthesis. The extra CO₂ also partly closes the stomata, so the plants lose less water.
[bio30-s4.3.b] Plants and humans both use CO₂ concentration to control gas exchange, but they respond in opposite ways. Comparing them shows that the difference comes from what CO₂ means to each organism.
[bio30-s4.3.0] What is detected, in plants: the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf, detected by the guard cells themselves. Plants have no nerves, so the response is local, in each stoma.
[bio30-s4.3.1] What is detected, in humans: the CO₂ concentration of the blood, through its effect on blood pH. Chemoreceptors (cells that detect chemical change) in the medulla oblongata and in the carotid and aortic bodies detect it and signal the breathing centre in the medulla.
[bio30-s4.3.2] Response to high CO₂: in plants the stomata close, reducing gas exchange. In humans the rate and depth of breathing increase, increasing gas exchange.
[bio30-s4.3.3] Response to low CO₂: in plants the stomata open. In humans breathing slows.
[bio30-s4.3.4] Why the responses are opposite, in plants: for a plant in the light, CO₂ is a raw material for photosynthesis, so low internal CO₂ signals that more is needed, and high internal CO₂ signals that none is being used, so water should be saved.
[bio30-s4.3.5] Why the responses are opposite, in humans: CO₂ is a waste product of respiration that makes the blood more acidic, so high CO₂ signals that more must be removed.
[bio30-s4.3.6] What is similar: in both, CO₂ concentration is the main controlling signal for gas exchange, and the control works by negative feedback, returning the CO₂ level towards normal.
[bio30-s4.3.7] In both, the controlled structures adjust how much gas passes across the exchange surface: the stomata in plants, and the diaphragm and intercostal muscles that ventilate the alveoli in humans. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.

CANDIDATES bio30-s5: Investigation: rate of transpiration
[bio30-s5.0.b] Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mostly through its leaves. The rate of transpiration is the volume, or mass, of water lost by a plant per unit time. It depends on how steep the water vapour concentration gradient is between the air spaces of the leaf, which are saturated with water vapour, and the outside air; how fast water evaporates; and how wide the stomata (the pores in the leaf surface) are open.
[bio30-s5.0.0] Air movement: wind blows away the layer of humid air that builds up around the leaf, keeping the outside air dry and the gradient steep, so the rate increases. In still air the humid layer builds up, the gradient flattens and the rate falls.
[bio30-s5.0.1] Temperature: warmer water molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), so they evaporate from the mesophyll cell walls (the moist walls of the cells inside the leaf) and diffuse out faster. Warm air can also hold more water vapour, so the gradient is steeper. The rate increases with temperature.
[bio30-s5.0.2] At very high temperatures the plant may begin to wilt and close its stomata, which stops water vapour escaping, and then the rate falls.
[bio30-s5.0.3] Surface area: more leaves, or larger leaves, mean more stomata and more evaporating surface, so the rate increases roughly in proportion to the leaf area.
[bio30-s5.0.4] Cuticle (the waxy covering of the leaf) is waterproof, so only a little water is lost through it; most is lost through the stomata. A thick cuticle means less loss than a thin one. A surface coated with an artificial waterproof layer, such as petroleum jelly, loses almost none.
[bio30-s5.0.5] Light intensity: stomata open in the light and close in darkness, so the rate increases with light intensity, until the stomata are fully open, and is very low in darkness. A lamp also warms the leaf, which raises the rate further unless the temperature is controlled.
[bio30-s5.0.6] CO₂ concentration: a high concentration of CO₂ around the leaf partly closes the stomata, so the rate falls; a low concentration opens them wider, so the rate rises. The Higher Level explanation is in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s5.0.7] Humidity, not named by the specification but controlled when the other factors are tested: humid air reduces the gradient, so the rate falls. This matters whenever a shoot is enclosed in a plastic bag, as in the CO₂ method, because the air in the bag becomes humid.
[bio30-s5.1.b] A good investigation changes one factor at a time, so that any change in the result can be blamed on that one factor. Planning starts with an aim and a hypothesis (a testable prediction).
[bio30-s5.1.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of one factor (air movement, temperature, surface area, cuticle, light intensity or CO₂ concentration) on the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot. Change one factor per investigation.
[bio30-s5.1.1] Example hypotheses, each resting on the theory above: “Increasing air movement increases the rate of transpiration”; “The rate of transpiration increases as temperature increases”; “The rate increases as leaf area increases”.
[bio30-s5.1.2] More example hypotheses: “Covering the lower surface of a leaf reduces water loss more than covering the upper surface”; “The rate increases as light intensity increases”; “Increasing the CO₂ concentration around a shoot reduces its rate of transpiration”.
[bio30-s5.1.3] The potometer (the apparatus used to measure uptake) measures water uptake, not water loss. The two are almost equal, because nearly all the water a shoot takes up is lost by transpiration.
[bio30-s5.1.4] Only a tiny amount of the water taken up is used in photosynthesis or to keep the cells turgid (firm and full of water). The mass-loss method measures water loss directly.
[bio30-s5.2.b] Once the aim is set, name what you change, what you measure and what you keep the same, and say what each comparison is checked against.
[bio30-s5.2.0] Independent variable (the one factor you change), using light intensity as the example: the lamp distance, or the light meter reading in lux. For the other factors, the independent variable is swapped and everything else is kept constant.
[bio30-s5.2.1] Dependent variable (the one you measure), using light intensity as the example: the rate of water uptake, which is the distance moved by the air bubble per minute, converted to a volume per minute.
[bio30-s5.2.2] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair), using light intensity as the example: the same shoot (same species, same number and size of leaves), temperature (a thermometer by the shoot and a heat shield), air movement (no fans, windows closed).
[bio30-s5.2.3] More controlled variables: humidity (the same room), CO₂ (the same room air), the time allowed to adjust, and the length of each timing.
[bio30-s5.2.4] Controls: a baseline reading for the same shoot in normal room conditions, still air, room temperature and normal light, to compare each change against. A leafless stem in the potometer, with all leaves removed and the cut surfaces sealed, shows that almost all the water is lost through the leaves.
[bio30-s5.2.5] Two more controls: for the cuticle investigation the control is an untreated leaf, and for the CO₂ investigation it is an identical bag with no CO₂ added or removed, so that any effect of the bag itself is allowed for.
[bio30-s5.3.b] The same basic kit serves every factor, with a few extra items added for particular methods. Know what each item is for, because exams ask you to name the apparatus and describe the set-up.
[bio30-s5.3.0] Bubble potometer: a capillary tube (a very narrow tube) with a scale, a reservoir or syringe with a tap for resetting the bubble, and rubber tubing or a bung to hold the shoot.
[bio30-s5.3.1] Other apparatus: clamp stand; a leafy shoot of a woody shrub with a stiff stem, e.g. laurel or privet; sharp knife or scalpel; a basin or sink of water; petroleum jelly (Vaseline); paper towels.
[bio30-s5.3.2] Measuring and fair-test apparatus: stopwatch; ruler; thermometer; bench lamp (LED preferred) and metre stick; light meter (optional); electric fan; heat shield, such as a flat-sided tank or beaker of water.
[bio30-s5.3.3] For the CO₂ method: a clear plastic bag with string or tape; soda lime, to absorb CO₂; and a CO₂ source, either a flask of marble chips with dilute hydrochloric acid connected by tubing, or a CO₂ cylinder.
[bio30-s5.3.4] For the cuticle and surface-area methods: graph paper, for measuring leaf area; and, for the alternative methods, an electronic balance, cobalt chloride paper, forceps and clear tape.
[bio30-s5.4.b] The first stage is to build a potometer that is full of water, airtight and free of air bubbles. If the water column is broken or the joints leak, the readings are meaningless.
[bio30-s5.4.0] 1. Cut a leafy shoot from the plant, then immediately recut the stem at a slant under water, a few centimetres further up, so that no air enters the xylem (the vessels that carry water up the stem).
[bio30-s5.4.1] An air bubble in the xylem would break the water columns, so the shoot could not draw water up. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s5.4.2] 2. Submerge the potometer in the basin of water so that it fills completely, with no air bubbles.
[bio30-s5.4.3] 3. Fit the shoot into the rubber tubing or bung under water, so that the joint is airtight.
[bio30-s5.4.4] 4. Take the assembled potometer out of the water and clamp it upright, with the capillary tube horizontal.
[bio30-s5.4.5] 5. Seal all joints with petroleum jelly to prevent leaks.
[bio30-s5.4.6] 6. Gently dry the leaves with a paper towel, since water on their surface would evaporate and add to the loss.
[bio30-s5.5.b] Once the apparatus is ready, you introduce a single air bubble and use its movement along the scale to measure how fast the shoot takes up water.
[bio30-s5.5.0] 7. Introduce one air bubble into the capillary tube by lifting its open end out of the water for a moment, then return it to a beaker of water.
[bio30-s5.5.1] 8. Leave the shoot for about 5 minutes to adjust to the conditions, so that the readings reflect the present conditions and not the previous ones.
[bio30-s5.5.2] 9. Record the starting position of the bubble on the scale, start the stopwatch, and record the distance the bubble moves towards the shoot in a set time, e.g. 5 minutes. The bubble moves towards the shoot because the shoot is drawing water out of the tube.
[bio30-s5.5.3] 10. Return the bubble to the start of the scale by opening the reservoir tap, or by pushing water in from the syringe.
[bio30-s5.5.4] 11. Repeat at least three times under the same conditions, and calculate the mean, so that one odd reading does not distort the result.
[bio30-s5.5.5] Diagram: a bubble potometer clamped on a stand. Label the leafy shoot sealed in with petroleum jelly, water-filled tube, reservoir with tap, horizontal capillary tube with scale, air bubble, beaker of water at the open end; draw an arrow for the bubble's direction.
[bio30-s5.6.b] Bubble movement is only a distance, so it has to be turned into a rate, and sometimes into a volume. These worked examples show each calculation step by step.
[bio30-s5.6.0] Rate of water uptake = distance moved by the bubble ÷ time, e.g. in mm per minute. Use this when all the readings use the same potometer, because then the capillary tube is the same and distances can be compared directly.
[bio30-s5.6.1] To give a volume, use the volume of a cylinder (the water in the capillary tube forms a cylinder): volume = πr² × distance moved, where r is the radius of the capillary tube.
[bio30-s5.6.2] Worked example: radius 0.5 mm, bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. Volume = 3.14 × 0.5² × 30 = 3.14 × 0.25 × 30 = 23.6 mm³ in 5 minutes, so the rate is 23.6 ÷ 5 = 4.7 mm³ per minute.
[bio30-s5.6.3] To compare shoots of different sizes, divide by the leaf area, because a bigger shoot loses more water simply by having more leaf. Draw round each leaf on 1 cm² graph paper, count the squares that are at least half covered, and add up the total for all the leaves.
[bio30-s5.6.4] Worked example: 4.7 mm³ per minute ÷ 50 cm² = 0.094 mm³ per minute per cm².
[bio30-s5.6.5] Mass-loss method: rate = mass lost ÷ time, e.g. in g per hour. Percentage mass loss = (mass lost ÷ starting mass) × 100, which allows leaves of different sizes to be compared.
[bio30-s5.6.6] Worked example: a leaf of 2.0 g that weighs 1.5 g after 24 hours has lost 0.5 g, which is 25%.
[bio30-s5.7.b] Here the independent variable is how much air moves past the shoot, set with an electric fan. The idea is to test whether moving air keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio30-s5.7.0] 1. Take the baseline reading in still air, as in the potometer setup.
[bio30-s5.7.1] 2. Place an electric fan at a fixed distance, e.g. 1 m, from the shoot on its lowest setting. Wait 5 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.7.2] 3. Repeat on the medium and highest settings, or with the fan at decreasing distances.
[bio30-s5.7.3] 4. Check that the fan does not change the temperature by reading the thermometer near the shoot, because a change in temperature would also change the rate.
[bio30-s5.7.4] Expected: the rate increases as air movement increases, because moving air carries away the humid layer around the leaf.
[bio30-s5.8.b] Temperature is changed by carrying out the readings in places that are at different, steady temperatures. The main difficulty is warming the shoot without also moving the air or changing the light.
[bio30-s5.8.0] 1. Take readings with the potometer in places at different, steady temperatures, e.g. a cool room, the laboratory and a warm room, measuring the air temperature beside the shoot with a thermometer each time.
[bio30-s5.8.1] 2. Allow the shoot about 10 minutes to reach each temperature before taking three readings.
[bio30-s5.8.2] 3. Do not use a fan heater blowing on the shoot, because it changes air movement as well as temperature. If a heater is used, place it at a distance, behind a heat shield.
[bio30-s5.8.3] 4. Keep the light intensity the same in each place, with the same lamp and no direct sunlight, because light also affects the rate.
[bio30-s5.8.4] Expected: the rate increases with temperature.
[bio30-s5.9.b] Here the independent variable is the total leaf area of the shoot, which is reduced stage by stage by removing leaves. Because each leaf carries stomata, less leaf means less surface to lose water from.
[bio30-s5.9.0] 1. Take readings with all the leaves on the shoot.
[bio30-s5.9.1] 2. Remove one or two leaves by cutting them off at the base of the leaf stalk, seal the cut surfaces with petroleum jelly so that no water escapes there, and take new readings.
[bio30-s5.9.2] 3. Repeat until no leaves remain.
[bio30-s5.9.3] 4. Measure the area of each removed leaf on graph paper, as in the calculation method, so that the total leaf area left at each stage is known. Alternatively, compare shoots with different total leaf areas.
[bio30-s5.9.4] Expected: the rate falls as the leaf area falls, roughly in proportion, and the leafless stem gives almost no movement of the bubble. Plot the rate against the leaf area.
[bio30-s5.10.b] This investigation compares leaves with and without a waterproof coating. Petroleum jelly acts as an artificial, thicker cuticle, so coating different surfaces shows where water is really lost.
[bio30-s5.10.0] 1. Pick four similar leaves of the same size from the same plant.
[bio30-s5.10.1] 2. Leave leaf A untreated, as the control. Coat the upper surface of leaf B with a thin layer of petroleum jelly, coat the lower surface of leaf C, and coat both surfaces of leaf D. The petroleum jelly acts as an artificial, thicker waterproof cuticle.
[bio30-s5.10.2] 3. Seal the cut end of each leaf stalk with petroleum jelly.
[bio30-s5.10.3] 4. Weigh each leaf on an electronic balance.
[bio30-s5.10.4] 5. Hang the leaves on a string across the room, in the same conditions.
[bio30-s5.10.5] 6. Reweigh each leaf every day for several days, and calculate the percentage mass loss. The loss in mass is the water lost.
[bio30-s5.10.6] Expected results: A loses the most mass. B loses nearly as much as A, because its upper surface has a thick cuticle and few stomata. C loses much less, because the stomata on its lower surface are blocked. D loses almost none.
[bio30-s5.10.7] Conclusion: the cuticle greatly reduces water loss, and most water is lost through the stomata on the lower surface.
[bio30-s5.11.b] The cuticle comparison can also be run on a potometer shoot instead of on separate hanging leaves, which gives a rate of water uptake rather than a mass loss.
[bio30-s5.11.0] Variation using the potometer: coat the upper surfaces, then the lower surfaces, of all the leaves on the potometer shoot, and take readings after each, compared with the untreated reading.
[bio30-s5.11.1] Alternative: compare shoots of plants with a thick, shiny waxy cuticle, such as laurel, and plants with thin, soft leaves of similar area.
[bio30-s5.12.b] Cobalt chloride paper is a quick way to show that water vapour leaves a leaf, and from which surface. It supports the main methods rather than replacing them.
[bio30-s5.12.0] Dry cobalt chloride paper is blue, and it turns pink when it absorbs water.
[bio30-s5.12.1] Using forceps, place a small square on the upper surface and one on the lower surface of a leaf still on the plant, and cover each with clear tape so that the paper only picks up water vapour from the leaf.
[bio30-s5.12.2] Time how long each square takes to turn pink.
[bio30-s5.12.3] Expected: the paper on the lower surface turns pink first, showing that more water vapour leaves through the lower surface, where most of the stomata are.
[bio30-s5.13.b] Light intensity is varied by moving a lamp to different distances from the shoot, with a heat shield so that the lamp changes only the light. Light matters because it controls whether the stomata are open.
[bio30-s5.13.0] 1. Work in a darkened room, so that room light does not add to the lamp.
[bio30-s5.13.1] 2. Place a lamp at a set distance from the shoot, e.g. 10 cm, with a heat shield between them. Wait 5 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.13.2] 3. Repeat at 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, measuring the distance from bulb to shoot with a metre stick. Alternatively, use a light meter, since intensity does not fall in simple proportion to distance. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of photosynthesis.
[bio30-s5.13.3] 4. Take a final set of readings in darkness, with the lamp off and the shoot covered with a black bag or box.
[bio30-s5.13.4] 5. Check the thermometer beside the shoot at each distance, because the lamp can warm the shoot.
[bio30-s5.13.5] Expected: the rate is highest with the lamp closest, and very low in darkness, when the stomata close.
[bio30-s5.14.b] CO₂ is varied by enclosing the shoot in a clear plastic bag and either adding CO₂ or absorbing it. The bag causes its own problem, because the air inside becomes humid, so the method needs a control bag.
[bio30-s5.14.0] 1. Take readings with the shoot in a clear plastic bag sealed loosely around the stem, with nothing added. This is the control for the effect of the bag and its humidity.
[bio30-s5.14.1] 2. Raised CO₂: pass CO₂ into the bag through a tube from a flask of marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid, or from a CO₂ cylinder. Tie the bag, wait 10 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.14.2] 3. Lowered CO₂: place a small open dish of soda lime, which absorbs CO₂, inside the bag, not touching the leaves. Tie the bag, wait 10 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.14.3] 4. Keep the shoot in bright light throughout, at a constant temperature, and keep each run short, because humidity builds up inside the bag.
[bio30-s5.14.4] Expected: raised CO₂ lowers the rate, and lowered CO₂ raises it slightly. The effect is smaller and harder to detect than the effects of light or air movement, so careful repeats and comparison with secondary data are needed.
[bio30-s5.15.b] This investigation combines sharp blades, glass, electricity near water and some hazardous chemicals. Each hazard has a simple precaution.
[bio30-s5.15.0] Cut away from the body on a tile, and take care with the knife or scalpel. Glass capillary tubes break easily, so handle them gently and report breakages.
[bio30-s5.15.1] Keep the lamp, the fan and their cables away from the basin and the potometer, and dry your hands before touching switches, because water and electricity are a dangerous mixture. Keep fingers clear of the fan blades.
[bio30-s5.15.2] Lamp bulbs, except LEDs, get very hot, so let them cool before moving them.
[bio30-s5.15.3] Cobalt chloride paper is toxic and can cause cancer. Handle it only with forceps, wear gloves, and wash your hands afterwards.
[bio30-s5.15.4] Soda lime is corrosive, because it contains sodium hydroxide. Wear safety glasses and gloves, and do not touch it. Dilute hydrochloric acid is an irritant, so wear safety glasses.
[bio30-s5.15.5] Wash hands after handling plants, since some, such as laurel, are poisonous if eaten, and after handling petroleum jelly.
[bio30-s5.16.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. They only become evidence when they are tabulated, graphed and explained using the theory of how each factor works.
[bio30-s5.16.0] Record the primary data in a table: the independent variable (e.g. lamp distance in cm, fan setting, temperature in °C or leaf area in cm²), the three readings of distance moved in the set time, the mean, and the rate in mm per minute or mm³ per minute.
[bio30-s5.16.1] For the cuticle investigation, record the starting mass, each day's mass and the percentage mass loss for each leaf.
[bio30-s5.16.2] Plot the mean rate on the y-axis against the independent variable on the x-axis, with units. Use a bar chart for categories, such as fan settings, treatments A to D, or still and moving air, and a line graph for continuous variables, such as temperature, leaf area or light intensity.
[bio30-s5.16.3] Expected patterns: the rate rises with air movement, temperature, leaf area and light intensity. It falls with a thick or artificial cuticle, especially on the lower surface, and with raised CO₂.
[bio30-s5.16.4] At high light intensity the rate levels off once the stomata are fully open, because they cannot open any wider.
[bio30-s5.16.5] Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data, explain it using the theory of how each factor works (gradient, evaporation and stomatal opening), and say whether it supports the hypothesis. Use the mean of the repeats, and compare with other groups' results.
[bio30-s5.16.6] Diagram: four leaves hanging on a string, labelled A untreated, B upper surface coated, C lower surface coated and D both surfaces coated, with a bar chart of the expected percentage mass loss, with A highest, B slightly lower, C much lower and D lowest.
[bio30-s5.17.b] Secondary data are results collected by someone else. They let you test your conclusions against a larger body of evidence, and they are especially useful where a school laboratory cannot show an effect clearly.
[bio30-s5.17.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, such as other groups' class results, published graphs and tables, weather data (temperature, humidity and wind speed from a weather station or Met Éireann, Ireland's national weather service), or results from electronic sensors and data loggers measuring humidity or mass loss.
[bio30-s5.17.1] Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility (whether other people get the same results).
[bio30-s5.17.2] Interpreting a graph of transpiration over 24 hours: the rate is very low at night, when the stomata are closed, and the air is often cooler and still. It rises after dawn as the stomata open, light increases and the air warms.
[bio30-s5.17.3] It peaks around the early afternoon, when light and temperature are highest and the air is driest, and it falls in the evening.
[bio30-s5.17.4] On a hot, dry afternoon the graph may dip if the plant begins to wilt and closes its stomata.
[bio30-s5.17.5] Because the effect of CO₂ is small in a school laboratory, published results from controlled-atmosphere experiments can be used for the CO₂ factor. These are plants grown at different CO₂ concentrations, with all other conditions fixed, and they support the conclusion that higher CO₂ reduces stomatal opening and transpiration.
[bio30-s5.17.6] Compare primary and secondary data by checking whether your results show the same trend as the published data. If they differ, look for an uncontrolled variable or a source of error, and evaluate the sources for reliability. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio30-s5.18.b] Every method has weaknesses that can make results unreliable. For each one, know both the problem and the fix.
[bio30-s5.18.0] The potometer measures water uptake, not water loss, and a small amount of the water taken up is used by the plant, so uptake is slightly higher than the true loss.
[bio30-s5.18.1] Air leaks at the joints, or air entering the cut stem, stop or slow the bubble and give a falsely low rate. Cut the stem under water and seal the joints with petroleum jelly.
[bio30-s5.18.2] Changing one factor often changes another: a lamp heats the shoot, a fan or heater moves air, and a plastic bag raises humidity. Use a heat shield, keep heaters away, use a control bag and check the thermometer.
[bio30-s5.18.3] Room conditions, such as temperature, humidity, sunlight through windows and people moving about, change during a lesson. Take readings close together in time, and repeat them.
[bio30-s5.18.4] Too little time to adjust after each change gives a rate that still reflects the previous condition. Wait about 5 to 10 minutes.
[bio30-s5.18.5] A cut shoot behaves differently from a whole plant and may wilt during a long investigation. Use a healthy shoot and a short investigation, or a fresh shoot for each run, and measure its leaf area.
[bio30-s5.18.6] Reading the bubble position by eye on a scale is imprecise. Read at eye level and repeat at least three times, calculating a mean.
