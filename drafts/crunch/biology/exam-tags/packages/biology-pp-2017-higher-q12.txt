QUESTION biology-pp-2017-higher-q12 (LC Biology Higher 2017 — Section C — Q12, 60 marks)

PART (a) (9 marks) subsections: bio35-s3, bio35-s2
MODEL ANSWER: Extract the DNA from cells (e.g. blood or cheek cells). Cut the DNA into fragments with restriction enzymes. Separate the fragments by size using gel electrophoresis: the fragments are placed in wells in a gel and an electric current is applied, so the negatively charged DNA moves towards the positive electrode, with the smallest fragments moving furthest. The pattern of bands is the DNA profile.

PART (b)(i) (9 marks) subsections: bio12-s2, bio31-s5
MODEL ANSWER: Organisms produce more offspring than can survive, but resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete in a struggle for existence. Those best adapted (best suited) to the environment survive and reproduce; those not suited die before reproducing. The number of deaths therefore balances the number of births (death rate equals reproduction rate), so the population stays roughly constant.

PART (b)(ii) (18 marks) subsections: bio11-s11, bio11-s10
MODEL ANSWER: Haemophilia is a recessive, sex-linked (X-linked) condition. Father is haemophiliac: XnY (Xn = haemophilia allele, XN = normal allele). A son gets his Y from his father's sperm and his X from his mother's egg. The son is not haemophiliac, so he is XNY, and he got XN from his mother's egg. Mother must therefore be XNXN (normal) or XNXn (carrier). Cross: mother XNXn (or XNXN) x father XnY. Gametes: mother XN, Xn (or XN only); father Xn, Y. Son (male, XY) gets Y from the father's sperm and XN from the mother's egg, giving XNY, a normal son.

PART (c)(i) (6 marks) subsections: bio10-s1
MODEL ANSWER: Adenine and guanine.

PART (c)(ii) (12 marks) subsections: bio17-s2
MODEL ANSWER: mRNA gets (copies) the genetic code from the DNA (gene) in the nucleus. mRNA carries this code to the ribosome. rRNA, together with protein, forms the ribosome (its two subunits) and holds the mRNA and tRNAs in position. tRNA carries a specific amino acid to the ribosome, where its anticodon pairs with the complementary codon on the mRNA, so it translates the mRNA code into the amino acid sequence.

PART (c)(iii) (6 marks) subsections: bio17-s2, bio10-s2
MODEL ANSWER: Folding: the polypeptide chain folds into its particular three-dimensional shape (held by bonds between amino acids), which gives the protein its function.

CANDIDATES bio10-s1: Nucleotides, DNA and RNA
[bio10-s1.0.b] Cells need a way to store instructions for building and running themselves. They use nucleic acids, long molecules made by joining many small units, and the unit that is repeated is the nucleotide. There are four kinds of nucleotide, which differ in their base.
[bio10-s1.0.0] Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are large molecules (polymers) built from many small units called nucleotides joined in a chain, much as beads are threaded on a string.
[bio10-s1.0.1] DNA is deoxyribonucleic acid; RNA is ribonucleic acid. Both contain the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus.
[bio10-s1.0.2] Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base (a nitrogen-containing molecule) attached to the sugar.
[bio10-s1.0.3] The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen atom than ribose, which is where its name comes from: deoxy means an oxygen has been removed.
[bio10-s1.0.4] Each nucleic acid has four bases. DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). RNA: adenine (A), uracil (U), cytosine (C) and guanine (G); uracil takes the place of thymine.
[bio10-s1.0.5] Adenine and guanine are larger, double-ringed bases, called purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are smaller, single-ringed bases, called pyrimidines.
[bio10-s1.0.6] A nucleotide is the building block of DNA and RNA, and its base is one letter of the genetic information. The order, or sequence, of bases along a strand carries the information, just as the order of letters makes a word.
[bio10-s1.0.7] Modified nucleotides also carry energy. There is more on this in ATP.
[bio10-s1.0.8] Diagram: one nucleotide drawn as a circle (phosphate) joined to a pentagon (sugar) joined to a rectangle (base), labelled phosphate, deoxyribose or ribose, and nitrogenous base.
[bio10-s1.1.b] One nucleotide holds one letter of information, so to store a whole message the cell must link many of them together. The way they link leaves a strong chain with the bases free to carry the code.
[bio10-s1.1.0] Nucleotides join by bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next. These bonds are made in condensation reactions, in which a molecule of water is removed for each bond.
[bio10-s1.1.1] The repeating sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate chain is the sugar–phosphate backbone. The bonds within it are strong covalent bonds, not hydrogen bonds.
[bio10-s1.1.2] The bases stick out from the backbone, one per sugar. The backbone is the same in every DNA molecule; only the sequence of bases differs, so the bases carry the information and the backbone gives strength and support.
[bio10-s1.2.b] DNA is not one chain but two, held together in a regular pattern. This shape explains how DNA can store information and be copied accurately.
[bio10-s1.2.0] DNA is double-stranded: two strands of nucleotides lie side by side, with the sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside and the bases pointing inwards, like the rails and rungs of a ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.1] The bases pair up: adenine always pairs with thymine (A–T) and cytosine always pairs with guanine (C–G). These are complementary base pairs.
[bio10-s1.2.2] Each pair is one purine with one pyrimidine, so every rung is the same width and the ladder stays an even shape.
[bio10-s1.2.3] The bases in a pair are held together by hydrogen bonds (weak attractions between molecules): two between A and T, and three between C and G.
[bio10-s1.2.4] Each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated, or unzipped, when DNA is copied or read. There are millions of hydrogen bonds, so the molecule as a whole is stable.
[bio10-s1.2.5] The two linked strands are twisted around each other into a spiral called a double helix, like a twisted ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.6] Because pairing is fixed, the two strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand decides the sequence of the other. For example, the strand ATGCCTAG pairs with TACGGATC.
[bio10-s1.2.7] Diagram: a short section of DNA drawn as a straight ladder of about four base pairs, labelled sugar (deoxyribose), phosphate, sugar–phosphate backbone, base pairs A–T and C–G, and hydrogen bonds; with a sketch of the twisted double helix.
[bio10-s1.3.b] Because A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G, the amounts of the bases in double-stranded DNA are linked. This lets you work out the percentage of every base from just one.
[bio10-s1.3.0] In double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of C equals the amount of G, because every A is paired with a T and every C with a G.
[bio10-s1.3.1] For example, if 20% of the bases in a DNA sample are adenine, then 20% are thymine. That leaves 60% shared equally between cytosine and guanine, so each is 30%.
[bio10-s1.4.b] The order of bases in DNA is the cell's set of instructions. DNA is kept in different places in different kinds of cell.
[bio10-s1.4.0] DNA stores the genetic information (genes) that controls the cell's activities by coding for proteins. There is more on genes and proteins in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio10-s1.4.1] DNA is copied before a cell divides, so each new cell receives the full set of information, which means that it is passed from parents to offspring.
[bio10-s1.4.2] In eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), DNA is found mostly in the nucleus, in the chromosomes, with small amounts in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio10-s1.4.3] In bacteria, DNA forms a loop in the cytoplasm and is also found in small rings called plasmids.
[bio10-s1.5.b] The structure of DNA was worked out by combining physical evidence with model building. It is a good example of how scientists build on each other's data.
[bio10-s1.5.0] The double-helix model was published by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953. It was based on X-ray diffraction images of DNA (patterns made when X-rays pass through DNA, which reveal its shape) made by Rosalind Franklin, working with Maurice Wilkins.
[bio10-s1.6.b] DNA stays safe in the nucleus, so the cell needs a working copy of its instructions to take out and use. That working copy is RNA, a similar molecule with a few key differences.
[bio10-s1.6.0] RNA is usually single-stranded: one chain of nucleotides with its own sugar–phosphate backbone. RNA molecules are much shorter than DNA molecules.
[bio10-s1.6.1] RNA nucleotides contain ribose instead of deoxyribose, and uracil instead of thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine (A–U) whenever RNA pairs with DNA or with another RNA.
[bio10-s1.6.2] A single strand can fold back on itself where its bases pair up, giving some RNA molecules a fixed shape.
[bio10-s1.6.3] RNA uses the information in DNA to make proteins, so it acts as the go-between for DNA and the protein-making machinery.
[bio10-s1.6.4] Three main types of RNA are named here, with no more detail. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries a copy of a gene from the nucleus to a ribosome (the structure that makes proteins).
[bio10-s1.6.5] Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids to the ribosome; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms part of the ribosome. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s1.6.6] RNA is made in the nucleus and works in the cytoplasm, so it carries information out to where proteins are made. It is found in the nucleus (including the nucleolus), the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.6.7] Diagram: a short single RNA strand of about four nucleotides, labelled ribose, phosphate, backbone and bases, including uracil.
[bio10-s1.7.b] Put side by side, DNA and RNA differ in structure, stability and job. The differences fit their roles: DNA is a long-term store and RNA is a short-lived working copy.
[bio10-s1.7.0] Strands: DNA is a double-stranded helix, which suits long-term storage; RNA is usually single-stranded.
[bio10-s1.7.1] Sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose; RNA contains ribose.
[bio10-s1.7.2] Bases: DNA has A, T, C and G; RNA has A, U, C and G, with uracil instead of thymine.
[bio10-s1.7.3] Pairing: A–T in DNA; A–U in RNA.
[bio10-s1.7.4] Size and life span: DNA is very long and stable and is kept for the life of the cell; RNA molecules are shorter and are broken down after use, so each copy is only a temporary message.
[bio10-s1.7.5] Location: DNA is mainly in the nucleus, with some in mitochondria and chloroplasts; RNA is in the nucleus, the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.7.6] Function: DNA stores the genetic information; RNA carries and uses that information to make proteins.

CANDIDATES bio10-s2: Genes, proteins and traits
[bio10-s2.0.b] Three words link what is written in DNA to what an organism looks like and does: gene, protein and trait. Getting each one clear is the first step, because exam answers lose marks when they are mixed up.
[bio10-s2.0.0] A gene is a section of DNA that contains the instructions, or code, for making a particular protein (strictly, a polypeptide, which is a chain of amino acids). A gene is not itself a protein: it is the recipe for one.
[bio10-s2.0.1] Genes are carried on chromosomes (the long DNA molecules in the nucleus), and each chromosome holds many genes, so one chromosome carries the instructions for many different proteins.
[bio10-s2.0.2] A protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a particular shape. About 20 different amino acids are used to make proteins, and different orders of them give different proteins.
[bio10-s2.0.3] A trait is an inherited characteristic of an organism, such as eye colour, blood group, seed shape in peas, or whether a person can make a particular enzyme. The trait is not the protein: it is the visible or measurable result of what the protein does.
[bio10-s2.1.b] A gene's code is turned into a working protein in a fixed chain of events. The order of the code decides the protein's shape, and the shape decides what the protein can do.
[bio10-s2.1.0] The sequence of bases in a gene (the order of the chemical letters along the DNA) determines the sequence of amino acids in the protein it codes for. Change the order of the bases and the order of amino acids can change.
[bio10-s2.1.1] The sequence of amino acids determines how the chain folds, and so the protein's three-dimensional shape. This is why every protein folds into its own shape rather than a random tangle.
[bio10-s2.1.2] The shape determines the protein's function, because other molecules can only fit or bind to the right shape. Examples are the active site of an enzyme (the pocket where the reaction happens) and the binding site of a receptor (the spot that a signal molecule attaches to).
[bio10-s2.1.3] The cell's machinery reads the gene. The code is copied into a messenger molecule, mRNA, which travels from the nucleus to a ribosome (a tiny structure that builds proteins), where the amino acids are joined in the correct order.
[bio10-s2.1.4] Information therefore flows in one direction: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein → trait. There is more on how the copying and joining work in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s2.2.b] Proteins are the link between a gene and a trait because they do most of the work in cells and build much of their structure. Different versions of a gene can therefore give different versions of a trait, and different cells use different genes.
[bio10-s2.2.0] Enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions) control the chemical reactions that make substances such as pigments. If the enzyme is missing or faulty, the substance is not made and the trait changes.
[bio10-s2.2.1] Structural proteins build hair, skin, muscle and connective tissue, for example keratin (hair, nails, skin) and collagen (connective tissue, skin, tendons).
[bio10-s2.2.2] Transport and channel proteins move substances, for example across membranes or around the body. Hormones such as insulin, and receptor proteins, carry signals between cells.
[bio10-s2.2.3] Different versions of a gene, called alleles, have slightly different base sequences. They can give a protein with a different amino acid sequence, a different shape, or no working protein at all, which produces a different version of the trait.
[bio10-s2.2.4] Every body cell contains the same genes, but each cell type uses, or expresses, only some of them. For example, the insulin gene is used to make insulin only in cells of the pancreas, and haemoglobin is made only in developing red blood cells.
[bio10-s2.3.b] Four examples show the whole chain in action, each as gene → protein → trait. In each one, a change to the protein changes the trait that you can see.
[bio10-s2.3.0] Albinism (one form): the gene for the enzyme tyrosinase → tyrosinase is needed to make the dark pigment melanin → people with two non-working copies make little or no melanin, so they have very pale skin, white or very fair hair and light-sensitive eyes.
[bio10-s2.3.1] In albinism the gene codes for the enzyme, not for the pigment: the pigment (melanin) is not a protein, and it is missing only because the enzyme that makes it does not work.
[bio10-s2.3.2] Cystic fibrosis: the CFTR gene → the CFTR protein, a channel in cell membranes that lets chloride ions out of cells → a faulty channel causes thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas, leading to lung infections and poor digestion.
[bio10-s2.3.3] Haemoglobin: the haemoglobin genes → haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen → normal oxygen transport. There is more on how the change arises in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s2.3.4] A changed form of one of the haemoglobin genes gives sickle-cell haemoglobin, which makes red blood cells sickle-shaped when oxygen is low. This causes sickle-cell anaemia.
[bio10-s2.3.5] Seed shape in peas (Mendel's trait): the gene for starch-branching enzyme → the enzyme helps convert sugar into starch in the developing seed → seeds with a working copy are round.
[bio10-s2.3.6] Seeds with two non-working copies (rr) make less starch and keep more sugar. They take up more water while developing, and so wrinkle as they dry out.
[bio10-s2.3.7] Diagram: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein (amino acid chain folded into a shape) → trait, with the albinism example written under it.
[bio10-s2.4.b] The chain from gene to protein to trait is a useful model, but real traits are often more complicated. Genes are not the only thing that shapes an organism.
[bio10-s2.4.0] Many traits are controlled by several genes acting together, such as height and skin colour in humans, so there is no single gene for a trait. Each gene adds a small part of the final result.
[bio10-s2.4.1] The environment also affects many traits. A person's height depends on diet as well as genes, and a plant's height depends on light and nutrients.
[bio10-s2.4.2] The trait, or phenotype (what the organism is actually like), results from genes and environment together. This is why two organisms with the same genes can still look different.

CANDIDATES bio11-s10: Sex determination
[bio11-s10.0.b] Whether a person is female or male depends on one pair of chromosomes out of 23. Knowing what that pair looks like in each sex is the starting point for working out how sex is inherited.
[bio11-s10.0.0] A human body cell has 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes (the chromosomes that are the same in both sexes) and one pair of sex chromosomes, which decide the person's sex. There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio11-s10.0.1] Females have two X chromosomes (XX); males have one X and one Y (XY). Every body cell carries this pair, not just the gametes (sex cells).
[bio11-s10.0.2] The X is a large chromosome carrying roughly 800–900 protein-coding genes, so it carries many genes for the body generally.
[bio11-s10.0.3] The Y is much smaller and carries well under 100 genes, most of them concerned with male development and sperm production.
[bio11-s10.0.4] A karyotype is a photograph or diagram of a cell's chromosomes arranged in their pairs by size; it shows the sex chromosomes as XX or XY, so the sex of the person can be read straight from it.
[bio11-s10.0.5] Diagram: a simplified karyotype of 22 numbered autosome pairs plus the sex-chromosome pair, with a female (XX) and a male (XY) version of pair 23, the Y drawn clearly smaller than the X.
[bio11-s10.1.b] Body cells have two sex chromosomes, but gametes are made by meiosis (the cell division that halves the chromosome number), so each gamete gets only one. Writing out which gametes each parent makes lets you model the cross and predict the sexes of the children.
[bio11-s10.1.0] Gametes are haploid (they have half the usual chromosome number), with 23 chromosomes: 22 autosomes and one sex chromosome.
[bio11-s10.1.1] All of a woman's eggs carry an X (23,X), because she has only X chromosomes to pass on.
[bio11-s10.1.2] A man's sperm carry either an X (23,X) or a Y (23,Y), in equal numbers, because the X and Y separate into different cells in meiosis.
[bio11-s10.1.3] Parents' phenotypes (what they are): female × male.
[bio11-s10.1.4] Parents' genotypes (their sex chromosomes): XX × XY.
[bio11-s10.1.5] Gametes: X × X, Y.
[bio11-s10.1.6] Punnett square (a grid showing every possible gamete combination): XX, XX / XY, XY (or 2 × 1: XX, XY). Offspring genotypes: 1 XX : 1 XY. Offspring phenotypes: 1 female : 1 male (50% each).
[bio11-s10.1.7] Diagram: the Punnett square of this cross, with the mother's egg (X) on one side and the father's sperm (X and Y) on the other, each box labelled with genotype and sex.
[bio11-s10.2.b] The cross shows that every egg is the same but sperm come in two kinds. So the result of fertilisation, when a sperm and egg fuse, depends on which sperm wins.
[bio11-s10.2.0] The sex of a child is decided at fertilisation by the sperm: an X-carrying sperm gives a girl (XX); a Y-carrying sperm gives a boy (XY).
[bio11-s10.2.1] So the father's gamete decides the sex; the mother always gives an X, so her egg cannot make the difference.
[bio11-s10.2.2] Every pregnancy has an independent 1 in 2 chance of a girl or a boy, whatever the sex of earlier children, because each fertilisation is a fresh chance event.
[bio11-s10.2.3] Across a large population the sex ratio is close to 1 : 1, because X and Y sperm are made in equal numbers.
[bio11-s10.2.4] A boy receives his X from his mother and his Y from his father; a girl receives one X from each parent.
[bio11-s10.2.5] This is why X-linked traits (traits carried by genes on the X) pass from mother to son. There is more on this in Sex-linked traits.
[bio11-s10.3.b] Having XY or XX is only the start. The Y chromosome carries a particular gene that sets an embryo on the male path, and without it development follows the female path.
[bio11-s10.3.0] The Y chromosome carries the SRY gene (sex-determining region of the Y).
[bio11-s10.3.1] In an XY embryo, from about the sixth to seventh week of development, SRY switches on the development of the undifferentiated gonads (gonads that have not yet become either testes or ovaries) into testes.
[bio11-s10.3.2] The testes then make testosterone, which brings about the development of the male reproductive system.
[bio11-s10.3.3] In an XX embryo, with no Y and so no SRY, the gonads develop into ovaries and the female reproductive system develops.
[bio11-s10.3.4] So it is the presence of a Y (SRY), not the number of X chromosomes, that makes the embryo male.

CANDIDATES bio11-s11: Sex-linked traits
[bio11-s11.0.b] Some genes sit on the sex chromosomes rather than on the other chromosomes, and this changes how they are inherited. Because males and females carry different sex chromosomes, the same allele can have a different effect in each sex.
[bio11-s11.0.0] Sex-linked genes are almost all on the X chromosome (X-linked), because the Y carries very few genes.
[bio11-s11.0.1] A sex-linked trait is a trait controlled by such a gene. The Y has no matching allele for most X-linked genes, so in males the X-linked gene has no second copy to pair with.
[bio11-s11.0.2] Females (XX) have two copies of each X-linked gene, so they can be homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous (two different alleles).
[bio11-s11.0.3] A heterozygous female with one recessive allele is a carrier (she carries the allele but does not show the trait), because her dominant allele masks it.
[bio11-s11.0.4] Males (XY) have only one copy, so whatever allele their single X carries is expressed, with no second allele to mask it.
[bio11-s11.0.5] This is why a male cannot be a carrier, and why one recessive allele is enough for him to show the trait.
[bio11-s11.0.6] X-linked recessive conditions are therefore much more common in males than in females.
[bio11-s11.0.7] A female shows the condition only if she inherits the recessive allele from both parents, which means an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
[bio11-s11.1.b] To predict who inherits an X-linked allele, follow the sex chromosomes. A child's sex is decided by the sex chromosome the father contributes, and that also decides where the X-linked allele comes from.
[bio11-s11.1.0] A father passes his X to all his daughters and his Y to all his sons.
[bio11-s11.1.1] So an affected father never passes an X-linked condition to his sons, because sons get his Y, which carries no allele for the gene.
[bio11-s11.1.2] All his daughters are at least carriers, because each receives his X with the recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.1.3] A son's X-linked allele always comes from his mother, since his only X is the one she gave him.
[bio11-s11.2.b] Genetics questions use a fixed way of writing X-linked genotypes so that the sex and the alleles are both clear at a glance.
[bio11-s11.2.0] Write the allele as a superscript on the X, and the Y with no allele, because the Y has no matching allele for these genes.
[bio11-s11.2.1] Colour blindness: Xᴮ = normal colour vision, Xᵇ = colour blind. Haemophilia: Xᴴ = normal clotting, Xʰ = haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.2.2] Colour-blindness genotypes: female XᴮXᴮ (normal), XᴮXᵇ (carrier), XᵇXᵇ (colour blind); male XᴮY (normal), XᵇY (colour blind).
[bio11-s11.2.3] Use the letters a question gives.
[bio11-s11.3.b] Red-green colour blindness is the standard example of an X-linked recessive condition, and the one most often used in crosses.
[bio11-s11.3.0] Red-green colour blindness is an X-linked recessive condition in which a person has difficulty telling red and green apart.
[bio11-s11.3.1] The genes for the red- and green-sensitive light-absorbing pigments of the cone cells of the retina (the colour-detecting cells at the back of the eye) are on the X chromosome. The recessive allele gives a pigment that is missing or works abnormally.
[bio11-s11.3.2] It affects about 1 in 12 men (8%) and about 1 in 200 women (0.5%) of northern European ancestry. The difference fits the pattern that males need only one recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.3.3] It is detected with coloured-dot test charts (Ishihara plates).
[bio11-s11.3.4] It is not treatable, but most people adapt to it.
[bio11-s11.4.b] Haemophilia is the second named X-linked recessive condition. It shows how a single faulty gene can affect the whole body, because it removes one step in blood clotting.
[bio11-s11.4.0] Haemophilia is an X-linked recessive condition in which blood does not clot properly because a clotting protein is missing or faulty.
[bio11-s11.4.1] Haemophilia A lacks clotting factor VIII; haemophilia B lacks factor IX.
[bio11-s11.4.2] Bleeding after injury lasts much longer, and bleeding can occur into joints and muscles.
[bio11-s11.4.3] Haemophilia A affects about 1 in 5,000 male births.
[bio11-s11.4.4] It is treated by injections of the missing clotting factor, now mostly produced by genetically engineered cells. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio11-s11.5.b] Haemophilia in European royal families is a well-known real case of an X-linked allele passing through carrier daughters to affected grandsons.
[bio11-s11.5.0] Queen Victoria (1819–1901) was a carrier of haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.5.1] Her son Leopold had haemophilia. Through two of her daughters, who were carriers, it passed into the royal families of Russia (Tsarevich Alexei, son of Tsar Nicholas II) and Spain.
[bio11-s11.6.b] Haemophilia and colour blindness are not the only X-linked recessive conditions. A third example is useful when a question asks for one other than haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.6.0] Duchenne muscular dystrophy is another X-linked recessive condition, caused by a lack of the muscle protein dystrophin, which causes progressive muscle weakness (muscles get steadily weaker over time).
[bio11-s11.6.1] It is almost always in boys, which fits the pattern for X-linked recessive conditions.
[bio11-s11.7.b] A mother who is a carrier of colour blindness has a partner with normal vision, so we can work out what their children could inherit.
[bio11-s11.7.0] Parents' phenotypes: normal-vision (carrier) female × normal-vision male.
[bio11-s11.7.1] Parents' genotypes: carrier female XᴮXᵇ × normal male XᴮY.
[bio11-s11.7.2] Gametes (each carries one sex chromosome with its allele): carrier female Xᴮ, Xᵇ × normal male Xᴮ, Y.
[bio11-s11.7.3] Punnett square: XᴮXᴮ, XᴮY / XᴮXᵇ, XᵇY.
[bio11-s11.7.4] Offspring genotypes and phenotypes: 1 normal female (XᴮXᴮ) : 1 carrier female (XᴮXᵇ) : 1 normal male (XᴮY) : 1 colour-blind male (XᵇY).
[bio11-s11.7.5] Half the sons are expected to be colour blind and half the daughters carriers; no daughters are colour blind, because every daughter gets the father's normal Xᴮ. 25% of all children are colour blind.
[bio11-s11.7.6] Diagram: the Punnett square for this cross, with the mother's gametes Xᴮ and Xᵇ on one side and the father's Xᴮ and Y on the other, each box labelled with genotype and phenotype (normal female, carrier female, normal male, colour-blind male).
[bio11-s11.8.b] Here the father has haemophilia and the mother has two normal alleles. This cross shows what an affected father passes on.
[bio11-s11.8.0] Parents' phenotypes: normal female × affected male.
[bio11-s11.8.1] Parents' genotypes: normal female XᴴXᴴ × affected male XʰY.

[bio11-s11.11.b] A pedigree shows how a trait passes through several generations of a family. Before you can use one, you need to know what its symbols and lines mean.
[bio11-s11.11.0] A pedigree is a family-tree diagram showing which members of a family have a trait.
[bio11-s11.11.1] Symbols: square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected; half-shaded (or a dot in the centre) = carrier.
[bio11-s11.11.2] A horizontal line joining a male and a female = parents; a vertical line down to a horizontal line = their children, in birth order from left to right.
[bio11-s11.11.3] Generations are numbered I, II, III and individuals 1, 2, 3 within each, so every person can be named, for example II-2.
[bio11-s11.12.b] Four features of a pedigree point to an X-linked recessive trait. Each follows from the rules on how X chromosomes are passed on.
[bio11-s11.12.0] Many more affected males than females, because a male needs only one recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.12.1] Affected males usually have unaffected parents, because the mother is a carrier.
[bio11-s11.12.2] The trait can skip a generation, passing from an affected man through his carrier daughters to his grandsons.
[bio11-s11.12.3] It is never passed from father to son, because a son gets his father's Y, not his X.
[bio11-s11.13.b] To give the genotype of a named person, or the chance that a child is affected or a carrier, work through the pedigree in a fixed order. This starts with the people whose genotype is certain.
[bio11-s11.13.0] 1. Write XY for every male and XX for every female.

[bio11-s11.13.2] 3. Any daughter of an affected male is at least a carrier; any mother of an affected son is a carrier or affected.
[bio11-s11.13.3] 4. Use these to fill in the remaining females.
[bio11-s11.13.4] 5. Then work any further cross with a Punnett square.

CANDIDATES bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio17-s2: Transcription and translation
[bio17-s2.0.b] A cell can't send its DNA out to the ribosomes, so it makes a working copy of the gene instead. Three molecules are involved in making and carrying that copy: DNA, the enzyme RNA polymerase and mRNA.
[bio17-s2.0.0] DNA holds the gene, the base sequence (the order of A, T, C and G) that codes for the protein.
[bio17-s2.0.1] In transcription only one of the two strands of the gene is read, the template strand. The DNA is not used up or changed, and it stays in the nucleus, so the original instructions stay safe.
[bio17-s2.0.2] RNA polymerase is the enzyme that carries out transcription. It binds to the DNA at the start of a gene, unwinds and separates the two strands there, and joins free RNA nucleotides into a strand complementary to the template.
[bio17-s2.0.3] RNA polymerase moves along the gene until it reaches the end, building the new strand as it goes.
[bio17-s2.0.4] mRNA (messenger RNA) is a single-stranded copy of one gene's code, a series of codons (groups of three bases), and it carries the code from the nucleus to a ribosome.
[bio17-s2.0.5] mRNA is short-lived and is broken down after use, so a cell can stop making a protein by no longer making its mRNA.
[bio17-s2.1.b] Translation (turning the mRNA code into a chain of amino acids) needs a delivery molecule for the amino acids and a workbench to assemble them on. These are tRNA and the ribosome, which is built from rRNA. Together with mRNA, these are the three types of RNA in protein synthesis.
[bio17-s2.1.0] tRNA (transfer RNA) is a small RNA molecule in the cytoplasm that carries one specific amino acid to the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.1.1] Each tRNA's anticodon (three bases) pairs with a matching codon on the mRNA, so tRNA is the molecule that “translates” codons into amino acids.
[bio17-s2.1.2] tRNA brings amino acids to the ribosome; it does not make them.
[bio17-s2.1.3] rRNA (ribosomal RNA) makes up the ribosome together with proteins. It holds the mRNA and tRNAs in position.
[bio17-s2.1.4] The rRNA of the large subunit also catalyses the formation of peptide bonds (the bonds that join amino acids), so it acts as an enzyme.
[bio17-s2.1.5] The ribosome is the site of translation, made of rRNA and protein in two subunits: a small subunit that binds the mRNA and a large subunit with room for two tRNAs at a time.
[bio17-s2.1.6] The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time, reading the code in order.
[bio17-s2.1.7] Ribosomes are free in the cytoplasm, making proteins used in the cell, or attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum (a membrane network studded with ribosomes), making proteins for export or for membranes. There is more on this in Endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
[bio17-s2.2.b] Transcription is the making of an mRNA copy of a gene, and it takes place in the nucleus. It works by complementary base pairing, where each base on the DNA attracts one particular partner base.
[bio17-s2.2.0] 1. RNA polymerase attaches to the DNA at the start of the gene, unwinds the double helix there and separates the two strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs.
[bio17-s2.2.1] 2. One strand, the template strand, is read.
[bio17-s2.2.2] 2. Free RNA nucleotides pair with its exposed bases by complementary base pairing: A on DNA pairs with U on RNA, T with A, C with G, and G with C. RNA has U (uracil) where DNA has T.
[bio17-s2.2.3] 3. RNA polymerase joins the RNA nucleotides into a strand of mRNA as it moves along the gene, so the mRNA grows one nucleotide at a time.
[bio17-s2.2.4] 3. Behind it, the DNA strands re-join and rewind, so the DNA is left unchanged.
[bio17-s2.2.5] 4. At the end of the gene, RNA polymerase detaches and the finished mRNA is released.
[bio17-s2.2.6] 4. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore (a gap in the nuclear envelope) and goes to a ribosome.
[bio17-s2.2.7] Diagram: a section of DNA unwound by RNA polymerase, with the template strand and the mRNA strand growing alongside it; label DNA, template strand, RNA polymerase, free RNA nucleotides, mRNA, base pair A–U, direction of movement.
[bio17-s2.3.b] A tRNA has to do two jobs: carry the right amino acid and recognise the right codon. Its folded shape has one end for each job.
[bio17-s2.3.0] A tRNA is a single strand of about 80 RNA nucleotides that folds back on itself, so some regions lie side by side and can base-pair. Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in some regions hold the folded molecule in shape.
[bio17-s2.3.1] The folded molecule is a clover-leaf in two dimensions and folds further into an L shape in three dimensions.
[bio17-s2.3.2] At one end is the amino-acid attachment site, where one specific amino acid is attached by a specific enzyme, using energy from ATP.
[bio17-s2.3.3] At the opposite end is a loop with the anticodon: three unpaired bases that pair with a complementary codon on mRNA.
[bio17-s2.3.4] The code is read in codons of three bases, so each tRNA has an anticodon of exactly three bases and matches one codon (three bases of mRNA).
[bio17-s2.3.5] Each tRNA brings one amino acid. The attachment of a specific amino acid to each tRNA with a specific anticodon is what links each codon to its amino acid.
[bio17-s2.3.6] The ribosome holds two tRNAs side by side on neighbouring codons, so the code is read three bases at a time without gaps or overlaps.
[bio17-s2.3.7] Diagram: a clover-leaf tRNA, labelled: single RNA strand, regions of base pairing (hydrogen bonds), anticodon loop with three bases, amino-acid attachment site, amino acid.
[bio17-s2.4.b] Translation is the making of a polypeptide (a chain of amino acids) from the code in mRNA, and it takes place at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. It begins by assembling the ribosome on the mRNA and joining the first two amino acids.
[bio17-s2.4.0] 1. Start: the small ribosomal subunit attaches to the mRNA and moves along it to the start codon, AUG.
[bio17-s2.4.1] 1. A tRNA with the anticodon UAC, carrying methionine, pairs with the start codon, and the large subunit then joins.
[bio17-s2.4.2] 2. A second tRNA, whose anticodon is complementary to the next codon, arrives carrying its amino acid and pairs with that codon in the second position in the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.4.3] 3. A peptide bond forms between the two amino acids, a condensation reaction that releases water. It is catalysed by the rRNA of the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.4.4] 3. The first tRNA's amino acid is now attached to the second amino acid.
[bio17-s2.5.b] Once two amino acids are joined, the same cycle repeats along the mRNA until a stop signal is reached. Cells also run many ribosomes at once to make protein quickly.
[bio17-s2.5.0] 4. The ribosome moves along the mRNA by one codon, three bases. The first tRNA, now without its amino acid, is released into the cytoplasm, where it can pick up another of the same amino acid.
[bio17-s2.5.1] 4. A new tRNA pairs with the codon now in the empty position, because the move has exposed the next codon.
[bio17-s2.5.2] 5. Steps 2 to 4 repeat, the chain growing by one amino acid each time, in the order set by the codons.
[bio17-s2.5.3] 6. End: when a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA) is reached, no tRNA matches it, and a protein release factor binds instead.
[bio17-s2.5.4] 6. The polypeptide is released, and the ribosome's subunits separate from the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.5.5] Many ribosomes can translate the same mRNA at once, one behind the other, forming a polysome, so many copies of the protein are made quickly.
[bio17-s2.5.6] Translation also needs energy, both to attach amino acids to their tRNAs (from ATP) and to move the ribosome along the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.5.7] Diagram: a ribosome on an mRNA strand, with two tRNAs paired to neighbouring codons, a growing polypeptide on one, a peptide bond forming and a released tRNA leaving; label mRNA, codon, anticodon, tRNA, amino acid, peptide bond, ribosome (large and small subunits), polypeptide, direction of movement.
[bio17-s2.6.b] A newly made polypeptide is only a chain. It becomes a working protein when it folds into a particular three-dimensional shape, and the shape is set by its amino-acid sequence.
[bio17-s2.6.0] The sequence of amino acids (the primary structure) determines how the chain folds.
[bio17-s2.6.1] Bonds form between amino acids in different parts of the chain, such as hydrogen bonds and, in some proteins, sulphur bridges, giving the protein its particular three-dimensional shape.
[bio17-s2.6.2] Some proteins are made of more than one polypeptide, for example haemoglobin, which has four.
[bio17-s2.6.3] The shape determines the function, for example the active site of an enzyme or the binding site of an antibody or receptor. There is more on this in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio17-s2.6.4] A change in the amino-acid sequence can change the shape and stop the protein working, because the bonds that fold the chain form in different places.
[bio17-s2.6.5] Proteins made on the rough endoplasmic reticulum are folded and modified there and in the Golgi apparatus, then packaged into vesicles (small membrane sacs) for export, such as digestive enzymes and insulin.
[bio17-s2.6.6] In prokaryotes, which have no nucleus, so no nuclear envelope separates the steps, transcription and translation both take place in the cytoplasm, and ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed.
[bio17-s2.7.b] Both processes copy DNA using complementary base pairing, so they are easy to confuse. They differ in what is copied, which enzymes do it, and what happens to the product.
[bio17-s2.7.0] Product: replication makes a complete copy of all the DNA, giving two DNA molecules; transcription makes an RNA copy of one gene, the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.7.1] Strands used: replication uses both strands as templates, so both new molecules are complete; transcription uses only one strand of the gene.
[bio17-s2.7.2] Enzymes: DNA helicase and DNA polymerase in replication; RNA polymerase in transcription, so the enzyme names tell the two processes apart.
[bio17-s2.7.3] Nucleotides and pairing: DNA nucleotides contain deoxyribose (a sugar) and pair A with T; RNA nucleotides contain ribose, and A on DNA pairs with U.
[bio17-s2.7.4] When: replication happens once per cycle, in S phase; transcription happens throughout the cell's life, for whichever genes are needed, many times.
[bio17-s2.7.5] Fate of the product: new DNA stays in the nucleus, but mRNA leaves for the cytoplasm and is broken down after use.
[bio17-s2.8.b] Exam questions often give a DNA sequence and ask for the mRNA, tRNA anticodons and amino acids. You can do this with a fixed five-step method.
[bio17-s2.8.0] 1. Write the mRNA complementary to the template strand (A → U, T → A, C → G, G → C).
[bio17-s2.8.1] 2. Divide the mRNA into codons from the start codon, because the ribosome reads three bases at a time.
[bio17-s2.8.2] 3. Write the tRNA anticodon complementary to each codon, because the anticodon pairs with the codon.
[bio17-s2.8.3] 4. Use the codon table given to find the amino acids.
[bio17-s2.8.4] 5. Stop at a stop codon, because no amino acid is added there.
[bio17-s2.9.b] Here is the method applied to one short template strand, showing each product in turn.
[bio17-s2.9.0] Template DNA TAC AAA CCG ATT gives mRNA AUG UUU GGC UAA, by pairing T with A, A with U and C with G.
[bio17-s2.9.1] The anticodons are UAC, AAA and CCG, with none for the stop codon UAA, because no tRNA matches a stop codon.
[bio17-s2.9.2] The polypeptide is methionine, phenylalanine and glycine, and then the chain stops.
[bio17-s2.9.3] Three codons code for amino acids, so the worked example gives three amino acids joined by two peptide bonds.
[bio17-s2.10.b] You can also model the two processes with paper and cards. A model makes the order of events clear, but it leaves out some of the real chemistry.
[bio17-s2.10.0] Paper strips for DNA, in a “nucleus” area, are copied onto mRNA strips that are carried to a “ribosome” area.
[bio17-s2.10.1] Cards for tRNA have a three-base anticodon on one side and an amino acid on the other, so each card shows what a real tRNA does.
[bio17-s2.10.2] In the role-play, students match anticodons to codons in order and link the amino acids with clips to represent peptide bonds.
[bio17-s2.10.3] Limitations of the role-play: it is slow and flat, and ignores the enzymes, energy and folding, so it shows the order of events but not how they happen chemically.

CANDIDATES bio31-s5: Carrying capacity and limiting factors
[bio31-s5.0.b] A population is all the individuals of one species living in one area. Its size rises and falls, and in every habitat there is an upper limit that the environment can support. That limit is the carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.0.0] A population's size changes through four processes. Births (natality) and immigration (individuals arriving) increase it; deaths (mortality) and emigration (individuals leaving) decrease it.
[bio31-s5.0.1] A population grows when births plus immigration exceed deaths plus emigration. If the two sides are equal, the size stays steady.
[bio31-s5.0.2] Every population could grow very fast if nothing stopped it, because organisms produce more offspring than are needed to replace themselves. In reality, resources run short, so growth slows and stops.
[bio31-s5.0.3] The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum population size of a species that it can support indefinitely, given the food, water, space and other resources available. It is often given the symbol K.
[bio31-s5.0.4] A limiting factor is any factor that restricts the size, growth or distribution of a population. The factor in shortest supply relative to need sets the limit, even if all the others are plentiful, which is the same principle as limiting factors in photosynthesis.
[bio31-s5.0.5] Carrying capacity is set by the limiting factors acting together, not by any one of them alone.
[bio31-s5.0.6] Carrying capacity is not fixed. It changes when conditions change, such as in a wet or dry year, after a new predator or disease arrives, or with habitat loss or restoration. It also differs between species in the same habitat.
[bio31-s5.0.7] If a population rises above carrying capacity, it damages its resources, for example by overgrazing (eating plants faster than they regrow), and then its numbers fall.
[bio31-s5.1.b] Abiotic factors are the non-living parts of the environment. Each of them can cap the number of plants and animals a habitat supports, and the Irish examples below show how.
[bio31-s5.1.0] Water: all organisms need water. On the dry, free-draining sand of coastal sand dunes, water shortage limits which plants can grow and how many.
[bio31-s5.1.1] Marram grass grows on sand dunes because its rolled leaves and deep roots reduce water loss and reach water, so it survives where most plants cannot. In a dry summer, grass growth slows and fewer grazing animals can be supported.
[bio31-s5.1.2] Oxygen: aquatic animals depend on oxygen dissolved in water, which is scarce compared with air. Salmon, trout and stonefly nymphs need well-oxygenated, cool, fast-flowing water, so oxygen limits their numbers in slow, warm or polluted rivers.
[bio31-s5.1.3] Decomposing organic pollution uses up oxygen in a river, which is why polluted water supports fewer oxygen-demanding animals. In waterlogged bog soil, low oxygen limits roots and decomposers, so few plant species can grow there.
[bio31-s5.1.4] Space: organisms need space to attach, shelter, nest or hold a territory (an area an animal defends). On a rocky shore, bare rock is limited, so barnacles, mussels and seaweeds compete for space to attach.
[bio31-s5.1.5] Seabirds nest only on cliffs and islands with suitable ledges. Many songbirds, such as robins, defend territories, so the number of territories in a woodland limits the breeding population.
[bio31-s5.1.6] Temperature affects enzyme activity, growth and survival. Cold winters kill many small birds such as wrens, and low temperatures in the uplands shorten the growing season, limiting plant growth. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, so temperature also limits fish.
[bio31-s5.1.7] Soil type, pH, minerals, water content and air content limit plants, and so the animals that feed on them. Acidic, nitrogen-poor bog peat supports only specialised plants such as Sphagnum, heather and sundews.
[bio31-s5.2.b] Soil is one of the factors that limit which plants can grow in a place, and plants are the food base for animals. Light is not on the specification's list of abiotic factors, but it is a key limiting factor for plants.
[bio31-s5.2.0] The thin limestone soils of the Burren, Co. Clare, support lime-loving plants, because only plants suited to thin, lime-rich soil can grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.1] Fertile, well-drained lowland soils support productive grassland, because they are fertile and drain well.
[bio31-s5.2.2] Under a closed woodland canopy (the roof of leaves formed by the treetops) in summer, little light reaches the floor, so few plants grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.3] Woodland plants such as bluebells grow and flower in spring, before the leaves of the trees open, which means they get the light they need while the canopy is still open.
[bio31-s5.3.b] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment: other organisms and the diseases they carry. Food, disease, competition and predation all limit populations, and some of them also limit one another.
[bio31-s5.3.0] Food: the supply of food usually sets the carrying capacity for animals. For grazers it is the amount of grass or browse (leaves and shoots of shrubs and trees).
[bio31-s5.3.1] Seed-eaters such as squirrels and wood mice do better in years when trees produce large seed crops. Birds of prey such as barn owls are limited by the number of small mammals they can catch.
[bio31-s5.3.2] Disease: disease spreads faster in dense populations, so it limits large populations most. Squirrelpox virus, carried by grey squirrels, kills red squirrels.
[bio31-s5.3.3] Myxomatosis, a viral disease, killed most of Ireland's rabbits after it arrived in the 1950s. Ash dieback is killing ash trees.
[bio31-s5.3.4] Competition: organisms competing for the same limited resource, such as food, light, water, space or mates, reduce each other's share, so fewer survive or breed. Competition between members of the same species (intraspecific) and between different species (interspecific) both limit populations.
[bio31-s5.3.5] Grey squirrels outcompete red squirrels for food in broadleaf woods, and on a woodland floor taller plants shade out shorter ones. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio31-s5.3.6] Predation: predators kill and eat prey, keeping prey numbers below what the food supply alone would allow. Foxes take rabbits and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew.
[bio31-s5.3.7] Pine martens prey on grey squirrels, reducing their numbers where martens are common. Predator numbers are in turn limited by the number of prey, so the two populations affect each other.
[bio31-s5.4.b] Predation normally keeps herbivore numbers down. Where a predator is missing, food becomes the main limit, and the habitat can pay the price.
[bio31-s5.4.0] Ireland's deer, the native red deer and the sika deer introduced to Powerscourt, Co. Wicklow, in 1860, have had no natural predator since wolves died out in Ireland more than two centuries ago.
[bio31-s5.4.1] Their numbers are limited mainly by food, so in parts of Co. Wicklow they have risen high enough to browse away tree seedlings and stop woodland regenerating.
[bio31-s5.4.2] Culling (killing selected animals) by people is used to keep deer below the level that damages habitats, so people take over the role the predator once had.
[bio31-s5.5.b] Limiting factors fall into two groups, depending on whether their effect changes with how crowded the population is. This explains why some factors steady a population while others crash it without warning.
[bio31-s5.5.0] Density-dependent factors have a greater effect as the population becomes more crowded. They are food shortage, competition, disease and predation, and are mostly biotic.
[bio31-s5.5.1] They slow growth as a population approaches carrying capacity, so they regulate population size, pulling it back towards K.
[bio31-s5.5.2] Density-independent factors affect a population regardless of its size, for example a severe frost, a flood, a storm, a drought or a fire, and are mostly abiotic.
[bio31-s5.5.3] Density-independent factors can cause sudden crashes at any population size, because a frost kills the same fraction whether the population is small or large.
[bio31-s5.5.4] Human activity changes carrying capacity. Draining wet grassland and bogs removes curlew breeding habitat, lowering its carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.5.5] Rewetting a bog or planting hedgerows raises carrying capacity for the species that depend on them, and garden feeders increase the food available for some birds.
[bio31-s5.6.b] A grassland field shows how several limiting factors act on one species at once. For rabbits, the field's carrying capacity is the result of all of them together.
[bio31-s5.6.0] Food: grass is the main density-dependent limit. As rabbit numbers rise, the grass is grazed shorter and competition increases.
[bio31-s5.6.1] Predation by foxes, stoats and birds of prey removes many young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.2] Disease: myxomatosis causes crashes when the population is dense, because the virus spreads fastest between crowded animals.
[bio31-s5.6.3] Space: burrows need well-drained soil, for example on banks and slopes, which limits where warrens (burrow systems) can be dug.
[bio31-s5.6.4] Wet winters, an abiotic and density-independent factor, can flood burrows and kill young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.5] The carrying capacity of the field for rabbits is the population that these factors together allow it to support over time.
[bio31-s5.6.6] Diagram: a simple graph of a population rising and levelling off at a horizontal dashed line labelled “carrying capacity (K)”, with arrows labelled “limiting factors (environmental resistance)” pressing down on the curve.

CANDIDATES bio35-s2: DNA profiling
[bio35-s2.0.b] Every person's DNA is slightly different, and DNA profiling turns those differences into a pattern that can be compared between samples. It is used to link people to evidence, to each other or to a remains sample.
[bio35-s2.0.0] DNA profiling (also called DNA fingerprinting) is a method of making a pattern of bands (or peaks) from a sample of a person's DNA, so that the DNA of different samples can be compared.
[bio35-s2.0.1] Except for identical twins, the pattern is unique to each person, which is why it can identify an individual.
[bio35-s2.0.2] Identical twins come from one zygote (the single cell formed at fertilisation), so they have the same DNA and the same profile. Non-identical twins and other siblings do not share a profile.
[bio35-s2.0.3] How a profile is made, in outline: 1. DNA is extracted from cells. 2. Many copies of the repeat regions are made by PCR, so there is enough to detect (in older methods the DNA was cut with restriction enzymes).
[bio35-s2.0.4] 3. The fragments are separated by size by gel electrophoresis, so that different lengths form different bands. 4. The pattern is analysed and compared. There is more on each step in Generating a DNA profile (Higher Level).
[bio35-s2.1.b] A profile does not read a person's genes. It looks at stretches of DNA that do not code for protein, where short sequences are repeated, because the number of repeats differs so much from person to person.
[bio35-s2.1.0] DNA profiling is based on non-coding DNA (DNA that does not code for a protein): about 98% of human DNA is non-coding.
[bio35-s2.1.1] Parts of non-coding DNA contain short base sequences repeated many times one after another, for example short tandem repeats (STRs), a short sequence, often four bases (e.g. GATA), is repeated end to end, e.g. GATAGATAGATA…
[bio35-s2.1.2] The number of repeats at a particular place on a chromosome (a locus) varies greatly between people, so the length of that piece of DNA varies too. This length difference is what a profile detects.
[bio35-s2.1.3] Profiling is therefore not a test of a person's genes, their whole genome, their appearance or their health.
[bio35-s2.2.b] Because we inherit one of each chromosome pair from each parent, every person has two copies of each locus. This is what gives the pattern its structure and lets it be used for family relationships.
[bio35-s2.2.0] Each person has two copies of every locus, one on the chromosome inherited from the mother and one on the chromosome inherited from the father.
[bio35-s2.2.1] Each copy can have a different number of repeats (two alleles, giving two bands), or the same number (giving one band, twice as strong because both copies run to the same position).
[bio35-s2.2.2] So a child's profile at each locus has one band from the mother and one from the father. A child has half its bands from each parent, not most from the father.
[bio35-s2.3.b] One locus on its own would match many people by chance. Testing many loci together makes a full match very strong evidence.
[bio35-s2.3.0] A profile tests many loci at once; modern forensic profiles use more than ten.
[bio35-s2.3.1] The chance that two unrelated people match at every locus is extremely small, which is why a full match is strong evidence of identity.
[bio35-s2.3.2] The exception is identical twins, who share the same DNA and so the same profile at every locus.
[bio35-s2.4.b] In gel electrophoresis, DNA fragments are pulled through a gel by an electric field and sorted by size. Knowing which way they move and which sizes go furthest lets you read a profile.
[bio35-s2.4.0] On a gel, each band is a group of DNA fragments of the same length.
[bio35-s2.4.1] DNA is negatively charged (because of its phosphate groups), so in an electric field it moves through the gel towards the positive electrode, away from the wells (the small slots) where samples are loaded.
[bio35-s2.4.2] Shorter fragments (fewer repeats) move faster and travel further from the wells, because they pass through the gel more easily; longer fragments (more repeats) stay nearer the wells. So band position shows fragment length.
[bio35-s2.4.3] Comparing band positions with a ladder of fragments of known length (a size marker) lets lengths be read.
[bio35-s2.4.4] In modern laboratories the fragments are labelled with fluorescent dyes and separated by machine. The result is printed as a graph of peaks, each peak being one allele labelled with its number of repeats, rather than as bands. The rules for matching are the same.
[bio35-s2.4.5] Diagram: a gel with wells at the top (negative electrode) and the positive electrode at the bottom; lanes for a ladder, a crime-scene sample and three suspects; one suspect's bands match the crime-scene lane exactly. Label wells, lanes, bands, "shorter fragments travel further", + and − electrodes.
[bio35-s2.5.b] Once the bands are on the gel, you compare lanes. The rule for a match is strict: everything must line up.
[bio35-s2.5.0] Two samples match only if every band is in the same position.
[bio35-s2.5.1] A single band that differs, at any locus, shows that they did not come from the same person, because the same DNA would give identical bands; this is an exclusion.
[bio35-s2.5.2] Paternity uses the same logic: every band in a child's profile must come from the mother or the father, so a man whose profile lacks a band the child must have inherited from its father is excluded as the father.
[bio35-s2.6.b] DNA profiling is a recent method, and its first uses showed that it can both convict and clear people.
[bio35-s2.6.0] DNA profiling was invented by Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester, England, in 1984.
[bio35-s2.6.1] It was first used to convict a murderer, Colin Pitchfork, in 1988, which showed that it works as evidence in court.
[bio35-s2.6.2] The same investigation first cleared an innocent suspect, who had confessed to one of the killings but whose DNA did not match.
[bio35-s2.7.b] The two best-known uses compare a person's profile with other evidence or other people. In crime it links or excludes suspects, and in families it tests who is related to whom.
[bio35-s2.7.0] Forensic science (crime): DNA from blood, semen, saliva, skin cells or hair roots left at a crime scene is compared with a suspect's DNA (taken from a mouth swab).
[bio35-s2.7.1] A match links the suspect to the scene; a mismatch excludes them, because a single differing band shows the DNA came from someone else.
[bio35-s2.7.2] Profiles are stored in national DNA databases so that new crime-scene samples can be compared with them, which can identify a suspect.
[bio35-s2.7.3] Old crime-scene samples can be retested, solving "cold cases" (unsolved old crimes) and freeing people wrongly convicted.
[bio35-s2.7.4] Paternity and other family relationships: DNA profiling is also used to establish maternity, to reunite separated families, and in immigration cases where family relationships must be proven.
[bio35-s2.8.b] When a body is unrecognisable or old, a profile can still identify it by comparing with relatives or personal items. When nuclear DNA is not usable, other DNA can trace ancestry.
[bio35-s2.8.0] Identifying human remains, e.g. victims of disasters, wars and accidents, or missing persons, by comparing their DNA with that of relatives or with DNA from their personal items (e.g. a toothbrush), which carry the person's own DNA.
[bio35-s2.8.1] Mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome profiles trace family lines when nuclear DNA is too damaged or no close relative is available. Mitochondrial DNA is passed down the female line and the Y chromosome down the male line, so each follows one line of ancestors.
[bio35-s2.8.2] Example: the skeleton found under a car park in Leicester in 2012 was confirmed in 2013 to be King Richard III, partly by matching its mitochondrial DNA to living descendants of his sister's female line.
[bio35-s2.8.3] There is more on these two kinds of inheritance in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio35-s2.9.b] DNA profiling is not only for people. It is also applied to wildlife, farm and breeding animals, food and medicine.
[bio35-s2.9.0] Animals, plants and conservation: identifying illegally traded wildlife products (e.g. ivory, meat from protected species), and checking the pedigree (parentage) of animals used for breeding.
[bio35-s2.9.1] It also helps keep breeding programmes for endangered species from inbreeding (mating between close relatives).
[bio35-s2.9.2] Food: DNA tests identify the species in food, so they show fraud when the food is not what the label says. Species tests check for a species' DNA, not an individual's profile.
[bio35-s2.9.3] Example: in January 2013 the Food Safety Authority of Ireland found horse DNA in beef burgers on sale in Ireland, which revealed a Europe-wide horsemeat scandal.
[bio35-s2.9.4] Medicine: DNA-based tests also help match donors and patients for bone-marrow transplants, and identify the strain of a disease-causing microorganism in an outbreak.
[bio35-s2.10.b] The two are easily confused because both use DNA. They answer different questions.
[bio35-s2.10.0] DNA profiling compares band patterns of mostly non-coding DNA to identify a person or a relationship; it tells nothing about health.
[bio35-s2.10.1] Genetic screening (DNA testing) looks at particular genes to find whether a person has, or carries, an allele linked to a disease. There is more on this in Bioinformatics: genome databases and, for screening of newborns, in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio35-s2.11.b] DNA evidence is powerful but not perfect. Problems can arise before, during and after the test.
[bio35-s2.11.0] Contamination of a sample with another person's DNA (e.g. by investigators) can give a false link, so samples must be collected with gloves, masks and sealed bags.
[bio35-s2.11.1] DNA can be degraded by heat, moisture, bacteria or time, giving incomplete profiles that are harder to interpret.
[bio35-s2.11.2] A match shows that the DNA came from that person (or an identical twin), not when or how it got there; DNA can be transferred innocently, e.g. by touch or on shared objects.
[bio35-s2.11.3] So DNA evidence must be weighed with other evidence; it does not prove guilt alone.
[bio35-s2.11.4] Mistakes in labelling or interpretation, and the human judgement needed for mixed samples from several people, can lead to errors, for example if a sample is attributed to the wrong person.

CANDIDATES bio35-s3: Generating a DNA profile
[bio35-s3.0.b] A DNA profile is made by turning a tiny biological sample into a pattern of bands that can be compared between people. The process always follows the same five steps, and each step has its own reagents or equipment.
[bio35-s3.0.0] The steps are: 1. Collect a sample, because every cell with a nucleus carries the person's DNA.
[bio35-s3.0.1] 2. Extract the DNA, which means freeing it from the cells and separating it from everything else.
[bio35-s3.0.2] 3. Amplify (copy) the chosen repeat regions by PCR (the polymerase chain reaction), so there is enough DNA to detect.
[bio35-s3.0.3] 4. Separate the copies by size by gel electrophoresis.
[bio35-s3.0.4] 5. Make the bands visible and analyse (compare) the pattern.
[bio35-s3.0.5] In the earlier method, step 3 was replaced by cutting the DNA with restriction enzymes (enzymes that cut DNA at specific sequences). It is taught in the restriction enzymes block below.
[bio35-s3.0.6] Diagram: a flow diagram of the five steps, with the key reagent or equipment at each: sample (swab, blood) → extraction (detergent, salt, protease, cold ethanol) → PCR (primers, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, thermal cycler) → gel electrophoresis (agarose gel, wells, electric current) → stained bands compared.
[bio35-s3.1.b] DNA can be taken from any cell that has a nucleus, because the nucleus is where a human cell keeps its DNA. Extraction then frees the DNA from the cell in three steps, each aimed at removing one barrier.
[bio35-s3.1.0] Sources are any cells with a nucleus, e.g. white blood cells (mature red blood cells have no nucleus, so they contain no nuclear DNA), cheek cells from a mouth swab, skin cells, hair roots (not the cut hair shaft, which has no usable nucleus), saliva and semen.
[bio35-s3.1.1] Samples are collected with gloves and stored sealed and cold to avoid contamination (DNA from the collector or other sources getting in) and breakdown of the DNA.
[bio35-s3.1.2] Extraction, step 1 (lysis, meaning breaking open the cells): a detergent breaks down the phospholipid membranes (the cell membrane and the nuclear envelope), releasing the DNA.
[bio35-s3.1.3] Step 2: a protease enzyme digests proteins, including the histones (the proteins the DNA is wound around in a chromosome) bound to the DNA, leaving the DNA free.
[bio35-s3.1.4] Step 3: DNA is insoluble in cold ethanol, so it comes out of solution (precipitates) and can be collected. Salt helps the DNA strands clump together.
[bio35-s3.1.5] Forensic laboratories use the same principles (break cells, remove protein, purify DNA) with purified kits, because crime-scene samples are tiny and need careful handling.
[bio35-s3.2.b] The school version of extraction uses onion, a plant tissue, and everyday chemicals. The first five steps break the tissue down and protect the DNA from damage.
[bio35-s3.2.0] 1. Chop the onion finely, to break cell walls and give a large surface area.
[bio35-s3.2.1] 2. Add it to a beaker containing a solution of washing-up liquid (detergent) and table salt.
[bio35-s3.2.2] 3. Stand the beaker in a water bath at 60 °C for 15 minutes. The heat helps the detergent work and denatures enzymes that would break down the DNA; longer heating would start to break the DNA itself.
[bio35-s3.2.3] 4. Cool the beaker in an ice-water bath for 5 minutes, slowing any enzyme breakdown of the DNA.
[bio35-s3.2.4] 5. Blend in a food processor for no more than a few seconds, because longer blending would break the long DNA strands.
[bio35-s3.2.5] Kiwi or strawberry also work in place of onion.
[bio35-s3.3.b] The last four steps remove the debris, digest the proteins and then bring the DNA out of solution so it can be seen. Each reagent has one job.
[bio35-s3.3.0] 6. Filter through coffee filter paper (or several layers of muslin) to remove cell debris, collecting the liquid filtrate in a test tube.
[bio35-s3.3.1] 7. Add a few drops of protease to digest proteins.
[bio35-s3.3.2] 8. Pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side of the tilted test tube so it forms a layer on top of the filtrate.
[bio35-s3.3.3] 9. DNA appears at the boundary as white, stringy threads that can be wound onto a glass rod.
[bio35-s3.3.4] In short: detergent breaks the membranes, salt clumps the DNA, protease removes proteins and cold ethanol precipitates the DNA.
[bio35-s3.3.5] Safety for this practical: ethanol is highly flammable (no flames nearby); take care with blender blades and hot water; wear safety glasses.
[bio35-s3.4.b] A crime-scene sample may hold only a few cells, far too little DNA to see on a gel. PCR solves this by copying a chosen stretch of DNA many times over.
[bio35-s3.4.0] PCR makes millions to billions of copies of a chosen short region of DNA (here, the STR regions, short repeated sequences that differ between people) from a tiny sample, e.g. a single hair root or a spot of blood, so that there is enough DNA to see on a gel.
[bio35-s3.4.1] PCR is DNA replication carried out in a tube. It copies only the chosen region, not the whole genome.
[bio35-s3.4.2] The reaction mixture contains the extracted DNA (the template, the DNA to be copied).
[bio35-s3.4.3] It also contains primers, which are short single-stranded pieces of DNA made to be complementary to the sequences on either side of the region to be copied. This is why only that region is copied: the primers decide which region.
[bio35-s3.4.4] DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides onto an existing strand, so a primer is needed to start each new strand.
[bio35-s3.4.5] It also contains a supply of the four DNA nucleotides (A, T, C, G), the building blocks of the new strands, a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase), and a buffer solution.
[bio35-s3.4.6] The tubes are placed in a thermal cycler, a machine that changes the temperature automatically.
[bio35-s3.5.b] PCR involves repeated heating to a very high temperature, which would destroy most enzymes. The solution was an enzyme from a heat-loving organism.
[bio35-s3.5.0] Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium found in hot springs (first found in Yellowstone National Park, USA).
[bio35-s3.5.1] Its structure holds its shape at high temperatures, so it is not denatured (permanently unfolded) at 95 °C and works best at about 72 °C.
[bio35-s3.5.2] A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first heating step, and fresh enzyme would have to be added every cycle.
[bio35-s3.5.3] This is why PCR can be run automatically in a thermal cycler: the same enzyme survives every cycle.
[bio35-s3.6.b] Each PCR cycle has three temperature steps in a fixed order: hot, cool, then warm. Each step does one job in copying the DNA.
[bio35-s3.6.0] Step 1, denaturation: heat to about 95 °C. The hydrogen bonds between the complementary bases break and the two strands of each DNA molecule separate. Heat does here what helicase (the enzyme that unwinds DNA) does in cells.
[bio35-s3.6.1] Step 2, annealing: cool to about 55 °C (usually between 50 and 65 °C, depending on the primers). The primers bind (anneal) by complementary base pairing to their matching sequences on each single strand.
[bio35-s3.6.2] Step 3, extension: heat to about 72 °C, the optimum temperature of Taq polymerase. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to the end of each primer, complementary to the template strand, building a new strand.
[bio35-s3.6.3] Each double-stranded region becomes two.
[bio35-s3.6.4] Diagram: the PCR cycle as three stages in a circle: DNA at 95 °C → separated strands; primers binding at 55 °C; extension at 72 °C → two copies; “cycle repeats, doubling the DNA each time” (1 → 2 → 4 → 8). Labels: template strand, primer, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, new strand, three temperatures.
[bio35-s3.7.b] Because every cycle doubles the DNA, the number of copies grows very quickly. This is what makes a tiny sample usable.
[bio35-s3.7.0] Each cycle takes a few minutes and doubles the number of copies of the target region, so after n cycles one molecule gives 2ⁿ copies.
[bio35-s3.7.1] 10 cycles give 1,024 copies (2¹⁰); 30 cycles give 2³⁰, about 1 billion (1,073,741,824).
[bio35-s3.7.2] A run is usually 25–35 cycles and takes a few hours.
[bio35-s3.7.3] Kary Mullis invented PCR in 1983 and shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993.
[bio35-s3.7.4] Using Taq polymerase, an enzyme discovered in basic research on bacteria of hot springs, made PCR quick and automatic.
[bio35-s3.7.5] Other uses of PCR: diagnosing infections by detecting a pathogen’s DNA or RNA (e.g. PCR tests for COVID-19), detecting alleles that cause genetic diseases, and preparing DNA for sequencing (reading the order of bases).
[bio35-s3.8.b] Before PCR was used, DNA profiles were made by cutting the DNA rather than copying it. This is the method of Alec Jeffreys, who first developed DNA profiling.
[bio35-s3.8.0] In Alec Jeffreys’ original method, the extracted DNA was cut with restriction enzymes, which cut at specific recognition sequences on either side of the repeat regions.
[bio35-s3.8.1] Because people have different numbers of repeats between the cutting sites, the fragments differed in length between people, and these were separated by gel electrophoresis.
[bio35-s3.8.2] This needed much more DNA than PCR-based profiling, which has replaced it.
[bio35-s3.9.b] Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size using an electric current. Before the current is switched on, the gel, the buffer and the samples must be set up the right way round.
[bio35-s3.9.0] The gel is usually agarose, a jelly made from a polysaccharide extracted from seaweed; it is full of tiny pores that act like a sieve.
[bio35-s3.9.1] The gel is made with a row of small holes (wells) near one end and placed in a tank of buffer solution, which conducts electricity, with the wells at the negative electrode (cathode) end.
[bio35-s3.9.2] Each DNA sample is mixed with a loading dye, which is dense so the sample sinks into the well, and coloured so that the progress of the run can be seen.
[bio35-s3.9.3] Each sample is placed in its own well with a micropipette (a tool for measuring tiny volumes).
[bio35-s3.9.4] One well holds a DNA ladder (size marker): a mixture of fragments of known lengths, which lets you work out the size of the other fragments.
[bio35-s3.10.b] Once the current is on, the DNA moves and sorts itself by size. Because DNA is colourless, the bands then need to be made visible.
[bio35-s3.10.0] Running: when the current is switched on, the negatively charged DNA (its phosphate groups carry the charge) moves through the gel towards the positive electrode (anode).
[bio35-s3.10.1] Small fragments pass through the pores more easily and move faster, so after a set time fragments of different lengths are separated into bands, the smallest furthest from the wells.
[bio35-s3.10.2] The current is switched off before the loading dye reaches the end of the gel, so that no fragments run off the gel.
[bio35-s3.10.3] Making the bands visible: DNA is colourless, so the gel is stained. In schools a safe stain such as methylene blue is used, showing blue bands under white light.
[bio35-s3.10.4] Research laboratories use fluorescent stains that glow under ultraviolet light. The older stain ethidium bromide is a mutagen (it can cause mutations) and must be handled with gloves.
[bio35-s3.10.5] Modern forensic profiling uses primers labelled with fluorescent dyes and separates the fragments in a thin capillary tube in a machine, which reads the result as peaks.
[bio35-s3.10.6] Diagram: an electrophoresis tank, side and top views: gel in buffer, wells at the negative (black) end, positive (red) electrode at the other, power pack, ladder and sample lanes, arrow showing DNA direction. Labels: agarose gel, well, buffer solution, cathode (−), anode (+), DNA ladder, bands, “smaller fragments travel further”.
[bio35-s3.11.b] The final step compares the patterns of bands to decide whether DNA samples match.
[bio35-s3.11.0] The band positions (or peak sizes) of the samples are compared with each other and with the ladder, which gives the size of each fragment.
[bio35-s3.11.1] Matching at every locus (position on the DNA) indicates the same person.
[bio35-s3.11.2] Inheritance of one band per locus from each parent is used in paternity cases, because a child's DNA is a mix of the mother's and the father's.
[bio35-s3.11.3] There is more on this in Investigation: DNA-profile patterns.
