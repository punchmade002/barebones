CANDIDATES bio18-s1: The musculoskeletal system as a system for response
[bio18-s1.0.b] The musculoskeletal system is the framework of bones and joints together with the muscles that move it. Between them they let the body respond to a stimulus (a change in the surroundings) by moving.
[bio18-s1.0.0] The skeleton is the bones, together with the cartilage and ligaments that join them at joints (the places where two bones meet).
[bio18-s1.0.1] The skeletal muscles are attached to bones by tendons, which are the other part of the system.
[bio18-s1.0.2] Muscles provide the force, bones act as rigid levers, and joints are the pivots where movement happens, so a muscle pulling on a bone moves it at a joint.
[bio18-s1.0.3] The parts are only named here. There is more on the bones in "The axial skeleton" and "The appendicular skeleton", and on the joints and muscle pairs in "Muscles and joints".
[bio18-s1.0.4] The adult human skeleton has 206 bones, which are divided into two groups.
[bio18-s1.0.5] 80 of these form the axial skeleton: the skull, vertebral column (backbone), ribs and sternum (breastbone), which make up the central axis of the body.
[bio18-s1.0.6] The other 126 form the appendicular skeleton: the pectoral girdle (shoulders), the pelvic girdle (hips) and the limbs attached to them.
[bio18-s1.1.b] Bones look like lifeless sticks, but bone is a living tissue made of cells. Its structure explains why it is both hard and tough.
[bio18-s1.1.0] Bone is a living connective tissue, which means a tissue that supports and holds the body together.
[bio18-s1.1.1] Bone cells lie in a hard matrix (the material around the cells) of calcium phosphate, which gives bone its hardness and resists compression (being squashed).
[bio18-s1.1.2] Collagen fibres in the matrix give bone some flexibility and resist bending and snapping, so bone is not brittle like chalk.
[bio18-s1.1.3] Bone has a blood supply and nerves, can grow and repair itself, and changes shape slowly in response to the forces put on it.
[bio18-s1.2.b] The skeleton does much more than hold us up. It has five functions, and each one can be illustrated with an example.
[bio18-s1.2.0] Support: the skeleton is a rigid framework that holds the body up, gives it its shape and supports soft organs. The vertebral column supports the head and trunk, and the leg bones carry the body's weight.
[bio18-s1.2.1] Protection: bones surround delicate organs so that knocks do not damage them. The skull protects the brain, the ribs and sternum protect the heart and lungs, the vertebrae protect the spinal cord, and the pelvic girdle protects the bladder and reproductive organs.
[bio18-s1.2.2] Movement: bones provide attachment points for muscles and act as levers that the muscles pull on, moving the body at joints.
[bio18-s1.2.3] Making blood cells: red bone marrow, found in spongy bone (the light, honeycomb-like bone), makes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
[bio18-s1.2.4] In adults, red marrow is mainly in the flat bones (skull, ribs, sternum, vertebrae and pelvis) and in the ends of long bones such as the femur (thigh bone) and humerus (upper arm bone).
[bio18-s1.2.5] Yellow marrow fills the hollow shafts of long bones and stores fat.
[bio18-s1.2.6] Storing minerals: bone stores calcium and phosphate.
[bio18-s1.2.7] When blood calcium falls, calcium is released from bone under the control of parathyroid hormone, which helps keep blood calcium constant. This matters because nerves and muscles need calcium to work. There is more on this in "Glands, locations and hormones".
[bio18-s1.3.b] The body has three types of muscle, and they differ in where they are, whether you can control them, how they look and how they contract. Only one of them belongs to the musculoskeletal system.
[bio18-s1.3.0] Skeletal muscle is attached to bones by tendons and is the only muscle type in the musculoskeletal system. It is under voluntary (conscious) control as well as reflex control, and its cells look striped (striated) under the microscope.
[bio18-s1.3.1] Skeletal muscle contracts quickly and powerfully but tires (fatigues), which is why you cannot keep sprinting indefinitely.
[bio18-s1.3.2] Smooth muscle is found in the walls of the gut, blood vessels, bladder and uterus. It is involuntary (not under conscious control) and not striped.
[bio18-s1.3.3] Smooth muscle contracts slowly and can stay contracted for a long time, as in peristalsis (the waves of squeezing that push food along the gut).
[bio18-s1.3.4] Cardiac muscle is found only in the wall of the heart. It is involuntary and striped, and it contracts rhythmically throughout life without tiring. There is more on the heart in "The Circulatory System and Blood".
[bio18-s1.4.b] A skeletal muscle is the effector that carries out a response, in the same way as the other effectors (the parts that act on a nerve message) such as glands. There is more on this in "Structures and systems for response". To see how it works, start with what it is made of.
[bio18-s1.4.0] A skeletal muscle is made of bundles of long cells called muscle fibres.
[bio18-s1.4.1] Each fibre contains many nuclei and many mitochondria, so it has plenty of control and plenty of energy supply for contraction.
[bio18-s1.4.2] Each fibre is packed with two kinds of protein filament: thin filaments of actin and thick filaments of myosin.
[bio18-s1.4.3] The filaments are arranged in repeating units that give the striped appearance of skeletal muscle.
[bio18-s1.5.b] A skeletal muscle never contracts on its own. It contracts only when it is stimulated by a motor neuron (a nerve cell that carries impulses to a muscle), and the contraction follows a fixed sequence.
[bio18-s1.5.0] 1. Skeletal muscle contracts only when it is stimulated by a motor neuron. A voluntary movement starts in the brain; a reflex movement starts in the spinal cord. There is more on this in "The reflex action".
[bio18-s1.5.1] 2. Impulses travel along the motor neuron to its ending on the muscle fibre, a synapse (a gap between nerve and target) called the neuromuscular junction.
[bio18-s1.5.2] 3. At the neuromuscular junction the neurotransmitter (chemical messenger) acetylcholine is released, crosses the gap and binds to receptors on the muscle fibre, starting an impulse in the fibre.
[bio18-s1.5.3] 4. The impulse causes calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to be released from stores inside the fibre.
[bio18-s1.5.4] 5. The calcium allows myosin filaments to attach to actin filaments and pull them inwards, so the filaments slide past each other and the fibre shortens.
[bio18-s1.5.5] 6. Energy for this comes from ATP, the energy carrier of the cell, which is made in the many mitochondria by respiration.
[bio18-s1.6.b] Contraction is only half the story. The muscle must also relax, and the nervous system must be able to vary how hard the muscle pulls.
[bio18-s1.6.0] When the impulses stop, calcium is pumped back into its stores, the filaments detach and the muscle relaxes.
[bio18-s1.6.1] A relaxed muscle does not lengthen by itself: it is pulled back to its full length by an opposing muscle, which is why muscles work in pairs. There is more on this in "Muscles and joints".
[bio18-s1.6.2] A muscle can only pull by contracting; it can never push.
[bio18-s1.6.3] The force is passed through a tendon to a bone, which moves at a joint. This movement is the response.
[bio18-s1.6.4] Strength of response: the more motor neurons that fire and the more muscle fibres that contract, the stronger the contraction, so movements can be gentle or forceful.
[bio18-s1.6.5] Muscle tone: a slight, continuous partial contraction of muscles holds the body's posture without conscious effort.
[bio18-s1.6.6] Diagram: a flow diagram of a voluntary response: brain, spinal cord, motor neuron, neuromuscular junction, skeletal muscle (contracts), tendon, bone moves at joint. Also a sketch of a skeletal muscle cut open to show bundles of muscle fibres, with one fibre enlarged to show actin and myosin filaments, all labelled.
[bio18-s1.7.b] Because skeletal muscle is attached to bones, the system produces a wide range of responses, from obvious movements to constant small adjustments you never notice.
[bio18-s1.7.0] Movement of the whole body (locomotion: walking, running, swimming) to find food, escape danger or move to better conditions.
[bio18-s1.7.1] Movement of parts of the body, such as grasping or turning the head towards a sound.
[bio18-s1.7.2] Maintaining posture and balance: muscles of the back, neck and legs make constant small adjustments, in response to information from receptors, to keep the body upright.
[bio18-s1.7.3] Breathing: the diaphragm and intercostal muscles (the muscles between the ribs) move the ribs. There is more on this in "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism".
[bio18-s1.7.4] Speech, facial expression, chewing and swallowing all depend on skeletal muscle.
[bio18-s1.7.5] Shivering: rapid involuntary contractions that release heat when the body is cold.
[bio18-s1.8.b] Muscles and bones cannot respond alone. They depend on other body systems to start the contraction, to supply fuel and to prepare them for action.
[bio18-s1.8.0] Nervous system: every contraction of a skeletal muscle is started by a motor neuron.
[bio18-s1.8.1] Receptors in muscles and joints send information back to the brain about the position of the limbs, so that movements can be coordinated.
[bio18-s1.8.2] Circulatory and breathing systems: active muscles need more oxygen and glucose for respiration, and must remove more carbon dioxide and heat, so heart rate and breathing rate rise during exercise.
[bio18-s1.8.3] When oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough, muscle fibres respire anaerobically (without oxygen), producing lactic acid and much less ATP, and soon tire (fatigue).
[bio18-s1.8.4] The lactic acid is removed afterwards, when oxygen is available again; this is one reason breathing stays fast after exercise. There is more on this in "Respiration".
[bio18-s1.8.5] Endocrine system: adrenaline (a hormone) increases blood flow to skeletal muscles and raises blood glucose, preparing them for action.
[bio18-s1.8.6] Health: regular weight-bearing exercise, such as walking or running, strengthens bones and muscles.
[bio18-s1.8.7] A diet with enough protein (for muscle), calcium and vitamin D (for bone) supports the system.

CANDIDATES bio18-s3: The appendicular skeleton
[bio18-s3.0.b] The appendicular skeleton is the skeleton of the limbs and of the two girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton (the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum). It is the part of the skeleton that lets you reach, walk and run.
[bio18-s3.0.0] A girdle is a ring or half-ring of bones that anchors a pair of limbs to the trunk. The pectoral (shoulder) girdle attaches the arms, and the pelvic (hip) girdle attaches the legs.
[bio18-s3.0.1] Its main job is movement, because the limb bones act as levers for the muscles that pull on them. The legs and pelvic girdle also bear the body's weight, so they are built for strength as well as movement.
[bio18-s3.1.b] Most of the limb bones are long bones, and their structure shows how a bone can be both strong and light. Knowing the layers of a long bone also lets you label a section through one.
[bio18-s3.1.0] The limb bones named in the specification, except the patella, are long bones: a long shaft with a wider end at each joint. The patella is a small flat bone, so it is the exception.
[bio18-s3.1.1] The shaft is a hollow tube of dense compact bone. A tube is nearly as strong as a solid rod of the same width but much lighter, so the limb is strong but light enough to move quickly.
[bio18-s3.1.2] The wider ends are made of spongy bone, a lattice of thin bony struts that is light but strong along the lines of force, and that contains red bone marrow.
[bio18-s3.1.3] The central cavity of the shaft (the marrow cavity) contains yellow marrow.
[bio18-s3.1.4] The ends are covered with smooth articular cartilage (cartilage covering the joint surface) where they form joints. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.1.5] Apart from the joint surfaces, the whole bone is covered by a tough membrane, the periosteum, to which tendons (which join muscle to bone) and ligaments (which join bone to bone) attach.
[bio18-s3.1.6] Diagram: a long bone (the femur) in longitudinal section, labelling compact bone, spongy bone, marrow cavity with yellow marrow, red marrow in the spongy bone, articular cartilage, periosteum and shaft.
[bio18-s3.2.b] The pectoral girdle is the shoulder girdle that hangs the arm from the trunk. Its design favours freedom of movement over strength, which is the opposite of the pelvic girdle.
[bio18-s3.2.0] The pectoral girdle is formed on each side by a clavicle and a scapula. It attaches the arm to the axial skeleton and is built for a wide range of movement rather than for bearing weight.
[bio18-s3.2.1] The clavicle (collarbone) is a slender, S-shaped bone running across the top of the chest from the sternum (breastbone) to the scapula.
[bio18-s3.2.2] The clavicle acts as a strut, holding the shoulder out to the side so the arm can swing freely, and passes forces from the arm to the axial skeleton.
[bio18-s3.2.3] Its joint with the sternum is the only bony connection between the arm and the axial skeleton, which is why the arm is so mobile.
[bio18-s3.2.4] The scapula (shoulder blade) is a large, flat, triangular bone lying on the back of the rib cage.
[bio18-s3.2.5] The scapula is not joined to the ribs or vertebrae but is held in place by muscles, so it can slide and rotate, increasing the range of movement of the arm.
[bio18-s3.2.6] Its large flat surface and ridges give a large area for attachment of shoulder and arm muscles, including the biceps and triceps. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.2.7] The scapula has a shallow socket into which the rounded head of the humerus (the upper arm bone) fits, forming the shoulder joint, a ball-and-socket joint. The shallow socket allows the arm to move in almost every direction, including rotation, but makes the joint less stable than the hip.
[bio18-s3.3.b] The pelvic girdle is the hip girdle that joins the legs to the trunk. Because the legs carry the whole body, it is built for stability and strength rather than range of movement.
[bio18-s3.3.0] The pelvic girdle (pelvis) is a strong, bowl-shaped ring formed by the two hip bones, joined firmly to the sacrum (the base of the vertebral column) at the back and to each other at the front.
[bio18-s3.3.1] Each hip bone is made of several bones fused together; the names of these bones are not required.
[bio18-s3.3.2] The pelvic girdle is joined firmly to the vertebral column through the sacrum, so it is rigid. It carries the weight of the upper body and passes it to the legs, which makes it much stronger and less movable than the pectoral girdle.
[bio18-s3.3.3] It has a deep, cup-shaped socket on each side into which the head of the femur (the thigh bone) fits, forming the hip joint, a ball-and-socket joint.
[bio18-s3.3.4] The deep socket makes the hip very stable and suited to bearing weight, but it gives a smaller range of movement than the shoulder.
[bio18-s3.3.5] The pelvic girdle protects the organs of the lower abdomen: the bladder, lower intestine and reproductive organs, including the uterus.
[bio18-s3.3.6] Its broad surfaces anchor the large muscles of the legs and trunk.
[bio18-s3.3.7] The female pelvis is wider and shallower, with a larger, rounder central opening than the male pelvis, so that a baby's head can pass through during birth.
[bio18-s3.4.b] The two girdles do different jobs, and each difference in structure explains a difference in function. Set them side by side to answer comparison questions.
[bio18-s3.4.0] Attachment: the pectoral girdle is loosely attached to the axial skeleton by muscles, so the arm is free to move; the pelvic girdle is fused to the sacrum, so it is rigid.
[bio18-s3.4.1] Socket: the pectoral girdle has a shallow socket, which allows a wide range of movement; the pelvic girdle has a deep socket, which holds the femur firmly.
[bio18-s3.4.2] Function: the pectoral girdle gives mobility; the pelvic girdle gives stability and carries weight.
[bio18-s3.5.b] The arm has one bone in the upper arm and two in the forearm. Each joint between them is a different type, which gives the arm both stability and mobility.
[bio18-s3.5.0] The humerus is the long bone of the upper arm. Its rounded head fits into the socket of the scapula at the shoulder, a ball-and-socket joint.
[bio18-s3.5.1] The lower end of the humerus meets the radius and ulna at the elbow, a hinge joint (a joint that bends in one direction only). Its long shaft gives attachment to the muscles that move the shoulder and elbow.
[bio18-s3.5.2] The ulna is the longer forearm bone, on the little-finger side.
[bio18-s3.5.3] The upper end of the ulna hooks around the end of the humerus, forming the main part of the elbow hinge and the point of the elbow, which stops the arm bending backwards beyond straight.
[bio18-s3.5.4] The triceps (the muscle at the back of the upper arm) is attached to the ulna. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.5.5] The radius is the shorter forearm bone, on the thumb side. It forms most of the joint with the wrist.
[bio18-s3.5.6] At the elbow, the rounded head of the radius turns against the ulna in a pivot joint, so the radius can rotate around the ulna to turn the palm up or down. The biceps (the muscle at the front of the upper arm) is attached to the radius.
[bio18-s3.5.7] The two forearm bones make the hand both strong and highly mobile: the ulna gives a stable hinge at the elbow, and the radius allows rotation of the hand.
[bio18-s3.6.b] The leg has one bone in the thigh and two in the lower leg, plus the patella at the knee. These bones carry the body's weight and drive walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s3.6.0] The femur (thigh bone) is the longest and strongest bone in the body.
[bio18-s3.6.1] The rounded head of the femur fits into the deep socket of the pelvic girdle at the hip. Its lower end forms the knee joint, a hinge joint, with the tibia.
[bio18-s3.6.2] The thick shaft of compact bone carries the body's weight, and it gives attachment to the large thigh muscles used in walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s3.6.3] The patella (kneecap) is a small, flat, roughly triangular bone at the front of the knee, embedded in the tendon of the quadriceps, the large muscle at the front of the thigh.
[bio18-s3.6.4] The patella protects the front of the knee joint and holds the tendon away from the joint, improving the leverage of the quadriceps when it straightens the leg.
[bio18-s3.6.5] The tibia (shinbone) is the larger, inner bone of the lower leg, on the big-toe side. It forms the knee joint with the femur and the ankle joint below, and carries almost all the weight passed down from the femur.
[bio18-s3.6.6] The fibula is the thin outer bone of the lower leg. It is not part of the knee joint and carries little weight, but it provides attachment for muscles of the lower leg and helps form and stabilise the outer side of the ankle.
[bio18-s3.6.7] The wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones are not named in the specification; label them on diagrams as wrist bones, hand bones, ankle bones and foot bones. Many small bones give the hands their fine, flexible movements and give the feet a flexible, weight-spreading base.
[bio18-s3.7.b] The arm and the leg look different, but they follow the same basic plan. This similarity is a useful pattern to spot on a skeleton diagram.
[bio18-s3.7.0] The arm and the leg are built to the same plan: one bone in the upper part (humerus; femur), two in the lower part (radius and ulna; tibia and fibula), then many small bones in the wrist or ankle and five digits.
[bio18-s3.7.1] The same plan in the forelimbs of other vertebrates is evidence of common ancestry, because it suggests the animals inherited the pattern from a shared ancestor. There is more on this in Explaining biological phenomena.
[bio18-s3.8.b] Putting the axial and appendicular parts together on one diagram shows how the girdles and limbs attach to the central skeleton.
[bio18-s3.8.0] Diagram: front view of the whole human skeleton, labelling the axial parts (skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum) and the appendicular parts named in the specification (clavicle, scapula, pelvic girdle, humerus, radius, ulna, femur, patella, tibia and fibula), with the wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones labelled as groups.

CANDIDATES bio18-s4: Muscles and joints
[bio18-s4.0.b] Bones only help the body move where they meet other bones. These meeting places are called joints, and they are grouped by how much movement they allow.
[bio18-s4.0.0] A joint is a place where two or more bones meet. Joints are grouped by how much movement they allow, from none at all to a wide range.
[bio18-s4.0.1] Fixed (immovable) joints join bones with fibrous tissue and allow no movement, so they protect and hold structures rigid, for example the sutures of the cranium (the joins between the bones of the skull).
[bio18-s4.0.2] Slightly movable (cartilaginous) joints join bones with cartilage (a firm, flexible tissue) and allow a little movement, for example between vertebrae, which are joined by intervertebral discs. There is more on this in The axial skeleton.
[bio18-s4.0.3] Freely movable (synovial) joints have bones separated by a fluid-filled cavity and allow a wide range of movement, for example the shoulder, elbow, hip and knee.
[bio18-s4.1.b] Not all freely movable joints move in the same way. Their types are named after the shape of the surfaces and the movement this allows.
[bio18-s4.1.0] Ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip): a rounded head fits into a cup, so movement is possible in all directions and the limb can also rotate.
[bio18-s4.1.1] Hinge joints (elbow, knee): movement is in one plane only, bending and straightening, like a door opening and closing on its hinge.
[bio18-s4.1.2] Pivot joints: one bone rotates around another, for example the atlas (the top vertebra) turning on the axis (the second vertebra) to turn the head, and the radius turning against the ulna to rotate the forearm.
[bio18-s4.1.3] Gliding joints: flat surfaces slide over each other, for example between the small wrist and ankle bones, so only small sliding movements are possible.
[bio18-s4.2.b] A synovial joint has to move smoothly thousands of times without the bone ends wearing away. Three parts working together make this possible: the cartilage on the bone ends, the membrane that makes the fluid, and the fluid itself.
[bio18-s4.2.0] Articular cartilage (hyaline cartilage) is a smooth, slippery, slightly compressible layer covering the ends of the bones inside the joint.
[bio18-s4.2.1] Articular cartilage reduces friction so the bones glide over each other, absorbs shock to cushion the bones against impact, and prevents the bone ends from wearing each other away.
[bio18-s4.2.2] Cartilage has no blood vessels, so it gets its nutrients from the synovial fluid and heals slowly if it is damaged.
[bio18-s4.2.3] The synovial membrane is a thin membrane lining the inside of the joint capsule, but it does not cover the cartilage. It secretes synovial fluid into the joint cavity.
[bio18-s4.2.4] Synovial fluid is a thick, slippery fluid filling the joint cavity. It lubricates the joint, reducing friction and wear, and helps absorb shock.
[bio18-s4.2.5] Synovial fluid also supplies oxygen and nutrients to the cartilage and removes its wastes, which is how cartilage stays alive without a blood supply of its own.
[bio18-s4.3.b] Smooth surfaces are not enough: the joint must also be held together and be moved by muscles. The capsule and ligaments hold the bones in place, tendons connect the muscles that move them, and in the knee fibrocartilage pads add extra cushioning.
[bio18-s4.3.0] The joint capsule is a tough, fibrous sleeve that encloses the joint, holds the synovial fluid in and helps hold the bones together. It is strengthened by ligaments.
[bio18-s4.3.1] Ligaments are tough, strong bands of fibrous connective tissue, mainly collagen with some elastic fibres, that join bone to bone across the joint.
[bio18-s4.3.2] Ligaments hold the bones of the joint together and keep them in line. Being slightly elastic, they allow the joint to move in its normal directions while limiting movement in others, which prevents dislocation (the bones being forced out of position).
[bio18-s4.3.3] Some ligaments lie outside the capsule and some inside the joint, for example inside the knee.
[bio18-s4.3.4] Tendons are tough, strong, inelastic (non-stretchy) cords of fibrous connective tissue, mainly collagen, that join muscle to bone. The Achilles tendon, for example, joins the calf muscle to the heel bone.
[bio18-s4.3.5] A tendon transmits the pull of a contracting muscle to the bone, so the bone moves at the joint. Because it does not stretch, all of the muscle's pull goes into moving the bone.
[bio18-s4.3.6] Pads of fibrocartilage (cartilage with extra tough fibres) inside some joints, the menisci in the knee, deepen the joint surfaces, spread the load and absorb shock.
[bio18-s4.3.7] Diagram: a generalised synovial joint (or the knee or hip) in section, labelling the two bones, articular cartilage, joint cavity with synovial fluid, synovial membrane, joint capsule, ligament, and a tendon attaching a muscle to one bone.
[bio18-s4.4.b] The knee shows how every part of a synovial joint has its own job and how they combine to let the leg move without the joint falling apart.
[bio18-s4.4.0] At the knee, articular cartilage on the femur (thigh bone), tibia (shin bone) and the back of the patella (kneecap), together with the synovial fluid, lets the bones glide over each other.
[bio18-s4.4.1] Ligaments inside and outside the joint keep the femur and tibia aligned and stop the knee bending sideways or backwards.
[bio18-s4.4.2] The quadriceps tendon, which contains the patella, passes the pull of the thigh muscle to the tibia to straighten the leg.
[bio18-s4.5.b] Ligaments, tendons and cartilage are all tough tissues found at joints, so they are easily confused. They differ in what they join, how much they stretch and the job they do.
[bio18-s4.5.0] What each joins: a ligament joins bone to bone; a tendon joins muscle to bone; cartilage either covers the ends of bones (articular cartilage) or joins bones (discs).
[bio18-s4.5.1] Elasticity: a ligament is slightly elastic, allowing some movement; a tendon is inelastic, so it transmits force without wasting it; cartilage is firm but compressible, so it absorbs shock.
[bio18-s4.5.2] Main function: a ligament holds the joint together and prevents dislocation; a tendon moves the bone when the muscle contracts; cartilage reduces friction and absorbs shock.
[bio18-s4.6.b] Muscles can only do one thing: shorten. That limitation is why the body arranges muscles in opposing pairs around each joint.
[bio18-s4.6.0] A muscle can only contract and pull, never push, so each movement at a joint needs a second muscle to pull the bone back.
[bio18-s4.6.1] An antagonistic pair is two muscles that produce opposite movements at the same joint: when one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched.
[bio18-s4.6.2] The flexor bends a joint (flexion); the extensor straightens it (extension).
[bio18-s4.6.3] Each muscle is attached by tendons at both ends. The origin is the attachment to the bone that stays still; the insertion is the attachment to the bone that moves.
[bio18-s4.6.4] When the muscle contracts it shortens (and becomes fatter), pulling the insertion towards the origin and so moving the bone.
[bio18-s4.7.b] The biceps and triceps in the upper arm are the standard example of an antagonistic pair, and the thigh muscles at the knee are a second one.
[bio18-s4.7.0] At the elbow, the biceps lies at the front of the upper arm, with its origin on the scapula (shoulder blade) and its insertion on the radius.
[bio18-s4.7.1] The triceps lies at the back of the upper arm, with its origin on the scapula and humerus and its insertion on the ulna.
[bio18-s4.7.2] Bending the arm (flexion): the biceps contracts and shortens, pulling the radius and forearm up, while the triceps relaxes and is stretched. The biceps is the flexor.
[bio18-s4.7.3] Straightening the arm (extension): the triceps contracts, pulling the ulna and forearm down, while the biceps relaxes and is stretched. The triceps is the extensor.
[bio18-s4.7.4] A second example is the knee: the quadriceps (front of the thigh) contracts to straighten the leg, as the extensor, and the hamstrings (back of the thigh) contract to bend it, as the flexor.
[bio18-s4.8.b] The arm works as a lever, and the nervous system controls the pair so that movement is smooth or the joint is held still.
[bio18-s4.8.0] Lever action: the forearm acts as a lever with the elbow joint as the pivot (the fixed point it turns around).
[bio18-s4.8.1] The biceps is inserted close to the elbow, so a small shortening of the muscle moves the hand through a large distance quickly, at the cost of needing a large muscle force.
[bio18-s4.8.2] Coordination: the nervous system sends impulses to one muscle of the pair while the other is allowed to relax, so movements are smooth.
[bio18-s4.8.3] Both muscles of a pair contracting slightly together hold a joint steady in one position, for example when holding a weight still.
[bio18-s4.8.4] Diagram: two drawings of the arm, bent and straight, showing scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, elbow joint, biceps and triceps with their tendons, each muscle labelled contracted or relaxed in each position, with arrows showing the direction of pull.
[bio18-s4.9.b] A simple model of the arm lets you see antagonistic muscles work. Each part of the model stands for a real part of the arm.
[bio18-s4.9.0] A simple physical model uses two strips of stiff card or wood to represent the upper arm (humerus) and the forearm (radius and ulna), joined at one end by a split pin, which represents the elbow hinge joint.
[bio18-s4.9.1] Two pieces of string, elastic bands or long balloons represent the biceps and triceps. Each is fixed to the upper arm at one end (origin) and to the forearm at the other (insertion), one on the front and one on the back, with their ends standing for tendons.
[bio18-s4.9.2] Pulling (shortening) the front string raises the forearm while the back string slackens. This shows the biceps contracting and the triceps relaxing in flexion.
[bio18-s4.9.3] Pulling the back string lowers the forearm, showing extension.
[bio18-s4.9.4] Pushing on a string does nothing, which shows that a muscle can only pull.
[bio18-s4.9.5] The model shows that muscles work in pairs with opposite effects, that a muscle must be attached on both sides of a joint to move it, and that an insertion close to the joint gives a large movement of the forearm end (the lever action).
[bio18-s4.10.b] No model is perfect. Judging a model means saying what it leaves out or gets wrong compared with the real arm.
[bio18-s4.10.0] Real muscles shorten actively by contracting and get fatter, while the model's strings are pulled by hand.
[bio18-s4.10.1] The model has no articular cartilage, synovial fluid or ligaments, so it does not show how the joint surfaces glide or how the bones are held together.
[bio18-s4.10.2] A split pin allows movement in only one plane and does not show the rotation of the radius.
[bio18-s4.10.3] The model has no nervous control and no energy supply (ATP), whereas real muscles need impulses to contract and ATP as their energy source.
[bio18-s4.10.4] Real tendons are inelastic, while elastic bands stretch.

CANDIDATES bio19-s1: CNS and PNS
[bio19-s1.0.b] The nervous system detects stimuli (changes in the surroundings or inside the body) and coordinates responses by carrying nerve impulses. To do this it is split into two divisions: one that makes decisions and one that carries messages.
[bio19-s1.0.0] The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord, the control centre of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.1] The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is all the nerves outside the CNS, so it reaches every part of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.2] The CNS receives information, processes it and sends out instructions, which is why it is the part that decides what to do.
[bio19-s1.0.3] The PNS carries impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body, so it is the communication line that links the CNS to receptors and effectors.
[bio19-s1.0.4] A neuron is a nerve cell; a nerve is a bundle of many neuron fibres wrapped in connective tissue, so one nerve carries many separate signals at once.
[bio19-s1.0.5] A nerve impulse is an electrochemical signal travelling along a neuron, which is how information moves around the body.
[bio19-s1.1.b] Information has to travel into the CNS and instructions have to travel out again. The PNS does both jobs using two kinds of neuron, and its fibres run through the body bundled in nerves.
[bio19-s1.1.0] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect stimuli) to the CNS, so the CNS learns what is happening.
[bio19-s1.1.1] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors, which are muscles and glands, so the CNS's instructions produce a response such as a movement or a secretion.
[bio19-s1.1.2] Nerve fibres of the PNS run through the body inside nerves, which carry them to almost every tissue.
[bio19-s1.1.3] Humans have 31 pairs of spinal nerves, which leave the spinal cord between the vertebrae (the bones of the backbone).
[bio19-s1.1.4] Each spinal nerve joins the cord by two roots, short branches that attach it to the spinal cord, so sensory and motor fibres enter and leave by separate routes.
[bio19-s1.1.5] The dorsal (back) root carries sensory fibres into the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.1.6] The ventral (front) root carries motor fibres out of the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.b] A neuron's cell body (the part containing the nucleus) can sit in different places depending on the type of neuron. Knowing where each type lies tells you which part of the nervous system it belongs to.
[bio19-s1.2.0] Sensory neuron cell bodies lie in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on the dorsal root just outside the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.1] Motor neuron cell bodies lie in the grey matter of the CNS, inside the cord, and their fibres leave through the ventral root.
[bio19-s1.2.2] Interneurons (neurons that link one neuron to another) lie entirely within the CNS, so they never leave the brain or spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.3.b] The spinal cord runs from the base of the brain down the back, inside the vertebral column, which protects it. It is the main route between the brain and the rest of the body.
[bio19-s1.3.0] The spinal cord relays impulses between the body and the brain, so sensory information travels up to the brain and instructions travel back down.
[bio19-s1.3.1] It carries out spinal reflexes, which are fast automatic responses, such as the withdrawal reflex when you touch something painful. There is more on this in The reflex action.
[bio19-s1.4.b] Cut straight across (a transverse section), the spinal cord shows a clear pattern of grey and white tissue around a central canal. The two tissues differ because they are made of different parts of neurons.
[bio19-s1.4.0] A central canal, containing cerebrospinal fluid (a watery fluid that cushions the CNS), lies at the centre of the cord.
[bio19-s1.4.1] Around the canal is an H- or butterfly-shaped core of grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.2] White matter surrounds the grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.3] Grey matter is made of cell bodies and unmyelinated interneurons (interneurons without a fatty myelin sheath), which is why it looks grey.
[bio19-s1.4.4] White matter is made of myelinated axons (long fibres coated in myelin); it is white because myelin is fatty.
[bio19-s1.4.5] In the brain the arrangement is reversed: the cerebral cortex is grey matter on the outside, with white matter beneath.
[bio19-s1.4.6] Diagram: transverse section of the spinal cord labelling central canal, grey matter, white matter, dorsal root, dorsal root ganglion, ventral root and spinal nerve.
[bio19-s1.5.b] The brain and spinal cord control the whole body, so they need strong protection. The CNS is protected by bone, membranes and fluid.
[bio19-s1.5.0] The brain is protected by the skull (cranium), a rigid bony case.
[bio19-s1.5.1] The spinal cord is protected by the vertebrae, which form a bony tunnel around it.
[bio19-s1.5.2] Three membranes, the meninges, surround the brain and spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.5.3] Cerebrospinal fluid lies between the inner meninges and fills the brain's cavities and the central canal.
[bio19-s1.5.4] Cerebrospinal fluid cushions the CNS against shock, so a knock to the head or back is absorbed, and it supplies the CNS with nutrients.
[bio19-s1.5.5] Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, usually caused by bacterial or viral infection.
[bio19-s1.6.b] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and the part that handles most of its higher functions, such as thought and voluntary movement.
[bio19-s1.6.0] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain, divided into left and right cerebral hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum.
[bio19-s1.6.1] The cerebral cortex, its outer layer, is grey matter deeply folded to increase its surface area, and so the number of neurons.
[bio19-s1.6.2] Each cerebral hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body, so the left hemisphere controls the right side and vice versa.
[bio19-s1.6.3] The cerebrum controls voluntary movement (movement you choose to make) and interprets the senses.
[bio19-s1.6.4] It also governs memory, thought, language, intelligence and personality.
[bio19-s1.7.b] Smooth, accurate movement needs a part of the brain dedicated to coordinating it. That is the cerebellum.
[bio19-s1.7.0] The cerebellum lies at the back of the brain, below the cerebrum, and also has a folded surface.
[bio19-s1.7.1] It coordinates muscular movement and controls balance and posture.
[bio19-s1.8.b] Some vital actions must carry on without you thinking about them, even during sleep. The medulla oblongata controls these.
[bio19-s1.8.0] The medulla oblongata lies at the base of the brain, where it joins the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.8.1] It controls involuntary actions (those that happen without conscious control), including breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure.
[bio19-s1.8.2] It controls reflexes such as swallowing, coughing and sneezing.
[bio19-s1.9.b] The hypothalamus is a small region that keeps conditions in the body steady and connects the nervous system to the hormone system.
[bio19-s1.9.0] The hypothalamus lies below the cerebrum and above the pituitary gland, to which it is connected.
[bio19-s1.9.1] It controls homeostasis (keeping the internal environment steady): body temperature, water balance, hunger and thirst. There is more on this in Homeostasis.
[bio19-s1.9.2] It links the nervous and hormonal systems by controlling the pituitary gland, so nerve signals can lead to hormone release.
[bio19-s1.10.b] You may be asked to label the brain, so know where each part sits relative to the others.
[bio19-s1.10.0] Diagram: vertical section of the brain labelling cerebrum, cerebral cortex, corpus callosum, cerebellum, medulla oblongata, hypothalamus and pituitary gland, with the spinal cord continuing below.

CANDIDATES bio19-s2: The reflex action
[bio19-s2.0.b] Every reflex, and every nervous response, is built from the same three parts: something that triggers it, something that detects it and something that acts. A reflex begins with a stimulus that a receptor detects and ends with a response from an effector.
[bio19-s2.0.0] A stimulus is a change in the internal or external environment that can be detected, such as heat on the skin or an object approaching the eye.
[bio19-s2.0.1] A receptor detects the stimulus. It is a cell or nerve ending that is sensitive to that particular type of change, and it starts the impulse.
[bio19-s2.0.2] An effector, a muscle or a gland, carries out the response. A muscle responds by contracting; a gland responds by secreting.
[bio19-s2.1.b] Some situations need a response so quickly that there is no time to think. A reflex action is the body's built-in solution: a response that is fixed and does not wait for a decision.
[bio19-s2.1.0] A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, happening without conscious thought. Involuntary means you do not choose it or control it.
[bio19-s2.1.1] Reflexes protect the body from harm, for example pulling a hand from a hot object or blinking when something approaches the eye, so damage is limited before you even notice.
[bio19-s2.1.2] Reflexes also maintain posture and normal body functions; coughing and sneezing clear the airways, for example.
[bio19-s2.1.3] Reflexes are inborn, so they are not learned. You are born able to do them.
[bio19-s2.1.4] The same stimulus always produces the same response, which is what makes a reflex automatic and predictable.
[bio19-s2.2.b] A reflex works because the nerve pathway involved is short and fixed. The reflex arc is the pathway an impulse (the electrical signal carried by nerve cells) follows in a reflex action.
[bio19-s2.2.0] The pathway runs receptor, sensory neuron, interneuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord, motor neuron, effector. The sensory neuron carries the impulse in, the motor neuron carries it out, and the interneuron connects them.
[bio19-s2.2.1] The sensory neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root (the back root of the spinal nerve); its cell body lies in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on that root.
[bio19-s2.2.2] The motor neuron leaves the spinal cord through the ventral root (the front root), carrying the impulse out to the effector.
[bio19-s2.2.3] Diagram: reflex arc drawn through a transverse section of the spinal cord, labelling receptor in the skin, sensory neuron, dorsal root ganglion, interneuron, grey matter, white matter, motor neuron, ventral root and effector muscle, with arrows showing the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s2.3.b] Touching a hot object shows the whole reflex arc at work. Follow the impulse from the finger to the muscle that pulls the hand away.
[bio19-s2.3.0] 1. Pain and heat receptors in the skin of the finger are stimulated by the hot object.
[bio19-s2.3.1] 2. An impulse travels along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.3.2] 3. It passes across a synapse (the tiny gap between two neurons) to an interneuron in the grey matter.
[bio19-s2.3.3] 4. It passes across a synapse to a motor neuron.
[bio19-s2.3.4] 5. The motor neuron carries the impulse to the biceps, the effector.
[bio19-s2.3.5] 6. The biceps contracts and the hand is pulled away from the object.
[bio19-s2.4.b] A reflex is faster than a voluntary action because of the route it takes. The brain is not left out, though: it is told what happened a moment later.
[bio19-s2.4.0] The impulse takes a short route through the spinal cord, crossing few synapses. Each synapse adds a small delay, so fewer synapses means a faster response.
[bio19-s2.4.1] It does not wait for a decision by the brain, which is why reflexes are rapid.
[bio19-s2.4.2] Interneurons also carry impulses up the spinal cord to the brain, so pain is felt just after the hand has moved.
[bio19-s2.4.3] The brain can sometimes override a reflex, for example when a person holds on to a hot plate to avoid dropping it.
[bio19-s2.5.b] The knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is the simplest reflex arc, because it leaves out the interneuron. It is a useful contrast with the withdrawal reflex.
[bio19-s2.5.0] Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches the thigh muscle, which is the stimulus.
[bio19-s2.5.1] The sensory neuron synapses directly with the motor neuron in the spinal cord, with no interneuron, so the pathway has only one synapse.
[bio19-s2.5.2] The leg kicks. Doctors use this reflex to test the nervous system, because a normal response shows the reflex pathway is working.
[bio19-s2.6.b] Not every reflex passes through the spinal cord. Some are controlled by the brain instead, but they are still rapid, automatic and involuntary.
[bio19-s2.6.0] Some reflexes are controlled by the brain rather than the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.6.1] Examples are blinking, coughing, sneezing and the pupil narrowing in bright light.

CANDIDATES bio19-s3: Neurons
[bio19-s3.0.b] Your body has to pass information quickly between receptors (cells that detect changes), the brain and spinal cord, and muscles and glands. Neurons are the cells that do this job, and there are three types, each with its own role.
[bio19-s3.0.0] A neuron, or nerve cell, is the basic unit of the nervous system; it carries electrochemical nerve impulses (tiny electrical-and-chemical signals that travel along the cell).
[bio19-s3.0.1] Neurons are long and thin, so a single cell can connect distant parts of the body, for example the skin of the foot and the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.0.2] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect a stimulus) to the CNS (the central nervous system: brain and spinal cord).
[bio19-s3.0.3] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands, which carry out the response).
[bio19-s3.0.4] Interneurons, also called relay or connector neurons, carry impulses between neurons within the CNS, so they link the incoming and outgoing pathways.
[bio19-s3.1.b] Every neuron is built from the same three main regions: a cell body, branched dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that sends them on. The shape of each region suits its job.
[bio19-s3.1.0] The cell body contains the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm and organelles, which keeps the cell alive.
[bio19-s3.1.1] The cell body also makes the neurotransmitter (the chemical that passes the signal to the next cell) and the proteins the neuron needs.
[bio19-s3.1.2] Dendrites are short, branched extensions that receive impulses from other neurons or receptors and carry them towards the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.3] Branching gives dendrites a large surface, so one neuron can receive signals from many others.
[bio19-s3.1.4] The axon is a long single fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.5] The axon's length lets impulses reach distant effectors, for example a muscle in the foot.
[bio19-s3.2.b] Many axons are wrapped in a fatty coat that makes impulses travel faster. Fast conduction matters because the body must respond quickly, for example when you pull your hand from something hot.
[bio19-s3.2.0] The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer around many axons, so the impulse does not leak away along the way.
[bio19-s3.2.1] It speeds up the impulse, which jumps between the small gaps in the sheath called nodes of Ranvier.
[bio19-s3.2.2] Schwann cells are cells that wrap around the axon in the PNS (the peripheral nervous system: the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord), their membrane forming the myelin sheath.
[bio19-s3.3.b] At its far end an axon has to hand the impulse on to the next cell, whether another neuron, a muscle or a gland. The axon terminals are built for this hand-over.
[bio19-s3.3.0] The axon ends in swollen synaptic knobs, also called axon terminals.
[bio19-s3.3.1] Neurotransmitter vesicles are tiny sacs in the synaptic knobs that store neurotransmitter, so a supply is ready to be released.
[bio19-s3.3.2] The neurotransmitter is released to pass the impulse to the next cell. There is more on this in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s3.4.b] A motor neuron carries the command from the CNS out to an effector. Its structure is arranged to collect many inputs inside the CNS and then send one signal a long way out.
[bio19-s3.4.0] A motor neuron has its cell body at one end, inside the CNS, with many dendrites.
[bio19-s3.4.1] Its many dendrites receive input from many interneurons, so one motor neuron can combine signals from many sources.
[bio19-s3.4.2] It has one long axon, which ends in axon terminals on a muscle or gland, so the axon reaches the effector wherever it lies in the body.
[bio19-s3.5.b] A sensory neuron brings information in from a receptor to the CNS. It has a different layout from a motor neuron, with its cell body off to one side.
[bio19-s3.5.0] A sensory neuron has a long fibre, the dendron, which carries impulses from the receptor to the cell body. A dendron is not the same as a dendrite, which is short and branched.
[bio19-s3.5.1] Its cell body sits to one side of the fibre, in a ganglion (a cluster of cell bodies) outside the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.2] A shorter axon carries the impulse on into the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.3] The long dendron reaches from a receptor, for example in the skin of the foot, to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.5.4] The cell body lies off the main pathway, so it does not slow the impulse.
[bio19-s3.6.b] Between the sensory and motor neurons sits a third type that links them. Interneurons make the CNS a network rather than a set of separate wires.
[bio19-s3.6.0] Interneurons are short, with many branched connections, so each can link with many other neurons.
[bio19-s3.6.1] They are found only in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.6.2] They connect sensory neurons to motor neurons and link many neurons together.
[bio19-s3.7.b] You are often asked to label a neuron diagram or to say which type is shown. Practise drawing both types so that you can place every part and show the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.7.0] Diagram: a motor neuron and a sensory neuron, labelling cell body, nucleus, dendrites, dendron (sensory), axon, myelin sheath, Schwann cell, node of Ranvier, axon terminals, neurotransmitter vesicles, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.8.b] The importance of myelin shows when it is damaged. Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which this happens.
[bio19-s3.8.0] Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease (the immune system attacks the body's own tissue) in which the body's immune system damages the myelin sheath in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.8.1] Damaged myelin slows or blocks impulses, because the insulation that lets them jump between the nodes of Ranvier is lost.
[bio19-s3.8.2] Symptoms include problems with movement, balance and vision.

CANDIDATES bio19-s4: Neurotransmission
[bio19-s4.0.b] A nerve impulse (a signal carried by nerve cells, called neurons) rarely travels through just one neuron. It passes along a chain of neurons from receptor to effector, and the signal changes form along the way.
[bio19-s4.0.0] The chain runs receptor (detects the stimulus), sensory neuron (carries the signal towards the central nervous system), interneuron or interneurons (link neurons inside the central nervous system), motor neuron (carries the signal outwards), effector (the muscle or gland that responds).
[bio19-s4.0.1] Within a single neuron the impulse is electrical: a wave of electrical change along the membrane, caused by ions (charged particles) moving in and out of the neuron.
[bio19-s4.0.2] Between neurons the impulse is passed on chemically, across a synapse (the junction between two neurons). So the signal is electrical inside each neuron and chemical in the gaps between them.
[bio19-s4.1.b] The neurons in a chain do not physically touch, so something must carry the signal from one to the next. That job is done at the synapse, using a chemical messenger.
[bio19-s4.1.0] A synapse is the junction between two neurons, or between a neuron and an effector such as a muscle fibre (a single muscle cell).
[bio19-s4.1.1] The two cells do not touch; a tiny gap called the synaptic cleft separates them, which is why the electrical impulse cannot simply carry on across.
[bio19-s4.1.2] The neuron before the cleft is the presynaptic neuron; the one after it is the postsynaptic neuron. Pre means before and post means after.
[bio19-s4.1.3] The presynaptic axon (the long fibre of the neuron) ends in a swollen synaptic knob containing mitochondria, which supply energy, and vesicles (small membrane sacs) of neurotransmitter.
[bio19-s4.1.4] A neurotransmitter is a chemical released by a neuron that carries the impulse across a synapse.
[bio19-s4.1.5] Examples of neurotransmitters are acetylcholine, noradrenaline, dopamine and serotonin.
[bio19-s4.2.b] A labelled synapse diagram is a common exam task, and it also shows you where each part sits relative to the cleft.
[bio19-s4.2.0] Diagram: synapse labelling presynaptic neuron, synaptic knob, mitochondria, vesicles, neurotransmitter molecules, synaptic cleft, receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane, postsynaptic neuron, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s4.3.b] Crossing the cleft is a sequence of steps in which the electrical impulse is converted into a chemical signal and then back into an electrical one. Learn the steps in order.
[bio19-s4.3.0] 1. The impulse arrives at the synaptic knob.
[bio19-s4.3.1] 2. This causes calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to move into the knob.
[bio19-s4.3.2] 3. The calcium ions trigger vesicles to move to the presynaptic membrane, fuse with it and release neurotransmitter into the cleft.
[bio19-s4.3.3] 4. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft. The gap is tiny, so this is quick.
[bio19-s4.3.4] 5. It binds to specific receptor proteins on the postsynaptic membrane; each receptor fits only its own neurotransmitter, like a key in a lock.
[bio19-s4.3.5] 6. Binding opens ion channels in the postsynaptic membrane, ions move in, and a new impulse starts in the postsynaptic neuron if enough neurotransmitter binds.
[bio19-s4.3.6] Mitochondria in the knob supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) for making and releasing neurotransmitter.
[bio19-s4.4.b] Synapses are not just gaps to cross. Because the signal is chemical, they control how signals travel and which ones get through.
[bio19-s4.4.0] Impulses cross in one direction only, because only the presynaptic side has vesicles and only the postsynaptic side has receptors. The neurotransmitter therefore cannot send a signal backwards.
[bio19-s4.4.1] A weak stimulus may release too little neurotransmitter to start a new impulse, so synapses filter out unimportant signals.
[bio19-s4.4.2] One neuron can synapse with many others, so impulses can be spread to many cells or combined from many cells.
[bio19-s4.4.3] Many drugs act at synapses, since the chemical step can be altered. There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.

CANDIDATES bio19-s5: Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption
[bio19-s5.0.b] A synapse is the junction where one neuron passes a signal to the next across a tiny gap, the synaptic cleft. The signal crosses the gap as a chemical, a neurotransmitter, and the release of that chemical is switched on by ions.
[bio19-s5.0.0] Release is triggered by ions: calcium ions entering the synaptic knob (the swollen tip of the presynaptic neuron, the neuron sending the signal) are the trigger for the neurotransmitter to be let out.
[bio19-s5.0.1] Release lasts only a short time, so each impulse produces a brief burst of neurotransmitter rather than a steady flow.
[bio19-s5.0.2] This brief burst passes the impulse to the next neuron, and because it is short, each impulse arriving at the knob gives its own separate signal. There is more on how the release happens in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s5.1.b] Once the neurotransmitter has done its job of passing on the impulse, it cannot be left in the cleft. An enzyme (a biological catalyst) in the cleft inactivates it.
[bio19-s5.1.0] After it has passed on the impulse, the neurotransmitter is inactivated by an enzyme in the synaptic cleft, so its message stops being delivered.
[bio19-s5.1.1] Example: the enzyme acetylcholinesterase breaks the neurotransmitter acetylcholine down into choline and ethanoic acid (acetate).
[bio19-s5.2.b] Removing the neurotransmitter is not tidiness. It is what lets a synapse carry a series of separate signals.
[bio19-s5.2.0] If the neurotransmitter stayed in the cleft, it would keep binding to the receptors on the postsynaptic neuron (the neuron receiving the signal), so that neuron would keep firing.
[bio19-s5.2.1] The signal could not be switched off, so repeated signals could not be told apart: one long continuous signal would replace a series of separate ones.
[bio19-s5.3.b] Neurons do not waste neurotransmitter. The presynaptic neuron takes back either the breakdown products or the intact neurotransmitter and reuses them.
[bio19-s5.3.0] The breakdown products are reabsorbed by the presynaptic neuron and used to make new neurotransmitter; for example, choline from the breakdown of acetylcholine is reabsorbed.
[bio19-s5.3.1] Serotonin and dopamine are mainly reabsorbed whole by transporter proteins (proteins in the membrane that carry a substance across it) in the presynaptic membrane, a process called reuptake. This is why they are cleared mainly by reuptake rather than by an enzyme.
[bio19-s5.4.b] Putting the pieces together, a synapse works as a loop. The same neurotransmitter is released, removed and then remade, ready for the next impulse.
[bio19-s5.4.0] 1. An impulse arrives at the synaptic knob, and calcium ions enter the knob.
[bio19-s5.4.1] 2. Vesicles (small membrane-bound sacs) release neurotransmitter into the cleft.
[bio19-s5.4.2] 3. The neurotransmitter diffuses across the cleft.
[bio19-s5.4.3] 4. It binds to receptors (proteins that the neurotransmitter fits) on the postsynaptic membrane.
[bio19-s5.4.4] 5. A new impulse starts in the postsynaptic neuron.
[bio19-s5.4.5] 6. An enzyme breaks the neurotransmitter down, or it is taken back by reuptake.
[bio19-s5.4.6] 7. The presynaptic neuron reabsorbs the breakdown products or the neurotransmitter, and new neurotransmitter is made and stored in vesicles, ready for the next impulse.
[bio19-s5.4.7] Diagram: a synapse showing the presynaptic knob, vesicles, cleft and postsynaptic membrane with receptors. Label: enzyme in the cleft, reuptake arrows into the presynaptic knob.
[bio19-s5.5.b] Because the synapse depends on several steps working together, a fault at any step changes how signals pass. The result can be a disorder.
[bio19-s5.5.0] Disruption can come from too little or too much neurotransmitter, faulty receptors, or blocked breakdown or reuptake.
[bio19-s5.5.1] Disruption can cause short-term (acute) or long-term (chronic) disorders, depending on whether the fault is brief or lasting.
[bio19-s5.6.b] A quick, severe disorder shows how much depends on removing neurotransmitter. A poison that stops the removal step affects the synapses between nerves and muscles within a short time.
[bio19-s5.6.0] Nerve agents and organophosphate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that breaks down acetylcholine.
[bio19-s5.6.1] Acetylcholine then builds up at synapses with muscles, because it is no longer broken down, causing continuous muscle contraction and paralysis.
[bio19-s5.6.2] In severe cases death follows from failure of the breathing muscles.
[bio19-s5.7.b] Long-term disorders involve a lasting change in the neurons or the signalling that a neurotransmitter carries. Each example below involves a different neurotransmitter.
[bio19-s5.7.0] In Alzheimer's disease, neurons that use acetylcholine are lost, contributing to memory loss.
[bio19-s5.7.1] Depression is associated with altered signalling by serotonin and noradrenaline. Its causes are complex, so it should not be described as simply caused by low serotonin or as a chemical imbalance.
[bio19-s5.7.2] In Parkinson's disease, dopamine-producing neurons in the brain die, causing tremor, stiffness and slow movement.
[bio19-s5.8.b] A drug inhibits the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter, so the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft longer and its effect is enhanced.
[bio19-s5.8.0] Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, e.g. donepezil, are used to treat the symptoms of Alzheimer's disease: acetylcholine is broken down more slowly, so the remaining acetylcholine-using neurons are stimulated for longer.
[bio19-s5.8.1] Monoamine oxidase (MAO) inhibitors are antidepressants that slow the breakdown of serotonin, noradrenaline and dopamine, so these neurotransmitters stay in the cleft longer.
[bio19-s5.9.b] A drug blocks the transporter that reabsorbs a neurotransmitter, so more neurotransmitter stays in the cleft for longer.
[bio19-s5.9.0] Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), e.g. fluoxetine, are used to treat depression: with serotonin reabsorption blocked, serotonin acts on the postsynaptic neuron for longer.
[bio19-s5.9.1] Neither strategy cures the disorder; both treat symptoms by enhancing the remaining signalling, which means they work with the neurons that are still functioning.
[bio19-s5.10.b] Psychoactive drugs and painkillers work at synapses in similar ways to the treatments above. They block removal, copy a neurotransmitter, or block receptors.
[bio19-s5.10.0] A psychoactive drug alters brain function, affecting mood, perception or behaviour.
[bio19-s5.10.1] Cocaine blocks the reuptake of dopamine, so dopamine builds up in the cleft, producing euphoria. There is more on its effect on mood and addiction in Dopamine and endorphins.
[bio19-s5.10.2] Nicotine mimics acetylcholine, binding to and activating some of its receptors.
[bio19-s5.10.3] Opioid painkillers such as morphine bind to the same receptors as endorphins (the body's own pain-reducing neurotransmitters), mimicking them and reducing the sensation of pain.
[bio19-s5.10.4] A drug may instead block receptors, preventing transmission: curare blocks acetylcholine receptors on muscles, so acetylcholine cannot act on them, causing paralysis.
[bio19-s5.11.b] Every type of disruption ends in one of two outcomes for impulse travel: transmission is reduced or stopped, or it is prolonged or enhanced.
[bio19-s5.11.0] Blocked release or blocked receptors: less or no neurotransmitter acts, so transmission is reduced or stops.
[bio19-s5.11.1] Blocked breakdown or blocked reuptake: the neurotransmitter stays in the cleft, so transmission is prolonged or enhanced.
[bio19-s5.11.2] Mimics: a drug that activates receptors makes transmission occur without the natural neurotransmitter.

CANDIDATES bio19-s6: Dopamine and endorphins
[bio19-s6.0.b] Neurotransmitters are the chemical messengers that carry a signal from one neuron (nerve cell) to the next. The ones released in the brain shape how we feel and what we do, so two of them, dopamine and endorphins, are worth knowing in detail.
[bio19-s6.0.0] Neurotransmitters released in the brain affect mood, which is how we feel, and behaviour, which is what we do.
[bio19-s6.0.1] They do this by changing how groups of neurons signal, which means the same brain cells can send stronger, weaker or different messages depending on which chemicals are present.
[bio19-s6.0.2] Because mood and behaviour depend on how neurons signal, anything that changes neurotransmitter levels, such as exercise, diet or drugs, can change how a person feels and acts.
[bio19-s6.1.b] Dopamine is the neurotransmitter behind motivation and movement. The body has to build it from a raw material, which is why diet affects dopamine.
[bio19-s6.1.0] Dopamine is a neurotransmitter made by groups of neurons deep in the brain, from the amino acid tyrosine (one of the building blocks of proteins).
[bio19-s6.1.1] Because tyrosine is the starting material, the amount of dopamine the brain can make depends partly on a supply of this amino acid.
[bio19-s6.2.b] One of dopamine's main jobs is to link an experience to a sense of reward, so that we are drawn to do useful things again. The brain circuit involved is called the reward pathway.
[bio19-s6.2.0] Dopamine is released when we experience or expect something rewarding, such as food, social contact or achievement.
[bio19-s6.2.1] It produces feelings of pleasure and satisfaction and reinforces the behaviour (makes it more likely to happen again), so we are motivated to repeat it.
[bio19-s6.2.2] In short, dopamine is better described by its role in reward and motivation than as a 'pleasure chemical': it drives us to seek out and repeat rewarding behaviour.
[bio19-s6.3.b] Dopamine does more than reward. It also works in the parts of the brain that control movement and in those involved in learning.
[bio19-s6.3.0] Dopamine helps control smooth movement, so the muscles can produce steady, coordinated actions.
[bio19-s6.3.1] It is also involved in learning and attention, which means it helps us focus on and remember what matters.
[bio19-s6.3.2] Loss of dopamine-producing neurons causes Parkinson's disease, a condition where movement becomes difficult because the neurons that make dopamine are lost. There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.
[bio19-s6.4.b] Endorphins are the brain's own pain-relieving chemicals. The body releases them when it is under strain, so that pain and stress are easier to cope with.
[bio19-s6.4.0] Endorphins are neurotransmitters, specifically peptides (short chains of amino acids), produced in the brain, including by the pituitary gland and hypothalamus, among other brain regions.
[bio19-s6.4.1] They are produced in response to pain, stress and physical exertion, which are the situations where reducing discomfort is most useful.
[bio19-s6.4.2] They bind to opioid receptors (receptor proteins that the endorphin fits into) and reduce the sensation of pain, so they act as the body's natural painkillers.
[bio19-s6.4.3] They also produce a feeling of wellbeing and reduce stress.
[bio19-s6.5.b] Some drugs used for non-medical reasons act directly on the dopamine system. Stimulants (drugs that speed up activity in the nervous system) push dopamine levels in the reward pathway far above normal.
[bio19-s6.5.0] Cocaine blocks dopamine reuptake, which means dopamine is not taken back up into the neuron that released it, so it stays active for longer.
[bio19-s6.5.1] Amphetamines increase dopamine release, so more dopamine is put out into the gap between neurons.
[bio19-s6.5.2] Either way, both flood the reward pathway with dopamine, so the result is an intense high.
[bio19-s6.6.b] Stimulants are not the only drugs that act on these systems. Nicotine and alcohol work on dopamine, while opioids work on the endorphin system.
[bio19-s6.6.0] Nicotine and alcohol also increase dopamine release in the reward pathway, so they produce rewarding feelings that encourage repeated use.
[bio19-s6.6.1] Heroin and other opioids bind to endorphin (opioid) receptors, the same receptors natural endorphins use, producing pain relief and euphoria (an intense feeling of wellbeing).
[bio19-s6.7.b] Using a drug repeatedly does not leave the brain unchanged. The brain adapts to the abnormal levels of neurotransmitter, and these adaptations are what lead to addiction.
[bio19-s6.7.0] With repeated drug use the brain adapts, for example by reducing the number of dopamine receptors, so the same amount of dopamine has less effect.
[bio19-s6.7.1] More drug is then needed for the same effect; this is tolerance.
[bio19-s6.7.2] Normal rewards feel less pleasurable, because there are fewer receptors for ordinary dopamine release to act on.
[bio19-s6.7.3] Stopping causes low mood and cravings; this is dependence and withdrawal.
[bio19-s6.7.4] This is how addiction develops.
[bio19-s6.8.b] Exercise is a lifestyle choice that raises these neurotransmitters naturally. It links back to the conditions that trigger endorphin release: pain, stress and physical exertion.
[bio19-s6.8.0] Sustained exercise raises endorphin levels and increases dopamine signalling.
[bio19-s6.8.1] This improves mood, reduces stress and anxiety, and reduces the perception of pain during exertion, because endorphins act as natural painkillers and dopamine supports the reward pathway.
[bio19-s6.9.b] What we eat affects dopamine in two ways: some foods trigger its release, and the diet supplies the raw material to make it.
[bio19-s6.9.0] Foods high in sugar and fat trigger dopamine release in the reward pathway, which can drive overeating, because the reward encourages us to repeat the behaviour.
[bio19-s6.9.1] A diet with enough protein supplies tyrosine, the amino acid that dopamine is made from.
[bio19-s6.10.b] A good discussion compares how different lifestyle choices change neurotransmitter levels and what follows from each. The key difference is how far above normal the levels are pushed and what the brain does in response.
[bio19-s6.10.0] Exercise and social contact raise these neurotransmitters in ways that support long-term wellbeing.
[bio19-s6.10.1] Drugs raise them far above normal levels, and the brain adapts in ways that cause dependence.
[bio19-s6.10.2] Mood and behaviour depend on many factors, not on one neurotransmitter, so no single chemical explains how a person feels.

CANDIDATES bio23-s6: Hormones in reproduction
[bio23-s6.0.b] Reproduction is run by chemical messengers (hormones) carried in the blood. Three places make nearly all of them, and in pregnancy a fourth organ joins in.
[bio23-s6.0.0] Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain, the testes (the male gonads, which make sperm) and the ovaries (the female gonads, which make eggs).
[bio23-s6.0.1] The pituitary gland makes oxytocin and prolactin, and also the hormones that stimulate the gonads, which is how the brain controls the testes and ovaries.
[bio23-s6.0.2] The testes make testosterone, and the ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone. In pregnancy the placenta (the organ that links mother and foetus) also makes oestrogen and progesterone.
[bio23-s6.0.3] Prolactin is made by the pituitary gland, and its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts. There is more on where each gland sits in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.b] Puberty is the changeover from child to adult body. A hormone signal from the brain switches the reproductive system on.
[bio23-s6.1.0] Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes (sperm or eggs), and the secondary sexual characteristics develop. These are the body changes, apart from the reproductive organs themselves, that mark a person as male or female.
[bio23-s6.1.1] Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls, and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
[bio23-s6.1.2] It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads (the testes and ovaries). There is more on these pituitary hormones in the Higher Level block on the gonad-stimulating hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.3] As a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone, and the ovaries much more oestrogen. It is these sex hormones that cause the changes of puberty.
[bio23-s6.1.4] Testosterone at puberty starts sperm production, causes growth of the penis and testes, and causes the male secondary sexual characteristics. It also increases sex drive.
[bio23-s6.1.5] Oestrogen at puberty causes growth of the uterus (womb) and vagina, preparing the body for pregnancy, and the female secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.1.6] Oestrogen, working with progesterone, starts the menstrual cycles, so a girl becomes able to ovulate and conceive; the first period is called menarche.
[bio23-s6.2.b] Once puberty has started the gametes, hormones keep them coming. The pattern differs between the sexes: continuous in men, monthly in women.
[bio23-s6.2.0] Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes where sperm are made), and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.2.1] Testosterone is made by the interstitial cells between the tubules, not by the tubules themselves, which make sperm.
[bio23-s6.2.2] Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously rather than in a cycle, which is why men stay fertile from day to day.
[bio23-s6.2.3] Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle (a fluid-filled sac holding a developing egg) to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus). There is more on the hormone levels and feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.3.b] After fertilisation the embryo has to bury itself in the uterus lining (implantation) and the lining must then be kept, not shed. Two hormones make this possible.
[bio23-s6.3.0] Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms.
[bio23-s6.3.1] Without high progesterone, implantation cannot succeed, because the embryo would have no suitable lining to attach to or feed from.
[bio23-s6.3.2] hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation.
[bio23-s6.3.3] hCG keeps the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after ovulation) alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine, using a monoclonal-antibody test.
[bio23-s6.4.b] The corpus luteum cannot support a whole pregnancy, so the placenta takes over hormone production. Progesterone from it then keeps the pregnancy going in several ways.
[bio23-s6.4.0] From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
[bio23-s6.4.1] Progesterone during pregnancy maintains the endometrium and so the placenta, which is attached to it.
[bio23-s6.4.2] Progesterone keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it does not contract and expel the foetus early.
[bio23-s6.4.3] Progesterone keeps the cervical mucus plug (the mucus that seals the cervix, the neck of the uterus) thick, and prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts.
[bio23-s6.4.4] Progesterone, with oestrogen, prevents further ovulation and menstruation during pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.5.b] Oestrogen supports the growth of the foetus by building up the mother's tissues, and late in pregnancy it prepares her body for birth.
[bio23-s6.5.0] Oestrogen during pregnancy drives the growth of the foetus: it stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, and increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta, which supplies the foetus.
[bio23-s6.5.1] Oestrogen also develops the milk ducts of the breasts, so they are ready to carry milk after birth.
[bio23-s6.5.2] Late in pregnancy oestrogen makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
[bio23-s6.5.3] Level summary: progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.
[bio23-s6.6.b] Birth needs the powerful uterus muscle to contract strongly. A shift in hormone balance and the hormone oxytocin make this happen.
[bio23-s6.6.0] Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
[bio23-s6.6.1] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus (a part of the brain just above the pituitary) and released by the pituitary into the blood.
[bio23-s6.6.2] Oxytocin causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour, the process of giving birth.
[bio23-s6.7.b] Labour speeds itself up. Each contraction causes more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, a pattern called positive feedback, where a change leads to more of the same change.
[bio23-s6.7.0] 1. The baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix.
[bio23-s6.7.1] 2. Stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain.
[bio23-s6.7.2] 3. The pituitary releases more oxytocin.
[bio23-s6.7.3] 4. Stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more.
[bio23-s6.7.4] 5. The cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus (the stretching), so the loop stops.
[bio23-s6.7.5] A nervous pathway (the impulses) and a hormonal pathway (oxytocin in the blood) work together here. There is more on this in Nervous vs hormonal coordination.
[bio23-s6.7.6] Diagram: the positive feedback loop as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → back to the start; birth ends the loop.
[bio23-s6.8.b] Oxytocin's work does not stop when the baby arrives. It also helps the uterus recover, and doctors use a man-made version of it.
[bio23-s6.8.0] After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding.
[bio23-s6.8.1] Oxytocin also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size, because its contractions squeeze the muscle smaller.
[bio23-s6.8.2] Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour, and to reduce bleeding after birth.
[bio23-s6.9.b] The breasts are prepared during pregnancy, but milk only starts once the placenta has gone. The hormone that actually makes milk is prolactin.
[bio23-s6.9.0] During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises.
[bio23-s6.9.1] The high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced, even though prolactin is already rising.
[bio23-s6.9.2] When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
[bio23-s6.9.3] Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk, which is why it is called the milk-making hormone.
[bio23-s6.9.4] Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made: supply matches demand.
[bio23-s6.9.5] While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often do not return for some months; this is not reliable contraception. There is more on this in Fertility control and infertility.
[bio23-s6.10.b] Making milk is not the same as delivering it. Oxytocin does the second job, so a feed needs both hormones.
[bio23-s6.10.0] Oxytocin causes the milk to be released (called let-down, or milk ejection).
[bio23-s6.10.1] Suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple.
[bio23-s6.10.2] Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
[bio23-s6.11.b] The first milk a baby gets is different from the milk that follows. It is tuned to protect a newborn rather than to fatten it.
[bio23-s6.11.0] Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth.
[bio23-s6.11.1] Colostrum is a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar.
[bio23-s6.11.2] Its antibodies give the newborn passive immunity, which means protection made by the mother rather than by the baby's own immune system. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s6.11.3] Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose (milk sugar) for energy and growth.
[bio23-s6.12.b] A woman's fertility does not last for life. Menopause is the point where the ovaries stop responding, and the fall in hormones has effects across the body.
[bio23-s6.12.0] Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months.
[bio23-s6.12.1] It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
[bio23-s6.12.2] Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone. It is not simply that the eggs are used up by ovulation, because most follicles degenerate without ever ovulating.
[bio23-s6.12.3] Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, so there is nothing to shed and periods stop.
[bio23-s6.12.4] Effects of falling oestrogen in the short term: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, and vaginal dryness.
[bio23-s6.12.5] Effects of falling oestrogen in the longer term: loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease.
[bio23-s6.12.6] Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.
[bio23-s6.12.7] There is more on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.13.b] One bullet per hormone: its gland, its roles and how its level changes through life.
[bio23-s6.13.0] Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth, puberty, sperm production and sex drive. There is more on development before birth in Sex determination.
[bio23-s6.13.1] Testosterone level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, and declines slowly in later life.
[bio23-s6.13.2] Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, and keeping bones strong.
[bio23-s6.13.3] Oestrogen level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, and falls at menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.4] Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares the milk glands and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.13.5] Progesterone level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.6] Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth, and milk let-down. Its level rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
[bio23-s6.13.7] Prolactin (pituitary): milk production. Its level rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed, and falls when breastfeeding stops.
[bio23-s6.14.b] FSH and LH are the two pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads. In males they hold testosterone steady through negative feedback, and in females they drive the monthly cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.0] At puberty the hypothalamus, the part of the brain above the pituitary, signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.14.1] In males these hormones stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
[bio23-s6.14.2] In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, working with testosterone, so without FSH sperm output falls.
[bio23-s6.14.3] In males, LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
[bio23-s6.14.4] Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH and FSH from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again.
[bio23-s6.14.5] This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.6] Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone) have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.14.7] In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s6.15.b] FSH and LH levels follow the state of the ovaries and the other hormones. When oestrogen and progesterone are high they stay low, and when these hormones are lost they rise.
[bio23-s6.15.0] In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens.
[bio23-s6.15.1] While breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.15.2] After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
[bio23-s6.15.3] Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood and rise at puberty.
[bio23-s6.15.4] In men FSH and LH are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle, with an LH surge at ovulation.
[bio23-s6.15.5] In women FSH and LH are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and high after the menopause. The levels and feedback through the month are in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.16.b] A model shows how a control centre, a hormone, a target organ and feedback fit together. You can then use it to predict what happens when one part changes.
[bio23-s6.16.0] A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback, with − for inhibition and + for stimulation.
[bio23-s6.16.1] Use the model to predict effects: blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone, as in the pill, keeps FSH and LH low.
[bio23-s6.16.2] Diagram: two flow charts side by side, male then female. Male: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → testes → sperm and testosterone, with a “−” arrow from testosterone back to the pituitary.
[bio23-s6.16.3] Female flow chart: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with “−” arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a “+” arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge.

CANDIDATES bio24-s1: Flower structure and pollination
[bio24-s1.0.b] Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers, which produce the gametes (sex cells) and bring about pollination (the transfer of pollen) and fertilisation (the joining of male and female gametes). Three structures carry the process from gamete to new plant: the flower, the seed and the fruit.
[bio24-s1.0.0] The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle, the tip of the flower stalk. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
[bio24-s1.0.1] The flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination. It is the only one of the three structures that exists before fertilisation, because the seed and fruit develop from flower parts afterwards.
[bio24-s1.0.2] The seed, a fertilised and developed ovule, contains the embryo (a new plant) with a food store and a protective coat. The fruit, a developed ovary, protects the seeds and helps to disperse them, that is, spread them away from the parent plant.
[bio24-s1.0.3] Seed detail is in Seeds; dispersal is in Fruit and seed dispersal.
[bio24-s1.0.4] Many flowers are bisexual (hermaphrodite), meaning they have both male and female parts in the same flower, e.g. buttercup.
[bio24-s1.0.5] Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel, whose male flowers hang in catkins and whose small female flowers have red stigmas. In some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly.
[bio24-s1.1.b] A flower is built in rings, listed here from the outside inwards. The outer parts hold the flower together, protect it and, in many species, attract the animals that carry its pollen.
[bio24-s1.1.0] Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached, so it holds the flower together. For example, in strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
[bio24-s1.1.1] Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud, and some photosynthesise (make food using light).
[bio24-s1.1.2] Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, which attracts insects.
[bio24-s1.1.3] Many insect-pollinated petals have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent, because there is no need to attract animals.
[bio24-s1.1.4] Nectary: a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects so that they keep coming back. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
[bio24-s1.2.b] The male part of the flower is the stamen. Its job is to make pollen, the package that carries the male gametes, and to hold it where a pollinator can pick it up.
[bio24-s1.2.0] Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament, so that the pollen is held up where it can be picked up. The stamens together form the androecium.
[bio24-s1.2.1] Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis (a cell division that halves the chromosome number), so the pollen grains are haploid, with one set of chromosomes. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
[bio24-s1.2.2] Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position, where insects brush against it or out in the wind. It also carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand (a thin tube of transport tissue).
[bio24-s1.2.3] Diagram: a stamen drawn on its own. Labels: anther, filament.
[bio24-s1.3.b] The female part of the flower is the carpel. It catches pollen, provides a route for the male gametes to reach the egg cell, and protects the ovules that become seeds.
[bio24-s1.3.0] Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
[bio24-s1.3.1] Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers, so pollen carried on an insect adheres, and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses, so it can catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s1.3.2] Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube (the thin tube a pollen grain grows) grows down through it to the ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.3] Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them, so the developing seeds are not exposed. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
[bio24-s1.3.4] Ovule: a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis, so it is haploid. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
[bio24-s1.3.5] Diagram: a carpel drawn on its own. Labels: stigma, style, ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.6] Diagram: a half-flower (vertical section) of an insect-pollinated flower such as buttercup or wallflower. Labels: receptacle, sepal, petal, nectary, stamen (anther, filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), ovule.
[bio24-s1.4.b] Pollination must happen before fertilisation can take place. Flowers can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated, and several features favour cross-pollination.
[bio24-s1.4.0] Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. It is only the arrival of pollen: fertilisation, the fusion of gamete nuclei in the ovule, comes later.
[bio24-s1.4.1] Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
[bio24-s1.4.2] Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation. The species is better able to adapt to change, such as new diseases or climate, and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding (breeding between close relatives).
[bio24-s1.4.3] Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but it gives less variation. It suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
[bio24-s1.4.4] Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly).
[bio24-s1.4.5] In primrose two flower forms exist: pin flowers, with a long style and low anthers, and thrum flowers, with a short style and high anthers. Insects carry pollen between the two forms, so the pollen reaches a different plant.
[bio24-s1.4.6] Agents of pollination (the carriers of pollen): insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak and birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. There is more on this in Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants.
[bio24-s1.5.b] The flower's structure matches the agent of pollination, whether an animal or the wind. Each type of flower is built to make the transfer of pollen as reliable as possible.
[bio24-s1.5.0] In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them.
[bio24-s1.5.1] Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
[bio24-s1.5.2] Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip, having learned the flower they are faithful to, so pollen is carried to the right species.
[bio24-s1.5.3] Plant and pollinator both benefit, because the plant is pollinated and the insect is fed: this is a mutualism (a relationship in which both partners gain). There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio24-s1.5.4] In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma. There is nothing to attract animals, so there are no showy petals, scent or nectar.
[bio24-s1.5.5] Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries and oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease.
[bio24-s1.5.6] Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre. It encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators, e.g. by leaving wildflowers and mowing less often.
[bio24-s1.5.7] Health link: wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.
[bio24-s1.6.b] Once pollen reaches a stigma of the right species, a sequence of events leads to fertilisation. Afterwards the flower parts change into the seed and fruit.
[bio24-s1.6.0] Fertilisation in five steps: 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma.
[bio24-s1.6.1] 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes.
[bio24-s1.6.2] 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it.
[bio24-s1.6.3] 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes, the first cell of the new plant).
[bio24-s1.6.4] 5. The zygote divides by mitosis (ordinary cell division that makes identical cells) to form the embryo.
[bio24-s1.6.5] Double fertilisation: a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store.
[bio24-s1.6.6] After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed, and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa, the seed coat. The ovary becomes the fruit, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp). The petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.
[bio24-s1.6.7] Diagram: a carpel in section, with a pollen grain on the stigma, the pollen tube growing down the style and entering an ovule through the micropyle. Labels: pollen grain, pollen tube, style, ovary, ovule, egg cell, micropyle.

CANDIDATES bio24-s2: Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants
[bio24-s2.0.b] A flower's structure follows from how its pollen travels. Insects must be lured in and rewarded, so insect-pollinated flowers advertise; the wind needs no persuading, so wind-pollinated flowers drop the advertising. Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second, and the flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio24-s2.0.0] Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides (markings that lead the insect towards the nectar), to attract insects.
[bio24-s2.0.1] Wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, because there is no animal to attract and large petals would block pollen in the wind.
[bio24-s2.0.2] Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries (the glands that produce nectar, a sugary liquid), so the insect is rewarded for visiting.
[bio24-s2.0.3] Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries, because there is no visitor to reward.
[bio24-s2.0.4] Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters, so that insects can spot them.
[bio24-s2.0.5] Wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins (dangling spikes of tiny flowers) or spikes held high above the leaves, where the wind can reach them.
[bio24-s2.0.6] Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
[bio24-s2.0.7] Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
[bio24-s2.1.b] The anther (the pollen-producing part of the stamen, held up on a stalk called the filament) and the stigma (the sticky or feathery top of the carpel that receives pollen) are positioned to suit the carrier. Insects touch the flower's parts; the wind blows past them.
[bio24-s2.1.0] Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them, so pollen rubs onto its body.
[bio24-s2.1.1] Wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
[bio24-s2.1.2] Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them and pollen sticks to them.
[bio24-s2.1.3] Wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s2.1.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of a buttercup or wallflower half-flower (large petal, nectary, anther inside, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas, anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
[bio24-s2.2.b] The pollen itself also differs. Insects deliver pollen accurately, whereas the wind scatters it at random, and the grains are built for the different journeys.
[bio24-s2.2.0] Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower.
[bio24-s2.2.1] Wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species, so many grains are needed for a few to land.
[bio24-s2.2.2] Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs.
[bio24-s2.2.3] Wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump together.
[bio24-s2.2.4] Diagram: an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.
[bio24-s2.3.b] This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.
[bio24-s2.3.0] Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
[bio24-s2.3.1] Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
[bio24-s2.3.2] What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features (recorded in words) are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface.
[bio24-s2.3.3] Quantitative features (measured as numbers) are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
[bio24-s2.3.4] Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen, so every flower is at the same stage. Examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements, to reduce the effect of one unusual flower.
[bio24-s2.3.5] Measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification, so the two types can be compared fairly.
[bio24-s2.3.6] Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.3.7] Also needed: microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.
[bio24-s2.4.b] The method moves from the whole flower, to its separated parts, to the pollen under the microscope, and repeats everything for the second flower type.
[bio24-s2.4.0] 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
[bio24-s2.4.1] 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
[bio24-s2.4.2] 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
[bio24-s2.4.3] 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
[bio24-s2.4.4] 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
[bio24-s2.4.5] 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
[bio24-s2.4.6] 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power.
[bio24-s2.4.7] 8. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
[bio24-s2.5.b] Scalpels, pollen, some plants and glass slides each carry a risk, so the practical needs a precaution for each.
[bio24-s2.5.0] Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely, so nobody is cut.
[bio24-s2.5.1] Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
[bio24-s2.5.2] Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
[bio24-s2.5.3] Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
[bio24-s2.5.4] Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.
[bio24-s2.6.b] Recording results in a table puts the two flowers side by side, so each difference can be seen and then explained.
[bio24-s2.6.0] Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
[bio24-s2.6.1] Expected primary data (collected by you) for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.6.2] Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.7.b] A conclusion links what was seen to why it matters for pollination, and secondary data add evidence the class could not collect itself.
[bio24-s2.7.0] Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations above. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio24-s2.7.1] Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen.
[bio24-s2.7.2] Other secondary data: published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; and labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
[bio24-s2.7.3] Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio24-s2.8.b] Every practical has weaknesses. Each limitation below has a matching improvement, and you should be able to give both.
[bio24-s2.8.0] Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
[bio24-s2.8.1] Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
[bio24-s2.8.2] Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.8.3] Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes.
[bio24-s2.8.4] The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
[bio24-s2.8.5] Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective, because different people judge differently; have two people record them.
[bio24-s2.8.6] Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.
