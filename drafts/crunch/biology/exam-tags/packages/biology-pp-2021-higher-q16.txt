CANDIDATES bio19-s2: The reflex action
[bio19-s2.0.b] Every reflex, and every nervous response, is built from the same three parts: something that triggers it, something that detects it and something that acts. A reflex begins with a stimulus that a receptor detects and ends with a response from an effector.
[bio19-s2.0.0] A stimulus is a change in the internal or external environment that can be detected, such as heat on the skin or an object approaching the eye.
[bio19-s2.0.1] A receptor detects the stimulus. It is a cell or nerve ending that is sensitive to that particular type of change, and it starts the impulse.
[bio19-s2.0.2] An effector, a muscle or a gland, carries out the response. A muscle responds by contracting; a gland responds by secreting.
[bio19-s2.1.b] Some situations need a response so quickly that there is no time to think. A reflex action is the body's built-in solution: a response that is fixed and does not wait for a decision.
[bio19-s2.1.0] A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, happening without conscious thought. Involuntary means you do not choose it or control it.
[bio19-s2.1.1] Reflexes protect the body from harm, for example pulling a hand from a hot object or blinking when something approaches the eye, so damage is limited before you even notice.
[bio19-s2.1.2] Reflexes also maintain posture and normal body functions; coughing and sneezing clear the airways, for example.
[bio19-s2.1.3] Reflexes are inborn, so they are not learned. You are born able to do them.
[bio19-s2.1.4] The same stimulus always produces the same response, which is what makes a reflex automatic and predictable.
[bio19-s2.2.b] A reflex works because the nerve pathway involved is short and fixed. The reflex arc is the pathway an impulse (the electrical signal carried by nerve cells) follows in a reflex action.
[bio19-s2.2.0] The pathway runs receptor, sensory neuron, interneuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord, motor neuron, effector. The sensory neuron carries the impulse in, the motor neuron carries it out, and the interneuron connects them.
[bio19-s2.2.1] The sensory neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root (the back root of the spinal nerve); its cell body lies in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on that root.
[bio19-s2.2.2] The motor neuron leaves the spinal cord through the ventral root (the front root), carrying the impulse out to the effector.
[bio19-s2.2.3] Diagram: reflex arc drawn through a transverse section of the spinal cord, labelling receptor in the skin, sensory neuron, dorsal root ganglion, interneuron, grey matter, white matter, motor neuron, ventral root and effector muscle, with arrows showing the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s2.3.b] Touching a hot object shows the whole reflex arc at work. Follow the impulse from the finger to the muscle that pulls the hand away.
[bio19-s2.3.0] 1. Pain and heat receptors in the skin of the finger are stimulated by the hot object.
[bio19-s2.3.1] 2. An impulse travels along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.3.2] 3. It passes across a synapse (the tiny gap between two neurons) to an interneuron in the grey matter.
[bio19-s2.3.3] 4. It passes across a synapse to a motor neuron.
[bio19-s2.3.4] 5. The motor neuron carries the impulse to the biceps, the effector.
[bio19-s2.3.5] 6. The biceps contracts and the hand is pulled away from the object.
[bio19-s2.4.b] A reflex is faster than a voluntary action because of the route it takes. The brain is not left out, though: it is told what happened a moment later.
[bio19-s2.4.0] The impulse takes a short route through the spinal cord, crossing few synapses. Each synapse adds a small delay, so fewer synapses means a faster response.
[bio19-s2.4.1] It does not wait for a decision by the brain, which is why reflexes are rapid.
[bio19-s2.4.2] Interneurons also carry impulses up the spinal cord to the brain, so pain is felt just after the hand has moved.
[bio19-s2.4.3] The brain can sometimes override a reflex, for example when a person holds on to a hot plate to avoid dropping it.
[bio19-s2.5.b] The knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is the simplest reflex arc, because it leaves out the interneuron. It is a useful contrast with the withdrawal reflex.
[bio19-s2.5.0] Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches the thigh muscle, which is the stimulus.
[bio19-s2.5.1] The sensory neuron synapses directly with the motor neuron in the spinal cord, with no interneuron, so the pathway has only one synapse.
[bio19-s2.5.2] The leg kicks. Doctors use this reflex to test the nervous system, because a normal response shows the reflex pathway is working.
[bio19-s2.6.b] Not every reflex passes through the spinal cord. Some are controlled by the brain instead, but they are still rapid, automatic and involuntary.
[bio19-s2.6.0] Some reflexes are controlled by the brain rather than the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.6.1] Examples are blinking, coughing, sneezing and the pupil narrowing in bright light.

CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio20-s1.10.5] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; oestrogen; female secondary sexual characteristics and thickening of the endometrium.
[bio20-s1.10.6] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; progesterone; maintains the endometrium and pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio22-s2: Innate and adaptive immunity
[bio22-s2.0.b] Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection by pathogens (disease-causing organisms such as bacteria and viruses). The immune system does this by telling self from non-self, and it has two parts that work at different speeds.
[bio22-s2.0.0] The immune system consists of physical and chemical barriers, white blood cells (made from stem cells in the bone marrow), the lymphatic system (lymph nodes, spleen and thymus) and defence proteins in the blood, such as antibodies and complement.
[bio22-s2.0.1] The body's own cells carry their own marker molecules on their surface, which the immune system normally ignores, so foreign cells and molecules, which lack these markers, are attacked.
[bio22-s2.0.2] Innate immunity, also called non-specific immunity, is present from birth, acts against any pathogen in the same way and acts within minutes to hours.
[bio22-s2.0.3] Adaptive immunity, also called acquired or specific immunity, develops during life after exposure to a particular pathogen and acts against that pathogen only.
[bio22-s2.0.4] Adaptive immunity takes days to develop the first time, but it remembers the pathogen, which is why a later infection is dealt with much faster.
[bio22-s2.0.5] Innate defences are found in all animals and in plants; adaptive immunity, with antibodies and lymphocytes (the white blood cells that run it), is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.1.b] The best way to deal with a pathogen is to stop it getting in. Skin and the linings of the body's openings form physical barriers that block or trap microorganisms before they can cause infection.
[bio22-s2.1.0] Skin is a tough outer layer of dead, keratin-filled cells (keratin is a tough protein) that pathogens can't easily cross.
[bio22-s2.1.1] Oil (sebum) from skin glands makes the surface slightly acidic, which slows bacterial growth.
[bio22-s2.1.2] Breaks in the skin are sealed by blood clotting, which stops blood loss and blocks pathogen entry.
[bio22-s2.1.3] Mucous membranes line the breathing, digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts. Their sticky mucus traps microorganisms and dust, so these don't reach the cells beneath.
[bio22-s2.1.4] In the trachea and bronchi (the main airways to the lungs), cilia (tiny hair-like projections) beat the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.
[bio22-s2.1.5] Coughing and sneezing are reflexes (automatic responses) that expel irritants, so trapped microorganisms are blown out of the airways.
[bio22-s2.2.b] Some barriers work by chemistry rather than by physically blocking entry. Acids and enzymes kill or inhibit microorganisms, and harmless resident microorganisms add a living defence of their own.
[bio22-s2.2.0] Hydrochloric acid in the stomach, at about pH 2, kills most microorganisms swallowed with food.
[bio22-s2.2.1] Lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus, breaks down bacterial cell walls, so the bacteria burst or die.
[bio22-s2.2.2] The acidic conditions in the vagina inhibit many pathogens.
[bio22-s2.2.3] Harmless microorganisms living on the skin and in the gut and vagina, the normal microbiota, compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, which leaves pathogens less room to establish an infection. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s2.3.b] If a pathogen gets past the barriers, the innate response moves on to cells that destroy it. Phagocytes find the invader, swallow it and digest it in a process called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.0] Phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens, and the process is called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.1] Neutrophils are the most numerous white blood cells and the first to arrive at an infection. Macrophages, formed from monocytes (another type of white blood cell), are larger and longer-lived.
[bio22-s2.3.2] 1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals released by pathogens and damaged cells.
[bio22-s2.3.3] 2. It binds the pathogen.
[bio22-s2.3.4] 3. It surrounds the pathogen with extensions of its membrane and takes it into a vesicle (a small membrane-bound sac inside the cell).
[bio22-s2.3.5] 4. Digestive enzymes are released into the vesicle and break the pathogen down.
[bio22-s2.3.6] 5. The harmless products are absorbed or released.
[bio22-s2.3.7] Diagram: the stages of phagocytosis in four sketches: a phagocyte approaching a bacterium; membrane extensions surrounding it; the bacterium enclosed in a vesicle; the bacterium being digested. Label phagocyte, nucleus, bacterium, vesicle and digestive enzymes.
[bio22-s2.4.b] Inflammation and fever are whole-body or whole-area responses that bring more defences to an infection and make conditions harder for the pathogen. The signs you notice when you are ill are the immune system at work.
[bio22-s2.4.0] Inflammation: damaged cells and mast cells (cells in tissues that release signals when damaged) release histamine and other chemicals, which widen local blood vessels and make capillaries leakier.
[bio22-s2.4.1] More blood flow brings phagocytes and defence proteins to the site, and fluid leaks into the tissue, giving redness, heat, swelling and pain at the site.
[bio22-s2.4.2] Pus is a mixture of dead phagocytes, pathogens and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.4.3] Fever: chemicals released by white blood cells reset the temperature set point in the hypothalamus (the brain region that controls body temperature) upwards, raising body temperature. There is more on this in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio22-s2.4.4] A higher temperature slows the reproduction of many pathogens and speeds up the reactions of the immune system, so fever helps the body fight infection.
[bio22-s2.5.b] Besides phagocytes, the innate response uses proteins in the blood and released by infected cells. Plants have no adaptive immunity, so their innate defences have to do all the work.
[bio22-s2.5.0] Complement, a group of blood proteins, coats pathogens so that phagocytes engulf them more easily, and it can punch holes in bacterial membranes, bursting them.
[bio22-s2.5.1] Interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that make neighbouring cells resist virus replication, so the infection is slowed before it spreads.
[bio22-s2.5.2] Natural killer cells also act in the innate response, in addition to phagocytes and defence proteins.
[bio22-s2.5.3] Plants have innate defences only: physical barriers (cuticle, bark and cell walls), protective chemicals, and the death of infected cells to wall off a pathogen. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio22-s2.6.b] Adaptive immunity depends on recognising a particular pathogen. The molecules it recognises are called antigens, and the immune system's ability to tell them apart from the body's own molecules is what makes the response specific.
[bio22-s2.6.0] An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen or on a toxin it releases, that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s2.6.1] Antigens trigger a specific immune response, including antibody production.
[bio22-s2.6.2] Each pathogen carries several antigens, so more than one kind of antibody can be made against it. The body's own cell markers are self antigens, which are normally tolerated, so the body doesn't attack itself.
[bio22-s2.7.b] An antibody is the body's protein that matches one antigen. Its Y shape and the shape of its tips explain why each antibody works against only one antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.0] An antibody, also called an immunoglobulin, is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells, which are derived from B lymphocytes. It binds to one specific antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.1] An antibody has four polypeptide chains (chains of amino acids), two long heavy chains and two short light chains, held together by disulfide bonds.
[bio22-s2.7.2] The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ between antibodies and form two identical antigen-binding sites. The rest of the molecule is the constant region, which is the same in all antibodies of a class.
[bio22-s2.7.3] Specificity: the shape of the antigen-binding site is complementary to the shape of one antigen, so each antibody binds only that antigen, like a key in a lock. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio22-s2.7.4] Diagram: an antibody as a Y shape, labelled heavy chain, light chain, variable region, antigen-binding site (two), constant region and disulfide bonds, with an antigen fitting one binding site.
[bio22-s2.8.b] Once antibodies have bound to an antigen, they can stop the pathogen in four different ways. The Y shape matters again here, because the two arms bind the antigen while the stem is recognised by other defences.
[bio22-s2.8.0] Agglutination: because each antibody has two binding sites, antibodies link pathogens together into clumps that can't spread and are easily engulfed.
[bio22-s2.8.1] Neutralisation: antibodies cover the parts of viruses that attach to cells, or bind to toxins (antitoxins), so that they can't act.
[bio22-s2.8.2] Opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens, marking them for phagocytes, which have receptors for the antibody's constant region.
[bio22-s2.8.3] Complement activation: antibodies activate complement (the blood proteins of the innate response), which bursts bacterial cells.
[bio22-s2.9.b] The body can't know in advance which pathogen it will meet, so it keeps a huge variety of B cells ready. Only the ones that fit the invader are chosen to multiply, a process called clonal selection.
[bio22-s2.9.0] Lymphocytes are the white blood cells of adaptive immunity, and there are two main kinds.
[bio22-s2.9.1] B lymphocytes (B cells) produce antibodies. This is the antibody-mediated or humoral response, acting on pathogens and toxins in blood and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.9.2] T lymphocytes (T cells) act directly on infected body cells in the cell-mediated response (Higher Level).
[bio22-s2.9.3] Clonal selection: the body has millions of different B cells, each with one type of antibody fixed in its membrane as a receptor, made before any infection.
[bio22-s2.9.4] When a pathogen enters, only the B cells whose receptors fit its antigen bind to it. These are selected and, with chemical signals from helper T cells, divide rapidly by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells.
[bio22-s2.9.5] The clone differentiates (becomes specialised) into plasma cells and memory B cells.
[bio22-s2.9.6] Plasma cells secrete large amounts of the matching antibody into the blood, thousands of molecules per second, for a few days, and then die.
[bio22-s2.9.7] Memory cells stay in the body for years, ready to respond to the same antigen.
[bio22-s2.10.b] The first time you meet a pathogen, the response is slow. A second meeting is much faster, because memory cells are already there. This difference is the reason you rarely get the same infection twice.
[bio22-s2.10.0] Primary response, on first exposure: there is a delay of several days, often about a week, while matching B cells are selected and cloned. Antibody levels rise slowly and stay fairly low.
[bio22-s2.10.1] During the primary response the person may become ill, because the pathogen multiplies before enough antibodies are made.
[bio22-s2.10.2] Secondary response, on later exposure to the same antigen: memory cells recognise it at once and quickly produce many plasma cells, so antibodies appear within a few days, reach a much higher level and last longer.
[bio22-s2.10.3] The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear, so the person is immune. This is the basis of vaccination. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s2.10.4] Diagram: a graph of antibody concentration (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis), with a first exposure giving a small, slow primary peak and a second exposure giving a faster, much taller, longer secondary peak. Label both exposures, the lag phase, and the primary and secondary responses.
[bio22-s2.11.b] Antibodies work in blood and tissue fluid, but some pathogens hide inside body cells. The cell-mediated response uses T cells to find and deal with those infected cells, and it begins with the cell showing that it is infected.
[bio22-s2.11.0] The cell-mediated response is the part of adaptive immunity carried out by T lymphocytes acting directly on cells, not by antibodies.
[bio22-s2.11.1] It is needed because antibodies can't reach pathogens inside body cells, such as viruses and some bacteria, for example the TB bacterium, which lives inside macrophages.
[bio22-s2.11.2] It also destroys cancer cells and cells of transplanted organs, which is why transplant patients take drugs that suppress the immune system.
[bio22-s2.11.3] Antigen presentation: an infected body cell displays fragments of the pathogen's antigens on its surface, held in its self-marker proteins (MHC proteins).
[bio22-s2.11.4] Macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells that have engulfed a pathogen display its antigens in the same way.
[bio22-s2.11.5] T cells can't recognise free antigens, so this presentation is how they detect infection.
[bio22-s2.12.b] Helper T cells act as the managers of adaptive immunity. Once one is activated, it signals to the other cells that need to act, so it links the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.12.0] Activation of helper T cells: a helper T cell whose receptor fits the displayed antigen binds to the antigen-presenting cell and is activated. It divides to form a clone of helper T cells, as B cells do.
[bio22-s2.12.1] Helper T cells coordinate the response by releasing chemical signals called cytokines.
[bio22-s2.12.2] Cytokines stimulate the matching killer T cells to divide.
[bio22-s2.12.3] Cytokines also stimulate the matching B cells to divide into plasma cells and memory cells, because B cells need this help to respond fully.
[bio22-s2.12.4] Cytokines also attract and activate macrophages, which increases phagocytosis at the infection.
[bio22-s2.12.5] Helper T cells therefore link the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.13.b] After helper T cells give the signal, the killer T cells destroy infected cells, the B cells add antibodies, and suppressor T cells end the response. Memory cells are left behind for next time.
[bio22-s2.13.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) of the clone bind to body cells displaying the antigen and kill them, destroying the pathogens inside before they can multiply. There is more on this in White blood cells.
[bio22-s2.13.1] When the infection is cleared, suppressor T cells switch off the response by stopping the activity of killer T cells and B cells, so that healthy tissue isn't damaged.
[bio22-s2.13.2] Memory T cells remain, so a second infection triggers a faster, larger cell-mediated response.
[bio22-s2.13.3] Role of B lymphocytes: B cells act as antigen-presenting cells, taking in the antigen they bind and displaying it to helper T cells.
[bio22-s2.13.4] The antibodies released by the plasma cells of B cells mark infected cells and pathogens for destruction.
[bio22-s2.13.5] Diagram: draw a flow diagram in this order: pathogen; antigen presented by macrophage or infected cell; helper T cell activated and cloned; cytokines; killer T cells cloned, infected cells killed; B cells cloned into plasma cells, antibodies; memory T and B cells formed; suppressor T cells end response.
[bio22-s2.14.b] The two systems differ in speed, specificity and memory, but they are not separate. A comparison of the two is a standard exam question, so learn each as a pair.
[bio22-s2.14.0] Presence: innate immunity is present from birth, while adaptive immunity develops after exposure to a pathogen.
[bio22-s2.14.1] Specificity: innate immunity is non-specific, giving the same response to every pathogen, while adaptive immunity is specific, with each response against one antigen.
[bio22-s2.14.2] Memory: innate immunity gives the same response each time, while adaptive immunity has memory, so a second response is faster and stronger.
[bio22-s2.14.3] Speed: innate immunity acts within minutes to hours, while adaptive immunity takes days on first exposure.
[bio22-s2.14.4] Mechanisms: innate immunity uses barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages), inflammation, fever, complement, interferons and natural killer cells; adaptive immunity uses B and T lymphocytes and antibodies.
[bio22-s2.14.5] Distribution: innate defences are found in all animals and in plants, while adaptive immunity is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.14.6] The two systems work together: macrophages of the innate system present antigens to start the adaptive response, and antibodies of the adaptive system make phagocytosis and complement more effective.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio22-s5: White blood cells
[bio22-s5.0.b] The immune system is run by several different kinds of white blood cell, and they all start from the same place. Knowing where each type comes from and matures explains how the body learns to attack invaders but not itself.
[bio22-s5.0.0] White blood cells (leucocytes) are the cells of the immune system. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, which holds the genes they need to make the proteins for their defensive jobs.
[bio22-s5.0.1] All white blood cells develop from stem cells (cells that can divide and become different cell types) in the red bone marrow, the soft tissue inside some bones.
[bio22-s5.0.2] They travel in the blood and lymph (the fluid that drains from tissues), and many leave the capillaries to enter the tissues, because that is where pathogens (disease-causing organisms) are found.
[bio22-s5.0.3] There are two broad groups. Phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages) engulf pathogens, taking them into the cell.
[bio22-s5.0.4] The second group is the lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells). These are small cells with a large round nucleus filling most of the cell and little cytoplasm.
[bio22-s5.0.5] B lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow. T lymphocytes leave the marrow and mature in the thymus, a gland in the chest behind the breastbone (T for thymus).
[bio22-s5.0.6] During maturation, lymphocytes that would attack the body's own cells are removed, so mature lymphocytes respond only to non-self antigens (molecules on a pathogen's surface that the immune system recognises as foreign).
[bio22-s5.1.b] Monocytes are the white blood cells that travel in the blood and then settle in tissues as macrophages. Macrophages are the body's general-purpose cleaners and also the link between the fast, general response and the specific one.
[bio22-s5.1.0] Monocytes are the largest white blood cells, with a large kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus, which makes them easy to tell from lymphocytes under the microscope.
[bio22-s5.1.1] They circulate in the blood for a few days, then move into tissues and develop into macrophages, which can live for months.
[bio22-s5.1.2] Macrophages carry out phagocytosis (engulfing and digesting) of pathogens, dead cells and debris. There is more on the stages in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.1.3] Macrophages are antigen-presenting cells: they display fragments of engulfed pathogens on their surface to activate helper T cells, so they link innate immunity (the fast, non-specific response) and adaptive immunity (the slower, specific one).
[bio22-s5.1.4] Macrophages release chemicals that cause inflammation and fever and attract other white blood cells to the site of infection.
[bio22-s5.1.5] Diagram: a monocyte (large cell, kidney-shaped nucleus) beside a lymphocyte (small cell, large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm). Label nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane.
[bio22-s5.2.b] Natural killer (NK) cells are a fast first line of attack against body cells that have gone wrong, either because a virus is hiding inside them or because they have become cancerous. They check cells for a missing marker rather than for a particular pathogen.
[bio22-s5.2.0] Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes of the innate system. They are not specific to one antigen and need no earlier exposure, so they act within hours of an infection, before B and T cells have responded.
[bio22-s5.2.1] Healthy body cells display normal self-marker (MHC) proteins on their surface, which tell the immune system the cell belongs to the body.
[bio22-s5.2.2] Many virus-infected cells and cancer cells display fewer of these markers, or display stress molecules instead. NK cells kill cells showing this missing self pattern.
[bio22-s5.2.3] NK cells also kill cells coated with antibodies, the proteins that bind to antigens.
[bio22-s5.2.4] NK cells release perforin, a protein that makes pores in the target cell's membrane, and enzymes called granzymes that enter through the pores and trigger the cell to self-destruct (apoptosis, programmed cell death).
[bio22-s5.2.5] The dying cell breaks into fragments that macrophages engulf, so the virus inside is destroyed without spreading.
[bio22-s5.2.6] NK cells also release chemicals that activate macrophages, which helps clear the fragments.
[bio22-s5.3.b] B lymphocytes are the cells behind antibodies. Each B cell is committed to one antibody shape, so the body needs a huge range of B cells to cover many different antigens.
[bio22-s5.3.0] B lymphocytes carry antibody molecules in their membrane as receptors, which let them bind a matching antigen. Each B cell and its descendants make only one antibody shape.
[bio22-s5.3.1] B lymphocytes are responsible for antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity, meaning protection by antibodies carried in body fluids. Clonal selection, plasma cells and memory B cells are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.3.2] Plasma cells, the antibody-secreting form of B cells, are larger than resting B cells, with extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum (a network of membranes studded with ribosomes) to make and secrete large amounts of antibody protein.
[bio22-s5.3.3] Antibodies act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, in blood, tissue fluid and mucus, not on pathogens hidden inside cells, because antibodies cannot get into body cells.
[bio22-s5.4.b] T lymphocytes are the cells that control and carry out cell-mediated immunity, the response that deals with infected body cells. Helper T cells are the coordinators that most of the adaptive response depends on.
[bio22-s5.4.0] All T cells have T-cell receptors on their surface that recognise one antigen, but only when it is displayed on a cell's surface (presented), never as a free molecule.
[bio22-s5.4.1] T cells don't make antibodies. That is the job of plasma cells, which come from B cells.
[bio22-s5.4.2] Helper T cells carry the surface marker CD4.
[bio22-s5.4.3] Helper T cells are the coordinators of the adaptive response, signalling with cytokines (chemical messengers between immune cells). Which cells they activate, and in what order, is taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.4.4] Almost every adaptive response depends on helper T cells, so HIV, which infects and destroys CD4 helper T cells, leaves the body unable to fight many infections.
[bio22-s5.5.b] Killer T cells deal with threats that antibodies cannot reach, because the pathogen is hidden inside a body cell. They destroy the whole infected cell.
[bio22-s5.5.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) carry the surface marker CD8.
[bio22-s5.5.1] Killer T cells bind to body cells displaying their matching antigen, such as virus-infected cells, cancer cells and cells of transplanted tissue.
[bio22-s5.5.2] They kill these cells by releasing perforin and granzymes, which triggers apoptosis (the cell's programmed self-destruction).
[bio22-s5.5.3] Killer T cells use the same killing method as NK cells, but only against cells showing their one specific antigen, whereas NK cells look for missing self-markers.
[bio22-s5.5.4] Each killer T cell can kill many target cells in turn.
[bio22-s5.6.b] An immune response needs brakes, and it needs a record of past infections. Suppressor T cells provide the brakes, and memory T cells provide the record that makes a second infection easier to fight.
[bio22-s5.6.0] Suppressor T cells, now usually called regulatory T cells, are the brakes of the immune system.
[bio22-s5.6.1] Suppressor T cells end a response once an infection is cleared, so the body is not damaged by an immune response that carries on. The details are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.6.2] They also suppress lymphocytes that react to the body's own antigens, helping to prevent autoimmune disease (the body attacking its own tissues) and allergy.
[bio22-s5.6.3] Memory T cells form from the clone of activated T cells (the identical cells that divide from one activated T cell) and remain in the blood and lymph tissue for years or decades.
[bio22-s5.6.4] On re-exposure to the same antigen, memory T cells quickly produce new helper and killer T cells, giving a faster, stronger cell-mediated response. Memory B cells do the same for antibodies.
[bio22-s5.6.5] Memory cells are why vaccination works: the vaccine exposes you to the antigen safely, so memory cells are ready if the real pathogen arrives.
[bio22-s5.7.b] The outcome asks you to compare the roles of different white blood cells. The first comparison is between the fast, general innate cells and the slower, specific adaptive cells, and then between the ways each one destroys pathogens.
[bio22-s5.7.0] Innate versus adaptive: monocytes, macrophages and NK cells are part of innate immunity, which is non-specific, acts quickly and has no memory.
[bio22-s5.7.1] B and T lymphocytes are adaptive: they are specific to one antigen, slower on first exposure, and they form memory cells.
[bio22-s5.7.2] Macrophages engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis. NK and killer T cells kill infected body cells by perforin and apoptosis.
[bio22-s5.7.3] B cells, as plasma cells, secrete antibodies. Helper T cells kill nothing themselves but signal to other cells.
[bio22-s5.7.4] Suppressor T cells regulate the response. Memory cells kill nothing but enable a rapid future response.
[bio22-s5.8.b] Cells also differ in what they attack and in how they recognise it. This explains why different cells are needed to cover both free pathogens and pathogens hidden inside body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.0] Where the target is: antibodies from B cells act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, because antibodies cannot enter body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.1] Killer T and NK cells act on pathogens inside body cells by killing the cell. Macrophages engulf free pathogens.
[bio22-s5.8.2] Recognition: B cells recognise free antigens with membrane antibodies, while T cells recognise only antigens presented on cell surfaces.
[bio22-s5.8.3] NK cells recognise missing or abnormal self-markers, not a specific antigen.
[bio22-s5.8.4] Macrophages recognise general features of pathogens and antibody-coated particles, so they need no specific receptor for each pathogen.
[bio22-s5.9.b] This list puts each cell side by side: whether it is innate or adaptive, whether it is specific, its main role, and where it matures. Use it to compare cells quickly.
[bio22-s5.9.0] Monocyte or macrophage: innate; non-specific; main role is phagocytosis and antigen presentation; matures in the bone marrow, then in tissues.
[bio22-s5.9.1] NK cell: innate; non-specific; kills infected and cancer cells; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.2] B lymphocyte: adaptive; specific; main role is antibodies (via plasma cells) and memory; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.3] Helper T cell: adaptive; specific; activates B cells, killer T cells and macrophages (CD4); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.4] Killer T cell: adaptive; specific; kills infected, cancer and transplant cells (CD8); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.5] Suppressor T cell: adaptive; ends responses and prevents attack on self; matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.6] Memory B and T cells: adaptive; specific; long-lived, giving a fast secondary response.
[bio22-s5.10.b] In a real infection the cells act as a team in sequence. Influenza, a virus that infects the cells lining the airways, shows how the innate and adaptive cells combine.
[bio22-s5.10.0] First, infected cells release interferons (chemical signals that warn neighbouring cells of a virus) and NK cells kill some of them within hours. Macrophages engulf viruses and present antigens, and helper T cells are activated.
[bio22-s5.10.1] Next, killer T cells destroy infected cells while plasma cells release antibodies that neutralise free viruses, so both hidden and free viruses are dealt with.
[bio22-s5.10.2] Finally, suppressor T cells end the response and memory cells remain. Because flu antigens change, memory cells may not recognise next year's strain.

CANDIDATES bio23-s1: The male reproductive system
[bio23-s1.0.b] The male reproductive system has two jobs: making sperm and a hormone, and getting the sperm to the female. Knowing the route sperm take makes each organ's role easy to place.
[bio23-s1.0.0] The male reproductive system produces sperm, the male gametes (sex cells), and the hormone testosterone, and delivers sperm into the female's vagina during sexual intercourse, so that a sperm can fertilise an egg.
[bio23-s1.0.1] Sperm pass in order: testis → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra (where fluids from the seminal vesicles and prostate are added) → out through the penis. Sperm are made in the testis, so that is the starting point.
[bio23-s1.0.2] Semen is sperm suspended in seminal fluid. Seminal fluid, made by the seminal vesicles and the prostate, gives the sperm a liquid to swim in and supplies an energy source.
[bio23-s1.0.3] Seminal fluid is slightly alkaline overall, which neutralises the acidity of the vagina and of any urine left in the urethra, because acid damages sperm.
[bio23-s1.0.4] Sperm make up only a small part of the volume of semen, because most of it is seminal fluid.
[bio23-s1.1.b] Sperm cannot be made at normal body temperature, so the testes hang outside the body. The scrotum is the sac that holds them and keeps them at the right temperature.
[bio23-s1.1.0] Scrotum: a sac of skin and muscle hanging outside the abdomen that holds the two testes. Muscles in it pull the testes closer to the body when it is cold, to keep them warm, and relax to let them hang lower when it is warm.
[bio23-s1.1.1] The scrotum keeps the testes about 2–3 °C below core body temperature (37 °C), which sperm production needs; this is why the testes are outside the body.
[bio23-s1.2.b] The testes are where sperm and testosterone are made. Inside, two different tissues do these two jobs.
[bio23-s1.2.0] Testes (singular testis): the two oval male gonads, which are the primary sex organs (the organs that make the gametes).
[bio23-s1.2.1] Each testis is packed with many long, tightly coiled seminiferous tubules, whose walls produce sperm by meiosis (cell division that halves the chromosome number), continuously from puberty.
[bio23-s1.2.2] The coiling packs a very long length of tubule, and so a large sperm-producing surface, into a small organ, because a long tube can fit in little space when coiled.
[bio23-s1.2.3] Between the seminiferous tubules are interstitial cells, which secrete testosterone into the blood; the testes are therefore also endocrine glands (glands that release hormones into the blood, so the hormone reaches the whole body).
[bio23-s1.2.4] Testosterone has roles in puberty, sperm production and the secondary sexual characteristics (the body changes that develop at puberty). There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s1.2.5] The testes form inside the abdomen of the foetus and normally descend into the scrotum before birth, which puts them in the cooler position sperm production needs.
[bio23-s1.2.6] A testis that has not descended cannot produce sperm normally, which is a known cause of male infertility.
[bio23-s1.3.b] Once made, sperm travel through a series of tubes. Each one has a different job: storage, transport and exit from the body.
[bio23-s1.3.0] Epididymis: a single, very long, tightly coiled tube lying against the back of each testis.
[bio23-s1.3.1] Sperm from the seminiferous tubules pass into the epididymis, where they are stored and mature, gaining the ability to swim. The coiling gives a long tube for storage in a small space.
[bio23-s1.3.2] Vas deferens (sperm duct; plural vasa deferentia): a muscular tube carrying sperm from the epididymis up into the abdomen and around the bladder, to join the urethra.
[bio23-s1.3.3] During ejaculation the smooth muscle of the vas deferens contracts in waves (peristalsis) to push sperm along, which is why the tube has a thick muscular wall.
[bio23-s1.3.4] The vas deferens is the tube that is cut and tied in a vasectomy, the male sterilisation operation, so that sperm can no longer reach the urethra.
[bio23-s1.3.5] Urethra: the tube running from the bladder through the length of the penis to the outside, so it is the final exit for both urine and semen.
[bio23-s1.3.6] In males the urethra carries both urine and semen, but never at the same time: during ejaculation a ring of muscle (sphincter) at the base of the bladder closes, so urine cannot mix with semen.
[bio23-s1.3.7] In females the urethra carries only urine and opens separately from the vagina. There is more on this in The female reproductive system.
[bio23-s1.4.b] Sperm need a liquid to swim in and an energy source. Two glands add fluids that supply these, so that sperm can swim and reach the egg.
[bio23-s1.4.0] Seminal vesicles: two glands behind the bladder whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
[bio23-s1.4.1] The seminal vesicles secrete a thick, alkaline fluid rich in the sugar fructose; the sperm's mitochondria respire fructose to release energy for swimming.
[bio23-s1.4.2] This fluid makes up most of the volume of semen, so the seminal vesicles supply the bulk of the liquid sperm swim in.
[bio23-s1.4.3] Prostate gland: a single gland, about the size of a walnut, just below the bladder, surrounding the start of the urethra.
[bio23-s1.4.4] The prostate secretes a thin, milky fluid containing enzymes that keep the semen liquid after ejaculation, so the sperm can swim free.
[bio23-s1.4.5] The secretions of the seminal vesicles and the prostate together form seminal fluid, which is why seminal fluid as a whole is slightly alkaline.
[bio23-s1.4.6] Because the prostate surrounds the urethra, an enlarged prostate, common in older men, can press on the urethra and make urination difficult.
[bio23-s1.5.b] The penis delivers semen into the vagina and also lets urine out. Its tip and the skin covering it have their own roles.
[bio23-s1.5.0] Penis: the male organ that places semen in the vagina during intercourse, and through which urine and semen leave the body via the urethra.
[bio23-s1.5.1] The penis contains spongy erectile tissue. During sexual arousal its blood vessels widen and the spaces fill with blood under pressure, so the penis becomes larger, firm and erect, which allows it to enter the vagina.
[bio23-s1.5.2] Glans: the rounded, sensitive tip of the penis, with many nerve endings; stimulation of these leads to ejaculation.
[bio23-s1.5.3] The urethra opens at the tip of the glans, so semen and urine leave the body there.
[bio23-s1.5.4] Foreskin: a fold of loose skin that covers and protects the glans when the penis is not erect, and pulls back (retracts) during an erection.
[bio23-s1.5.5] The foreskin is removed in circumcision, an operation that leaves the glans uncovered.
[bio23-s1.5.6] Ejaculation: at the peak of sexual stimulation, waves of muscle contraction in the epididymis, vasa deferentia, glands and urethra expel semen from the penis.
[bio23-s1.5.7] The semen from one ejaculation (a few millilitres) contains many millions of sperm, so many sperm are available to reach the egg.
[bio23-s1.6.b] Exams ask you to draw and label this system, so it helps to know where each part sits in relation to the bladder.
[bio23-s1.6.0] Diagram: side view (longitudinal section) with the bladder for orientation. Label testis, scrotum, epididymis, vas deferens (sperm duct), seminal vesicle, prostate gland, urethra, penis, glans, foreskin, bladder. Draw the vas deferens running up from the epididymis and looping up and over the ureter behind the bladder to join the urethra just below the bladder.
[bio23-s1.7.b] Each point gives the part, where it is, what it does and how its structure suits that job. They run in the order of the sperm pathway.
[bio23-s1.7.0] Scrotum – holds the testes outside the abdomen – keeps them 2–3 °C below body temperature – muscles pull the testes closer to the body in cold conditions.
[bio23-s1.7.1] Testis – the sperm-producing organ, inside the scrotum – makes sperm in the coiled seminiferous tubules and testosterone in the interstitial cells – very long coiled tubules give a large surface in a small organ.
[bio23-s1.7.2] Epididymis – on the back of each testis – stores and matures sperm so they can swim – a single very long, coiled tube that stores a lot in a small space.
[bio23-s1.7.3] Vas deferens – from the epididymis, up into the abdomen and around the bladder to the urethra – carries sperm along the route – a muscular tube whose walls contract in peristalsis.
[bio23-s1.8.b] The pathway continues with the glands that add fluid, then the urethra and the penis, which delivers the semen.
[bio23-s1.8.0] Seminal vesicle – behind the bladder – adds a thick, alkaline, fructose-rich fluid that gives energy and makes up most of the semen – a gland whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
[bio23-s1.8.1] Prostate – below the bladder, around the urethra – adds an enzyme-containing fluid that keeps semen liquid – a gland that surrounds the start of the urethra.
[bio23-s1.8.2] Urethra – from the bladder through the penis – carries semen and urine, never together – a sphincter closes the bladder during ejaculation.
[bio23-s1.8.3] Penis – outside the body – places semen in the vagina – erectile tissue fills with blood so it becomes firm enough to enter the vagina.
[bio23-s1.8.4] Glans – tip of the penis – detects stimulation and leads to ejaculation – many nerve endings in its sensitive tip, and the urethra opens there.
[bio23-s1.8.5] Foreskin – fold of skin over the glans – protects the glans and pulls back during an erection – loose skin that retracts.

CANDIDATES bio23-s3: Gametes and fertilisation
[bio23-s3.0.b] Gametes are the cells that carry genes from one generation to the next, and they are made by a special kind of cell division. Knowing where this division happens, and which cells do it, is the starting point for sperm and egg production.
[bio23-s3.0.0] A gamete is a haploid sex cell, meaning it has only one chromosome of each pair. In humans the haploid number is 23 (n = 23).
[bio23-s3.0.1] Sperm and eggs are made by meiosis (the cell division that halves the chromosome number), so each gamete carries one chromosome of each pair, and each gamete is genetically different from the others. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio23-s3.0.2] At fertilisation two haploid gametes fuse to give a diploid zygote (the first cell of a new individual, with both chromosomes of each pair) with 46 chromosomes (2n = 46).
[bio23-s3.0.3] Meiosis happens only in the gonads (the organs that make gametes): in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes) for sperm, and in the ovaries for eggs.
[bio23-s3.0.4] The cells that undergo meiosis are diploid. Every other cell in the body divides by mitosis, which keeps the chromosome number the same.
[bio23-s3.1.b] Unlike eggs, sperm are made continuously, in huge numbers, from puberty onwards. The process happens in the testes and finishes in a storage tube beside them.
[bio23-s3.1.0] Sperm production begins at puberty and then continues every day throughout a man's life, though it declines in old age.
[bio23-s3.1.1] Many millions of sperm are made each day in the walls of the seminiferous tubules, from diploid cells that first multiply by mitosis (which keeps a supply of cells available) and then divide by meiosis.
[bio23-s3.1.2] Each cell that completes meiosis gives four sperm, all of equal size.
[bio23-s3.1.3] Half the sperm carry an X chromosome and half carry a Y, because the dividing cell has one X and one Y. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio23-s3.1.4] The new sperm move into the epididymis (a coiled tube on the back of each testis), where they are stored and mature, becoming able to swim.
[bio23-s3.1.5] It takes about two to three months for a sperm to be produced and matured, so a change in lifestyle or health affects sperm only after some weeks.
[bio23-s3.1.6] Production needs the lower temperature of the scrotum (the sac that holds the testes outside the body) and the hormones FSH and testosterone.
[bio23-s3.2.b] Egg production runs on a very different timetable from sperm production. A girl's eggs begin forming before she is born, and she never makes more.
[bio23-s3.2.0] Egg production starts before birth: while a girl is a foetus (an unborn baby), her ovaries form all the immature eggs she will ever have, each inside a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac of cells that nourishes the egg).
[bio23-s3.2.1] Each of these eggs begins meiosis but stops part-way.
[bio23-s3.2.2] A baby girl is born with about one to two million immature eggs. Most degenerate (break down), and only about 400–500 are ever released in her life.
[bio23-s3.2.3] No new eggs are made after birth, which is why the supply is fixed.
[bio23-s3.2.4] From puberty, usually one follicle each month develops fully and releases its egg at ovulation (the release of an egg from the ovary). The monthly cycle is dealt with in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s3.2.5] Meiosis continues around ovulation but is completed only if a sperm enters the egg, so an egg that is never fertilised never finishes meiosis.
[bio23-s3.3.b] Meiosis in the ovary does not split the cell evenly, and the eggs it makes are as old as the woman herself. Both facts matter for the early embryo and for fertility.
[bio23-s3.3.0] Division in egg production is unequal: almost all the cytoplasm goes to one cell, the egg, and the other products are tiny cells called polar bodies, which degenerate.
[bio23-s3.3.1] So one cell undergoing meiosis gives one egg, not four.
[bio23-s3.3.2] This unequal split gives the egg a large store of cytoplasm, food reserves and organelles (the small working structures inside a cell) to support the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.3.3] Because the eggs are as old as the woman, they age with her. In older women more eggs have errors in chromosome separation, which is why the risk of conditions such as Down syndrome rises with the mother's age. There is more on this in Mutations.
[bio23-s3.3.4] Fertility also falls with age because of these egg changes.
[bio23-s3.4.b] A sperm has one job, to carry the father's haploid nucleus to the egg. Every part of its structure helps it do that, so it is small, streamlined and powered for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.0] A sperm is a very small, mobile cell, about 0.05 mm long.
[bio23-s3.4.1] The head contains the haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes) and, at its tip, the acrosome, a cap containing enzymes that digest a path through the layers around the egg.
[bio23-s3.4.2] The middle piece is packed with mitochondria that release energy (ATP) by aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen) to power the tail.
[bio23-s3.4.3] The tail is a flagellum (a whip-like tail) that beats to make the sperm swim.
[bio23-s3.4.4] A sperm has very little cytoplasm, which keeps it small and streamlined for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.5] Diagram: a sperm cell. Label the head, acrosome, nucleus, middle piece, mitochondria and tail.
[bio23-s3.5.b] The egg has a different job from the sperm. It cannot move, so its structure is built to supply and protect the new life once a sperm arrives.
[bio23-s3.5.0] The egg (ovum) is one of the largest cells in the body, about 0.1 mm across, just visible to the naked eye. It cannot move by itself.
[bio23-s3.5.1] It contains a haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes, always including an X), a cell membrane, and a large volume of cytoplasm holding food reserves and many mitochondria, which feed and power the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.5.2] Around the egg is a thick, clear, jelly-like coat called the zona pellucida, and outside that an outer layer of follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.5.3] Diagram: an egg, drawn much larger than the sperm. Label the nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, zona pellucida (jelly coat) and follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.6.b] Sperm and eggs are both haploid gametes, but their differences follow from their jobs: the sperm travels and delivers, and the egg stores food and waits. Compare them in pairs, one feature at a time.
[bio23-s3.6.0] Size: sperm are tiny, about 0.05 mm long, while the egg is far larger, about 0.1 mm across, with a far greater volume.
[bio23-s3.6.1] Movement: the sperm swims with its tail, but the egg cannot swim and is moved along the fallopian tube by cilia (tiny hair-like structures lining the tube).
[bio23-s3.6.2] Numbers: there are millions of sperm per ejaculation, but usually one egg a month.
[bio23-s3.6.3] Cytoplasm and food: sperm have almost none, but the egg is a large store.
[bio23-s3.6.4] Sex chromosome: a sperm carries an X or a Y, but the egg always carries an X.
[bio23-s3.6.5] Production: sperm are made continuously from puberty, four per meiosis, while eggs begin to form before birth, one per meiosis.
[bio23-s3.7.b] Fertilisation needs sperm and egg to meet in the right place at the right time. Only a tiny fraction of sperm survive the journey, and the timing is tight because both gametes live for a limited time.
[bio23-s3.7.0] During intercourse semen (the fluid containing the sperm) is ejaculated into the top of the vagina, near the cervix (the neck of the uterus).
[bio23-s3.7.1] Sperm swim through the cervical mucus, which is easiest to pass around ovulation when it is thin, then through the uterus and into the fallopian tubes, helped by contractions of the uterus.
[bio23-s3.7.2] Only a few hundred of the millions of sperm reach the egg. Most die in the acidic vagina, are blocked by mucus, or go into the tube with no egg.
[bio23-s3.7.3] Sperm can survive up to about five days in the female tract.
[bio23-s3.7.4] An egg survives only about 12–24 hours after ovulation.
[bio23-s3.7.5] So intercourse in the five days before ovulation, or on the day of ovulation, can lead to fertilisation, because live sperm and a live egg are then present together.
[bio23-s3.8.b] Fertilisation is more than a sperm touching an egg. It is the fusion of two nuclei, and it happens in a set order that stops more than one sperm getting in.
[bio23-s3.8.0] Fertilisation is the fusion of the haploid nucleus of a sperm with the haploid nucleus of an egg to form a diploid zygote.
[bio23-s3.8.1] In humans it normally takes place in the upper part of a fallopian tube, within a day of ovulation.
[bio23-s3.8.2] 1. Sperm reach the egg and push between the follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.8.3] 2. The acrosome of a sperm releases its enzymes, which digest a path through the zona pellucida. This is called the acrosome reaction.
[bio23-s3.8.4] 3. The membrane of one sperm fuses with the egg's membrane, and the sperm's nucleus enters the egg. The tail is left outside, so the sperm's mitochondria stay out.
[bio23-s3.8.5] 4. At once the egg's outer layer changes and the zona pellucida hardens, so no other sperm can enter. This ensures the zygote receives only one set of chromosomes from the father.
[bio23-s3.8.6] 5. The egg completes its meiosis.
[bio23-s3.8.7] 6. The sperm nucleus and egg nucleus fuse, giving a zygote with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent.
[bio23-s3.9.b] Fertilisation settles several things at once: the child's sex, where its mitochondria come from, and the start of a new genetically unique individual.
[bio23-s3.9.0] At fertilisation the sex of the child is decided by whether the sperm carries an X or a Y, because the egg always carries an X. The zygote receives its mitochondria from the egg only. There is more on this in Sex determination and in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio23-s3.9.1] Fertilisation restores the diploid number, which would otherwise halve in every generation.
[bio23-s3.9.2] It combines genetic material from two parents, so the offspring is genetically unique. Together with meiosis, this is a source of variation.
[bio23-s3.9.3] Fertilisation starts the development of the embryo. The zygote begins to divide by mitosis as it moves down the tube. There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s3.9.4] Diagram: a sequence showing a sperm entering an egg (the acrosome digesting the zona pellucida), the sperm nucleus inside the egg, and the two nuclei fusing to form the zygote. Label the sperm nucleus, egg nucleus, zona pellucida, zygote, n and 2n.
[bio23-s3.10.b] Twins can arise in two different ways, and the difference explains why some twins are genetically identical and others are not.
[bio23-s3.10.0] Identical twins come from one zygote that splits into two embryos early in development, so they have the same genes.
[bio23-s3.10.1] Non-identical (fraternal) twins come from two eggs released at the same time and fertilised by two different sperm, so they are no more alike genetically than other brothers and sisters.

CANDIDATES bio23-s6: Hormones in reproduction
[bio23-s6.0.b] Reproduction is run by chemical messengers (hormones) carried in the blood. Three places make nearly all of them, and in pregnancy a fourth organ joins in.
[bio23-s6.0.0] Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain, the testes (the male gonads, which make sperm) and the ovaries (the female gonads, which make eggs).
[bio23-s6.0.1] The pituitary gland makes oxytocin and prolactin, and also the hormones that stimulate the gonads, which is how the brain controls the testes and ovaries.
[bio23-s6.0.2] The testes make testosterone, and the ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone. In pregnancy the placenta (the organ that links mother and foetus) also makes oestrogen and progesterone.
[bio23-s6.0.3] Prolactin is made by the pituitary gland, and its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts. There is more on where each gland sits in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.b] Puberty is the changeover from child to adult body. A hormone signal from the brain switches the reproductive system on.
[bio23-s6.1.0] Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes (sperm or eggs), and the secondary sexual characteristics develop. These are the body changes, apart from the reproductive organs themselves, that mark a person as male or female.
[bio23-s6.1.1] Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls, and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
[bio23-s6.1.2] It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads (the testes and ovaries). There is more on these pituitary hormones in the Higher Level block on the gonad-stimulating hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.3] As a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone, and the ovaries much more oestrogen. It is these sex hormones that cause the changes of puberty.
[bio23-s6.1.4] Testosterone at puberty starts sperm production, causes growth of the penis and testes, and causes the male secondary sexual characteristics. It also increases sex drive.
[bio23-s6.1.5] Oestrogen at puberty causes growth of the uterus (womb) and vagina, preparing the body for pregnancy, and the female secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.1.6] Oestrogen, working with progesterone, starts the menstrual cycles, so a girl becomes able to ovulate and conceive; the first period is called menarche.
[bio23-s6.2.b] Once puberty has started the gametes, hormones keep them coming. The pattern differs between the sexes: continuous in men, monthly in women.
[bio23-s6.2.0] Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes where sperm are made), and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.2.1] Testosterone is made by the interstitial cells between the tubules, not by the tubules themselves, which make sperm.
[bio23-s6.2.2] Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously rather than in a cycle, which is why men stay fertile from day to day.
[bio23-s6.2.3] Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle (a fluid-filled sac holding a developing egg) to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus). There is more on the hormone levels and feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.3.b] After fertilisation the embryo has to bury itself in the uterus lining (implantation) and the lining must then be kept, not shed. Two hormones make this possible.
[bio23-s6.3.0] Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms.
[bio23-s6.3.1] Without high progesterone, implantation cannot succeed, because the embryo would have no suitable lining to attach to or feed from.
[bio23-s6.3.2] hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation.
[bio23-s6.3.3] hCG keeps the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after ovulation) alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine, using a monoclonal-antibody test.
[bio23-s6.4.b] The corpus luteum cannot support a whole pregnancy, so the placenta takes over hormone production. Progesterone from it then keeps the pregnancy going in several ways.
[bio23-s6.4.0] From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
[bio23-s6.4.1] Progesterone during pregnancy maintains the endometrium and so the placenta, which is attached to it.
[bio23-s6.4.2] Progesterone keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it does not contract and expel the foetus early.
[bio23-s6.4.3] Progesterone keeps the cervical mucus plug (the mucus that seals the cervix, the neck of the uterus) thick, and prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts.
[bio23-s6.4.4] Progesterone, with oestrogen, prevents further ovulation and menstruation during pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.5.b] Oestrogen supports the growth of the foetus by building up the mother's tissues, and late in pregnancy it prepares her body for birth.
[bio23-s6.5.0] Oestrogen during pregnancy drives the growth of the foetus: it stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, and increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta, which supplies the foetus.
[bio23-s6.5.1] Oestrogen also develops the milk ducts of the breasts, so they are ready to carry milk after birth.
[bio23-s6.5.2] Late in pregnancy oestrogen makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
[bio23-s6.5.3] Level summary: progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.
[bio23-s6.6.b] Birth needs the powerful uterus muscle to contract strongly. A shift in hormone balance and the hormone oxytocin make this happen.
[bio23-s6.6.0] Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
[bio23-s6.6.1] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus (a part of the brain just above the pituitary) and released by the pituitary into the blood.
[bio23-s6.6.2] Oxytocin causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour, the process of giving birth.
[bio23-s6.7.b] Labour speeds itself up. Each contraction causes more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, a pattern called positive feedback, where a change leads to more of the same change.
[bio23-s6.7.0] 1. The baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix.
[bio23-s6.7.1] 2. Stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain.
[bio23-s6.7.2] 3. The pituitary releases more oxytocin.
[bio23-s6.7.3] 4. Stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more.
[bio23-s6.7.4] 5. The cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus (the stretching), so the loop stops.
[bio23-s6.7.5] A nervous pathway (the impulses) and a hormonal pathway (oxytocin in the blood) work together here. There is more on this in Nervous vs hormonal coordination.
[bio23-s6.7.6] Diagram: the positive feedback loop as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → back to the start; birth ends the loop.
[bio23-s6.8.b] Oxytocin's work does not stop when the baby arrives. It also helps the uterus recover, and doctors use a man-made version of it.
[bio23-s6.8.0] After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding.
[bio23-s6.8.1] Oxytocin also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size, because its contractions squeeze the muscle smaller.
[bio23-s6.8.2] Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour, and to reduce bleeding after birth.
[bio23-s6.9.b] The breasts are prepared during pregnancy, but milk only starts once the placenta has gone. The hormone that actually makes milk is prolactin.
[bio23-s6.9.0] During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises.
[bio23-s6.9.1] The high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced, even though prolactin is already rising.
[bio23-s6.9.2] When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
[bio23-s6.9.3] Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk, which is why it is called the milk-making hormone.
[bio23-s6.9.4] Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made: supply matches demand.
[bio23-s6.9.5] While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often do not return for some months; this is not reliable contraception. There is more on this in Fertility control and infertility.
[bio23-s6.10.b] Making milk is not the same as delivering it. Oxytocin does the second job, so a feed needs both hormones.
[bio23-s6.10.0] Oxytocin causes the milk to be released (called let-down, or milk ejection).
[bio23-s6.10.1] Suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple.
[bio23-s6.10.2] Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
[bio23-s6.11.b] The first milk a baby gets is different from the milk that follows. It is tuned to protect a newborn rather than to fatten it.
[bio23-s6.11.0] Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth.
[bio23-s6.11.1] Colostrum is a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar.
[bio23-s6.11.2] Its antibodies give the newborn passive immunity, which means protection made by the mother rather than by the baby's own immune system. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s6.11.3] Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose (milk sugar) for energy and growth.
[bio23-s6.12.b] A woman's fertility does not last for life. Menopause is the point where the ovaries stop responding, and the fall in hormones has effects across the body.
[bio23-s6.12.0] Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months.
[bio23-s6.12.1] It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
[bio23-s6.12.2] Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone. It is not simply that the eggs are used up by ovulation, because most follicles degenerate without ever ovulating.
[bio23-s6.12.3] Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, so there is nothing to shed and periods stop.
[bio23-s6.12.4] Effects of falling oestrogen in the short term: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, and vaginal dryness.
[bio23-s6.12.5] Effects of falling oestrogen in the longer term: loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease.
[bio23-s6.12.6] Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.
[bio23-s6.12.7] There is more on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.13.b] One bullet per hormone: its gland, its roles and how its level changes through life.
[bio23-s6.13.0] Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth, puberty, sperm production and sex drive. There is more on development before birth in Sex determination.
[bio23-s6.13.1] Testosterone level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, and declines slowly in later life.
[bio23-s6.13.2] Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, and keeping bones strong.
[bio23-s6.13.3] Oestrogen level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, and falls at menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.4] Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares the milk glands and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.13.5] Progesterone level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.6] Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth, and milk let-down. Its level rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
[bio23-s6.13.7] Prolactin (pituitary): milk production. Its level rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed, and falls when breastfeeding stops.
[bio23-s6.14.b] FSH and LH are the two pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads. In males they hold testosterone steady through negative feedback, and in females they drive the monthly cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.0] At puberty the hypothalamus, the part of the brain above the pituitary, signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.14.1] In males these hormones stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
[bio23-s6.14.2] In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, working with testosterone, so without FSH sperm output falls.
[bio23-s6.14.3] In males, LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
[bio23-s6.14.4] Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH and FSH from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again.
[bio23-s6.14.5] This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.6] Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone) have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.14.7] In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s6.15.b] FSH and LH levels follow the state of the ovaries and the other hormones. When oestrogen and progesterone are high they stay low, and when these hormones are lost they rise.
[bio23-s6.15.0] In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens.
[bio23-s6.15.1] While breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.15.2] After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
[bio23-s6.15.3] Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood and rise at puberty.
[bio23-s6.15.4] In men FSH and LH are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle, with an LH surge at ovulation.
[bio23-s6.15.5] In women FSH and LH are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and high after the menopause. The levels and feedback through the month are in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.16.b] A model shows how a control centre, a hormone, a target organ and feedback fit together. You can then use it to predict what happens when one part changes.
[bio23-s6.16.0] A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback, with − for inhibition and + for stimulation.
[bio23-s6.16.1] Use the model to predict effects: blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone, as in the pill, keeps FSH and LH low.
[bio23-s6.16.2] Diagram: two flow charts side by side, male then female. Male: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → testes → sperm and testosterone, with a “−” arrow from testosterone back to the pituitary.
[bio23-s6.16.3] Female flow chart: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with “−” arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a “+” arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge.

CANDIDATES bio23-s8: Fertility control and infertility
[bio23-s8.0.b] Fertility is the ability to produce offspring, but it is not available all the time in everyone. Males and females differ because sperm production runs continuously while egg release happens once per cycle.
[bio23-s8.0.0] A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.0.1] Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life, declining slowly in old age, and every ejaculation (release of semen) contains millions of sperm. This is why a man is fertile on any day.
[bio23-s8.0.2] Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle (ovulation), so fertility comes in a short window once each cycle rather than every day.
[bio23-s8.0.3] The egg survives only about 12–24 hours after release, while sperm can survive up to about five days in the female body.
[bio23-s8.0.4] So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation, when sperm already present can wait for the egg, and the day of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.0.5] In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because the day of ovulation varies from woman to woman and cycle to cycle, the window cannot be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
[bio23-s8.1.b] A woman's fertility is not the same at every age. It follows the state of her eggs, which she is born with and which do not renew.
[bio23-s8.1.0] Female fertility is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline.
[bio23-s8.1.1] Female fertility ends at the menopause, the time of life when ovulation and periods stop for good. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s8.2.b] Contraception means stopping a pregnancy from starting. Every method blocks one step on the route from egg release to implantation, and methods differ in how reliable they are and in what else they protect against.
[bio23-s8.2.0] Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.2.1] Methods work in one of three ways: by preventing ovulation, by preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or by preventing implantation, the embedding of the embryo in the lining of the uterus.
[bio23-s8.2.2] Methods are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
[bio23-s8.2.3] Effectiveness with perfect use: the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective.
[bio23-s8.2.4] With typical, real-life use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time, namely natural methods, condoms and the pill, fail more often, because people forget or make mistakes.
[bio23-s8.2.5] Long-acting methods, namely the implant, coils and sterilisation, stay above 99% effective with typical use, because there is nothing to remember.
[bio23-s8.2.6] Only condoms, male and female, protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), because they stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.2.7] People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.b] Natural methods use no drugs or devices. They rely on behaviour: avoiding intercourse altogether, or avoiding it on the days when pregnancy is possible.
[bio23-s8.3.0] Abstinence, not having intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.1] Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, which they find by combining several signs.
[bio23-s8.3.2] Sign 1, the calendar (rhythm) method: recording cycle lengths over time to estimate when ovulation happens.
[bio23-s8.3.3] Sign 2, body temperature: measured each morning before getting up, it rises by a small amount, a few tenths of a degree Celsius, just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of the hormone progesterone.
[bio23-s8.3.4] Sign 3, cervical mucus (the fluid made by the cervix, the neck of the uterus): it becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation.
[bio23-s8.3.5] Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used, giving a warning that ovulation is near. There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.4.b] Natural methods appeal to some people but are demanding to use and fail more often than other methods in real life. Knowing both sides lets you discuss them fairly.
[bio23-s8.4.0] Advantages of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and they are acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception, for example the teaching of the Catholic Church, which accepts only natural methods.
[bio23-s8.4.1] Limits of natural methods: they need training, daily records and regular cycles, and are much less reliable with typical use.
[bio23-s8.4.2] Natural methods are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, because cycles are then irregular. They also give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.4.3] Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation.
[bio23-s8.4.4] Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception, because ovulation can return without warning.
[bio23-s8.5.b] Barrier methods put a physical block between sperm and egg. Of the methods used during intercourse, only the condoms in this group protect against STIs, because they also stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.5.0] Male condom: a thin latex, or non-latex, sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm cannot enter the vagina.
[bio23-s8.5.1] The male condom also blocks the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV.
[bio23-s8.5.2] The male condom is cheap, widely available and has no hormones; it can tear or slip, and must be put on before any contact.
[bio23-s8.5.3] Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; it also protects against STIs.
[bio23-s8.5.4] Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide (a sperm-killing chemical) and left in for several hours afterwards; it does not protect well against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.b] The copper coil is a device rather than a barrier, but it is grouped with mechanical methods because it is a physical object placed in the body. It works without hormones.
[bio23-s8.6.0] Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse.
[bio23-s8.6.1] Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and it changes the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) so an embryo is unlikely to implant.
[bio23-s8.6.2] The copper coil lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility.
[bio23-s8.6.3] The copper coil can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days.
[bio23-s8.6.4] The copper coil can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.5] It is grouped as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical methods.
[bio23-s8.7.b] Sterilisation is a permanent operation that blocks the tubes gametes travel along. In men this is vasectomy, which stops sperm joining the semen without changing anything else about the man.
[bio23-s8.7.0] Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens (the tube carrying sperm from the testis) is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes. There is more on this in The male reproductive system.
[bio23-s8.7.1] After a vasectomy the man still ejaculates semen, because the fluids come from glands that are untouched; his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected.
[bio23-s8.7.2] The sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.7.3] A vasectomy is not effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
[bio23-s8.8.b] Female sterilisation works on the same principle as vasectomy: the tubes are blocked so egg and sperm cannot meet. Unlike vasectomy, it works straight away.
[bio23-s8.8.0] Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube, the tube that carries the egg from the ovary to the uterus, is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm cannot meet.
[bio23-s8.8.1] After tubal ligation ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before, because the egg is reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.8.2] Tubal ligation is effective at once.
[bio23-s8.9.b] Sterilisation suits some people very well and others badly. The key issue is that it is meant to be permanent.
[bio23-s8.9.0] Sterilisation is very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember, but it should be treated as irreversible: reversal is difficult and often fails.
[bio23-s8.9.1] Sterilisation carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.9.2] It suits people who are sure they want no more children.
[bio23-s8.9.3] Diagram: outline of the male system with the cut in each vas deferens marked, and of the female system with the cut or clip in each fallopian tube marked; label the vas deferens, testis, fallopian tube, ovary, uterus and “cut and tied”.
[bio23-s8.10.b] Chemical methods use hormones or sperm-killing chemicals. The pill is the best known: it is taken by mouth every day and uses synthetic versions of the body's own reproductive hormones.
[bio23-s8.10.0] The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation; the detail of how is in Hormonal manipulation. It also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass.
[bio23-s8.10.1] The combined pill is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed (a bleed like a period) happens.
[bio23-s8.10.2] The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who cannot take oestrogen.
[bio23-s8.11.b] Some hormonal methods release their hormone slowly over months or years, so the user has nothing to remember daily. This is why the implant and the coils, which are long-acting, stay above 99% effective in real life.
[bio23-s8.11.0] Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously.
[bio23-s8.11.1] The implant is a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years.
[bio23-s8.11.2] The injection is given every few months.
[bio23-s8.11.3] The hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS) is placed in the uterus and lasts several years; it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.
[bio23-s8.11.4] The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
[bio23-s8.12.b] Some chemical methods are used after the event or alongside another method rather than as a regular choice.
[bio23-s8.12.0] Emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, work mainly by delaying ovulation, so there is no egg for sperm to meet; one type works within 3 days and another within 5 days.
[bio23-s8.12.1] The copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method. Emergency pills are not for regular use.
[bio23-s8.12.2] Spermicides are chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
[bio23-s8.13.b] Hormonal methods are very effective and have extra health benefits, but they act on the whole body, so they also carry side effects and risks.
[bio23-s8.13.0] Hormonal methods are very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful.
[bio23-s8.13.1] The combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb).
[bio23-s8.13.2] Hormonal methods can cause side effects, for example headaches, mood changes and irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods.
[bio23-s8.13.3] The combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it is not prescribed to everyone.
[bio23-s8.13.4] Hormonal methods give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.13.5] The pill must be taken correctly every day; vomiting or some medicines can stop it working.
[bio23-s8.14.b] Some infections spread mainly through sexual contact, and some of them damage the reproductive system. This links contraception to infertility.
[bio23-s8.14.0] An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact.
[bio23-s8.14.1] Bacterial STIs are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are treated with antibiotics.
[bio23-s8.14.2] Viral STIs are HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B, which can be controlled but is not usually cured. There is more on pathogens in Pathogens.
[bio23-s8.14.3] Chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes, causing pelvic inflammatory disease, which scars and blocks the tubes.
[bio23-s8.14.4] Blocked tubes from pelvic inflammatory disease are a major preventable cause of female infertility, because the egg and sperm can no longer meet, and they raise the risk of ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy that starts outside the uterus).
[bio23-s8.14.5] In men chlamydia and gonorrhoea can inflame the epididymis, the coiled tube on the testis where sperm mature and are stored.
[bio23-s8.15.b] Because many STIs cause no symptoms and can damage fertility, prevention combines protection during sex, testing and treatment, and vaccination.
[bio23-s8.15.0] Prevention: correct use of condoms every time, and fewer sexual partners.
[bio23-s8.15.1] Prevention: regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners breaks the chain of transmission, because many infected people have no symptoms and would otherwise pass the infection on without knowing.
[bio23-s8.15.2] Prevention: vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B. There is more on HPV in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio23-s8.15.3] For HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP) lower the risk of infection.
[bio23-s8.15.4] Treatment of people with HIV reduces their virus to levels at which it is not passed on sexually.
[bio23-s8.15.5] Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics (the drugs no longer kill it), which makes prevention more important.
[bio23-s8.16.b] Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) lets a woman store her eggs while they are still young and healthy, to be used later. It uses a series of steps similar to the start of IVF.
[bio23-s8.16.0] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once, rather than the usual one.
[bio23-s8.16.1] 2. The eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation.
[bio23-s8.16.2] 3. The eggs are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, do not form, and they are stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
[bio23-s8.16.3] 4. Later the eggs are thawed and fertilised by IVF (fertilisation in a dish) or by ICSI (one sperm injected into an egg).
[bio23-s8.17.b] Egg freezing preserves fertility but cannot promise a baby. Who uses it, and why it is not a guarantee, are the points to know.
[bio23-s8.17.0] Uses: before cancer treatment, because chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries, or by women who wish to delay having children.
[bio23-s8.17.1] Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.17.2] Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen, since younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better.
[bio23-s8.17.3] Freezing gives no guarantee of a baby, and not every egg survives thawing.
[bio23-s8.17.4] The process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.
[bio23-s8.18.b] Infertility is common, and its causes can lie with the man, the woman, both, or neither. Finding the cause decides which treatment is suitable.
[bio23-s8.18.0] Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
[bio23-s8.18.1] The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives.
[bio23-s8.18.2] Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither, which is called unexplained infertility.
[bio23-s8.19.b] Male infertility usually comes down to too few sperm, sperm that cannot do their job, or sperm that cannot get out. Lifestyle can also reduce sperm production.
[bio23-s8.19.0] Low sperm count (too few sperm in the semen), and sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped, for example with a faulty tail, reduce the chance that one reaches the egg.
[bio23-s8.19.1] A blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens, for example after infection or vasectomy, stops sperm leaving the testes.
[bio23-s8.19.2] Undescended testes (testes that have not moved down into the scrotum) and low testosterone or low pituitary hormones are also male causes.
[bio23-s8.19.3] Lifestyle and environmental factors reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids and overheating of the testes. There is more on steroids in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s8.19.4] Male infertility is diagnosed by semen analysis, which tests sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope.
[bio23-s8.20.b] Female infertility arises when no egg is released, when egg and sperm cannot meet, or when the uterus cannot support an embryo.
[bio23-s8.20.0] Failure to ovulate, caused by a hormone imbalance, for example polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight, means no egg is released.
[bio23-s8.20.1] Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, for example after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI, stop egg and sperm meeting.
[bio23-s8.20.2] Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries.
[bio23-s8.20.3] Female causes also include problems of the uterus or cervical mucus, and age, because egg number and quality fall.
[bio23-s8.20.4] Female infertility is diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.
[bio23-s8.21.b] If the problem is that a woman does not release eggs regularly, the treatment is to make her ovaries release them. This is a first step before more complex techniques.
[bio23-s8.21.0] Stimulating ovulation is for women who do not ovulate regularly, for example because of PCOS.
[bio23-s8.21.1] Fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs.
[bio23-s8.21.2] The woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles (the fluid-filled sacs in the ovary that each contain an egg) develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation.
[bio23-s8.21.3] Risk: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely.
[bio23-s8.21.4] Risk: the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious.
[bio23-s8.21.5] Lifestyle change, for example weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.
[bio23-s8.22.b] Fertility drugs work by acting on the same hormone system that controls the menstrual cycle, either by boosting the brain's signals to the ovaries or by supplying them directly.
[bio23-s8.22.0] Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation. There is more on feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.22.1] Injected fertility drugs contain FSH, often with LH, and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop.
[bio23-s8.22.2] A final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge.
[bio23-s8.22.3] The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.
[bio23-s8.23.b] Artificial insemination helps when the sperm have trouble reaching the egg. It puts sperm into the uterus by hand, so more of them start closer to the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.0] Artificial insemination, or intrauterine insemination (IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.23.1] This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes, where fertilisation happens.
[bio23-s8.23.2] It is used for a mildly low sperm count or poor motility (swimming), cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm.
[bio23-s8.23.3] The woman's fallopian tubes must be open, because the sperm still have to travel along them to meet the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.4] The same technique is widely used in farming, for example to breed cattle from selected bulls.
[bio23-s8.24.b] IVF takes fertilisation out of the body. Eggs and sperm are brought together in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is then placed in the uterus.
[bio23-s8.24.0] IVF means “in glass”, that is, fertilisation outside the body.
[bio23-s8.24.1] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs.
[bio23-s8.24.2] 2. The eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound.
[bio23-s8.24.3] 3. The eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place.
[bio23-s8.24.4] 4. The embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days, up to the blastocyst stage (a ball of cells ready to implant). There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.24.5] 5. Usually one, sometimes two, embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube.
[bio23-s8.24.6] 6. A pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted.
[bio23-s8.24.7] Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts.
[bio23-s8.25.b] IVF is used when simpler treatments will not work or are unsuitable. It was developed in the twentieth century and is now widely used.
[bio23-s8.25.0] IVF is used for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (checking embryos for a genetic condition before transfer). There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s8.25.1] History: the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe.
[bio23-s8.25.2] Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010.
[bio23-s8.25.3] Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.
[bio23-s8.26.b] ICSI is a version of IVF designed for severe male infertility. Instead of leaving sperm to fertilise the egg in the dish, the laboratory places one sperm inside the egg.
[bio23-s8.26.0] ICSI is the same as IVF except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope.
[bio23-s8.26.1] Because the sperm does not have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility.
[bio23-s8.26.2] ICSI is used for very low sperm count, sperm that cannot swim or cannot penetrate the egg, and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF.
[bio23-s8.26.3] ICSI is also used when sperm are taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle, for example after a vasectomy or a blockage.
[bio23-s8.26.4] ICSI is also used with frozen eggs, which are thawed before the sperm is injected.
[bio23-s8.27.b] A flow chart shows IVF and ICSI together, because they share every step except how the egg is fertilised.
[bio23-s8.27.0] Diagram: a flow chart of IVF with ICSI as a branch: stimulate ovaries → collect eggs → (IVF: mix eggs and sperm in a dish | ICSI: inject one sperm into each egg) → grow embryos for 2–5 days → transfer an embryo to the uterus → pregnancy test.
[bio23-s8.28.b] Discussing contraception means weighing benefits against drawbacks. The benefits range from the individual to the whole planet.
[bio23-s8.28.0] Contraception lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and to space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies.
[bio23-s8.28.1] It prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers.
[bio23-s8.28.2] Condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit.
[bio23-s8.28.3] Hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods, and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer.
[bio23-s8.28.4] At a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats. This is a sustainability link; there is more on this in The cross-cutting themes.
[bio23-s8.29.b] Contraception also has drawbacks, some medical, some personal, and one environmental.
[bio23-s8.29.0] Hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots.
[bio23-s8.29.1] No method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one.
[bio23-s8.29.2] User-dependent methods often fail in real life, because they must be used correctly every time.
[bio23-s8.29.3] Sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted.
[bio23-s8.29.4] Some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds.
[bio23-s8.29.5] Synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.
[bio23-s8.30.b] Treatments for infertility bring benefits that are both personal and medical. Each argument rests on the biology of the technique.
[bio23-s8.30.0] It allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress.
[bio23-s8.30.1] ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers.
[bio23-s8.30.2] Egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.30.3] Preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on.
[bio23-s8.30.4] Donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children.
[bio23-s8.30.5] Research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
[bio23-s8.31.b] Infertility treatments also raise health, cost and ethical concerns. Some are risks to mother and baby, others are questions of fairness and identity.
[bio23-s8.31.0] Stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother; this is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured.
[bio23-s8.31.1] Ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous.
[bio23-s8.31.2] Success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who cannot pay.
[bio23-s8.31.3] ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility, for example some changes on the Y chromosome, can be passed to a son.
[bio23-s8.31.4] Spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s8.31.5] Children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity.
[bio23-s8.31.6] Treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk, for example a higher chance of chromosome conditions.
[bio23-s8.32.b] Egg freezing gives women more control over timing but is not a guarantee. Weigh the benefits against the limits.
[bio23-s8.32.0] For: it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.32.1] For: it gives women more choice over when to have children.
[bio23-s8.32.2] Against: success is uncertain, and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages.
[bio23-s8.32.3] Against: it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs.
[bio23-s8.32.4] Against: it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio26-s1: The urinary system
[bio26-s1.0.b] Every cell produces waste as it carries out its chemical reactions, and some of that waste is harmful if it stays in the body. The kidneys are the main organs that clear it away, and they do a second job at the same time: keeping the body's fluids balanced.
[bio26-s1.0.0] Excretion is the removal of the waste products of metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells). Faeces are not excreted: they are undigested food that was never absorbed, so passing them out is egestion.
[bio26-s1.0.1] The kidneys excrete urea, excess water, excess salts and excess hydrogen ions in urine. Other organs help: the lungs excrete carbon dioxide, and sweat removes some water, salts and a little urea.
[bio26-s1.0.2] Urea is made in the liver from excess amino acids (the units of proteins) by deamination, the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of an amino acid. It is carried dissolved in the blood plasma to the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.0.3] Urea is toxic if it builds up, so it must be removed continuously rather than in occasional bursts.
[bio26-s1.0.4] Transport between environments: blood (the internal environment) carries wastes to the kidneys, the kidneys transfer them from the blood into urine, and urine is passed out of the body (the external environment).
[bio26-s1.0.5] At the same time the kidneys return useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most of the water and most of the salts) to the blood, so nutrients are not lost in the urine.
[bio26-s1.0.6] The kidneys have two roles: excretion (removing urea and other wastes), and osmoregulation and homeostasis, which regulate the volume, water content, salt concentration and pH of the body fluids.
[bio26-s1.0.7] Both roles use the same two processes: filtration of the blood (squeezing small molecules out of it), then reabsorption (taking back what the body needs). What is not reabsorbed is urine.
[bio26-s1.1.b] The kidneys sit well protected at the back of the body and are supplied directly by a large artery. That position and supply let them filter a large volume of blood at high pressure.
[bio26-s1.1.0] The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs at the back of the abdomen, one on each side of the vertebral column (backbone), just below the diaphragm. The lowest ribs partly protect them.
[bio26-s1.1.1] The right kidney is slightly lower than the left because the liver lies above it and takes up space.
[bio26-s1.1.2] Each kidney is embedded in a layer of fat that cushions it against physical damage and helps hold it in place, and is covered by a tough outer membrane, the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.1.3] The adrenal glands (small hormone-producing glands) sit on top of the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.1.4] Each kidney receives blood from a renal artery, a short wide branch of the aorta (the body's main artery), so blood arrives at high pressure and in large volume, which suits filtration.
[bio26-s1.1.5] Filtered blood leaves by the renal vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart).
[bio26-s1.1.6] Blood in the renal vein has less urea, less oxygen and less glucose than blood in the renal artery, and more carbon dioxide because the kidney cells respire. It is the blood vessel with the lowest urea concentration in the body.
[bio26-s1.1.7] Diagram: front view of the urinary system. Label two kidneys, renal arteries from the aorta, renal veins to the vena cava, ureters, bladder, sphincter muscles and urethra. The adrenal glands on top of the kidneys may be shown.
[bio26-s1.2.b] Once urine has formed it must be carried away, stored and released at a suitable moment. Three structures do this in turn: the ureters carry it, the bladder stores it and the urethra lets it out.
[bio26-s1.2.0] There are two ureters: narrow muscular tubes, one from the renal pelvis (the funnel-shaped collecting space inside each kidney) down to the bladder.
[bio26-s1.2.1] The ureter walls contain smooth muscle that moves urine by peristalsis (waves of contraction), so urine reaches the bladder whatever the body position, not just by gravity.
[bio26-s1.2.2] The urinary bladder is a hollow muscular bag in the lower abdomen (pelvis) that stores urine.
[bio26-s1.2.3] The bladder wall is elastic and folded, so it can stretch to hold a large volume and contract to empty.
[bio26-s1.2.4] Two rings of sphincter muscle (muscle that closes an opening) at the bladder outlet keep it closed: an inner one that works automatically (involuntary), and an outer one under conscious (voluntary) control, which young children learn to control.
[bio26-s1.2.5] The urethra is the single tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
[bio26-s1.2.6] In males the urethra is longer, runs through the penis and also carries semen. In females it is short and opens just in front of the vagina.
[bio26-s1.3.b] Cut a kidney lengthways and you can see that it is not uniform inside. The three regions each have a different role in making and collecting urine.
[bio26-s1.3.0] A kidney cut lengthways shows three regions: cortex, medulla and renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.3.1] Cortex: the dark outer layer, under the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.3.2] Medulla: the paler inner layer, divided into cone-shaped renal pyramids.
[bio26-s1.3.3] Renal pelvis: a funnel-shaped space on the inner (concave) side that collects urine and leads into the ureter. No filtration happens in the pelvis and urine is not changed there, so it is only a collecting space.
[bio26-s1.4.b] The real work of the kidney is done by microscopic tubes called nephrons. Each one first filters the blood, then takes back what the body needs, and the leftover liquid becomes urine.
[bio26-s1.4.0] Each kidney contains about one million nephrons, microscopic tubules that are the kidney's filtering units, so together they give a huge filtering surface. Each runs from the cortex down into the medulla and back.
[bio26-s1.4.1] The nephron tubules drain into collecting ducts that run through the pyramids and open into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.4.2] Filtration takes place in the cortex: blood is forced at high pressure through knots of capillaries (glomeruli) into the cup-shaped tops of the nephrons (Bowman's capsules).
[bio26-s1.4.3] Small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea) are filtered out of the blood. Blood cells and plasma proteins are too large, so they stay in the blood. The filtered liquid is called the filtrate.
[bio26-s1.4.4] Reabsorption (taking useful substances back into the blood) takes place in the cortex, in the coiled tubules, and in the medulla, in the loops of Henle and the collecting ducts.
[bio26-s1.4.5] All the glucose and amino acids, most of the salts and most of the water are taken back into the blood. The remaining liquid, carrying urea and excess salts and water, is urine.
[bio26-s1.4.6] Diagram: longitudinal section of a kidney. Label renal capsule, cortex, medulla, renal pyramid, renal pelvis, ureter, renal artery and renal vein. Draw one nephron from cortex into medulla to show where filtration and reabsorption happen.
[bio26-s1.5.b] Urine is what is left of the filtrate once the body has taken back what it needs. Its volume and concentration change from hour to hour, which is how the kidneys keep the water content of the blood steady.
[bio26-s1.5.0] Urine is mostly water, with urea, salts (mainly sodium chloride) and other wastes dissolved in it.
[bio26-s1.5.1] Healthy urine contains no protein and no glucose, because protein is not filtered out of the blood and all the glucose is reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.5.2] After drinking a lot of water, the kidneys produce a large volume of dilute (pale) urine, which gets rid of the extra water.
[bio26-s1.5.3] When water is short (little drinking, heavy sweating in exercise or hot weather, or diarrhoea), a small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine is produced, so the body saves water.
[bio26-s1.5.4] The control is by the hormone ADH. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation; here you only need the effect on urine.
[bio26-s1.6.b] Water is not the only thing the kidneys balance. They also adjust salts and acidity, and together this keeps the body's internal conditions steady.
[bio26-s1.6.0] Salt balance: after a salty meal the kidneys excrete more salt, and when salt is short they reabsorb more.
[bio26-s1.6.1] Keeping the salt and water concentration of the blood steady stops body cells from gaining or losing water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio26-s1.6.2] pH: the kidneys remove excess hydrogen ions from the blood in urine, helping to keep blood pH at about 7.4. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio26-s1.6.3] This is homeostasis: the kidneys keep the internal environment constant whatever is eaten or drunk, much as a thermostat holds a room at one temperature. This works by negative feedback, which is taught in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio26-s1.7.b] Urine is made all the time, but it is passed out only now and then. This sequence shows how it travels and why release can be delayed.
[bio26-s1.7.0] 1. Urine forms continuously in the nephrons and drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.7.1] 2. Peristalsis in the ureter carries it to the bladder in small spurts.
[bio26-s1.7.2] 3. The bladder fills and its wall stretches. Stretch receptors in the wall send nerve impulses that give the urge to urinate.
[bio26-s1.7.3] 4. To urinate, the bladder's muscular wall contracts and the sphincters relax. The outer sphincter relaxes under voluntary control, which is learned after early childhood.
[bio26-s1.7.4] 5. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.
[bio26-s1.8.b] Each part of the urinary system has features that suit its job. Examiners ask you to link structure to function, so learn these as pairs.
[bio26-s1.8.0] Fat layer around each kidney: protects it from physical damage.
[bio26-s1.8.1] Renal artery branching directly from the aorta: delivers high-pressure blood for filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.2] About one million nephrons: a huge filtering surface.
[bio26-s1.8.3] Cortex full of capillary knots: the site of filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.4] Long tubules and loops in the medulla: the site of reabsorption.
[bio26-s1.8.5] Funnel-shaped renal pelvis: collects urine.
[bio26-s1.8.6] Muscular ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristalsis.
[bio26-s1.8.7] Elastic bladder with sphincters: stores urine and allows controlled release. The urethra is the exit for urine from the body.
[bio26-s1.9.b] Because healthy urine contains no glucose or protein, finding either one in a urine sample tells a doctor that something is wrong.
[bio26-s1.9.0] Glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes, because blood glucose is so high that not all of it can be reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.9.1] Protein in urine is a sign of damage to the kidney's filters, because healthy filters do not let protein through.
[bio26-s1.9.2] Urine tests are also used to detect drugs, for example in sport.
[bio26-s1.10.b] If the kidneys stop working, the body can no longer clear its wastes or balance its fluids. There are two treatments: a machine that does the kidney's job, or a replacement kidney.
[bio26-s1.10.0] If the kidneys stop working, urea, salts and water build up in the blood and the person will die without treatment.
[bio26-s1.10.1] Treatment is by dialysis or by a kidney transplant.
[bio26-s1.10.2] A transplant gives a working kidney, but the patient must take drugs to stop the immune system rejecting it, because the body treats the new kidney as foreign. There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio26-s1.11.b] A dialysis machine copies the kidney's filtering. It uses diffusion across a membrane to clean the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.0] The patient's blood is pumped through a machine where it flows past a selectively permeable membrane (one that lets only some substances through), with dialysis fluid on the other side.
[bio26-s1.11.1] Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid, because their concentration is higher in the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.2] The fluid contains glucose and salts at normal blood concentrations, so these are not lost from the blood. Blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane.
[bio26-s1.11.3] Dialysis must be repeated several times a week, because it does not work continuously the way healthy kidneys do.

CANDIDATES bio28-s1: Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism
[bio28-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a steady supply of oxygen and must get rid of the carbon dioxide it makes. The breathing system is the body's way of swapping these gases with the air.
[bio28-s1.0.0] Every cell respires aerobically (releases energy from food using oxygen), so every cell uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s1.0.1] The breathing system exchanges gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid, the fluid that bathes the cells): oxygen is taken into the blood, and carbon dioxide and some water vapour are removed.
[bio28-s1.0.2] Breathing, or ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. It is not respiration, which is the chemical release of energy inside cells, and gas exchange is not respiration either.
[bio28-s1.0.3] A large animal such as a human cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin, because its surface area is too small for its volume and the distances to its inner cells are too great.
[bio28-s1.0.4] So it needs a specialised exchange surface, the alveoli (tiny air sacs in the lungs), and a transport system, the blood, to carry gases to and from every cell.
[bio28-s1.1.b] The breathing tract is the pathway air follows from outside to the lungs. The first parts clean, warm and moisten the air and keep food out of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.0] Nose (nasal cavity): hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and microorganisms. The lining has a rich blood supply that warms the air, and the mucus moistens it, so the delicate alveoli do not dry out or chill.
[bio28-s1.1.1] Breathing through the mouth skips this filtering, warming and moistening, which is why nose breathing is better for the lungs.
[bio28-s1.1.2] Pharynx (throat): the passage behind the nose and mouth that is shared by air and food.
[bio28-s1.1.3] Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage (firm, flexible tissue) that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink pass into the oesophagus (the food tube) instead of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.4] Choking happens when food enters the trachea instead.
[bio28-s1.1.5] Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea. It contains the vocal cords, which vibrate as air passes over them, producing sound.
[bio28-s1.2.b] Below the larynx the airway divides again and again, like an upside-down tree, until it reaches the alveoli where gases actually cross into the blood.
[bio28-s1.2.0] Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation.
[bio28-s1.2.1] The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
[bio28-s1.2.2] Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium (a surface layer of cells carrying tiny hair-like cilia) with mucus-secreting goblet cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms.
[bio28-s1.2.3] The cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the body's first line of defence against infection.
[bio28-s1.2.4] Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one to each lung. They are also supported by cartilage.
[bio28-s1.2.5] Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow.
[bio28-s1.2.6] The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels). The alveoli are the site of gas exchange.
[bio28-s1.3.b] The lungs have no muscle of their own, so they depend on the chest wall and on the muscles around it. The pleural membranes are what link the lungs to the chest wall.
[bio28-s1.3.0] The two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (ribs, sternum and vertebrae) and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm.
[bio28-s1.3.1] Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move.
[bio28-s1.3.2] The fluid also makes the lungs cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
[bio28-s1.3.3] Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax.
[bio28-s1.3.4] Intercostal muscles: two sets, external and internal, lying between the ribs, which move the rib cage.
[bio28-s1.3.5] Diagram: the breathing system in a front view of the chest. Label the nasal cavity, pharynx, epiglottis, larynx, trachea with cartilage rings, two bronchi, bronchioles, lungs, pleural membranes, ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm and an outline of the heart. Add an enlarged inset of alveoli wrapped in capillaries.
[bio28-s1.4.b] Breathing in works by changing pressure. The muscles enlarge the thorax, the pressure in the lungs drops below the pressure outside, and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.0] Air always moves from a region of higher pressure to one of lower pressure. When the thorax volume increases, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure (the pressure of the air outside) and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.1] When the thorax volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs follow the chest wall because they have no muscle of their own.
[bio28-s1.4.2] Inhalation (inspiration) is an active process, which means it uses energy. 1. The brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles.
[bio28-s1.4.3] 2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards.
[bio28-s1.4.4] 3. The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and sternum upwards and outwards.
[bio28-s1.4.5] 4. The volume of the thorax increases.
[bio28-s1.4.6] 5. The lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.4.7] 6. Air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP (the cell's energy currency), so inhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.5.b] Breathing out at rest needs no muscle contraction at all: the body simply springs back. During exercise, however, extra muscles make exhalation active.
[bio28-s1.5.0] Exhalation (expiration) at rest is a passive process, which means it uses no energy. 1. The impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax.
[bio28-s1.5.1] 2. The diaphragm returns to its dome shape, pushed up by the abdominal organs, and the ribs move down and in under their own weight.
[bio28-s1.5.2] 3. The stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils, like a stretched elastic band springing back.
[bio28-s1.5.3] 4. The volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.5.4] 5. Air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used.
[bio28-s1.5.5] Forced exhalation, as in exercise, coughing, blowing or speaking loudly: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up.
[bio28-s1.5.6] This expels air faster and more completely, and because muscles contract, forced exhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.6.b] Putting the two halves of the breathing cycle side by side shows that every change in one is reversed in the other.
[bio28-s1.6.0] Diaphragm: it contracts and flattens in inhalation, but relaxes and domes upwards in exhalation at rest.
[bio28-s1.6.1] External intercostal muscles: they contract in inhalation but relax in exhalation. The ribs move up and out in inhalation, and down and in in exhalation.
[bio28-s1.6.2] Thorax volume increases in inhalation and decreases in exhalation. Lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure in inhalation and rises above it in exhalation, so air moves in, then out.
[bio28-s1.6.3] Inhalation is active, whereas exhalation at rest is passive.
[bio28-s1.6.4] The bell-jar model shows the volume and pressure principle: pulling the rubber sheet (the diaphragm) down inflates the balloons (the lungs). There is more on this in Models.
[bio28-s1.6.5] Diagram: two side-view sketches of the thorax, one at inhalation and one at exhalation, showing the diaphragm (flat or domed) and the ribs (raised or lowered), with arrows for rib movement and air flow. Label the diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, lungs and trachea.
[bio28-s1.7.b] Breathing continues while you sleep because the brain controls it automatically. The signal that adjusts it is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood.
[bio28-s1.7.0] Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain. It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
[bio28-s1.7.1] We can change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum (the thinking part of the brain), for example by holding our breath, speaking or singing. The automatic control takes over again afterwards.
[bio28-s1.7.2] The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises, as during exercise when muscles respire faster, the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.3] This removes more CO₂ and takes in more oxygen. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.4] An adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath, the tidal volume.
[bio28-s1.7.5] During hard exercise both the rate and the depth (the volume of air per breath) increase greatly.
[bio28-s1.8.b] Gas exchange is a simple physical process: oxygen and carbon dioxide each move down their own concentration gradient between the air in the alveolus and the blood in the capillary beside it.
[bio28-s1.8.0] Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used, so it is not active transport.
[bio28-s1.8.1] Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration than the deoxygenated blood arriving from the body in the pulmonary artery.
[bio28-s1.8.2] Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma (the liquid part of the blood), and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio28-s1.8.3] Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than the air in the alveoli. CO₂, carried mostly in the plasma, diffuses out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
[bio28-s1.8.4] The oxygenated blood leaves the lungs in the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio28-s1.8.5] The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air.
[bio28-s1.8.6] Blood flow also keeps the gradients steep, because it constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.
[bio28-s1.9.b] The alveoli are built so that diffusion is as fast as possible. Each feature either gives gases more room to cross, a shorter distance to travel, or a steeper gradient.
[bio28-s1.9.0] Very large total surface area: about 300 million alveoli in the two lungs give an exchange surface of roughly 70 m², so a large amount of gas can diffuse at once.
[bio28-s1.9.1] Very thin walls: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick and pressed close together, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio28-s1.9.2] Moist lining: gases dissolve in the moisture before they diffuse across the wall.
[bio28-s1.9.3] Dense capillary network: every alveolus is surrounded by capillaries with a continuous blood flow, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio28-s1.9.4] Narrow capillaries: they are just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, pressed against the wall. This shortens the distance further and slows the blood, giving time for exchange.
[bio28-s1.9.5] Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation, which helps push air out.
[bio28-s1.9.6] Diagram: a single alveolus with a capillary wrapped around it. Label alveolus wall (one cell thick), capillary wall (one cell thick), moist lining, red blood cell and plasma. Add arrows: O₂ into the blood, CO₂ into the alveolus, and blood flow from pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) to pulmonary vein (oxygenated).
[bio28-s1.10.b] The air we breathe out differs from the air we breathe in, because the body takes oxygen from it and adds carbon dioxide and water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.0] Inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with a variable amount of water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.1] Exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. It is saturated with water vapour and is warmer than inhaled air.
[bio28-s1.10.2] Nitrogen is not used by the body, so its proportion is the same in both.
[bio28-s1.10.3] Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths work in CPR (the emergency procedure that keeps a casualty's blood supplied with oxygen).
[bio28-s1.10.4] Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing that exhaled air contains more CO₂.
[bio28-s1.11.b] Damage to the breathing system shows why its structures matter: when cilia, alveoli or bronchioles stop working properly, gas exchange suffers.
[bio28-s1.11.0] Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production, so mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing (smoker's cough) and frequent chest infections.
[bio28-s1.11.1] Emphysema, mainly caused by smoking: if alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces, the surface area for gas exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless. This shows why the large surface area of the alveoli matters.
[bio28-s1.11.2] Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the amount of oxygen the blood can carry. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio28-s1.11.3] Asthma: the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts, and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that less air reaches the alveoli. Inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle.

CANDIDATES bio28-s2: CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing
[bio28-s2.0.b] Every living cell respires, so every cell keeps adding carbon dioxide to the blood. The body has to match its breathing to that supply, and it does so by monitoring CO₂ rather than oxygen.
[bio28-s2.0.0] Carbon dioxide is produced by every respiring cell. If it is not removed, it accumulates in the blood, where it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (carbonic acid, hydrogen ions and hydrogencarbonate ions).
[bio28-s2.0.1] The reaction is speeded up by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells. A rise in CO₂ therefore makes the blood more acidic, lowering its pH, because more hydrogen ions are released.
[bio28-s2.0.2] Blood pH is normally kept within a narrow range around 7.4, slightly alkaline. A fall in pH alters the shape of enzymes and other proteins and disturbs cell function, so the CO₂ level must be controlled. Breathing is the body's fastest way of adjusting it.
[bio28-s2.0.3] In a healthy person at sea level, the CO₂ level, not the oxygen level, is the main stimulus for breathing. The body is far more sensitive to a small rise in CO₂ than to a fall in O₂.
[bio28-s2.0.4] Oxygen receptors only strongly increase breathing when the oxygen level falls a lot, for example at high altitude. This is why the urge to breathe when you hold your breath comes from rising CO₂, not falling O₂.
[bio28-s2.1.b] A change in blood CO₂ has to be detected before breathing can respond. Special receptors in two sets of locations do this and report to the breathing centre in the brain.
[bio28-s2.1.0] A chemoreceptor is a receptor that detects the concentration of a chemical, here CO₂ and the hydrogen ions it produces.
[bio28-s2.1.1] Central chemoreceptors lie in the medulla oblongata (the lowest part of the brain, joining the spinal cord), close to the breathing centre. They are responsible for most of the response to a rise in CO₂.
[bio28-s2.1.2] CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the cerebrospinal fluid (the fluid surrounding the brain), where it forms carbonic acid and lowers the pH. The central receptors respond to this rise in hydrogen ions, so they detect CO₂ through its effect on pH.
[bio28-s2.1.3] Peripheral chemoreceptors lie in the carotid bodies, small structures in the wall of each carotid artery in the neck (the arteries that carry blood to the brain), and in the aortic bodies, in the wall of the arch of the aorta.
[bio28-s2.1.4] Peripheral chemoreceptors detect a rise in CO₂ (and H⁺) and also a large fall in O₂ in the arterial blood. They send impulses along sensory nerves to the breathing centre.
[bio28-s2.1.5] Diagram: outline of the head, neck and chest. Label the medulla oblongata (breathing centre and central chemoreceptors), carotid bodies on the carotid arteries, aortic bodies on the aortic arch, nerves to the breathing centre, and nerves from the breathing centre to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
[bio28-s2.2.b] Detection is only the start. The signal then passes through a chain from stimulus to response, which brings the CO₂ level back to normal.
[bio28-s2.2.0] 1. Stimulus: CO₂ in the blood rises, for example when muscles respire faster during exercise.
[bio28-s2.2.1] 2. Receptors: the central and peripheral chemoreceptors detect the rise in CO₂, through the fall in pH.
[bio28-s2.2.2] 3. Control centre: impulses reach the breathing centre in the medulla oblongata, which coordinates the response.
[bio28-s2.2.3] 4. Effectors (the muscles that carry out the response): the breathing centre sends impulses more often along motor nerves to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, and in heavy breathing to the abdominal muscles.
[bio28-s2.2.4] 5. Response: the muscles contract more strongly and more often, so breathing becomes deeper and faster.
[bio28-s2.2.5] 6. Return to normal: more CO₂ is exhaled from the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) and more oxygen is taken in, so the CO₂ level in the blood falls back towards normal and the pH rises.
[bio28-s2.2.6] 7. Reset: the receptors detect the return to normal, the breathing centre sends fewer impulses, and breathing returns to the resting rate.
[bio28-s2.3.b] The loop above is an example of negative feedback, the pattern the body uses to keep conditions steady. The same CO₂ signal also speeds up the heart so that the extra air is matched by faster blood flow.
[bio28-s2.3.0] This is negative feedback: the response, removing CO₂, cancels the change that caused it (the rise in CO₂), keeping the blood CO₂ level and pH near a set point.
[bio28-s2.3.1] It works in both directions: if CO₂ falls below normal, the breathing centre reduces the rate and depth of breathing, so CO₂ builds up again.
[bio28-s2.3.2] The cardiac centre in the medulla (the part of the brain that controls heart rate) responds to the same signals. A rise in CO₂ also increases the heart rate, so blood carries CO₂ to the lungs and oxygen to the muscles faster. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio28-s2.3.3] Diagram: a feedback loop flow chart. CO₂ rises, then chemoreceptors, breathing centre in the medulla, diaphragm and intercostal muscles, faster and deeper breathing, CO₂ falls, back to normal, with a loop arrow returning to the start. Draw a matching loop for CO₂ falling.
[bio28-s2.4.b] The same loop explains several everyday and exam situations. In each case, follow what happens to blood CO₂ and the signal it sends.
[bio28-s2.4.0] Exercise: muscles respire faster and release more CO₂ into the blood. The rise is detected and breathing rate and depth increase within seconds, removing CO₂ and supplying the extra oxygen.
[bio28-s2.4.1] Breathing stays fast for a time after exercise stops, while the extra CO₂ is removed and the oxygen debt from anaerobic respiration (lactic acid) is repaid. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s2.4.2] Holding the breath: CO₂ builds up in the blood and the signal from the chemoreceptors to the breathing centre becomes stronger and stronger. Eventually the automatic control overrides the voluntary control by the cerebrum, forcing a breath.
[bio28-s2.4.3] This is why a person cannot hold their breath until they lose consciousness at rest. The urge to breathe comes from rising CO₂, not from falling O₂.
[bio28-s2.4.4] Hyperventilation (deliberate fast, deep breathing) removes extra CO₂, so the CO₂ level falls below normal and the urge to breathe is reduced; the person may feel dizzy.
[bio28-s2.4.5] Before swimming underwater, hyperventilation is dangerous: the urge to breathe comes too late, so the oxygen level can fall far enough to cause blacking out before the swimmer feels the need to surface.
[bio28-s2.4.6] Breathing air with a raised CO₂ content, for example in a poorly ventilated, crowded room or when rebreathing air from a bag, makes breathing faster and deeper through the same loop.
[bio28-s2.4.7] Link to plants: carbon dioxide concentration is also a controlling factor in plants, where it acts on the stomata. That, and the comparison of the plant and human responses, is in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.

CANDIDATES bio5-s1: Characteristics of living things
[bio5-s1.0.b] Biology is the study of living things, called organisms. No single feature separates living from non-living, so the specification lists eleven characteristics: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis. Together they define life.
[bio5-s1.0.0] An organism shows all eleven characteristics at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once. A dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but it is still alive.
[bio5-s1.0.1] Some non-living things show one or two characteristics. A crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells.
[bio5-s1.0.2] A fire seems to 'use oxygen', 'release energy' and 'spread', but it has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions, so it is not alive.
[bio5-s1.0.3] Something is alive only if it shows the full set, not just one or two features. Viruses are the main borderline case. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio5-s1.1.b] Living things are not random mixtures of chemicals: they are built in an ordered way, and the basic building block is the cell. Two of the eleven characteristics, organisation and cellular basis, describe this.
[bio5-s1.1.0] Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms (those made of many cells), cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs.
[bio5-s1.1.1] Even a single cell has organised parts called organelles (small structures inside a cell, each with its own job). There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio5-s1.1.2] Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life, so it is the basic unit of structure and function.
[bio5-s1.1.3] Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process. Multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells, each adapted to one job.
[bio5-s1.1.4] All cells share a cell membrane, cytoplasm (the jelly-like fluid inside the cell), ribosomes (which make proteins) and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
[bio5-s1.1.5] All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division. New cells are never made from non-living material.
[bio5-s1.1.6] These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century. Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
[bio5-s1.2.b] Every organism needs a supply of food to stay alive and to build and repair its body. Nutrition is how it gets that food, and organisms fall into two groups depending on whether they make it or take it in.
[bio5-s1.2.0] Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
[bio5-s1.2.1] Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances (ones not made by living things, such as carbon dioxide and water). Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio5-s1.2.2] A few bacteria are chemosynthetic: instead of light, they use energy from chemical reactions to make their food, so they can live where there is no sunlight.
[bio5-s1.2.3] Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals.
[bio5-s1.2.4] Fungi and many bacteria are also heterotrophs: they absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio5-s1.3.b] Food is only useful once its stored energy can be released in a form cells can use. Respiration does this inside every living cell, and it is easily confused with breathing.
[bio5-s1.3.0] Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food, mainly glucose, inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy), which powers the cell's activities.
[bio5-s1.3.1] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
[bio5-s1.3.2] Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio5-s1.3.3] In human muscle, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid. In yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[bio5-s1.3.4] Respiration is not the same as breathing. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange), while respiration is a chemical process in cells.
[bio5-s1.3.5] Plants respire all the time, day and night, because every living cell needs energy continuously. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio5-s1.4.b] Living cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Metabolism is the name for all of them, and they fall into two types: those that build up and those that break down.
[bio5-s1.4.0] Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction).
[bio5-s1.4.1] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose made from carbon dioxide and water) and protein synthesis from amino acids.
[bio5-s1.4.2] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, and the digestion of starch to maltose.
[bio5-s1.4.3] In short, anabolic means building up and using energy, while catabolic means breaking down and releasing energy. There is more on this in Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life.
[bio5-s1.5.b] Chemical reactions in cells make some products that the body cannot use. Excretion removes them before they cause harm.
[bio5-s1.5.0] Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism, which are substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed. If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
[bio5-s1.5.1] Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs. Urea, made in the liver from excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in urine. Excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
[bio5-s1.5.2] Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata (the tiny pores in leaves). Some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
[bio5-s1.5.3] Excretion is not egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.b] Cells work properly only when the conditions around them stay within narrow limits, even if the outside world changes. Homeostasis is the control that keeps them steady.
[bio5-s1.6.0] Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, such as body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance and pH, despite changes outside or inside the organism.
[bio5-s1.6.1] It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH, so a large change would slow or stop the reactions of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.2] For example, human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C, and insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. There is more on the mechanism, negative feedback, in Homeostasis.
[bio5-s1.7.b] Organisms must cope with a changing environment and must also produce new individuals, because every organism eventually dies. Response and reproduction cover these two needs.
[bio5-s1.7.0] Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
[bio5-s1.7.1] Animals respond quickly through the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio5-s1.7.2] Plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure, e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short, which reduces water loss. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio5-s1.7.3] Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
[bio5-s1.7.4] Asexual reproduction: one parent and no gametes (sex cells). The offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria and budding in yeast.
[bio5-s1.7.5] Sexual reproduction: two gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote (the first cell of the new organism). The offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans and flowering plants.
[bio5-s1.8.b] Organisms get bigger as they develop, and they pass their features on to the next generation. Growth and heredity describe these two processes.
[bio5-s1.8.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism, the building reactions of metabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
[bio5-s1.8.1] Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips. Most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
[bio5-s1.8.2] Heredity: the passing of genetic information, carried by genes made of DNA, from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. 'Like produces like', e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
[bio5-s1.8.3] Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits (its characteristics). Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance.
[bio5-s1.9.b] Here is the full set in one place for quick revision. An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life.
[bio5-s1.9.0] Organisation: highly ordered structure, from cells up to organ systems.
[bio5-s1.9.1] Response: detecting a stimulus and reacting to it.
[bio5-s1.9.2] Respiration: releasing energy from food inside cells.
[bio5-s1.9.3] Nutrition: obtaining food for energy and raw materials.
[bio5-s1.9.4] Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.9.5] Reproduction: producing new individuals of the same species.
[bio5-s1.10.b] These are the remaining five characteristics, covering growth, chemistry, stability, inheritance and cells.
[bio5-s1.10.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass.
[bio5-s1.10.1] Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism.
[bio5-s1.10.2] Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment relatively constant.
[bio5-s1.10.3] Heredity: passing genetic information from parents to offspring.
[bio5-s1.10.4] Cellular basis: made of cells, which arise only from pre-existing cells.

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.
