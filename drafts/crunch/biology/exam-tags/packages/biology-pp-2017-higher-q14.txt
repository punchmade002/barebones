MODEL ANSWER: 1. The virus attaches to a host cell: proteins on its coat bind to specific receptor proteins on the cell membrane.
2. The virus (or just its nucleic acid, DNA or RNA) enters the cell.
3. The viral genes take over the cell, which uses its own ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to copy the viral nucleic acid and make viral proteins.
4. The new viral nucleic acid and proteins are assembled into many new virus particles inside the cell.
5. The cell bursts (lysis), or the viruses bud out, releasing the new viruses to infect other cells.

PART (a)(v) (6 marks) subsections: bio6-s1, bio5-s1
MODEL ANSWER: A virus cannot reproduce on its own. It can only reproduce inside a living host cell, using the host's machinery, so it is an obligate parasite. A virus is also non-cellular. It has no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and almost no enzymes, it has no metabolism of its own (no respiration or nutrition) and has only one type of nucleic acid, DNA or RNA. So it does not show all the characteristics of living things.

CANDIDATES bio19-s3: Neurons
[bio19-s3.0.b] Your body has to pass information quickly between receptors (cells that detect changes), the brain and spinal cord, and muscles and glands. Neurons are the cells that do this job, and there are three types, each with its own role.
[bio19-s3.0.0] A neuron, or nerve cell, is the basic unit of the nervous system; it carries electrochemical nerve impulses (tiny electrical-and-chemical signals that travel along the cell).
[bio19-s3.0.1] Neurons are long and thin, so a single cell can connect distant parts of the body, for example the skin of the foot and the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.0.2] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect a stimulus) to the CNS (the central nervous system: brain and spinal cord).
[bio19-s3.0.3] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands, which carry out the response).
[bio19-s3.0.4] Interneurons, also called relay or connector neurons, carry impulses between neurons within the CNS, so they link the incoming and outgoing pathways.
[bio19-s3.1.b] Every neuron is built from the same three main regions: a cell body, branched dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that sends them on. The shape of each region suits its job.
[bio19-s3.1.0] The cell body contains the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm and organelles, which keeps the cell alive.
[bio19-s3.1.1] The cell body also makes the neurotransmitter (the chemical that passes the signal to the next cell) and the proteins the neuron needs.
[bio19-s3.1.2] Dendrites are short, branched extensions that receive impulses from other neurons or receptors and carry them towards the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.3] Branching gives dendrites a large surface, so one neuron can receive signals from many others.
[bio19-s3.1.4] The axon is a long single fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.5] The axon's length lets impulses reach distant effectors, for example a muscle in the foot.
[bio19-s3.2.b] Many axons are wrapped in a fatty coat that makes impulses travel faster. Fast conduction matters because the body must respond quickly, for example when you pull your hand from something hot.
[bio19-s3.2.0] The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer around many axons, so the impulse does not leak away along the way.
[bio19-s3.2.1] It speeds up the impulse, which jumps between the small gaps in the sheath called nodes of Ranvier.
[bio19-s3.2.2] Schwann cells are cells that wrap around the axon in the PNS (the peripheral nervous system: the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord), their membrane forming the myelin sheath.
[bio19-s3.3.b] At its far end an axon has to hand the impulse on to the next cell, whether another neuron, a muscle or a gland. The axon terminals are built for this hand-over.
[bio19-s3.3.0] The axon ends in swollen synaptic knobs, also called axon terminals.
[bio19-s3.3.1] Neurotransmitter vesicles are tiny sacs in the synaptic knobs that store neurotransmitter, so a supply is ready to be released.
[bio19-s3.3.2] The neurotransmitter is released to pass the impulse to the next cell. There is more on this in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s3.4.b] A motor neuron carries the command from the CNS out to an effector. Its structure is arranged to collect many inputs inside the CNS and then send one signal a long way out.
[bio19-s3.4.0] A motor neuron has its cell body at one end, inside the CNS, with many dendrites.
[bio19-s3.4.1] Its many dendrites receive input from many interneurons, so one motor neuron can combine signals from many sources.
[bio19-s3.4.2] It has one long axon, which ends in axon terminals on a muscle or gland, so the axon reaches the effector wherever it lies in the body.
[bio19-s3.5.b] A sensory neuron brings information in from a receptor to the CNS. It has a different layout from a motor neuron, with its cell body off to one side.
[bio19-s3.5.0] A sensory neuron has a long fibre, the dendron, which carries impulses from the receptor to the cell body. A dendron is not the same as a dendrite, which is short and branched.
[bio19-s3.5.1] Its cell body sits to one side of the fibre, in a ganglion (a cluster of cell bodies) outside the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.2] A shorter axon carries the impulse on into the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.3] The long dendron reaches from a receptor, for example in the skin of the foot, to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.5.4] The cell body lies off the main pathway, so it does not slow the impulse.
[bio19-s3.6.b] Between the sensory and motor neurons sits a third type that links them. Interneurons make the CNS a network rather than a set of separate wires.
[bio19-s3.6.0] Interneurons are short, with many branched connections, so each can link with many other neurons.
[bio19-s3.6.1] They are found only in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.6.2] They connect sensory neurons to motor neurons and link many neurons together.
[bio19-s3.7.b] You are often asked to label a neuron diagram or to say which type is shown. Practise drawing both types so that you can place every part and show the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.7.0] Diagram: a motor neuron and a sensory neuron, labelling cell body, nucleus, dendrites, dendron (sensory), axon, myelin sheath, Schwann cell, node of Ranvier, axon terminals, neurotransmitter vesicles, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.8.b] The importance of myelin shows when it is damaged. Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which this happens.
[bio19-s3.8.0] Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease (the immune system attacks the body's own tissue) in which the body's immune system damages the myelin sheath in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.8.1] Damaged myelin slows or blocks impulses, because the insulation that lets them jump between the nodes of Ranvier is lost.
[bio19-s3.8.2] Symptoms include problems with movement, balance and vision.

CANDIDATES bio22-s1: Pathogens
[bio22-s1.0.b] Some organisms and particles make us ill, and the biology of disease starts with naming them. A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host, the organism it lives in or on.
[bio22-s1.0.0] An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, which is why such diseases can spread through a population.
[bio22-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful, so bacteria and fungi should not be lumped together as 'germs'. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s1.0.2] Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: they invade and destroy host cells or tissues, they release toxins, or they take nutrients from the host.
[bio22-s1.0.3] Invasion means the pathogen destroys cells: viruses burst the cells they reproduce in, and the malaria parasite bursts red blood cells.
[bio22-s1.0.4] Toxins are poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work; the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms.
[bio22-s1.0.5] In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage, for example inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia.
[bio22-s1.1.b] A pathogen only survives if it can keep moving from host to host. So each one is adapted to its host and, often, to a carrier that helps it spread.
[bio22-s1.1.0] Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host.
[bio22-s1.1.1] Many pathogens need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease: the mosquito for malaria and aphids (small sap-sucking insects) for many plant viruses.
[bio22-s1.1.2] The routes by which pathogens pass between hosts are called routes of transmission. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.2.b] A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is neither a cell nor a virus. It is the odd one out among pathogens, because a protein alone causes the disease.
[bio22-s1.2.0] The name comes from proteinaceous infectious particle, a term coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
[bio22-s1.2.1] A prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein, the prion protein, which is found on nerve cells, especially in the brain.
[bio22-s1.2.2] When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction.
[bio22-s1.2.3] Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so that the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope. This is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathy.
[bio22-s1.2.4] Prion diseases take years to appear, are always fatal and have no cure.
[bio22-s1.3.b] Prion diseases have been found in cattle, sheep and humans. Because prions are very hard to destroy, prevention depends on keeping infected material out of the food chain.
[bio22-s1.3.0] Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, mad cow disease) in cattle spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals.
[bio22-s1.3.1] Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans is linked to eating BSE-infected beef and was first described in the UK in 1996.
[bio22-s1.3.2] Scrapie affects sheep. Kuru affected the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
[bio22-s1.3.3] Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants.
[bio22-s1.3.4] The immune system does not attack prions, because they are the body's own protein in a different shape, so the body has no defence against them.
[bio22-s1.3.5] Prevention therefore keeps prions out of the food chain: meat-and-bone meal is banned from the feed of farm animals, and the brain and spinal cord of cattle are removed from food as specified risk material.
[bio22-s1.4.b] A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat. It is an obligate intracellular parasite, meaning it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s1.4.0] Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work.
[bio22-s1.4.1] Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins (molecules on the cell surface that it fits onto), so it infects only certain hosts and tissues: HIV infects helper T cells and influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways.
[bio22-s1.4.2] Human viral diseases include influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) and AIDS (HIV).
[bio22-s1.4.3] Animal viral diseases include foot-and-mouth disease, which affects cattle, sheep and pigs, and avian influenza, which affects birds.
[bio22-s1.4.4] Plant viral disease: tobacco mosaic virus infects tobacco and tomatoes.
[bio22-s1.4.5] Antibiotics don't affect viruses, because viruses have no cell structures for the drugs to target and reproduce inside host cells.
[bio22-s1.5.b] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes with no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, about 1–10 µm long. Only some bacteria are pathogens, but those that are can multiply very quickly inside the body.
[bio22-s1.5.0] Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission (one cell splitting into two) in host tissues, so numbers rise fast.
[bio22-s1.5.1] Most bacteria cause harm by releasing toxins; some invade and destroy tissue.
[bio22-s1.5.2] Most bacterial diseases can be treated with antibiotics, which target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s1.5.3] Antibiotic-resistant strains (bacteria no longer killed by the drug) are increasing, for example MRSA.
[bio22-s1.6.b] Each bacterial disease has a named bacterium and a usual route of spread. Learn them as pairs, and note the plant and cattle examples as well as the human ones.
[bio22-s1.6.0] Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spreads in airborne droplets and mainly affects the lungs.
[bio22-s1.6.1] Food poisoning is caused by Salmonella, which spreads through contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs.
[bio22-s1.6.2] Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae in water contaminated with faeces, and causes severe diarrhoea.
[bio22-s1.6.3] Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani. Its spores in soil enter a wound, and its toxin causes muscle spasms, known as lockjaw.
[bio22-s1.6.4] Lyme disease is caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and is passed on by tick bites, so the tick is the vector.
[bio22-s1.6.5] In plants, fire blight of apple and pear trees is caused by Erwinia amylovora.
[bio22-s1.6.6] In cattle, bovine TB is caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
[bio22-s1.7.b] Fungi are eukaryotes (their cells have a nucleus) with cell walls of chitin. Most are made of thread-like hyphae, and some, such as yeasts, are single cells. Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts.
[bio22-s1.7.0] Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, and the fungus absorbs the products.
[bio22-s1.7.1] Fungi spread by spores, which are often carried by wind, water or contact.
[bio22-s1.7.2] Athlete's foot and ringworm are skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (the protein in skin); ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
[bio22-s1.7.3] Thrush is caused by the yeast Candida albicans in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check.
[bio22-s1.7.4] Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
[bio22-s1.7.5] Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.
[bio22-s1.8.b] Fungi infect plants and animals as well as people. The examples below affect trees, cereal crops and amphibians.
[bio22-s1.8.0] Ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, is killing ash trees across Europe.
[bio22-s1.8.1] Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, is spread by elm bark beetles, which act as vectors.
[bio22-s1.8.2] Cereal rusts such as wheat rust are caused by Puccinia species.
[bio22-s1.8.3] Chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.9.b] Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are neither animals, plants nor fungi (kingdom Protista). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host. Malaria is one example.
[bio22-s1.9.0] Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, which is passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector.
[bio22-s1.9.1] The parasite reproduces first in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia (too few red blood cells).
[bio22-s1.9.2] Malaria is controlled with insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining of standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs such as artemisinin. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.10.b] Other protists cause gut and blood diseases in people, and one protist-like organism caused a famine in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.10.0] Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, which spreads in water or food contaminated with faeces.
[bio22-s1.10.1] African sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei, which is spread by tsetse flies, the vector.
[bio22-s1.10.2] Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus, being thread-like and spread by spores, but it is classed with the protists.
[bio22-s1.10.3] Its spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather, and it rots leaves and tubers.
[bio22-s1.10.4] Potato late blight caused the Irish Great Famine from 1845, because potatoes were the main crop and the disease destroyed them. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.11.b] A parasite lives in or on another organism, its host, feeding on it and harming it. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods. Endoparasites live inside the host.
[bio22-s1.11.0] Tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers.
[bio22-s1.11.1] Humans become infected with tapeworms by eating undercooked infected pork or beef.
[bio22-s1.11.2] Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) is a flatworm of sheep and cattle that damages the liver.
[bio22-s1.11.3] The liver fluke needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.11.4] Onchocerca volvulus is a roundworm spread by blackflies that causes river blindness. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.12.b] Ectoparasites live on the outside of the host. Worms inside the body are treated with drugs, and prevention targets how the parasite reaches us.
[bio22-s1.12.0] Head lice are ectoparasites that feed on blood from the scalp.
[bio22-s1.12.1] Ticks feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio22-s1.12.2] Parasitic worms are treated with anthelmintic drugs that kill them, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s1.12.3] Prevention: cook meat thoroughly, maintain sanitation, and control the second host or vector, because breaking the life cycle stops the parasite spreading.
[bio22-s1.13.b] The six types of pathogen differ in whether they are cells, what genetic material they carry, their size and how they are treated. The treatment follows from the structure, which is why antibiotics work only on bacteria.
[bio22-s1.13.0] Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment.
[bio22-s1.13.1] Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; treated with antivirals, not antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.2] Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; treated with antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.3] Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; from single cells to large networks of hyphae; treated with antifungals.
[bio22-s1.13.4] Protist: eukaryotic and mostly single-celled; DNA; treated with antiprotozoal drugs such as antimalarials.
[bio22-s1.13.5] Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; from millimetres to metres in size; treated with anthelmintics.

CANDIDATES bio22-s2: Innate and adaptive immunity
[bio22-s2.0.b] Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection by pathogens (disease-causing organisms such as bacteria and viruses). The immune system does this by telling self from non-self, and it has two parts that work at different speeds.
[bio22-s2.0.0] The immune system consists of physical and chemical barriers, white blood cells (made from stem cells in the bone marrow), the lymphatic system (lymph nodes, spleen and thymus) and defence proteins in the blood, such as antibodies and complement.
[bio22-s2.0.1] The body's own cells carry their own marker molecules on their surface, which the immune system normally ignores, so foreign cells and molecules, which lack these markers, are attacked.
[bio22-s2.0.2] Innate immunity, also called non-specific immunity, is present from birth, acts against any pathogen in the same way and acts within minutes to hours.
[bio22-s2.0.3] Adaptive immunity, also called acquired or specific immunity, develops during life after exposure to a particular pathogen and acts against that pathogen only.
[bio22-s2.0.4] Adaptive immunity takes days to develop the first time, but it remembers the pathogen, which is why a later infection is dealt with much faster.
[bio22-s2.0.5] Innate defences are found in all animals and in plants; adaptive immunity, with antibodies and lymphocytes (the white blood cells that run it), is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.1.b] The best way to deal with a pathogen is to stop it getting in. Skin and the linings of the body's openings form physical barriers that block or trap microorganisms before they can cause infection.
[bio22-s2.1.0] Skin is a tough outer layer of dead, keratin-filled cells (keratin is a tough protein) that pathogens can't easily cross.
[bio22-s2.1.1] Oil (sebum) from skin glands makes the surface slightly acidic, which slows bacterial growth.
[bio22-s2.1.2] Breaks in the skin are sealed by blood clotting, which stops blood loss and blocks pathogen entry.
[bio22-s2.1.3] Mucous membranes line the breathing, digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts. Their sticky mucus traps microorganisms and dust, so these don't reach the cells beneath.
[bio22-s2.1.4] In the trachea and bronchi (the main airways to the lungs), cilia (tiny hair-like projections) beat the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.
[bio22-s2.1.5] Coughing and sneezing are reflexes (automatic responses) that expel irritants, so trapped microorganisms are blown out of the airways.
[bio22-s2.2.b] Some barriers work by chemistry rather than by physically blocking entry. Acids and enzymes kill or inhibit microorganisms, and harmless resident microorganisms add a living defence of their own.
[bio22-s2.2.0] Hydrochloric acid in the stomach, at about pH 2, kills most microorganisms swallowed with food.
[bio22-s2.2.1] Lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus, breaks down bacterial cell walls, so the bacteria burst or die.
[bio22-s2.2.2] The acidic conditions in the vagina inhibit many pathogens.
[bio22-s2.2.3] Harmless microorganisms living on the skin and in the gut and vagina, the normal microbiota, compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, which leaves pathogens less room to establish an infection. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s2.3.b] If a pathogen gets past the barriers, the innate response moves on to cells that destroy it. Phagocytes find the invader, swallow it and digest it in a process called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.0] Phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens, and the process is called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.1] Neutrophils are the most numerous white blood cells and the first to arrive at an infection. Macrophages, formed from monocytes (another type of white blood cell), are larger and longer-lived.
[bio22-s2.3.2] 1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals released by pathogens and damaged cells.
[bio22-s2.3.3] 2. It binds the pathogen.
[bio22-s2.3.4] 3. It surrounds the pathogen with extensions of its membrane and takes it into a vesicle (a small membrane-bound sac inside the cell).
[bio22-s2.3.5] 4. Digestive enzymes are released into the vesicle and break the pathogen down.
[bio22-s2.3.6] 5. The harmless products are absorbed or released.
[bio22-s2.3.7] Diagram: the stages of phagocytosis in four sketches: a phagocyte approaching a bacterium; membrane extensions surrounding it; the bacterium enclosed in a vesicle; the bacterium being digested. Label phagocyte, nucleus, bacterium, vesicle and digestive enzymes.
[bio22-s2.4.b] Inflammation and fever are whole-body or whole-area responses that bring more defences to an infection and make conditions harder for the pathogen. The signs you notice when you are ill are the immune system at work.
[bio22-s2.4.0] Inflammation: damaged cells and mast cells (cells in tissues that release signals when damaged) release histamine and other chemicals, which widen local blood vessels and make capillaries leakier.
[bio22-s2.4.1] More blood flow brings phagocytes and defence proteins to the site, and fluid leaks into the tissue, giving redness, heat, swelling and pain at the site.
[bio22-s2.4.2] Pus is a mixture of dead phagocytes, pathogens and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.4.3] Fever: chemicals released by white blood cells reset the temperature set point in the hypothalamus (the brain region that controls body temperature) upwards, raising body temperature. There is more on this in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio22-s2.4.4] A higher temperature slows the reproduction of many pathogens and speeds up the reactions of the immune system, so fever helps the body fight infection.
[bio22-s2.5.b] Besides phagocytes, the innate response uses proteins in the blood and released by infected cells. Plants have no adaptive immunity, so their innate defences have to do all the work.
[bio22-s2.5.0] Complement, a group of blood proteins, coats pathogens so that phagocytes engulf them more easily, and it can punch holes in bacterial membranes, bursting them.
[bio22-s2.5.1] Interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that make neighbouring cells resist virus replication, so the infection is slowed before it spreads.
[bio22-s2.5.2] Natural killer cells also act in the innate response, in addition to phagocytes and defence proteins.
[bio22-s2.5.3] Plants have innate defences only: physical barriers (cuticle, bark and cell walls), protective chemicals, and the death of infected cells to wall off a pathogen. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio22-s2.6.b] Adaptive immunity depends on recognising a particular pathogen. The molecules it recognises are called antigens, and the immune system's ability to tell them apart from the body's own molecules is what makes the response specific.
[bio22-s2.6.0] An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen or on a toxin it releases, that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s2.6.1] Antigens trigger a specific immune response, including antibody production.
[bio22-s2.6.2] Each pathogen carries several antigens, so more than one kind of antibody can be made against it. The body's own cell markers are self antigens, which are normally tolerated, so the body doesn't attack itself.
[bio22-s2.7.b] An antibody is the body's protein that matches one antigen. Its Y shape and the shape of its tips explain why each antibody works against only one antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.0] An antibody, also called an immunoglobulin, is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells, which are derived from B lymphocytes. It binds to one specific antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.1] An antibody has four polypeptide chains (chains of amino acids), two long heavy chains and two short light chains, held together by disulfide bonds.
[bio22-s2.7.2] The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ between antibodies and form two identical antigen-binding sites. The rest of the molecule is the constant region, which is the same in all antibodies of a class.
[bio22-s2.7.3] Specificity: the shape of the antigen-binding site is complementary to the shape of one antigen, so each antibody binds only that antigen, like a key in a lock. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio22-s2.7.4] Diagram: an antibody as a Y shape, labelled heavy chain, light chain, variable region, antigen-binding site (two), constant region and disulfide bonds, with an antigen fitting one binding site.
[bio22-s2.8.b] Once antibodies have bound to an antigen, they can stop the pathogen in four different ways. The Y shape matters again here, because the two arms bind the antigen while the stem is recognised by other defences.
[bio22-s2.8.0] Agglutination: because each antibody has two binding sites, antibodies link pathogens together into clumps that can't spread and are easily engulfed.
[bio22-s2.8.1] Neutralisation: antibodies cover the parts of viruses that attach to cells, or bind to toxins (antitoxins), so that they can't act.
[bio22-s2.8.2] Opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens, marking them for phagocytes, which have receptors for the antibody's constant region.
[bio22-s2.8.3] Complement activation: antibodies activate complement (the blood proteins of the innate response), which bursts bacterial cells.
[bio22-s2.9.b] The body can't know in advance which pathogen it will meet, so it keeps a huge variety of B cells ready. Only the ones that fit the invader are chosen to multiply, a process called clonal selection.
[bio22-s2.9.0] Lymphocytes are the white blood cells of adaptive immunity, and there are two main kinds.
[bio22-s2.9.1] B lymphocytes (B cells) produce antibodies. This is the antibody-mediated or humoral response, acting on pathogens and toxins in blood and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.9.2] T lymphocytes (T cells) act directly on infected body cells in the cell-mediated response (Higher Level).
[bio22-s2.9.3] Clonal selection: the body has millions of different B cells, each with one type of antibody fixed in its membrane as a receptor, made before any infection.
[bio22-s2.9.4] When a pathogen enters, only the B cells whose receptors fit its antigen bind to it. These are selected and, with chemical signals from helper T cells, divide rapidly by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells.
[bio22-s2.9.5] The clone differentiates (becomes specialised) into plasma cells and memory B cells.
[bio22-s2.9.6] Plasma cells secrete large amounts of the matching antibody into the blood, thousands of molecules per second, for a few days, and then die.
[bio22-s2.9.7] Memory cells stay in the body for years, ready to respond to the same antigen.
[bio22-s2.10.b] The first time you meet a pathogen, the response is slow. A second meeting is much faster, because memory cells are already there. This difference is the reason you rarely get the same infection twice.
[bio22-s2.10.0] Primary response, on first exposure: there is a delay of several days, often about a week, while matching B cells are selected and cloned. Antibody levels rise slowly and stay fairly low.
[bio22-s2.10.1] During the primary response the person may become ill, because the pathogen multiplies before enough antibodies are made.
[bio22-s2.10.2] Secondary response, on later exposure to the same antigen: memory cells recognise it at once and quickly produce many plasma cells, so antibodies appear within a few days, reach a much higher level and last longer.
[bio22-s2.10.3] The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear, so the person is immune. This is the basis of vaccination. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s2.10.4] Diagram: a graph of antibody concentration (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis), with a first exposure giving a small, slow primary peak and a second exposure giving a faster, much taller, longer secondary peak. Label both exposures, the lag phase, and the primary and secondary responses.
[bio22-s2.11.b] Antibodies work in blood and tissue fluid, but some pathogens hide inside body cells. The cell-mediated response uses T cells to find and deal with those infected cells, and it begins with the cell showing that it is infected.
[bio22-s2.11.0] The cell-mediated response is the part of adaptive immunity carried out by T lymphocytes acting directly on cells, not by antibodies.
[bio22-s2.11.1] It is needed because antibodies can't reach pathogens inside body cells, such as viruses and some bacteria, for example the TB bacterium, which lives inside macrophages.
[bio22-s2.11.2] It also destroys cancer cells and cells of transplanted organs, which is why transplant patients take drugs that suppress the immune system.
[bio22-s2.11.3] Antigen presentation: an infected body cell displays fragments of the pathogen's antigens on its surface, held in its self-marker proteins (MHC proteins).
[bio22-s2.11.4] Macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells that have engulfed a pathogen display its antigens in the same way.
[bio22-s2.11.5] T cells can't recognise free antigens, so this presentation is how they detect infection.
[bio22-s2.12.b] Helper T cells act as the managers of adaptive immunity. Once one is activated, it signals to the other cells that need to act, so it links the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.12.0] Activation of helper T cells: a helper T cell whose receptor fits the displayed antigen binds to the antigen-presenting cell and is activated. It divides to form a clone of helper T cells, as B cells do.
[bio22-s2.12.1] Helper T cells coordinate the response by releasing chemical signals called cytokines.
[bio22-s2.12.2] Cytokines stimulate the matching killer T cells to divide.
[bio22-s2.12.3] Cytokines also stimulate the matching B cells to divide into plasma cells and memory cells, because B cells need this help to respond fully.
[bio22-s2.12.4] Cytokines also attract and activate macrophages, which increases phagocytosis at the infection.
[bio22-s2.12.5] Helper T cells therefore link the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.13.b] After helper T cells give the signal, the killer T cells destroy infected cells, the B cells add antibodies, and suppressor T cells end the response. Memory cells are left behind for next time.
[bio22-s2.13.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) of the clone bind to body cells displaying the antigen and kill them, destroying the pathogens inside before they can multiply. There is more on this in White blood cells.
[bio22-s2.13.1] When the infection is cleared, suppressor T cells switch off the response by stopping the activity of killer T cells and B cells, so that healthy tissue isn't damaged.
[bio22-s2.13.2] Memory T cells remain, so a second infection triggers a faster, larger cell-mediated response.
[bio22-s2.13.3] Role of B lymphocytes: B cells act as antigen-presenting cells, taking in the antigen they bind and displaying it to helper T cells.
[bio22-s2.13.4] The antibodies released by the plasma cells of B cells mark infected cells and pathogens for destruction.
[bio22-s2.13.5] Diagram: draw a flow diagram in this order: pathogen; antigen presented by macrophage or infected cell; helper T cell activated and cloned; cytokines; killer T cells cloned, infected cells killed; B cells cloned into plasma cells, antibodies; memory T and B cells formed; suppressor T cells end response.
[bio22-s2.14.b] The two systems differ in speed, specificity and memory, but they are not separate. A comparison of the two is a standard exam question, so learn each as a pair.
[bio22-s2.14.0] Presence: innate immunity is present from birth, while adaptive immunity develops after exposure to a pathogen.
[bio22-s2.14.1] Specificity: innate immunity is non-specific, giving the same response to every pathogen, while adaptive immunity is specific, with each response against one antigen.
[bio22-s2.14.2] Memory: innate immunity gives the same response each time, while adaptive immunity has memory, so a second response is faster and stronger.
[bio22-s2.14.3] Speed: innate immunity acts within minutes to hours, while adaptive immunity takes days on first exposure.
[bio22-s2.14.4] Mechanisms: innate immunity uses barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages), inflammation, fever, complement, interferons and natural killer cells; adaptive immunity uses B and T lymphocytes and antibodies.
[bio22-s2.14.5] Distribution: innate defences are found in all animals and in plants, while adaptive immunity is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.14.6] The two systems work together: macrophages of the innate system present antigens to start the adaptive response, and antibodies of the adaptive system make phagocytosis and complement more effective.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio22-s4: Viruses: replication
[bio22-s4.0.b] A virus is not a cell, so it lacks the machinery that every living cell uses to build new copies of itself. This is why a virus can only reproduce by taking over a host cell (the living cell it infects).
[bio22-s4.0.0] A virus has no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), almost no enzymes and no energy supply, so it can't make anything by itself.
[bio22-s4.0.1] It reproduces by delivering its nucleic acid (its DNA or RNA) into a host cell. The host cell then makes new virus particles using its own ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier), nucleotides (the building blocks of nucleic acids) and amino acids (the building blocks of proteins).
[bio22-s4.1.b] Every virus follows the same basic cycle of six stages, which ends with new viruses leaving the cell. The first three get the virus's nucleic acid inside the right kind of cell and free to act.
[bio22-s4.1.0] Stage 1, attachment: proteins on the capsid (the protein coat) or the envelope (the outer membrane some viruses have) bind to specific receptor proteins on the host cell membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.1] HIV binds to the CD4 receptor on helper T cells, and the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds to the ACE2 receptor on cells lining the airways. Receptor binding decides which cells a virus can infect, because only cells with the matching receptor can be attached to.
[bio22-s4.1.2] Stage 2, entry: a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria) injects only its nucleic acid through the bacterial wall, leaving the empty coat outside.
[bio22-s4.1.3] Animal viruses enter whole, either by the host membrane folding in around them (endocytosis) or, for enveloped viruses, by the envelope fusing with the host membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.4] Stage 3, uncoating: inside the cell, the capsid breaks down and releases the viral nucleic acid, so its genes can be read.
[bio22-s4.2.b] Once the viral nucleic acid is free inside the cell, the virus uses the cell's machinery to make its parts, put them together and escape. The new viruses then repeat the cycle in other cells.
[bio22-s4.2.0] Stage 4, replication and synthesis: the viral genes take over the cell. Viral mRNA (the message copied from a gene) is made, and the host's ribosomes translate it into viral proteins, which are capsid proteins and viral enzymes.
[bio22-s4.2.1] In stage 4 the viral nucleic acid is copied many times, and host-cell activities are often shut down so that the cell's resources go into making viruses.
[bio22-s4.2.2] Stage 5, assembly: new nucleic acid and capsid proteins come together to form many new virus particles, often hundreds per cell. Viruses are assembled from parts, not grown or divided.
[bio22-s4.2.3] Stage 6, release: either the cell bursts (lysis), releasing all the viruses at once and killing the cell, or, for enveloped viruses, each new virus pushes out through the membrane and takes a piece of it as its envelope (budding), so the cell can survive for a time.
[bio22-s4.2.4] The new viruses infect neighbouring cells, and the cycle repeats.
[bio22-s4.2.5] Diagram: the lytic cycle of a bacteriophage in five or six sketches in a circle: phage attached by tail fibres; DNA injected; phage DNA copied and proteins made; new phages assembled; cell bursting. Label phage, bacterial cell wall, phage DNA, bacterial chromosome, new phages.
[bio22-s4.2.6] Diagram: an enveloped animal virus, such as influenza, entering a cell by endocytosis or fusion and leaving by budding, with the host membrane forming the new envelope.
[bio22-s4.3.b] A virus does not always burst its host straight away. Some bacteriophages (and some human viruses) can instead hide inside the host for a time, so there are two possible routes after infection.
[bio22-s4.3.0] Lytic cycle: the virus replicates immediately, following the stages above, and the host cell bursts. Example: bacteriophage T4 infecting the bacterium E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.1] Lysogenic cycle: some viruses insert their DNA into the host cell's chromosome, where it stays inactive and is copied every time the cell divides, so all daughter cells carry it.
[bio22-s4.3.2] In bacteria the inserted DNA is called a prophage. Example: bacteriophage lambda in E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.3] A trigger, such as stress like UV light, can cut the viral DNA out of the chromosome and start the lytic cycle.
[bio22-s4.3.4] Latency in human viruses: some viruses hide in cells in an inactive state for years and reactivate later.
[bio22-s4.3.5] Herpes simplex virus stays in nerve cells after the first infection and reactivates to cause repeated cold sores.
[bio22-s4.3.6] Varicella-zoster virus causes chickenpox, stays in nerve cells, and can reactivate decades later as shingles.
[bio22-s4.3.7] A latent virus is hidden from the immune system and from antiviral drugs, because it is not making viral proteins or new viruses that they could target.
[bio22-s4.4.b] Viruses cause disease in several different ways, from simply destroying cells to turning the body's own defences against it. Each mode of action follows from what a virus does to the cells it infects.
[bio22-s4.4.0] Killing cells: lysis destroys infected cells. Influenza destroys cells lining the airways, causing a sore throat and cough, and the cold virus damages cells of the nose and throat.
[bio22-s4.4.1] Disrupting cell function: the virus diverts the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and energy to making viruses, so the cell stops its normal work.
[bio22-s4.4.2] Damaging the immune system: HIV infects and destroys helper T cells, the white blood cells that coordinate the immune response.
[bio22-s4.4.3] As helper T cell numbers fall over years, the immune system can no longer coordinate its responses, and the person develops AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome).
[bio22-s4.4.4] People with AIDS die of opportunistic infections and cancers, which a healthy immune system would control but which take hold when the immune system is weakened.
[bio22-s4.4.5] Causing cancer: some viruses insert genes that interfere with the control of cell division, e.g. human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio22-s4.4.6] Triggering harmful immune responses: much of the fever, aches and tissue damage in viral illness is caused by the body's own immune response (inflammation) to infected cells.
[bio22-s4.5.b] Viruses are grouped by the type of nucleic acid they carry, and this affects where they replicate, which enzymes they need and how fast they change. Comparing the two groups is a likely exam question.
[bio22-s4.5.0] Genetic material: a DNA virus has DNA as its genetic material; an RNA virus has RNA.
[bio22-s4.5.1] DNA viruses include herpes simplex and varicella-zoster (chickenpox) viruses, human papillomavirus (HPV), adenoviruses, the smallpox virus, and bacteriophages T4 and lambda.
[bio22-s4.5.2] RNA viruses include influenza, measles, mumps, polio, rhinoviruses (the common cold), SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Ebola and tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s4.5.3] Where they replicate: most DNA viruses enter the host nucleus and use the host's own enzymes, such as DNA polymerase and RNA polymerase, to copy their DNA and make mRNA.
[bio22-s4.5.4] Poxviruses such as the smallpox virus are an exception: they replicate in the cytoplasm with their own enzymes.
[bio22-s4.5.5] Most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm and must carry or code for their own enzyme, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, to copy RNA from RNA, because host cells have no enzyme that does this.
[bio22-s4.5.6] Mutation rate: the enzyme that copies RNA has no proofreading (no checking for mistakes), so RNA viruses make many more copying errors and mutate much faster than DNA viruses, whose DNA is copied by host polymerases that proofread.
[bio22-s4.5.7] RNA viruses such as influenza and HIV change their surface antigens quickly, so new strains escape existing immunity. This is why flu vaccines are updated every year, and drug resistance appears quickly.
[bio22-s4.6.b] Once inside the cell, an RNA virus's genes still have to be turned into proteins. RNA viruses do this in one of two ways, depending on the virus.
[bio22-s4.6.0] In some RNA viruses, such as poliovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the RNA acts directly as mRNA when it enters the cell and is translated at once by the host's ribosomes.
[bio22-s4.6.1] In others, such as influenza and measles, the RNA must first be copied into a complementary strand that acts as mRNA.
[bio22-s4.6.2] DNA is a more stable molecule than RNA, which fits with DNA viruses mutating more slowly.
[bio22-s4.6.3] Mutation as a factor in disease emergence is covered further in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s4.7.b] Retroviruses break the usual rule that information flows from DNA to RNA. HIV is the key example, and its cycle has a few extra steps compared with the general cycle.
[bio22-s4.7.0] A retrovirus is an RNA virus that makes a DNA copy of its RNA inside the host cell, using its own enzyme, reverse transcriptase, which reverses the usual direction of DNA to RNA. HIV is an example.
[bio22-s4.7.1] HIV replication, step 1: its envelope protein binds the CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
[bio22-s4.7.2] Step 2: the envelope fuses with the cell membrane, and the capsid enters.
[bio22-s4.7.3] Step 3: reverse transcriptase makes double-stranded DNA from the viral RNA.
[bio22-s4.7.4] Step 4: a second viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this DNA into the host's chromosome, where it is called a provirus and can stay hidden for years.
[bio22-s4.7.5] Step 5: when the cell is activated, the provirus is transcribed into viral mRNA and new viral RNA, and viral proteins are made.
[bio22-s4.7.6] Step 6: new viruses assemble and bud from the cell, taking an envelope of host membrane.
[bio22-s4.7.7] Reverse transcriptase makes many errors, so HIV mutates very quickly, which is one reason there is still no effective HIV vaccine.
[bio22-s4.7.8] Diagram: an HIV particle (envelope with surface proteins, capsid, two RNA strands and reverse transcriptase), and a flow of the six steps into and out of a helper T cell. Label receptor, viral RNA, viral DNA, host chromosome (provirus) and budding virus.
[bio22-s4.8.b] Antibiotics don't work on viruses, so viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs that interrupt one stage of the replication cycle. Each drug targets a step that depends on a viral protein.
[bio22-s4.8.0] Antiviral drugs block a step in the cycle that uses a viral protein, so the host cell isn't harmed.
[bio22-s4.8.1] Aciclovir blocks the DNA polymerase of herpes viruses, which treats cold sores and shingles.
[bio22-s4.8.2] Oseltamivir blocks neuraminidase, the influenza enzyme that releases new viruses from the cell surface.
[bio22-s4.8.3] HIV drugs include reverse transcriptase inhibitors, of which zidovudine (AZT), approved in 1987, was the first.
[bio22-s4.8.4] HIV drugs also include protease inhibitors, which stop new viral proteins being cut into their working form.
[bio22-s4.8.5] HIV is treated with a combination of several antiretroviral drugs at once (antiretroviral therapy).
[bio22-s4.8.6] Antiretroviral therapy keeps the virus at very low levels and lets the immune system recover, but it doesn't cure the infection, because the provirus remains in the host DNA.
[bio22-s4.8.7] Using several drugs together makes resistance much less likely, since the virus would need several mutations at once.
[bio22-s4.9.b] The syllabus asks you to model how viruses replicate within cells. A model turns the six stages into something you can draw, handle and use to make predictions, while leaving out some detail.
[bio22-s4.9.0] To model the cycle, first list the stages in order: attachment, entry, uncoating, replication and synthesis, assembly and release. Then draw each as a labelled sketch or box, showing the host-cell part used at that stage (receptor, membrane, ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides).
[bio22-s4.9.1] Join the stages with arrows into a cycle, because the new viruses go on to infect other cells.
[bio22-s4.9.2] Mark how a DNA virus, an RNA virus and a retrovirus differ at stage 4.
[bio22-s4.9.3] Mark where an antiviral drug would act.
[bio22-s4.9.4] A physical model can use card or modelling clay to represent viruses and cells, to show attachment and budding.
[bio22-s4.9.5] The model shows the order of events, the dependence on host machinery, and why one infected cell releases many viruses. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s4.9.6] The model simplifies the timing (a cycle can take minutes to hours), the numbers released, the molecular steps, and the differences between viruses.
[bio22-s4.9.7] Worked prediction: if each infected cell releases 100 viruses and each of those infects a new cell, two cycles from one virus give 100 × 100 = 10,000 viruses. This is an illustrative calculation, not a figure for a real virus.
[bio22-s4.9.8] Diagram: a flow chart of the cycle, with stages as labelled boxes joined by arrows into a loop, and the host-cell part used at each stage marked on it.

CANDIDATES bio22-s5: White blood cells
[bio22-s5.0.b] The immune system is run by several different kinds of white blood cell, and they all start from the same place. Knowing where each type comes from and matures explains how the body learns to attack invaders but not itself.
[bio22-s5.0.0] White blood cells (leucocytes) are the cells of the immune system. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, which holds the genes they need to make the proteins for their defensive jobs.
[bio22-s5.0.1] All white blood cells develop from stem cells (cells that can divide and become different cell types) in the red bone marrow, the soft tissue inside some bones.
[bio22-s5.0.2] They travel in the blood and lymph (the fluid that drains from tissues), and many leave the capillaries to enter the tissues, because that is where pathogens (disease-causing organisms) are found.
[bio22-s5.0.3] There are two broad groups. Phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages) engulf pathogens, taking them into the cell.
[bio22-s5.0.4] The second group is the lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells). These are small cells with a large round nucleus filling most of the cell and little cytoplasm.
[bio22-s5.0.5] B lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow. T lymphocytes leave the marrow and mature in the thymus, a gland in the chest behind the breastbone (T for thymus).
[bio22-s5.0.6] During maturation, lymphocytes that would attack the body's own cells are removed, so mature lymphocytes respond only to non-self antigens (molecules on a pathogen's surface that the immune system recognises as foreign).
[bio22-s5.1.b] Monocytes are the white blood cells that travel in the blood and then settle in tissues as macrophages. Macrophages are the body's general-purpose cleaners and also the link between the fast, general response and the specific one.
[bio22-s5.1.0] Monocytes are the largest white blood cells, with a large kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus, which makes them easy to tell from lymphocytes under the microscope.
[bio22-s5.1.1] They circulate in the blood for a few days, then move into tissues and develop into macrophages, which can live for months.
[bio22-s5.1.2] Macrophages carry out phagocytosis (engulfing and digesting) of pathogens, dead cells and debris. There is more on the stages in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.1.3] Macrophages are antigen-presenting cells: they display fragments of engulfed pathogens on their surface to activate helper T cells, so they link innate immunity (the fast, non-specific response) and adaptive immunity (the slower, specific one).
[bio22-s5.1.4] Macrophages release chemicals that cause inflammation and fever and attract other white blood cells to the site of infection.
[bio22-s5.1.5] Diagram: a monocyte (large cell, kidney-shaped nucleus) beside a lymphocyte (small cell, large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm). Label nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane.
[bio22-s5.2.b] Natural killer (NK) cells are a fast first line of attack against body cells that have gone wrong, either because a virus is hiding inside them or because they have become cancerous. They check cells for a missing marker rather than for a particular pathogen.
[bio22-s5.2.0] Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes of the innate system. They are not specific to one antigen and need no earlier exposure, so they act within hours of an infection, before B and T cells have responded.
[bio22-s5.2.1] Healthy body cells display normal self-marker (MHC) proteins on their surface, which tell the immune system the cell belongs to the body.
[bio22-s5.2.2] Many virus-infected cells and cancer cells display fewer of these markers, or display stress molecules instead. NK cells kill cells showing this missing self pattern.
[bio22-s5.2.3] NK cells also kill cells coated with antibodies, the proteins that bind to antigens.
[bio22-s5.2.4] NK cells release perforin, a protein that makes pores in the target cell's membrane, and enzymes called granzymes that enter through the pores and trigger the cell to self-destruct (apoptosis, programmed cell death).
[bio22-s5.2.5] The dying cell breaks into fragments that macrophages engulf, so the virus inside is destroyed without spreading.
[bio22-s5.2.6] NK cells also release chemicals that activate macrophages, which helps clear the fragments.
[bio22-s5.3.b] B lymphocytes are the cells behind antibodies. Each B cell is committed to one antibody shape, so the body needs a huge range of B cells to cover many different antigens.
[bio22-s5.3.0] B lymphocytes carry antibody molecules in their membrane as receptors, which let them bind a matching antigen. Each B cell and its descendants make only one antibody shape.
[bio22-s5.3.1] B lymphocytes are responsible for antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity, meaning protection by antibodies carried in body fluids. Clonal selection, plasma cells and memory B cells are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.3.2] Plasma cells, the antibody-secreting form of B cells, are larger than resting B cells, with extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum (a network of membranes studded with ribosomes) to make and secrete large amounts of antibody protein.
[bio22-s5.3.3] Antibodies act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, in blood, tissue fluid and mucus, not on pathogens hidden inside cells, because antibodies cannot get into body cells.
[bio22-s5.4.b] T lymphocytes are the cells that control and carry out cell-mediated immunity, the response that deals with infected body cells. Helper T cells are the coordinators that most of the adaptive response depends on.
[bio22-s5.4.0] All T cells have T-cell receptors on their surface that recognise one antigen, but only when it is displayed on a cell's surface (presented), never as a free molecule.
[bio22-s5.4.1] T cells don't make antibodies. That is the job of plasma cells, which come from B cells.
[bio22-s5.4.2] Helper T cells carry the surface marker CD4.
[bio22-s5.4.3] Helper T cells are the coordinators of the adaptive response, signalling with cytokines (chemical messengers between immune cells). Which cells they activate, and in what order, is taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.4.4] Almost every adaptive response depends on helper T cells, so HIV, which infects and destroys CD4 helper T cells, leaves the body unable to fight many infections.
[bio22-s5.5.b] Killer T cells deal with threats that antibodies cannot reach, because the pathogen is hidden inside a body cell. They destroy the whole infected cell.
[bio22-s5.5.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) carry the surface marker CD8.
[bio22-s5.5.1] Killer T cells bind to body cells displaying their matching antigen, such as virus-infected cells, cancer cells and cells of transplanted tissue.
[bio22-s5.5.2] They kill these cells by releasing perforin and granzymes, which triggers apoptosis (the cell's programmed self-destruction).
[bio22-s5.5.3] Killer T cells use the same killing method as NK cells, but only against cells showing their one specific antigen, whereas NK cells look for missing self-markers.
[bio22-s5.5.4] Each killer T cell can kill many target cells in turn.
[bio22-s5.6.b] An immune response needs brakes, and it needs a record of past infections. Suppressor T cells provide the brakes, and memory T cells provide the record that makes a second infection easier to fight.
[bio22-s5.6.0] Suppressor T cells, now usually called regulatory T cells, are the brakes of the immune system.
[bio22-s5.6.1] Suppressor T cells end a response once an infection is cleared, so the body is not damaged by an immune response that carries on. The details are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.6.2] They also suppress lymphocytes that react to the body's own antigens, helping to prevent autoimmune disease (the body attacking its own tissues) and allergy.
[bio22-s5.6.3] Memory T cells form from the clone of activated T cells (the identical cells that divide from one activated T cell) and remain in the blood and lymph tissue for years or decades.
[bio22-s5.6.4] On re-exposure to the same antigen, memory T cells quickly produce new helper and killer T cells, giving a faster, stronger cell-mediated response. Memory B cells do the same for antibodies.
[bio22-s5.6.5] Memory cells are why vaccination works: the vaccine exposes you to the antigen safely, so memory cells are ready if the real pathogen arrives.
[bio22-s5.7.b] The outcome asks you to compare the roles of different white blood cells. The first comparison is between the fast, general innate cells and the slower, specific adaptive cells, and then between the ways each one destroys pathogens.
[bio22-s5.7.0] Innate versus adaptive: monocytes, macrophages and NK cells are part of innate immunity, which is non-specific, acts quickly and has no memory.
[bio22-s5.7.1] B and T lymphocytes are adaptive: they are specific to one antigen, slower on first exposure, and they form memory cells.
[bio22-s5.7.2] Macrophages engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis. NK and killer T cells kill infected body cells by perforin and apoptosis.
[bio22-s5.7.3] B cells, as plasma cells, secrete antibodies. Helper T cells kill nothing themselves but signal to other cells.
[bio22-s5.7.4] Suppressor T cells regulate the response. Memory cells kill nothing but enable a rapid future response.
[bio22-s5.8.b] Cells also differ in what they attack and in how they recognise it. This explains why different cells are needed to cover both free pathogens and pathogens hidden inside body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.0] Where the target is: antibodies from B cells act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, because antibodies cannot enter body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.1] Killer T and NK cells act on pathogens inside body cells by killing the cell. Macrophages engulf free pathogens.
[bio22-s5.8.2] Recognition: B cells recognise free antigens with membrane antibodies, while T cells recognise only antigens presented on cell surfaces.
[bio22-s5.8.3] NK cells recognise missing or abnormal self-markers, not a specific antigen.
[bio22-s5.8.4] Macrophages recognise general features of pathogens and antibody-coated particles, so they need no specific receptor for each pathogen.
[bio22-s5.9.b] This list puts each cell side by side: whether it is innate or adaptive, whether it is specific, its main role, and where it matures. Use it to compare cells quickly.
[bio22-s5.9.0] Monocyte or macrophage: innate; non-specific; main role is phagocytosis and antigen presentation; matures in the bone marrow, then in tissues.
[bio22-s5.9.1] NK cell: innate; non-specific; kills infected and cancer cells; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.2] B lymphocyte: adaptive; specific; main role is antibodies (via plasma cells) and memory; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.3] Helper T cell: adaptive; specific; activates B cells, killer T cells and macrophages (CD4); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.4] Killer T cell: adaptive; specific; kills infected, cancer and transplant cells (CD8); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.5] Suppressor T cell: adaptive; ends responses and prevents attack on self; matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.6] Memory B and T cells: adaptive; specific; long-lived, giving a fast secondary response.
[bio22-s5.10.b] In a real infection the cells act as a team in sequence. Influenza, a virus that infects the cells lining the airways, shows how the innate and adaptive cells combine.
[bio22-s5.10.0] First, infected cells release interferons (chemical signals that warn neighbouring cells of a virus) and NK cells kill some of them within hours. Macrophages engulf viruses and present antigens, and helper T cells are activated.
[bio22-s5.10.1] Next, killer T cells destroy infected cells while plasma cells release antibodies that neutralise free viruses, so both hidden and free viruses are dealt with.
[bio22-s5.10.2] Finally, suppressor T cells end the response and memory cells remain. Because flu antigens change, memory cells may not recognise next year's strain.

CANDIDATES bio22-s6: Emergence and spread of infectious disease
[bio22-s6.0.b] Disease specialists use precise words for how common a disease is and how fast it is changing. You need these terms to describe any outbreak, and they appear throughout the rest of this subsection.
[bio22-s6.0.0] An emerging infectious disease is one that has newly appeared in a population, or one that was known but is rapidly increasing in incidence (the number of new cases) or in geographic range.
[bio22-s6.0.1] A re-emerging disease is one that had declined and is increasing again, e.g. measles where vaccination rates fall, or TB with drug-resistant strains.
[bio22-s6.0.2] Endemic means always present at a fairly steady level in a region, so the number of cases is predictable from year to year.
[bio22-s6.0.3] An epidemic, or outbreak, is a sudden increase in cases above the expected level in a region.
[bio22-s6.0.4] A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across several continents or worldwide, e.g. COVID-19, which the World Health Organization declared a pandemic on 11 March 2020.
[bio22-s6.1.b] Some terms describe where a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) lives between outbreaks and who can pass it on. These explain why some diseases are so hard to get rid of.
[bio22-s6.1.0] A zoonosis is an infectious disease that passes from animals to humans, e.g. avian influenza or rabies.
[bio22-s6.1.1] Most emerging human diseases are zoonoses, often from wild animals, because people have little immunity to pathogens they have never met. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.1.2] A reservoir is a host population in which a pathogen persists and from which it infects others, e.g. badgers for bovine TB in Ireland, or wild birds for avian influenza.
[bio22-s6.1.3] A carrier is an infected individual who shows no symptoms but can pass on the pathogen, so nobody knows to avoid them.
[bio22-s6.2.b] A pathogen can only cause an outbreak if it can get from one host to the next. There are several routes, and each has typical diseases that use it, in both animals and plants.
[bio22-s6.2.0] Airborne droplets and aerosols (tiny particles hanging in the air) from coughs, sneezes and breath spread influenza, TB, measles and COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.2.1] Wind-borne spores spread ash dieback, cereal rusts and potato blight in plants, because spores are light enough to be carried between fields.
[bio22-s6.2.2] Direct contact, touch and skin to skin, spreads ringworm and athlete's foot.
[bio22-s6.2.3] Indirect contact via contaminated objects and surfaces (fomites), hands, tools and clothing spreads disease. In plants, contact with hands and tools spreads tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s6.2.4] Contaminated food and water, the faecal–oral route (pathogens in faeces reach the mouth), spreads cholera, Salmonella food poisoning and amoebic dysentery.
[bio22-s6.2.5] Body fluids spread HIV and hepatitis B through sexual contact, blood (shared needles and unscreened transfusions) and mother to baby.
[bio22-s6.2.6] Vectors are animals that carry pathogens between hosts: mosquitoes (malaria), ticks (Lyme disease), tsetse flies (sleeping sickness), elm bark beetles (Dutch elm disease), aphids (many plant viruses, e.g. barley yellow dwarf virus) and biting midges (bluetongue virus of sheep and cattle).
[bio22-s6.2.7] In plants, rain splash, soil, infected seed, tubers and cuttings also spread disease. Vegetative propagation (growing new plants from parts of old ones) passes viruses and blight to the next crop, and grafting does too.
[bio22-s6.3.b] Diseases spread when infected organisms, or the things they touch, move. Over short distances this happens through everyday contact, and over a whole country it follows the trade and travel routes of animals and people.
[bio22-s6.3.0] Local spread occurs within a household, school, hospital or farm, between neighbouring fields by wind and rain splash, and between neighbouring farms by animals straying, shared equipment, vehicles and people's boots.
[bio22-s6.3.1] Regional spread occurs when farm animals move through marts (livestock markets) and between farms, when plants and animals are traded within a country or the EU, when wild animals move seasonally, and when people commute and holiday.
[bio22-s6.3.2] In the 2001 foot-and-mouth disease epidemic in Britain, infected sheep were moved through markets across the country before the disease was detected, spreading it to many farms.
[bio22-s6.3.3] Control of that epidemic needed a ban on animal movement and the culling of millions of animals, which shows how costly it is once movement has carried a disease widely.
[bio22-s6.4.b] Modern travel and trade mean a pathogen no longer stays where it started. A disease can reach another continent before anyone knows it is there.
[bio22-s6.4.0] Air travel can carry an infected person to any continent within a day, faster than many diseases take to show symptoms, so travellers can carry infection without knowing it.
[bio22-s6.4.1] COVID-19 reached every continent within months of its first identification in Wuhan, China, in December 2019.
[bio22-s6.4.2] International trade moves pathogens and vectors in plants, wood, soil, live animals and goods. Ash dieback spread through Europe partly in imported young ash plants and was found in Ireland in 2012.
[bio22-s6.4.3] The second, more aggressive epidemic of Dutch elm disease reached Britain from the late 1960s in imported elm logs.
[bio22-s6.4.4] The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus), a vector of several viruses, has spread to many countries in used tyres and plants.
[bio22-s6.4.5] Migrating wild birds carry avian influenza between continents, because infected birds fly long distances without any border controls.
[bio22-s6.5.b] Trade in live animals can move a disease into places where the local species have never met it. Amphibians show how serious this can be.
[bio22-s6.5.0] The pet and wildlife trade spread chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) around the world in the trade in amphibians.
[bio22-s6.5.1] Chytrid is linked to the decline of hundreds of amphibian species and the extinction of some.
[bio22-s6.6.b] Emergence and spread depend on several interrelated factors, and the first is persistence. The longer a host carries a pathogen in a way that can pass on, the more chances it has to infect others.
[bio22-s6.6.0] The longer a host is infectious, the more others it can infect.
[bio22-s6.6.1] Persistence includes long infectious periods, carriers without symptoms, latent infections (the pathogen is present but inactive) that reactivate, and reservoir hosts.
[bio22-s6.6.2] HIV may cause no symptoms for years while the person can still pass it on.
[bio22-s6.6.3] Herpes viruses stay latent in nerve cells for life, so the person is never rid of the virus. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.6.4] TB bacteria can stay dormant (inactive) in the lungs for years and become active later.
[bio22-s6.7.b] Persistence shows up in each kind of host, from a single healthy-looking person to wildlife and crops.
[bio22-s6.7.0] Mary Mallon, known as Typhoid Mary, was born in Co. Tyrone in 1869 and worked as a cook in New York. She carried the typhoid bacterium, Salmonella Typhi, without symptoms and infected many of the families she cooked for.
[bio22-s6.7.1] Health authorities isolated her for most of the rest of her life, because as a carrier she could not be identified by illness.
[bio22-s6.7.2] Bovine TB persists in Irish cattle partly because badgers act as a wildlife reservoir that reinfects herds, so clearing the disease from cattle alone is not enough.
[bio22-s6.7.3] Plant viruses persist in perennial plants (which live for years), weeds and tubers, so infected seed potatoes carry viruses into the next year's crop.
[bio22-s6.7.4] Phytophthora infestans, the cause of potato blight, survives the winter in infected tubers left in the ground or in dumps.
[bio22-s6.8.b] A mutation is a random change in a pathogen's genetic material. Pathogens that reproduce quickly produce huge numbers of changed copies, so some will spread better than the original.
[bio22-s6.8.0] Pathogens with short generation times and large populations produce many mutants, because each new copy is another chance for a change.
[bio22-s6.8.1] RNA viruses mutate fastest because their copying enzyme doesn't proofread (check for copying errors). There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.8.2] Antigenic drift: small mutations gradually change a virus's surface antigens (the molecules the immune system recognises), so memory cells and antibodies from earlier infections or vaccines no longer fit well.
[bio22-s6.8.3] Antigenic drift is why influenza returns every winter and why flu vaccines are updated each year.
[bio22-s6.8.4] Mutations likewise produced new SARS-CoV-2 variants, such as Delta and Omicron, that spread faster or partly escaped immunity, causing new waves of COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.9.b] Mutations can also change what a pathogen is able to do, giving it a new host or defeating a crop's defences.
[bio22-s6.9.0] Antigenic shift (influenza): if two different flu viruses infect the same cell, e.g. in a pig, their RNA segments can mix to form a new strain with surface antigens that no human population has met.
[bio22-s6.9.1] Antigenic shift has caused flu pandemics, e.g. the 2009 H1N1 (swine flu) pandemic, because with no existing immunity the new strain spreads easily.
[bio22-s6.9.2] Mutations can let a pathogen infect a new host species (spillover, e.g. a bird virus gaining the ability to infect mammals), become more transmissible, or overcome resistance bred into crops.
[bio22-s6.9.3] The Ug99 strain of wheat stem rust, identified in Uganda in 1999, overcame resistance genes used in most of the world's wheat varieties.
[bio22-s6.10.b] Resistance means a pathogen or pest survives a drug or chemical that used to kill it. It arises by natural selection (survival of the individuals best suited to their conditions), which happens in four steps. There is more on this in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
[bio22-s6.10.0] 1. Random mutations, or genes passed between bacteria on plasmids (small rings of DNA), make a few individuals resistant.
[bio22-s6.10.1] 2. The antibiotic or chemical kills the susceptible individuals.
[bio22-s6.10.2] 3. The resistant survivors reproduce with less competition.
[bio22-s6.10.3] 4. The next generation is mostly resistant. The chemical doesn't cause the mutation; it selects it.
[bio22-s6.11.b] Resistance arises by natural selection when a drug or chemical kills susceptible individuals, and some practices make it spread faster. The examples cover people, crops and livestock.
[bio22-s6.11.0] Overuse and misuse of antibiotics speed up resistance: use for viral infections (which antibiotics cannot treat), not finishing courses, and use in farm animals.
[bio22-s6.11.1] Poor infection control in hospitals and heavy repeated use of one pesticide also speed up resistance.
[bio22-s6.11.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many antibiotics and spreads in hospitals.
[bio22-s6.11.3] Multidrug-resistant TB, malaria parasites resistant to some antimalarial drugs, and mosquitoes resistant to insecticides, which weakens malaria control.
[bio22-s6.11.4] Fungi that cause crop diseases are resistant to some fungicides, and parasitic worms of sheep and cattle are resistant to some anthelmintics (drugs that kill worms).
[bio22-s6.11.5] Resistant pathogens persist longer in hosts and spread further, linking factors 1 and 3.
[bio22-s6.12.b] A pathogen needs susceptible hosts, meaning individuals with no immunity to it. How many of these there are in a population strongly affects how far and how fast an outbreak goes.
[bio22-s6.12.0] A pathogen spreads only if it can reach susceptible (non-immune) hosts. The higher the proportion of immune individuals, the fewer susceptible hosts there are and the slower the spread. This is herd immunity. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s6.12.1] A pathogen new to a population meets no immunity at all, so it spreads fast and can be very severe, e.g. COVID-19 in 2020, before vaccines.
[bio22-s6.12.2] Smallpox and measles, brought by Europeans to the Americas from the late 1400s, killed very large numbers of Indigenous people, who had never been exposed to them.
[bio22-s6.12.3] Immunity falls when vaccination rates drop, as when measles outbreaks return.
[bio22-s6.12.4] Immunity also falls when protection from vaccination or infection wanes over time, and where many people have weakened immune systems, e.g. from untreated HIV or malnutrition, or the very young and very old.
[bio22-s6.13.b] Plants have no antibody-based immunity like animals, but the same principle applies. What matters is whether any individuals in the crop can resist the pathogen.
[bio22-s6.13.0] In plants, the equivalent is genetic uniformity. A monoculture (a crop of one genetically identical variety) has no resistant individuals, so a pathogen that infects one plant can infect them all.
[bio22-s6.13.1] In Ireland in the 1840s, most of the poor depended on one potato variety, the Lumper, grown from tubers (genetically identical clones) and susceptible to blight.
[bio22-s6.13.2] When Phytophthora infestans arrived in 1845, crops failed across the country in successive years. About one million people died and over a million emigrated in the Great Famine.
[bio22-s6.14.b] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more other hosts they meet, so the further a pathogen travels. Mobility applies to people, livestock, wildlife and vectors.
[bio22-s6.14.0] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more contacts each has and the further the pathogen spreads. The routes are those in local, regional and global spread above.
[bio22-s6.14.1] Crowding in cities, refugee camps, festivals and mass gatherings, travel and migration, and the movement of livestock all increase spread.
[bio22-s6.14.2] Migration of wild animals, such as birds, also moves pathogens into new areas.
[bio22-s6.14.3] Vector mobility: climate warming lets vectors survive further north and for longer each year, carrying disease into new regions.
[bio22-s6.14.4] Bluetongue, a midge-borne virus of sheep and cattle, spread into northern Europe from 2006.
[bio22-s6.14.5] Reducing mobility reduces spread: animal movement bans in foot-and-mouth outbreaks, travel restrictions and quarantine in human epidemics, and import controls on plants. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.15.b] To compare outbreaks and predict whether one will grow, scientists use a single number, R₀. It measures how contagious a pathogen is when nobody has immunity.
[bio22-s6.15.0] The basic reproduction number, R₀ ('R nought'), is the average number of new (secondary) infections caused by one infected individual in a population where everyone is susceptible, with no immunity and no control measures.
[bio22-s6.15.1] R₀ greater than 1: each case causes more than one new case on average, so numbers grow and an epidemic can occur.
[bio22-s6.15.2] R₀ equal to 1: numbers stay steady, which is endemic.
[bio22-s6.15.3] R₀ less than 1: each case causes fewer than one new case, so the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.16.b] Each infected person passes the pathogen to R₀ others, so cases multiply by R₀ every generation of infection (one round of people infecting the next). Working through numbers shows why R₀ above or below 1 matters.
[bio22-s6.16.0] Growth example: with R₀ = 2, one case leads to 2, then 4, then 8, then 16 new cases per generation of infection. After 10 generations, one case has led to 2¹⁰ = 1,024 new cases in that generation.
[bio22-s6.16.1] Decline example: with R₀ = 0.5, 100 cases give 50, then 25, then about 12, and the outbreak fades.
[bio22-s6.16.2] Diagram: a branching tree from one case, giving 2, then 4, then 8 cases (R₀ = 2), beside a single chain with one case per generation (R₀ = 1). Label each generation.
[bio22-s6.17.b] R₀ is not a fixed property of a pathogen. It combines features of the pathogen, the host and the environment, which are the factors already covered.
[bio22-s6.17.0] R₀ depends on how long a host is infectious (persistence), so a longer infectious period raises R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.1] R₀ depends on how many contacts an infected host has per day (mobility and crowding), so more contacts raise R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.2] R₀ depends on the chance that a contact passes on the infection (the transmission route, and mutations that make a pathogen more transmissible).
[bio22-s6.17.3] So R₀ is not fixed for a disease: it differs between populations and settings.
[bio22-s6.18.b] In real outbreaks some people are already immune or control measures are in place. The effective reproduction number measures spread under those real conditions.
[bio22-s6.18.0] The effective reproduction number, R (sometimes written Rt), is the average number of new infections per case at a given time, in a population where some people are immune or control measures are in place.
[bio22-s6.18.1] Approximately, R = R₀ × the proportion still susceptible. For example, R₀ = 3 with half the population immune gives R = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5.
[bio22-s6.18.2] Control aims to bring R below 1, so that each case infects fewer than one other person and the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.19.b] If R₀ tells us how contagious a disease is, we can work out how many people must be immune to stop it spreading. The more contagious the disease, the higher that proportion.
[bio22-s6.19.0] Herd-immunity threshold: to stop sustained spread, the proportion immune must be at least 1 − 1/R₀.
[bio22-s6.19.1] Examples: R₀ = 2 needs 1 − ½ = 50% immune; R₀ = 4 needs 1 − ¼ = 75% immune; R₀ = 10 needs 90% immune.
[bio22-s6.19.2] Measles is one of the most contagious diseases, with R₀ usually quoted as 12–18, so about 95% of people must be immune.
[bio22-s6.19.3] The World Health Organization therefore recommends at least 95% coverage with two doses of measles vaccine.
[bio22-s6.20.b] R₀ comes from a simple model, and a model is only as good as its assumptions. Real outbreaks differ in important ways.
[bio22-s6.20.0] The model's assumptions are that everyone is equally likely to meet everyone else and that R₀ stays constant. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s6.20.1] Real populations are clustered, behaviour changes during outbreaks and new variants change R₀, so estimates are updated as data come in.
[bio22-s6.21.b] The six factors do not work one at a time. Real outbreaks happen when several combine, and other changes in how we live bring pathogens and new hosts into contact.
[bio22-s6.21.0] The factors act together, not one at a time. A mutation that makes a virus more transmissible raises R₀, and a population with low immunity and high mobility then lets it spread widely.
[bio22-s6.21.1] Long persistence in carriers lets a pathogen reach new areas undetected, and resistance to drugs lets infections last longer, raising R₀ further.
[bio22-s6.21.2] Plant example: a genetically uniform crop (low immunity), a pathogen spread by wind and trade (mobility) and survival in tubers (persistence) together explain the speed of the potato famine.
[bio22-s6.21.3] Other drivers increase contact between pathogens and new hosts: clearing forests and expanding farms into wild areas (more contact with wild animals), intensive farming of large numbers of genetically similar animals kept close together, climate change (vectors' ranges), poverty, poor sanitation, and conflict, which weakens health systems.

CANDIDATES bio22-s7: Emerging diseases and society
[bio22-s7.0.b] A virus can cause a new disease without being new to the world: it only has to be new to the species it infects. Most of the new viruses that infect people come from other animals.
[bio22-s7.0.0] A novel virus is one not previously known to infect a species, usually because it has jumped from another host (a jump called spillover) or has changed by mutation or by mixing of genes.
[bio22-s7.0.1] Most new human viruses come from animals. A disease passed from animals to people is called a zoonosis, and the animals involved are especially bats, birds and other mammals.
[bio22-s7.0.2] Contact between wildlife and people increases when people clear forests, hunt or trade wild animals, or farm animals intensively, because each of these creates new opportunities for a virus to cross from one species to another.
[bio22-s7.1.b] Two viruses that reached people from animals changed the world in very different ways: one spread silently over decades, the other around the globe within months.
[bio22-s7.1.0] HIV crossed into humans from a related virus, SIV (simian immunodeficiency virus), in chimpanzees in central Africa in the early twentieth century, probably through hunting and butchering them for meat.
[bio22-s7.1.1] HIV was identified in 1983 by Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Luc Montagnier at the Institut Pasteur, Paris (Nobel Prize 2008).
[bio22-s7.1.2] HIV's long symptom-free period let it spread worldwide unnoticed for decades, because infected people had no reason to suspect they carried it.
[bio22-s7.1.3] There is still no HIV vaccine, but antiretroviral therapy (drugs that stop the virus replicating) has turned it from a fatal disease into a long-term condition. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s7.1.4] SARS-CoV-2, the coronavirus that causes COVID-19, was first identified in Wuhan, China, in December 2019. It spreads by droplets and aerosols (tiny airborne particles), including from people without symptoms, and air travel carried it worldwide.
[bio22-s7.1.5] Its genome sequence was shared online in January 2020, and vaccines were developed and approved within a year.
[bio22-s7.1.6] Mutations produced more transmissible variants (forms that spread more easily), so vaccines were updated to match them.
[bio22-s7.2.b] Animal viruses show how a virus moves between wildlife, farm animals and people, and what controlled each outbreak. For each one, know what it is, where it came from, how it spread and how it was stopped.
[bio22-s7.2.0] Avian influenza H5N1 is a highly pathogenic (very disease-causing) bird flu virus that kills poultry and wild birds. A strain that spread worldwide from about 2020, carried by migrating wild birds, has infected many mammals, and in 2024 it was found in dairy cattle in the USA.
[bio22-s7.2.1] Human infections with H5N1 are rare and mostly occur in people in close contact with infected birds or animals. The concern is that mutation, or mixing with a human flu virus, could produce a strain that spreads between people.
[bio22-s7.2.2] Avian influenza is controlled by culling infected flocks, keeping poultry housed when the risk is high (which keeps them away from wild birds), and surveillance.
[bio22-s7.2.3] Ebola virus causes severe haemorrhagic fever (fever with bleeding). Fruit bats are thought to be its natural reservoir, the animal host in which the virus normally lives. The 2014–2016 epidemic in West Africa killed more than 11,000 people.
[bio22-s7.2.4] Ebola spreads by contact with the body fluids of sick or dead people, so caring for the sick and traditional burial practices spread it. It was controlled by isolation, contact tracing, safe burials and, later, a vaccine.
[bio22-s7.2.5] Nipah virus emerged in Malaysia in 1998–1999, passing from fruit bats to pigs on farms near fruit trees, and from pigs to pig farmers, causing brain inflammation (encephalitis).
[bio22-s7.2.6] Nipah was controlled by culling over a million pigs. It shows how farming changes create new routes from wildlife to people.
[bio22-s7.2.7] Schmallenberg virus, an animal virus, was identified in 2011 in Germany and is named after the town where it was found. Spread by biting midges, it causes birth defects in lambs and calves when the mother is infected in early pregnancy.
[bio22-s7.2.8] It spread across northern Europe, including Ireland, within about a year.
[bio22-s7.3.b] Plants get emerging virus diseases too. Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV) is a recent example that threatens a major glasshouse crop.
[bio22-s7.3.0] ToBRFV is a tobamovirus, related to tobacco mosaic virus, first identified in the Middle East in the mid-2010s, and it then spread to many countries.
[bio22-s7.3.1] It infects tomatoes and peppers, causing wrinkled, discoloured fruit that can't be sold, so growers lose income.
[bio22-s7.3.2] It spreads by contact (hands, tools and clothing), in infected seed and in the trade in plants and fruit, and it stays infectious on surfaces for long periods.
[bio22-s7.3.3] It is serious because it overcomes the resistance genes bred into tomato varieties to protect them against related viruses, so the older protection no longer works.
[bio22-s7.3.4] Control: EU plant-health rules on imported seed and plants, testing of seed, hygiene and disinfection in glasshouses, destroying infected crops, and breeding new resistant varieties.
[bio22-s7.4.b] Several human activities and natural changes help plant viruses reach new places and crops. Because plants cannot be treated once infected, prevention is the only real defence.
[bio22-s7.4.0] Why plant viruses emerge: global trade in seeds and plants; monocultures of genetically uniform crops (one variety grown over huge areas, so one virus can overcome them all); insect vectors (carriers) spreading into new areas as the climate warms; and mutation overcoming plants' resistance genes.
[bio22-s7.4.1] Plants have no adaptive immune system (the antibody- and T-cell-based defence animals use) and can't be vaccinated or cured of viral infection, so control depends on prevention: clean seed and stock, controlling vectors, removing infected plants, quarantine and resistant varieties.
[bio22-s7.4.2] Economic and food-security importance: crop losses raise food prices and threaten farmers' incomes.
[bio22-s7.4.3] Diseases of staple crops (the main food crops) are most serious for poor countries that depend on one crop, because there is no alternative food if it fails.
[bio22-s7.5.b] The immune system is meant to attack invaders and leave the body alone. In an autoimmune disease it gets this wrong and attacks the body's own tissues.
[bio22-s7.5.0] An autoimmune disease is one in which the immune system attacks the body's own cells or tissues as if they were foreign, because it fails to tell self from non-self.
[bio22-s7.5.1] Normally, lymphocytes (white blood cells that recognise antigens) that react to self are removed during maturation, and the rest are held in check by suppressor (regulatory) T cells.
[bio22-s7.5.2] In autoimmune disease this tolerance (not attacking self) breaks down, and antibodies or killer T cells attack a self antigen, an antigen that is part of the body's own cells.
[bio22-s7.6.b] Each autoimmune disease is defined by which tissue the immune system attacks, and the symptoms follow from the job that tissue does.
[bio22-s7.6.0] Type 1 diabetes: T cells destroy the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas, causing high blood glucose. It is treated with insulin injections for life. There is more on this in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio22-s7.6.1] Multiple sclerosis: the myelin sheath (the insulating layer) around nerve fibres in the brain and spinal cord is damaged, slowing or blocking impulses.
[bio22-s7.6.2] Rheumatoid arthritis: the lining of the joints becomes inflamed, causing painful, swollen and damaged joints.
[bio22-s7.6.3] Coeliac disease: eating gluten, a protein in wheat, barley and rye, triggers an immune attack that damages the villi of the small intestine (the finger-like folds that absorb food), causing poor absorption. Treatment is a lifelong gluten-free diet.
[bio22-s7.6.4] Graves' disease: antibodies stimulate the thyroid gland, producing too much thyroxine.
[bio22-s7.7.b] Autoimmune diseases have no single cause. Inherited genes make some people more susceptible, and something in the environment then sets the disease off.
[bio22-s7.7.0] Causes: a combination of inherited susceptibility, since some alleles make these diseases more likely and they run in families, and environmental triggers.
[bio22-s7.7.1] Infections can trigger autoimmunity when a pathogen's antigen resembles a self antigen, so antibodies made against the pathogen also attack the body.
[bio22-s7.7.2] Rheumatic fever: antibodies against Streptococcus bacteria, from a throat infection, attack the heart valves.
[bio22-s7.7.3] Guillain–Barré syndrome is a paralysis that can follow some infections, including Campylobacter food poisoning and Zika virus.
[bio22-s7.8.b] Autoimmune diseases are not infectious, yet they are grouped with emerging diseases because more people have several of them than before. Treatment can manage them but rarely cures them.
[bio22-s7.8.0] Autoimmune diseases are grouped with emerging diseases because the number of people with several autoimmune diseases has been rising in many countries.
[bio22-s7.8.1] The hygiene hypothesis proposes that less early-life exposure to microorganisms leaves the immune system poorly regulated. It is supported by some evidence but not proven, so it remains a hypothesis. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s7.8.2] An autoimmune disease is not caught from another person. AIDS is an immune deficiency caused by a virus, not an autoimmune disease.
[bio22-s7.8.3] There is usually no cure. Treatments replace what is lost (insulin, thyroid hormone), avoid the trigger (a gluten-free diet), or suppress the immune system with drugs such as corticosteroids, which relieves symptoms but makes infections more likely.
[bio22-s7.9.b] Knowing which factors make a disease spread lets us target each one. Each strategy below is linked to the factor it acts on, which is how exams expect you to present it. There is more on the factors in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s7.9.0] Surveillance and early detection: doctors and vets must report notifiable diseases (ones the law requires them to report), and laboratories sequence pathogens' genomes to detect new variants and resistance.
[bio22-s7.9.1] Agencies such as the World Health Organization and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control share data. Early detection lets control start while case numbers are low.
[bio22-s7.9.2] Reducing R (targets R₀, contact and transmission): isolating cases, contact tracing and quarantine reduce the infectious period in the community, because infected people have fewer days in which to pass the disease on.
[bio22-s7.9.3] Masks, ventilation, hand hygiene and physical distancing reduce the chance of transmission per contact, and limiting mass gatherings reduces the number of contacts. The aim is to bring R (the average number of people each case infects) below 1, so the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s7.9.4] Raising immunity (targets the immunity level): vaccination campaigns aimed at the herd-immunity threshold (the proportion immune that stops an outbreak spreading), with priority for the most vulnerable.
[bio22-s7.9.5] Updating vaccines to match new variants targets mutation.
[bio22-s7.10.b] Other strategies target how pathogens travel, how crops are protected, how resistance develops and how animal viruses reach people in the first place.
[bio22-s7.10.0] Limiting mobility (targets movement): travel restrictions and screening; animal movement bans and culling during outbreaks of foot-and-mouth disease or avian influenza; and disinfecting vehicles and footwear at farms.
[bio22-s7.10.1] Plant biosecurity: plant passports and import inspections, quarantine of imported plants, and bans on importing high-risk plants (e.g. ash from infected areas).
[bio22-s7.10.2] Plant biosecurity also uses certified disease-free seed and planting material, removing and destroying infected plants, and breeding resistant varieties, which counters genetic uniformity.
[bio22-s7.10.3] Fighting resistance (targets resistance): antimicrobial stewardship, meaning prescribing antibiotics only when needed; infection control in hospitals; restricting antibiotic use in farm animals; rotating pesticides and fungicides; and developing new drugs.
[bio22-s7.10.4] Reducing spillover: controlling vectors, regulating wildlife trade and markets, protecting habitats, and biosecurity on farms.
[bio22-s7.10.5] The One Health approach treats human, animal and environmental health as linked, and coordinates doctors, vets and ecologists.
[bio22-s7.11.b] Control measures work best when they are chosen using evidence and when the public is willing to follow them.
[bio22-s7.11.0] Research and public information: understanding a new pathogen's transmission and R₀ tells authorities which measures will work, so effort is not wasted on measures that won't help.
[bio22-s7.11.1] Clear public information increases vaccine uptake and cooperation and counters misinformation (false or misleading claims). There is more on this in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s7.12.b] Understanding how new diseases arise and spread benefits health, the economy, food supplies, the environment and ordinary citizens.
[bio22-s7.12.0] Health: early recognition and control save lives and prevent disability.
[bio22-s7.12.1] Knowing the factors lets health systems prepare, with stockpiles, hospital capacity and vaccine platforms, before the next outbreak.
[bio22-s7.12.2] Protecting vulnerable groups depends on high immunity in everyone else, which means those who can't be vaccinated are shielded by those who can.
[bio22-s7.12.3] Economic: epidemics close businesses, schools and travel, and cost health systems heavily, as COVID-19 did from 2020.
[bio22-s7.12.4] Animal and plant diseases cause culling, lost exports and crop losses, e.g. the 2001 foot-and-mouth epidemic in Britain. Prevention and early control usually cost far less than a large outbreak.
[bio22-s7.12.5] Food security and the environment: plant and animal diseases threaten the food supply, especially where people depend on one crop. The Irish Great Famine shows the human cost, and there is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s7.12.6] Diseases of wild species threaten biodiversity, e.g. chytrid fungus in amphibians, and ash dieback removing a native tree species and the organisms that depend on it.
[bio22-s7.12.7] Informed citizens: understanding how diseases spread, and why vaccines and antibiotics must be used carefully, lets people make informed choices, follow public-health advice and judge claims in the media.
[bio22-s7.13.b] A full discussion gives arguments for, then the costs and tensions of control measures, then a judgement. Examiners expect both sides.
[bio22-s7.13.0] Global cooperation: pathogens ignore borders, so a disease anywhere is a risk everywhere. Sharing data, genome sequences and vaccines between countries benefits all.
[bio22-s7.13.1] Tensions and costs: control measures limit personal freedom, such as quarantine, travel bans and lockdowns, and their social and economic costs, such as lost income and education, isolation and poor mental health, fall unequally.
[bio22-s7.13.2] Culling destroys farmers' livelihoods and healthy animals, vaccine requirements raise questions of consent, and poorer countries have less access to vaccines and drugs.
[bio22-s7.13.3] Acting early on uncertain evidence may later look like over-reaction, while waiting for certainty lets a disease spread. This is the precautionary principle, and there is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s7.13.4] Judgement: knowledge of emerging diseases matters because it allows targeted, proportionate measures, which cost less and do less harm than either doing nothing or applying blanket restrictions.
[bio22-s7.13.5] The more is known about a disease's factors, the more precisely its spread can be limited.

CANDIDATES bio24-s1: Flower structure and pollination
[bio24-s1.0.b] Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers, which produce the gametes (sex cells) and bring about pollination (the transfer of pollen) and fertilisation (the joining of male and female gametes). Three structures carry the process from gamete to new plant: the flower, the seed and the fruit.
[bio24-s1.0.0] The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle, the tip of the flower stalk. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
[bio24-s1.0.1] The flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination. It is the only one of the three structures that exists before fertilisation, because the seed and fruit develop from flower parts afterwards.
[bio24-s1.0.2] The seed, a fertilised and developed ovule, contains the embryo (a new plant) with a food store and a protective coat. The fruit, a developed ovary, protects the seeds and helps to disperse them, that is, spread them away from the parent plant.
[bio24-s1.0.3] Seed detail is in Seeds; dispersal is in Fruit and seed dispersal.
[bio24-s1.0.4] Many flowers are bisexual (hermaphrodite), meaning they have both male and female parts in the same flower, e.g. buttercup.
[bio24-s1.0.5] Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel, whose male flowers hang in catkins and whose small female flowers have red stigmas. In some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly.
[bio24-s1.1.b] A flower is built in rings, listed here from the outside inwards. The outer parts hold the flower together, protect it and, in many species, attract the animals that carry its pollen.
[bio24-s1.1.0] Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached, so it holds the flower together. For example, in strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
[bio24-s1.1.1] Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud, and some photosynthesise (make food using light).
[bio24-s1.1.2] Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, which attracts insects.
[bio24-s1.1.3] Many insect-pollinated petals have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent, because there is no need to attract animals.
[bio24-s1.1.4] Nectary: a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects so that they keep coming back. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
[bio24-s1.2.b] The male part of the flower is the stamen. Its job is to make pollen, the package that carries the male gametes, and to hold it where a pollinator can pick it up.
[bio24-s1.2.0] Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament, so that the pollen is held up where it can be picked up. The stamens together form the androecium.
[bio24-s1.2.1] Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis (a cell division that halves the chromosome number), so the pollen grains are haploid, with one set of chromosomes. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
[bio24-s1.2.2] Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position, where insects brush against it or out in the wind. It also carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand (a thin tube of transport tissue).
[bio24-s1.2.3] Diagram: a stamen drawn on its own. Labels: anther, filament.
[bio24-s1.3.b] The female part of the flower is the carpel. It catches pollen, provides a route for the male gametes to reach the egg cell, and protects the ovules that become seeds.
[bio24-s1.3.0] Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
[bio24-s1.3.1] Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers, so pollen carried on an insect adheres, and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses, so it can catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s1.3.2] Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube (the thin tube a pollen grain grows) grows down through it to the ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.3] Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them, so the developing seeds are not exposed. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
[bio24-s1.3.4] Ovule: a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis, so it is haploid. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
[bio24-s1.3.5] Diagram: a carpel drawn on its own. Labels: stigma, style, ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.6] Diagram: a half-flower (vertical section) of an insect-pollinated flower such as buttercup or wallflower. Labels: receptacle, sepal, petal, nectary, stamen (anther, filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), ovule.
[bio24-s1.4.b] Pollination must happen before fertilisation can take place. Flowers can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated, and several features favour cross-pollination.
[bio24-s1.4.0] Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. It is only the arrival of pollen: fertilisation, the fusion of gamete nuclei in the ovule, comes later.
[bio24-s1.4.1] Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
[bio24-s1.4.2] Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation. The species is better able to adapt to change, such as new diseases or climate, and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding (breeding between close relatives).
[bio24-s1.4.3] Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but it gives less variation. It suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
[bio24-s1.4.4] Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly).
[bio24-s1.4.5] In primrose two flower forms exist: pin flowers, with a long style and low anthers, and thrum flowers, with a short style and high anthers. Insects carry pollen between the two forms, so the pollen reaches a different plant.
[bio24-s1.4.6] Agents of pollination (the carriers of pollen): insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak and birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. There is more on this in Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants.
[bio24-s1.5.b] The flower's structure matches the agent of pollination, whether an animal or the wind. Each type of flower is built to make the transfer of pollen as reliable as possible.
[bio24-s1.5.0] In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them.
[bio24-s1.5.1] Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
[bio24-s1.5.2] Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip, having learned the flower they are faithful to, so pollen is carried to the right species.
[bio24-s1.5.3] Plant and pollinator both benefit, because the plant is pollinated and the insect is fed: this is a mutualism (a relationship in which both partners gain). There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio24-s1.5.4] In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma. There is nothing to attract animals, so there are no showy petals, scent or nectar.
[bio24-s1.5.5] Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries and oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease.
[bio24-s1.5.6] Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre. It encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators, e.g. by leaving wildflowers and mowing less often.
[bio24-s1.5.7] Health link: wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.
[bio24-s1.6.b] Once pollen reaches a stigma of the right species, a sequence of events leads to fertilisation. Afterwards the flower parts change into the seed and fruit.
[bio24-s1.6.0] Fertilisation in five steps: 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma.
[bio24-s1.6.1] 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes.
[bio24-s1.6.2] 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it.
[bio24-s1.6.3] 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes, the first cell of the new plant).
[bio24-s1.6.4] 5. The zygote divides by mitosis (ordinary cell division that makes identical cells) to form the embryo.
[bio24-s1.6.5] Double fertilisation: a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store.
[bio24-s1.6.6] After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed, and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa, the seed coat. The ovary becomes the fruit, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp). The petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.
[bio24-s1.6.7] Diagram: a carpel in section, with a pollen grain on the stigma, the pollen tube growing down the style and entering an ovule through the micropyle. Labels: pollen grain, pollen tube, style, ovary, ovule, egg cell, micropyle.

CANDIDATES bio24-s2: Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants
[bio24-s2.0.b] A flower's structure follows from how its pollen travels. Insects must be lured in and rewarded, so insect-pollinated flowers advertise; the wind needs no persuading, so wind-pollinated flowers drop the advertising. Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second, and the flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio24-s2.0.0] Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides (markings that lead the insect towards the nectar), to attract insects.
[bio24-s2.0.1] Wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, because there is no animal to attract and large petals would block pollen in the wind.
[bio24-s2.0.2] Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries (the glands that produce nectar, a sugary liquid), so the insect is rewarded for visiting.
[bio24-s2.0.3] Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries, because there is no visitor to reward.
[bio24-s2.0.4] Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters, so that insects can spot them.
[bio24-s2.0.5] Wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins (dangling spikes of tiny flowers) or spikes held high above the leaves, where the wind can reach them.
[bio24-s2.0.6] Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
[bio24-s2.0.7] Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
[bio24-s2.1.b] The anther (the pollen-producing part of the stamen, held up on a stalk called the filament) and the stigma (the sticky or feathery top of the carpel that receives pollen) are positioned to suit the carrier. Insects touch the flower's parts; the wind blows past them.
[bio24-s2.1.0] Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them, so pollen rubs onto its body.
[bio24-s2.1.1] Wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
[bio24-s2.1.2] Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them and pollen sticks to them.
[bio24-s2.1.3] Wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s2.1.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of a buttercup or wallflower half-flower (large petal, nectary, anther inside, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas, anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
[bio24-s2.2.b] The pollen itself also differs. Insects deliver pollen accurately, whereas the wind scatters it at random, and the grains are built for the different journeys.
[bio24-s2.2.0] Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower.
[bio24-s2.2.1] Wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species, so many grains are needed for a few to land.
[bio24-s2.2.2] Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs.
[bio24-s2.2.3] Wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump together.
[bio24-s2.2.4] Diagram: an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.
[bio24-s2.3.b] This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.
[bio24-s2.3.0] Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
[bio24-s2.3.1] Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
[bio24-s2.3.2] What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features (recorded in words) are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface.
[bio24-s2.3.3] Quantitative features (measured as numbers) are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
[bio24-s2.3.4] Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen, so every flower is at the same stage. Examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements, to reduce the effect of one unusual flower.
[bio24-s2.3.5] Measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification, so the two types can be compared fairly.
[bio24-s2.3.6] Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.3.7] Also needed: microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.
[bio24-s2.4.b] The method moves from the whole flower, to its separated parts, to the pollen under the microscope, and repeats everything for the second flower type.
[bio24-s2.4.0] 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
[bio24-s2.4.1] 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
[bio24-s2.4.2] 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
[bio24-s2.4.3] 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
[bio24-s2.4.4] 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
[bio24-s2.4.5] 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
[bio24-s2.4.6] 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power.
[bio24-s2.4.7] 8. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
[bio24-s2.5.b] Scalpels, pollen, some plants and glass slides each carry a risk, so the practical needs a precaution for each.
[bio24-s2.5.0] Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely, so nobody is cut.
[bio24-s2.5.1] Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
[bio24-s2.5.2] Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
[bio24-s2.5.3] Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
[bio24-s2.5.4] Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.
[bio24-s2.6.b] Recording results in a table puts the two flowers side by side, so each difference can be seen and then explained.
[bio24-s2.6.0] Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
[bio24-s2.6.1] Expected primary data (collected by you) for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.6.2] Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.7.b] A conclusion links what was seen to why it matters for pollination, and secondary data add evidence the class could not collect itself.
[bio24-s2.7.0] Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations above. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio24-s2.7.1] Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen.
[bio24-s2.7.2] Other secondary data: published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; and labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
[bio24-s2.7.3] Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio24-s2.8.b] Every practical has weaknesses. Each limitation below has a matching improvement, and you should be able to give both.
[bio24-s2.8.0] Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
[bio24-s2.8.1] Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
[bio24-s2.8.2] Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.8.3] Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes.
[bio24-s2.8.4] The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
[bio24-s2.8.5] Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective, because different people judge differently; have two people record them.
[bio24-s2.8.6] Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

CANDIDATES bio26-s2: The nephron
[bio26-s2.0.b] Urine is made by tiny tubes called nephrons, so you need to know the parts of one in the order the fluid flows through them. Each part has its own job, and its position in the kidney is part of the answer.
[bio26-s2.0.0] A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney. It is the kidney's functional unit (the smallest part that does the kidney's job), where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one cell thick throughout.
[bio26-s2.0.1] Bowman's capsule is the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex (the outer layer of the kidney), wrapped around the glomerulus (a capillary knot). Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the capsule's inner space.
[bio26-s2.0.2] The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the first, long, coiled (convoluted) section after the capsule, in the cortex. Proximal means nearer the capsule.
[bio26-s2.0.3] The loop of Henle is a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla (the inner part of the kidney), with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
[bio26-s2.0.4] The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) is the second coiled section, back in the cortex. Distal means further from the capsule. It empties into a collecting duct.
[bio26-s2.0.5] The collecting duct is a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla, through a pyramid, and opens into the renal pelvis (the funnel that collects urine).
[bio26-s2.0.6] The syllabus does not list the collecting duct, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH. Some textbooks do not count it as part of the nephron.
[bio26-s2.1.b] A nephron works because blood is brought right up against it. The blood vessels are arranged so that pressure builds up for filtering, and so that the tubule is wrapped in a second set of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) to take back what it reabsorbs.
[bio26-s2.1.0] A branch of the renal artery (the artery that brings blood to the kidney) divides into arterioles (small arteries). The afferent arteriole (afferent means carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
[bio26-s2.1.1] The capillaries of the glomerulus rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent means carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. The afferent is the wider one, so do not say the efferent is wider.
[bio26-s2.1.2] Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus, like water backing up behind a narrowed nozzle. This high pressure drives filtration.
[bio26-s2.1.3] The glomerulus is unusual because it is a capillary network lying between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule (a small vein) as capillaries normally do.
[bio26-s2.1.4] The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries, so they must be close to the tubule.
[bio26-s2.1.5] These capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration, because the cells need ATP for active transport, and they carry away the cells' carbon dioxide.
[bio26-s2.1.6] The capillaries join into venules, then into a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
[bio26-s2.1.7] Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below; mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).
[bio26-s2.2.b] The first step in making urine is filtration. It happens in the glomerulus and capsule, where pressure forces fluid out of the blood without checking whether the body needs it.
[bio26-s2.2.0] Filtration, also called ultrafiltration or filtration under pressure, is the forcing of small molecules out of the blood by the high pressure in the glomerulus. They pass through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
[bio26-s2.2.1] Substances that pass into the filtrate (the fluid that enters the capsule) are water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea (the waste made from breaking down amino acids), vitamins and some hormones.
[bio26-s2.2.2] Substances that are not filtered and stay in the blood are red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
[bio26-s2.2.3] Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances such as glucose and amino acids are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
[bio26-s2.2.4] Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood); this is why reabsorption is essential.
[bio26-s2.2.5] Structure suits filtration: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arteriole raise the pressure, and the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous, so fluid passes through easily.
[bio26-s2.2.6] The many capillaries in each glomerulus, with about a million glomeruli in the kidney, give a large filtering surface, so a large volume of fluid can be filtered every day.
[bio26-s2.3.b] Filtration lets everything small through, so the body has to take back what it wants. Most of this reabsorption happens in the PCT, whose structure is built for the job.
[bio26-s2.3.0] Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
[bio26-s2.3.1] The PCT is the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here.
[bio26-s2.3.2] Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, which uses ATP (the cell's energy currency) to move them into the cells. Water follows by osmosis, and some urea diffuses back into the blood.
[bio26-s2.3.3] Water is always reabsorbed passively by osmosis; it is never actively pumped. Only salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.3.4] The PCT is long and coiled, which gives time and surface area for reabsorption. Its cells have microvilli (tiny finger-like projections) on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area.
[bio26-s2.3.5] The PCT cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. The tubule is closely surrounded by capillaries, and its wall is one cell thick, so the distance to the blood is short.
[bio26-s2.3.6] The carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.
[bio26-s2.4.b] After the PCT, the rest of the nephron fine-tunes the filtrate. The loop of Henle makes the medulla salty, and this lets the collecting duct take back more water.
[bio26-s2.4.0] The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty, with the highest solute concentration deep in the medulla.
[bio26-s2.4.1] The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
[bio26-s2.4.2] In the DCT more salts and water are reabsorbed, with the amounts adjusted to the body's needs.
[bio26-s2.4.3] The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption.
[bio26-s2.4.4] In the collecting duct, as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood, so the urine becomes more concentrated. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH, a hormone. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation.
[bio26-s2.4.5] What remains in the collecting duct is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s2.5.b] Comparing the composition of blood plasma, glomerular filtrate and urine shows what each part of the nephron has done. Each difference between them has one explanation.
[bio26-s2.5.0] Protein is present in plasma but absent from the filtrate and urine, because it is too large to be filtered.
[bio26-s2.5.1] Glucose and amino acids are present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration, because filtration lets them through freely. They are absent from urine, because all of them are reabsorbed in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.5.2] Urea is at the same concentration in plasma and filtrate, because it is freely filtered. It is much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
[bio26-s2.5.3] Salts are similar in plasma and filtrate. In urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
[bio26-s2.5.4] Skill: given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference. Absent from the filtrate means not filtered (too large); absent from the urine means reabsorbed; more concentrated in the urine means water was reabsorbed and the substance was not.
[bio26-s2.6.b] Following the fluid from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, each part of the nephron does one main job.
[bio26-s2.6.0] Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule: filtration under pressure.
[bio26-s2.6.1] Proximal convoluted tubule: reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and most water.
[bio26-s2.6.2] Loop of Henle: makes the medulla salty; water is reabsorbed from the descending limb.
[bio26-s2.6.3] Distal convoluted tubule: adjusts salts and water, and secretes hydrogen ions, potassium ions and some drugs.
[bio26-s2.6.4] Collecting duct: water is reabsorbed under ADH control.
[bio26-s2.6.5] Renal pelvis: receives the urine that is left.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio5-s1: Characteristics of living things
[bio5-s1.0.b] Biology is the study of living things, called organisms. No single feature separates living from non-living, so the specification lists eleven characteristics: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis. Together they define life.
[bio5-s1.0.0] An organism shows all eleven characteristics at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once. A dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but it is still alive.
[bio5-s1.0.1] Some non-living things show one or two characteristics. A crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells.
[bio5-s1.0.2] A fire seems to 'use oxygen', 'release energy' and 'spread', but it has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions, so it is not alive.
[bio5-s1.0.3] Something is alive only if it shows the full set, not just one or two features. Viruses are the main borderline case. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio5-s1.1.b] Living things are not random mixtures of chemicals: they are built in an ordered way, and the basic building block is the cell. Two of the eleven characteristics, organisation and cellular basis, describe this.
[bio5-s1.1.0] Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms (those made of many cells), cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs.
[bio5-s1.1.1] Even a single cell has organised parts called organelles (small structures inside a cell, each with its own job). There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio5-s1.1.2] Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life, so it is the basic unit of structure and function.
[bio5-s1.1.3] Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process. Multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells, each adapted to one job.
[bio5-s1.1.4] All cells share a cell membrane, cytoplasm (the jelly-like fluid inside the cell), ribosomes (which make proteins) and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
[bio5-s1.1.5] All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division. New cells are never made from non-living material.
[bio5-s1.1.6] These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century. Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
[bio5-s1.2.b] Every organism needs a supply of food to stay alive and to build and repair its body. Nutrition is how it gets that food, and organisms fall into two groups depending on whether they make it or take it in.
[bio5-s1.2.0] Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
[bio5-s1.2.1] Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances (ones not made by living things, such as carbon dioxide and water). Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio5-s1.2.2] A few bacteria are chemosynthetic: instead of light, they use energy from chemical reactions to make their food, so they can live where there is no sunlight.
[bio5-s1.2.3] Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals.
[bio5-s1.2.4] Fungi and many bacteria are also heterotrophs: they absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio5-s1.3.b] Food is only useful once its stored energy can be released in a form cells can use. Respiration does this inside every living cell, and it is easily confused with breathing.
[bio5-s1.3.0] Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food, mainly glucose, inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy), which powers the cell's activities.
[bio5-s1.3.1] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
[bio5-s1.3.2] Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio5-s1.3.3] In human muscle, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid. In yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[bio5-s1.3.4] Respiration is not the same as breathing. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange), while respiration is a chemical process in cells.
[bio5-s1.3.5] Plants respire all the time, day and night, because every living cell needs energy continuously. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio5-s1.4.b] Living cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Metabolism is the name for all of them, and they fall into two types: those that build up and those that break down.
[bio5-s1.4.0] Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction).
[bio5-s1.4.1] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose made from carbon dioxide and water) and protein synthesis from amino acids.
[bio5-s1.4.2] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, and the digestion of starch to maltose.
[bio5-s1.4.3] In short, anabolic means building up and using energy, while catabolic means breaking down and releasing energy. There is more on this in Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life.
[bio5-s1.5.b] Chemical reactions in cells make some products that the body cannot use. Excretion removes them before they cause harm.
[bio5-s1.5.0] Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism, which are substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed. If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
[bio5-s1.5.1] Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs. Urea, made in the liver from excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in urine. Excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
[bio5-s1.5.2] Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata (the tiny pores in leaves). Some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
[bio5-s1.5.3] Excretion is not egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.b] Cells work properly only when the conditions around them stay within narrow limits, even if the outside world changes. Homeostasis is the control that keeps them steady.
[bio5-s1.6.0] Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, such as body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance and pH, despite changes outside or inside the organism.
[bio5-s1.6.1] It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH, so a large change would slow or stop the reactions of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.2] For example, human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C, and insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. There is more on the mechanism, negative feedback, in Homeostasis.
[bio5-s1.7.b] Organisms must cope with a changing environment and must also produce new individuals, because every organism eventually dies. Response and reproduction cover these two needs.
[bio5-s1.7.0] Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
[bio5-s1.7.1] Animals respond quickly through the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio5-s1.7.2] Plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure, e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short, which reduces water loss. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio5-s1.7.3] Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
[bio5-s1.7.4] Asexual reproduction: one parent and no gametes (sex cells). The offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria and budding in yeast.
[bio5-s1.7.5] Sexual reproduction: two gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote (the first cell of the new organism). The offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans and flowering plants.
[bio5-s1.8.b] Organisms get bigger as they develop, and they pass their features on to the next generation. Growth and heredity describe these two processes.
[bio5-s1.8.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism, the building reactions of metabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
[bio5-s1.8.1] Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips. Most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
[bio5-s1.8.2] Heredity: the passing of genetic information, carried by genes made of DNA, from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. 'Like produces like', e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
[bio5-s1.8.3] Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits (its characteristics). Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance.
[bio5-s1.9.b] Here is the full set in one place for quick revision. An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life.
[bio5-s1.9.0] Organisation: highly ordered structure, from cells up to organ systems.
[bio5-s1.9.1] Response: detecting a stimulus and reacting to it.
[bio5-s1.9.2] Respiration: releasing energy from food inside cells.
[bio5-s1.9.3] Nutrition: obtaining food for energy and raw materials.
[bio5-s1.9.4] Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.9.5] Reproduction: producing new individuals of the same species.
[bio5-s1.10.b] These are the remaining five characteristics, covering growth, chemistry, stability, inheritance and cells.
[bio5-s1.10.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass.
[bio5-s1.10.1] Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism.
[bio5-s1.10.2] Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment relatively constant.
[bio5-s1.10.3] Heredity: passing genetic information from parents to offspring.
[bio5-s1.10.4] Cellular basis: made of cells, which arise only from pre-existing cells.

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.
