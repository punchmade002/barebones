CANDIDATES bio18-s1: The musculoskeletal system as a system for response
[bio18-s1.0.b] The musculoskeletal system is the framework of bones and joints together with the muscles that move it. Between them they let the body respond to a stimulus (a change in the surroundings) by moving.
[bio18-s1.0.0] The skeleton is the bones, together with the cartilage and ligaments that join them at joints (the places where two bones meet).
[bio18-s1.0.1] The skeletal muscles are attached to bones by tendons, which are the other part of the system.
[bio18-s1.0.2] Muscles provide the force, bones act as rigid levers, and joints are the pivots where movement happens, so a muscle pulling on a bone moves it at a joint.
[bio18-s1.0.3] The parts are only named here. There is more on the bones in "The axial skeleton" and "The appendicular skeleton", and on the joints and muscle pairs in "Muscles and joints".
[bio18-s1.0.4] The adult human skeleton has 206 bones, which are divided into two groups.
[bio18-s1.0.5] 80 of these form the axial skeleton: the skull, vertebral column (backbone), ribs and sternum (breastbone), which make up the central axis of the body.
[bio18-s1.0.6] The other 126 form the appendicular skeleton: the pectoral girdle (shoulders), the pelvic girdle (hips) and the limbs attached to them.
[bio18-s1.1.b] Bones look like lifeless sticks, but bone is a living tissue made of cells. Its structure explains why it is both hard and tough.
[bio18-s1.1.0] Bone is a living connective tissue, which means a tissue that supports and holds the body together.
[bio18-s1.1.1] Bone cells lie in a hard matrix (the material around the cells) of calcium phosphate, which gives bone its hardness and resists compression (being squashed).
[bio18-s1.1.2] Collagen fibres in the matrix give bone some flexibility and resist bending and snapping, so bone is not brittle like chalk.
[bio18-s1.1.3] Bone has a blood supply and nerves, can grow and repair itself, and changes shape slowly in response to the forces put on it.
[bio18-s1.2.b] The skeleton does much more than hold us up. It has five functions, and each one can be illustrated with an example.
[bio18-s1.2.0] Support: the skeleton is a rigid framework that holds the body up, gives it its shape and supports soft organs. The vertebral column supports the head and trunk, and the leg bones carry the body's weight.
[bio18-s1.2.1] Protection: bones surround delicate organs so that knocks do not damage them. The skull protects the brain, the ribs and sternum protect the heart and lungs, the vertebrae protect the spinal cord, and the pelvic girdle protects the bladder and reproductive organs.
[bio18-s1.2.2] Movement: bones provide attachment points for muscles and act as levers that the muscles pull on, moving the body at joints.
[bio18-s1.2.3] Making blood cells: red bone marrow, found in spongy bone (the light, honeycomb-like bone), makes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
[bio18-s1.2.4] In adults, red marrow is mainly in the flat bones (skull, ribs, sternum, vertebrae and pelvis) and in the ends of long bones such as the femur (thigh bone) and humerus (upper arm bone).
[bio18-s1.2.5] Yellow marrow fills the hollow shafts of long bones and stores fat.
[bio18-s1.2.6] Storing minerals: bone stores calcium and phosphate.
[bio18-s1.2.7] When blood calcium falls, calcium is released from bone under the control of parathyroid hormone, which helps keep blood calcium constant. This matters because nerves and muscles need calcium to work. There is more on this in "Glands, locations and hormones".
[bio18-s1.3.b] The body has three types of muscle, and they differ in where they are, whether you can control them, how they look and how they contract. Only one of them belongs to the musculoskeletal system.
[bio18-s1.3.0] Skeletal muscle is attached to bones by tendons and is the only muscle type in the musculoskeletal system. It is under voluntary (conscious) control as well as reflex control, and its cells look striped (striated) under the microscope.
[bio18-s1.3.1] Skeletal muscle contracts quickly and powerfully but tires (fatigues), which is why you cannot keep sprinting indefinitely.
[bio18-s1.3.2] Smooth muscle is found in the walls of the gut, blood vessels, bladder and uterus. It is involuntary (not under conscious control) and not striped.
[bio18-s1.3.3] Smooth muscle contracts slowly and can stay contracted for a long time, as in peristalsis (the waves of squeezing that push food along the gut).
[bio18-s1.3.4] Cardiac muscle is found only in the wall of the heart. It is involuntary and striped, and it contracts rhythmically throughout life without tiring. There is more on the heart in "The Circulatory System and Blood".
[bio18-s1.4.b] A skeletal muscle is the effector that carries out a response, in the same way as the other effectors (the parts that act on a nerve message) such as glands. There is more on this in "Structures and systems for response". To see how it works, start with what it is made of.
[bio18-s1.4.0] A skeletal muscle is made of bundles of long cells called muscle fibres.
[bio18-s1.4.1] Each fibre contains many nuclei and many mitochondria, so it has plenty of control and plenty of energy supply for contraction.
[bio18-s1.4.2] Each fibre is packed with two kinds of protein filament: thin filaments of actin and thick filaments of myosin.
[bio18-s1.4.3] The filaments are arranged in repeating units that give the striped appearance of skeletal muscle.
[bio18-s1.5.b] A skeletal muscle never contracts on its own. It contracts only when it is stimulated by a motor neuron (a nerve cell that carries impulses to a muscle), and the contraction follows a fixed sequence.
[bio18-s1.5.0] 1. Skeletal muscle contracts only when it is stimulated by a motor neuron. A voluntary movement starts in the brain; a reflex movement starts in the spinal cord. There is more on this in "The reflex action".
[bio18-s1.5.1] 2. Impulses travel along the motor neuron to its ending on the muscle fibre, a synapse (a gap between nerve and target) called the neuromuscular junction.
[bio18-s1.5.2] 3. At the neuromuscular junction the neurotransmitter (chemical messenger) acetylcholine is released, crosses the gap and binds to receptors on the muscle fibre, starting an impulse in the fibre.
[bio18-s1.5.3] 4. The impulse causes calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to be released from stores inside the fibre.
[bio18-s1.5.4] 5. The calcium allows myosin filaments to attach to actin filaments and pull them inwards, so the filaments slide past each other and the fibre shortens.
[bio18-s1.5.5] 6. Energy for this comes from ATP, the energy carrier of the cell, which is made in the many mitochondria by respiration.
[bio18-s1.6.b] Contraction is only half the story. The muscle must also relax, and the nervous system must be able to vary how hard the muscle pulls.
[bio18-s1.6.0] When the impulses stop, calcium is pumped back into its stores, the filaments detach and the muscle relaxes.
[bio18-s1.6.1] A relaxed muscle does not lengthen by itself: it is pulled back to its full length by an opposing muscle, which is why muscles work in pairs. There is more on this in "Muscles and joints".
[bio18-s1.6.2] A muscle can only pull by contracting; it can never push.
[bio18-s1.6.3] The force is passed through a tendon to a bone, which moves at a joint. This movement is the response.
[bio18-s1.6.4] Strength of response: the more motor neurons that fire and the more muscle fibres that contract, the stronger the contraction, so movements can be gentle or forceful.
[bio18-s1.6.5] Muscle tone: a slight, continuous partial contraction of muscles holds the body's posture without conscious effort.
[bio18-s1.6.6] Diagram: a flow diagram of a voluntary response: brain, spinal cord, motor neuron, neuromuscular junction, skeletal muscle (contracts), tendon, bone moves at joint. Also a sketch of a skeletal muscle cut open to show bundles of muscle fibres, with one fibre enlarged to show actin and myosin filaments, all labelled.
[bio18-s1.7.b] Because skeletal muscle is attached to bones, the system produces a wide range of responses, from obvious movements to constant small adjustments you never notice.
[bio18-s1.7.0] Movement of the whole body (locomotion: walking, running, swimming) to find food, escape danger or move to better conditions.
[bio18-s1.7.1] Movement of parts of the body, such as grasping or turning the head towards a sound.
[bio18-s1.7.2] Maintaining posture and balance: muscles of the back, neck and legs make constant small adjustments, in response to information from receptors, to keep the body upright.
[bio18-s1.7.3] Breathing: the diaphragm and intercostal muscles (the muscles between the ribs) move the ribs. There is more on this in "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism".
[bio18-s1.7.4] Speech, facial expression, chewing and swallowing all depend on skeletal muscle.
[bio18-s1.7.5] Shivering: rapid involuntary contractions that release heat when the body is cold.
[bio18-s1.8.b] Muscles and bones cannot respond alone. They depend on other body systems to start the contraction, to supply fuel and to prepare them for action.
[bio18-s1.8.0] Nervous system: every contraction of a skeletal muscle is started by a motor neuron.
[bio18-s1.8.1] Receptors in muscles and joints send information back to the brain about the position of the limbs, so that movements can be coordinated.
[bio18-s1.8.2] Circulatory and breathing systems: active muscles need more oxygen and glucose for respiration, and must remove more carbon dioxide and heat, so heart rate and breathing rate rise during exercise.
[bio18-s1.8.3] When oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough, muscle fibres respire anaerobically (without oxygen), producing lactic acid and much less ATP, and soon tire (fatigue).
[bio18-s1.8.4] The lactic acid is removed afterwards, when oxygen is available again; this is one reason breathing stays fast after exercise. There is more on this in "Respiration".
[bio18-s1.8.5] Endocrine system: adrenaline (a hormone) increases blood flow to skeletal muscles and raises blood glucose, preparing them for action.
[bio18-s1.8.6] Health: regular weight-bearing exercise, such as walking or running, strengthens bones and muscles.
[bio18-s1.8.7] A diet with enough protein (for muscle), calcium and vitamin D (for bone) supports the system.

CANDIDATES bio18-s2: The axial skeleton
[bio18-s2.0.b] The skeleton has two parts, and the axial skeleton is the one that forms the central axis (the upright line) of the body. The appendicular skeleton, the limbs and the girdles that attach them, is attached to it. There is more on this in The appendicular skeleton.
[bio18-s2.0.0] The axial skeleton is made of four parts: the skull, the vertebral column (the vertebrae, which are the individual back bones, plus the discs between them), the ribs and the sternum (breastbone).
[bio18-s2.0.1] Its main jobs are protecting the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, which are soft and easily damaged, and supporting the head and trunk (the body without the head and limbs).
[bio18-s2.1.b] The skull is the bony case of the head. It protects the brain and the sense organs, and it is built from two parts with different jobs.
[bio18-s2.1.0] The skull has two parts. The cranium is a dome of curved, flat bones that encloses and protects the brain.
[bio18-s2.1.1] The facial bones form the face, hold the teeth, and form the eye sockets (orbits) and the nasal cavity (the space inside the nose).
[bio18-s2.1.2] The bones of the cranium are joined by sutures: wavy, interlocking joints filled with fibrous tissue that allow no movement, so the cranium acts as one rigid, strong box. Its domed shape also spreads the force of a blow.
[bio18-s2.1.3] The lower jaw (mandible) is the only skull bone that moves. It joins the cranium at a freely movable joint on each side, allowing chewing and speech.
[bio18-s2.1.4] The skull also protects the sense organs: the eyes sit in the bony orbits, and the inner ear is enclosed in bone.
[bio18-s2.1.5] A large opening at the base of the cranium, the foramen magnum, lets the spinal cord pass down from the brain. The skull rests on the first vertebra.
[bio18-s2.1.6] In a newborn baby the cranial bones are not yet fused (joined into one). Soft, membrane-covered gaps called fontanelles allow the skull to change shape slightly during birth and allow the brain to grow; they close in the first two years.
[bio18-s2.1.7] Diagram: side view of the skull, labelling cranium, sutures, orbit (eye socket), facial bones, upper jaw, lower jaw (mandible), teeth and foramen magnum (or opening for spinal cord).
[bio18-s2.2.b] The vertebral column is the backbone. It is made of many small bones stacked in a line, so it is strong enough to carry the body yet flexible enough to bend. It is divided into five regions, each suited to its job.
[bio18-s2.2.0] The vertebral column (backbone or spine) is a flexible column of 33 vertebrae stacked one on another, from the skull to the pelvis.
[bio18-s2.2.1] It supports the head and trunk, protects the spinal cord, provides attachment for ribs and muscles, and joins the axial skeleton to the pelvic girdle (the hip bones).
[bio18-s2.2.2] The 7 cervical vertebrae (neck) are small and light, so they can support the head and allow it to nod and turn.
[bio18-s2.2.3] The 12 thoracic vertebrae (chest) each join a pair of ribs.
[bio18-s2.2.4] The 5 lumbar vertebrae (lower back) are the largest and strongest. They carry most of the weight of the upper body and allow bending.
[bio18-s2.2.5] The 5 sacral vertebrae are fused into one bone, the sacrum, which is joined to the pelvic girdle and passes the body's weight to the legs.
[bio18-s2.2.6] About 4 coccygeal vertebrae are fused to form the coccyx (tailbone), which anchors some muscles; the number varies from 3 to 5.
[bio18-s2.2.7] Vertebrae get larger from the neck to the lower back, because each one carries the weight of everything above it.
[bio18-s2.3.b] The top two vertebrae are shaped differently from the rest so that the head can move in two ways. Seen from the side, the whole column also bends in an S shape.
[bio18-s2.3.0] The atlas (first cervical vertebra) carries the skull and allows the head to nod.
[bio18-s2.3.1] The axis (second cervical vertebra) has a peg on which the atlas turns, allowing the head to rotate from side to side.
[bio18-s2.3.2] The column has an S-shaped curve when seen from the side: forward in the neck, back in the chest and forward in the lower back.
[bio18-s2.3.3] The curves act like a spring, absorbing shock when walking and running, and keep the body's weight balanced over the legs.
[bio18-s2.3.4] Diagram: side view of the vertebral column labelling the cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and coccyx regions, the S-shaped curves and the discs between vertebrae.
[bio18-s2.4.b] A typical vertebra has a heavy part at the front to take weight, and a ring of bone at the back to protect the spinal cord. Each part of its structure suits its job.
[bio18-s2.4.0] The centrum (body) is a thick cylinder of bone at the front of the vertebra. It bears the body's weight.
[bio18-s2.4.1] The neural arch at the back surrounds the neural canal (vertebral canal), through which the spinal cord runs, so the cord is enclosed in bone.
[bio18-s2.4.2] The spinous process at the back and a transverse process on each side are projections that provide attachment for muscles and ligaments (tough bands that join bone to bone), acting as levers for the back muscles.
[bio18-s2.4.3] Facets are smooth surfaces that form small joints with the vertebrae above and below, guiding and limiting movement. In thoracic vertebrae, extra facets form the joints with the ribs.
[bio18-s2.4.4] Between neighbouring vertebrae there are small gaps on each side through which the spinal nerves leave the spinal cord. There is more on this in CNS and PNS.
[bio18-s2.4.5] Diagram: top view of a single lumbar or thoracic vertebra labelling centrum, neural arch, neural canal with spinal cord, spinous process, transverse processes and facets.
[bio18-s2.5.b] Bone on bone would be painful and would wear away, and it would not bend. Between the vertebrae lie intervertebral discs (inter means between), which solve these problems.
[bio18-s2.5.0] Position: an intervertebral disc lies between the centra of each pair of neighbouring vertebrae, from below the axis down to the sacrum, joining them together.
[bio18-s2.5.1] There are no discs within the fused sacrum and coccyx, because those vertebrae are fused into single bones and do not move against each other.
[bio18-s2.5.2] Structure: each disc is a pad of cartilage with a tough outer ring of fibrocartilage (fibrous cartilage) surrounding a soft, jelly-like centre.
[bio18-s2.5.3] Shock absorption: the discs compress under load and spring back, cushioning the vertebrae and the brain against the jolts of walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s2.5.4] Preventing friction and wear: the discs stop the bones of neighbouring vertebrae rubbing against each other.
[bio18-s2.5.5] Flexibility: each disc allows a small amount of movement between two vertebrae, so the joints between vertebrae are slightly movable. Added together along the column, these small movements let the back bend forwards, backwards and sideways, and twist.
[bio18-s2.5.6] Holding the column together: the discs, with the ligaments, bind the vertebrae into one column while allowing it to bend.
[bio18-s2.5.7] The discs are squeezed slightly during a day of standing and sitting and recover while lying down, so a person is slightly taller in the morning than in the evening.
[bio18-s2.6.b] The ribs and sternum together form the rib cage, a bony cage around the chest. It must be strong enough to protect the organs inside but also able to move, because the chest changes size when we breathe.
[bio18-s2.6.0] There are 12 pairs of ribs: curved, flat bones that form the rib cage around the chest. At the back, every rib is joined to a thoracic vertebra by small joints that let it move.
[bio18-s2.6.1] The first seven pairs (true ribs) are joined directly to the sternum by strips of flexible costal cartilage.
[bio18-s2.6.2] Pairs 8 to 10 (false ribs) are joined to the cartilage of the rib above them.
[bio18-s2.6.3] Pairs 11 and 12 (floating ribs) are attached only at the back, to the vertebrae, and are free at the front.
[bio18-s2.6.4] The rib cage protects the heart and lungs, and partly the liver, stomach and kidneys.
[bio18-s2.6.5] Because the ribs are curved and joined by flexible cartilage, the cage can absorb blows and can move. The intercostal muscles between the ribs raise and lower the rib cage, changing the volume of the chest in breathing. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio18-s2.6.6] The sternum (breastbone) is a long, flat bone in the centre of the front of the chest. It protects the heart and anchors the ribs, through the costal cartilages, and the clavicles (collarbones). Like other flat bones, it contains red bone marrow.
[bio18-s2.6.7] Diagram: front view of the rib cage labelling the sternum, ribs, costal cartilage, true, false and floating ribs, thoracic vertebrae (behind), and the position of the clavicles.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio23-s5: Pregnancy
[bio23-s5.0.b] Pregnancy starts with a single fertilised cell and, within about a week, a ball of cells that must attach itself to the wall of the uterus. The journey and the attachment (implantation) happen in a set order and in set places.
[bio23-s5.0.0] Fertilisation takes place in the fallopian tube (the tube that carries the egg from the ovary towards the uterus) and gives a diploid zygote (a cell with a full set of chromosomes), the first cell of the new individual. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s5.0.1] The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis (division that makes identical cells) as cilia (tiny moving hairs lining the tube) and muscle contractions of the tube wall carry it along towards the uterus.
[bio23-s5.0.2] The cells do not grow between divisions, so they become smaller with each division and the ball stays about the size of the egg.
[bio23-s5.0.3] After about five days the ball reaches the uterus, the muscular organ in which the baby will develop, as a small ball of cells.
[bio23-s5.0.4] Implantation: about 6–7 days after fertilisation the ball of cells sinks into the thick, spongy endometrium (the lining of the uterus) and embeds itself there, with enzymes from its outer cells digesting a way in. From implantation the woman is pregnant.
[bio23-s5.1.b] Once the ball of cells is embedded, the endometrium has to stay in place, or it would be shed in a period and the pregnancy would be lost. A hormone from the embryo makes sure it stays.
[bio23-s5.1.0] Implantation needs an endometrium kept thick by progesterone, a hormone made by the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after the egg is released).
[bio23-s5.1.1] After implantation, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so the endometrium is kept and menstruation (the monthly period) stops.
[bio23-s5.1.2] A missed period is often the first sign of pregnancy, and pregnancy tests work by detecting this hormone, hCG, in urine. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s5.1.3] If the ball of cells implants in the fallopian tube instead of the uterus, it is an ectopic pregnancy.
[bio23-s5.2.b] Soon after implantation the embryo needs a way to get food and oxygen from its mother and to get rid of its wastes. The placenta is the organ that does this, and it is built jointly by the embryo and the mother.
[bio23-s5.2.0] After implantation, the outer cells of the embryo grow many finger-like projections (villi) into the endometrium.
[bio23-s5.2.1] The villi, together with the mother's endometrium and the blood spaces around them, form the placenta, a disc-shaped organ attached to the wall of the uterus.
[bio23-s5.2.2] The placenta is therefore made by both the embryo (the villi) and the mother (the endometrium).
[bio23-s5.2.3] The placenta is fully formed by about the end of the third month (12 weeks) and keeps growing until birth.
[bio23-s5.3.b] The foetus (the developing baby) floats in the uterus, away from the placenta. The umbilical cord is the link between them, and it carries blood in two directions.
[bio23-s5.3.0] The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta.
[bio23-s5.3.1] The cord contains two umbilical arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) with wastes from the foetus to the placenta.
[bio23-s5.3.2] The cord contains one umbilical vein, which carries oxygenated blood with nutrients from the placenta to the foetus.
[bio23-s5.3.3] The vein carries oxygenated blood, the reverse of the adult pattern for most arteries and veins, because arteries are defined by carrying blood away from the heart and veins by carrying it towards the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio23-s5.4.b] Mother and foetus swap materials at the placenta, but their blood never meets. Everything that is exchanged has to cross a very thin barrier, and the placenta's structure is built to make that crossing quick.
[bio23-s5.4.0] The mother's and the foetus's blood do not mix. They are separated by a thin barrier of the villi, only a few cells thick.
[bio23-s5.4.1] This barrier protects the foetus from the mother's high blood pressure and from her immune cells, and it matters because their blood groups may differ. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio23-s5.4.2] The villi give a very large surface area, and the barrier is very thin, so the distance for diffusion is short.
[bio23-s5.4.3] The foetus's capillaries (tiny blood vessels) in the villi lie close to pools of the mother's blood, and blood flows on both sides, which keeps the concentration gradients high so diffusion carries on.
[bio23-s5.4.4] Substances cross by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), and some, such as glucose and amino acids, cross by facilitated diffusion or active transport.
[bio23-s5.5.b] Before birth the foetus's own lungs, gut and kidneys cannot do their jobs, so the placenta does them. It also protects and controls the pregnancy.
[bio23-s5.5.0] It supplies oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals and water to the foetus, so it acts as the foetus's lungs and gut.
[bio23-s5.5.1] It removes carbon dioxide and urea, which pass into the mother's blood to be excreted by her lungs and kidneys, so it acts as the foetus's kidneys.
[bio23-s5.5.2] It passes antibodies from mother to foetus, giving passive natural immunity (protection received ready-made, not made by the baby's own body) for the first months after birth. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s5.5.3] It secretes hormones, taking over the production of progesterone and oestrogen from the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s5.5.4] It forms a barrier to many pathogens (disease-causing organisms) and to the mother's blood cells.
[bio23-s5.6.b] The placenta is a barrier to many things, but not to everything. Some harmful substances in the mother's blood can cross to the foetus, which is why what a mother drinks, smokes, takes or catches matters.
[bio23-s5.6.0] Alcohol crosses and can cause foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, with damage to the brain and growth.
[bio23-s5.6.1] Nicotine and carbon monoxide from smoking reduce the oxygen supply to the foetus, and are linked to low birth weight and premature birth.
[bio23-s5.6.2] Many drugs, legal and illegal, cross the placenta; the thalidomide case is a well-known example. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio23-s5.6.3] Some viruses cross the placenta. Rubella (German measles) can cause deafness, blindness and heart defects if caught in early pregnancy, and HIV can also cross.
[bio23-s5.6.4] This is why antenatal advice (advice given to women during pregnancy) matters.
[bio23-s5.7.b] The foetus also needs protection from knocks and from drying out or overheating. A fluid-filled sac around it provides that.
[bio23-s5.7.0] The amnion is a thin membrane that forms a fluid-filled sac, the amniotic sac, around the embryo.
[bio23-s5.7.1] Amniotic fluid cushions the foetus against shocks, keeps its temperature steady, lets it move and so develop its muscles and bones, and prevents it sticking to the membranes.
[bio23-s5.7.2] Foetal cells in the fluid can be sampled for testing by amniocentesis. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s5.8.b] A labelled drawing of the foetus in the uterus pulls together the placenta, cord and amnion in one picture.
[bio23-s5.8.0] Diagram: a foetus in the uterus, showing the uterus wall and endometrium, the placenta attached to the uterus wall, the umbilical cord with its two arteries and one vein, the amnion and amniotic fluid, and the cervix with its mucus plug. Label each.
[bio23-s5.9.b] Over about nine months one cell becomes a baby with every tissue and organ. This follows a timetable, and the early weeks are the most delicate.
[bio23-s5.9.0] The baby develops by repeated mitosis (increasing cell number), cell growth, and cell differentiation (cells becoming specialised for different jobs), so that one zygote gives rise to all the tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.9.1] Length of pregnancy (gestation): about 38 weeks from fertilisation, which in practice is counted as about 40 weeks from the first day of the woman's last period, because the date of fertilisation is not known.
[bio23-s5.9.2] The pregnancy is divided into three trimesters of about three months each.
[bio23-s5.9.3] In the first two months all the main organs form, and the heart begins beating by the end of the first month.
[bio23-s5.9.4] By about 12 weeks the baby is recognisably human with all organs present, though tiny.
[bio23-s5.9.5] During the rest of pregnancy the organs grow and mature, the baby moves (felt by the mother from about 16–20 weeks), and fat is laid down under the skin in the last weeks.
[bio23-s5.9.6] The lungs are among the last organs to mature, which is why babies born very early have difficulty breathing. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s5.9.7] The first two months are when the baby is most at risk from harmful substances, because that is when the organs are forming; damage then can cause structural birth defects.
[bio23-s5.10.b] The ball of cells on its way to the uterus passes through two named stages. The names describe what the ball looks like at each point.
[bio23-s5.10.0] Zygote: the single diploid cell formed at fertilisation.
[bio23-s5.10.1] Morula: after about 3–4 days of division the zygote has become a solid ball of about 16 or more cells, still inside the zona pellucida (the protective coat around the egg), looking like a mulberry, which is the meaning of "morula".
[bio23-s5.10.2] The morula is still in the fallopian tube or just entering the uterus.
[bio23-s5.10.3] Blastocyst: by about day 5 the cells have rearranged into a hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity.
[bio23-s5.10.4] The blastocyst has two parts: an outer layer of cells, the trophoblast, which will form the embryo's part of the placenta and its membranes; and a small cluster inside, the inner cell mass, which will form the embryo itself.
[bio23-s5.10.5] The blastocyst hatches out of the zona pellucida and is the stage that implants, trophoblast first.
[bio23-s5.10.6] The cells of the inner cell mass can develop into any type of body cell, which is why they are embryonic stem cells. Their use and the ethical questions they raise are in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s5.10.7] Diagram: the sequence zygote → 2 cells → 4 cells → morula → blastocyst; the blastocyst in section (trophoblast, inner cell mass, fluid-filled cavity); and a side view of tube and uterus marking fertilisation, morula (both in the tube) and blastocyst implanting in the endometrium.
[bio23-s5.11.b] Soon after implantation the cells of the embryo sort themselves into three layers. Each layer goes on to form a particular set of tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.11.0] Germ layers: during the third week after fertilisation the cells of the inner cell mass move and arrange themselves into three layers, the germ layers, each of which gives rise to particular tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.11.1] Ectoderm (outer layer) forms the epidermis of the skin, hair and nails, the whole nervous system (brain, spinal cord and nerves), and parts of the eye.
[bio23-s5.11.2] Mesoderm (middle layer) forms muscles, bones and cartilage, the heart, blood and blood vessels, the kidneys and the reproductive organs.
[bio23-s5.11.3] Endoderm (inner layer) forms the lining of the digestive system and of the breathing system (trachea, bronchi and lungs), and the liver and pancreas.
[bio23-s5.11.4] Memory aid: ecto = outer skin and nerves; meso = movement, blood and support; endo = inner linings.
[bio23-s5.12.b] From implantation to birth the developing baby has two names. The boundary between them is when the organs have finished forming.
[bio23-s5.12.0] Embryo: the developing individual from implantation, about the second week after fertilisation, until the end of the eighth week, during which all the main organs form (organ formation).
[bio23-s5.12.1] By the end of week 8 the embryo is about 3 cm long and has all its major organs.
[bio23-s5.12.2] Foetus: from the ninth week until birth. No new organs form, but the existing ones grow, mature and begin to work.
[bio23-s5.12.3] The distinction: embryo means organs forming; foetus means organs present and growing.
[bio23-s5.12.4] Summary sequence: zygote (day 0, fallopian tube) → morula (days 3–4) → blastocyst (about day 5; implants about days 6–7) → germ layers form (week 3) → embryo (to the end of week 8; organs form) → foetus (week 9 to birth; growth and maturation).
[bio23-s5.13.b] Labour is the end of pregnancy. It starts when the uterus, which has held the baby for about nine months, begins to push it out.
[bio23-s5.13.0] Labour is the process by which the baby, then the placenta, is pushed out of the uterus.
[bio23-s5.13.1] Labour usually begins at about 40 weeks of pregnancy, when the baby is normally head down.
[bio23-s5.13.2] Hormonal changes start it, and the hormone oxytocin drives the contractions (tightening of the uterus muscle) by positive feedback (a change that leads to more of the same change). There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s5.14.b] The first stage prepares the way out. The cervix (the narrow neck at the bottom of the uterus), which stayed closed during pregnancy, has to open wide enough for the baby's head.
[bio23-s5.14.0] The muscle of the uterus wall begins to contract regularly, and the contractions become stronger and more frequent.
[bio23-s5.14.1] The contractions pull the cervix open until it is fully dilated, about 10 cm wide.
[bio23-s5.14.2] The mucus plug (the plug of mucus that sealed the cervix) comes away, and the amnion usually bursts, releasing the amniotic fluid; this is what is meant by "the waters break".
[bio23-s5.14.3] Stage 1 is the longest stage, often many hours in a first birth.
[bio23-s5.15.b] In the second stage the baby is pushed out through the birth canal. As soon as it is born it must switch from relying on the placenta to doing things for itself.
[bio23-s5.15.0] Strong contractions of the uterus, helped by the mother pushing with her abdominal muscles, push the baby, usually head first, out through the cervix and the vagina, the birth canal. This stage is also called expulsion.
[bio23-s5.15.1] The umbilical cord is then clamped and cut; the stump later dries and falls off, leaving the navel.
[bio23-s5.15.2] At birth the baby must start breathing for itself: the first breaths inflate the lungs, and its circulation changes so that blood goes to the lungs for oxygen instead of relying on the placenta.
[bio23-s5.16.b] Labour is not over when the baby is born: the placenta still has to come out. Sometimes a vaginal birth is not safe, and the baby is delivered by surgery instead.
[bio23-s5.16.0] Shortly after the birth, further contractions separate the placenta and membranes from the uterus wall and push them out; this is the afterbirth.
[bio23-s5.16.1] Contraction of the uterus also closes the blood vessels where the placenta was attached, which limits bleeding.
[bio23-s5.16.2] A Caesarean section is the delivery of the baby through a surgical cut in the abdomen and uterus, used when a vaginal birth would be unsafe for the mother or baby, for example a baby lying the wrong way, or a placenta blocking the cervix.

CANDIDATES bio23-s8: Fertility control and infertility
[bio23-s8.0.b] Fertility is the ability to produce offspring, but it is not available all the time in everyone. Males and females differ because sperm production runs continuously while egg release happens once per cycle.
[bio23-s8.0.0] A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.0.1] Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life, declining slowly in old age, and every ejaculation (release of semen) contains millions of sperm. This is why a man is fertile on any day.
[bio23-s8.0.2] Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle (ovulation), so fertility comes in a short window once each cycle rather than every day.
[bio23-s8.0.3] The egg survives only about 12–24 hours after release, while sperm can survive up to about five days in the female body.
[bio23-s8.0.4] So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation, when sperm already present can wait for the egg, and the day of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.0.5] In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because the day of ovulation varies from woman to woman and cycle to cycle, the window cannot be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
[bio23-s8.1.b] A woman's fertility is not the same at every age. It follows the state of her eggs, which she is born with and which do not renew.
[bio23-s8.1.0] Female fertility is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline.
[bio23-s8.1.1] Female fertility ends at the menopause, the time of life when ovulation and periods stop for good. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s8.2.b] Contraception means stopping a pregnancy from starting. Every method blocks one step on the route from egg release to implantation, and methods differ in how reliable they are and in what else they protect against.
[bio23-s8.2.0] Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.2.1] Methods work in one of three ways: by preventing ovulation, by preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or by preventing implantation, the embedding of the embryo in the lining of the uterus.
[bio23-s8.2.2] Methods are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
[bio23-s8.2.3] Effectiveness with perfect use: the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective.
[bio23-s8.2.4] With typical, real-life use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time, namely natural methods, condoms and the pill, fail more often, because people forget or make mistakes.
[bio23-s8.2.5] Long-acting methods, namely the implant, coils and sterilisation, stay above 99% effective with typical use, because there is nothing to remember.
[bio23-s8.2.6] Only condoms, male and female, protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), because they stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.2.7] People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.b] Natural methods use no drugs or devices. They rely on behaviour: avoiding intercourse altogether, or avoiding it on the days when pregnancy is possible.
[bio23-s8.3.0] Abstinence, not having intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.1] Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, which they find by combining several signs.
[bio23-s8.3.2] Sign 1, the calendar (rhythm) method: recording cycle lengths over time to estimate when ovulation happens.
[bio23-s8.3.3] Sign 2, body temperature: measured each morning before getting up, it rises by a small amount, a few tenths of a degree Celsius, just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of the hormone progesterone.
[bio23-s8.3.4] Sign 3, cervical mucus (the fluid made by the cervix, the neck of the uterus): it becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation.
[bio23-s8.3.5] Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used, giving a warning that ovulation is near. There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.4.b] Natural methods appeal to some people but are demanding to use and fail more often than other methods in real life. Knowing both sides lets you discuss them fairly.
[bio23-s8.4.0] Advantages of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and they are acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception, for example the teaching of the Catholic Church, which accepts only natural methods.
[bio23-s8.4.1] Limits of natural methods: they need training, daily records and regular cycles, and are much less reliable with typical use.
[bio23-s8.4.2] Natural methods are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, because cycles are then irregular. They also give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.4.3] Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation.
[bio23-s8.4.4] Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception, because ovulation can return without warning.
[bio23-s8.5.b] Barrier methods put a physical block between sperm and egg. Of the methods used during intercourse, only the condoms in this group protect against STIs, because they also stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.5.0] Male condom: a thin latex, or non-latex, sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm cannot enter the vagina.
[bio23-s8.5.1] The male condom also blocks the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV.
[bio23-s8.5.2] The male condom is cheap, widely available and has no hormones; it can tear or slip, and must be put on before any contact.
[bio23-s8.5.3] Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; it also protects against STIs.
[bio23-s8.5.4] Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide (a sperm-killing chemical) and left in for several hours afterwards; it does not protect well against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.b] The copper coil is a device rather than a barrier, but it is grouped with mechanical methods because it is a physical object placed in the body. It works without hormones.
[bio23-s8.6.0] Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse.
[bio23-s8.6.1] Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and it changes the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) so an embryo is unlikely to implant.
[bio23-s8.6.2] The copper coil lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility.
[bio23-s8.6.3] The copper coil can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days.
[bio23-s8.6.4] The copper coil can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.5] It is grouped as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical methods.
[bio23-s8.7.b] Sterilisation is a permanent operation that blocks the tubes gametes travel along. In men this is vasectomy, which stops sperm joining the semen without changing anything else about the man.
[bio23-s8.7.0] Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens (the tube carrying sperm from the testis) is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes. There is more on this in The male reproductive system.
[bio23-s8.7.1] After a vasectomy the man still ejaculates semen, because the fluids come from glands that are untouched; his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected.
[bio23-s8.7.2] The sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.7.3] A vasectomy is not effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
[bio23-s8.8.b] Female sterilisation works on the same principle as vasectomy: the tubes are blocked so egg and sperm cannot meet. Unlike vasectomy, it works straight away.
[bio23-s8.8.0] Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube, the tube that carries the egg from the ovary to the uterus, is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm cannot meet.
[bio23-s8.8.1] After tubal ligation ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before, because the egg is reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.8.2] Tubal ligation is effective at once.
[bio23-s8.9.b] Sterilisation suits some people very well and others badly. The key issue is that it is meant to be permanent.
[bio23-s8.9.0] Sterilisation is very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember, but it should be treated as irreversible: reversal is difficult and often fails.
[bio23-s8.9.1] Sterilisation carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.9.2] It suits people who are sure they want no more children.
[bio23-s8.9.3] Diagram: outline of the male system with the cut in each vas deferens marked, and of the female system with the cut or clip in each fallopian tube marked; label the vas deferens, testis, fallopian tube, ovary, uterus and “cut and tied”.
[bio23-s8.10.b] Chemical methods use hormones or sperm-killing chemicals. The pill is the best known: it is taken by mouth every day and uses synthetic versions of the body's own reproductive hormones.
[bio23-s8.10.0] The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation; the detail of how is in Hormonal manipulation. It also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass.
[bio23-s8.10.1] The combined pill is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed (a bleed like a period) happens.
[bio23-s8.10.2] The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who cannot take oestrogen.
[bio23-s8.11.b] Some hormonal methods release their hormone slowly over months or years, so the user has nothing to remember daily. This is why the implant and the coils, which are long-acting, stay above 99% effective in real life.
[bio23-s8.11.0] Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously.
[bio23-s8.11.1] The implant is a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years.
[bio23-s8.11.2] The injection is given every few months.
[bio23-s8.11.3] The hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS) is placed in the uterus and lasts several years; it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.
[bio23-s8.11.4] The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
[bio23-s8.12.b] Some chemical methods are used after the event or alongside another method rather than as a regular choice.
[bio23-s8.12.0] Emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, work mainly by delaying ovulation, so there is no egg for sperm to meet; one type works within 3 days and another within 5 days.
[bio23-s8.12.1] The copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method. Emergency pills are not for regular use.
[bio23-s8.12.2] Spermicides are chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
[bio23-s8.13.b] Hormonal methods are very effective and have extra health benefits, but they act on the whole body, so they also carry side effects and risks.
[bio23-s8.13.0] Hormonal methods are very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful.
[bio23-s8.13.1] The combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb).
[bio23-s8.13.2] Hormonal methods can cause side effects, for example headaches, mood changes and irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods.
[bio23-s8.13.3] The combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it is not prescribed to everyone.
[bio23-s8.13.4] Hormonal methods give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.13.5] The pill must be taken correctly every day; vomiting or some medicines can stop it working.
[bio23-s8.14.b] Some infections spread mainly through sexual contact, and some of them damage the reproductive system. This links contraception to infertility.
[bio23-s8.14.0] An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact.
[bio23-s8.14.1] Bacterial STIs are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are treated with antibiotics.
[bio23-s8.14.2] Viral STIs are HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B, which can be controlled but is not usually cured. There is more on pathogens in Pathogens.
[bio23-s8.14.3] Chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes, causing pelvic inflammatory disease, which scars and blocks the tubes.
[bio23-s8.14.4] Blocked tubes from pelvic inflammatory disease are a major preventable cause of female infertility, because the egg and sperm can no longer meet, and they raise the risk of ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy that starts outside the uterus).
[bio23-s8.14.5] In men chlamydia and gonorrhoea can inflame the epididymis, the coiled tube on the testis where sperm mature and are stored.
[bio23-s8.15.b] Because many STIs cause no symptoms and can damage fertility, prevention combines protection during sex, testing and treatment, and vaccination.
[bio23-s8.15.0] Prevention: correct use of condoms every time, and fewer sexual partners.
[bio23-s8.15.1] Prevention: regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners breaks the chain of transmission, because many infected people have no symptoms and would otherwise pass the infection on without knowing.
[bio23-s8.15.2] Prevention: vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B. There is more on HPV in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio23-s8.15.3] For HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP) lower the risk of infection.
[bio23-s8.15.4] Treatment of people with HIV reduces their virus to levels at which it is not passed on sexually.
[bio23-s8.15.5] Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics (the drugs no longer kill it), which makes prevention more important.
[bio23-s8.16.b] Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) lets a woman store her eggs while they are still young and healthy, to be used later. It uses a series of steps similar to the start of IVF.
[bio23-s8.16.0] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once, rather than the usual one.
[bio23-s8.16.1] 2. The eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation.
[bio23-s8.16.2] 3. The eggs are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, do not form, and they are stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
[bio23-s8.16.3] 4. Later the eggs are thawed and fertilised by IVF (fertilisation in a dish) or by ICSI (one sperm injected into an egg).
[bio23-s8.17.b] Egg freezing preserves fertility but cannot promise a baby. Who uses it, and why it is not a guarantee, are the points to know.
[bio23-s8.17.0] Uses: before cancer treatment, because chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries, or by women who wish to delay having children.
[bio23-s8.17.1] Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.17.2] Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen, since younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better.
[bio23-s8.17.3] Freezing gives no guarantee of a baby, and not every egg survives thawing.
[bio23-s8.17.4] The process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.
[bio23-s8.18.b] Infertility is common, and its causes can lie with the man, the woman, both, or neither. Finding the cause decides which treatment is suitable.
[bio23-s8.18.0] Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
[bio23-s8.18.1] The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives.
[bio23-s8.18.2] Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither, which is called unexplained infertility.
[bio23-s8.19.b] Male infertility usually comes down to too few sperm, sperm that cannot do their job, or sperm that cannot get out. Lifestyle can also reduce sperm production.
[bio23-s8.19.0] Low sperm count (too few sperm in the semen), and sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped, for example with a faulty tail, reduce the chance that one reaches the egg.
[bio23-s8.19.1] A blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens, for example after infection or vasectomy, stops sperm leaving the testes.
[bio23-s8.19.2] Undescended testes (testes that have not moved down into the scrotum) and low testosterone or low pituitary hormones are also male causes.
[bio23-s8.19.3] Lifestyle and environmental factors reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids and overheating of the testes. There is more on steroids in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s8.19.4] Male infertility is diagnosed by semen analysis, which tests sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope.
[bio23-s8.20.b] Female infertility arises when no egg is released, when egg and sperm cannot meet, or when the uterus cannot support an embryo.
[bio23-s8.20.0] Failure to ovulate, caused by a hormone imbalance, for example polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight, means no egg is released.
[bio23-s8.20.1] Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, for example after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI, stop egg and sperm meeting.
[bio23-s8.20.2] Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries.
[bio23-s8.20.3] Female causes also include problems of the uterus or cervical mucus, and age, because egg number and quality fall.
[bio23-s8.20.4] Female infertility is diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.
[bio23-s8.21.b] If the problem is that a woman does not release eggs regularly, the treatment is to make her ovaries release them. This is a first step before more complex techniques.
[bio23-s8.21.0] Stimulating ovulation is for women who do not ovulate regularly, for example because of PCOS.
[bio23-s8.21.1] Fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs.
[bio23-s8.21.2] The woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles (the fluid-filled sacs in the ovary that each contain an egg) develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation.
[bio23-s8.21.3] Risk: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely.
[bio23-s8.21.4] Risk: the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious.
[bio23-s8.21.5] Lifestyle change, for example weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.
[bio23-s8.22.b] Fertility drugs work by acting on the same hormone system that controls the menstrual cycle, either by boosting the brain's signals to the ovaries or by supplying them directly.
[bio23-s8.22.0] Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation. There is more on feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.22.1] Injected fertility drugs contain FSH, often with LH, and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop.
[bio23-s8.22.2] A final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge.
[bio23-s8.22.3] The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.
[bio23-s8.23.b] Artificial insemination helps when the sperm have trouble reaching the egg. It puts sperm into the uterus by hand, so more of them start closer to the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.0] Artificial insemination, or intrauterine insemination (IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.23.1] This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes, where fertilisation happens.
[bio23-s8.23.2] It is used for a mildly low sperm count or poor motility (swimming), cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm.
[bio23-s8.23.3] The woman's fallopian tubes must be open, because the sperm still have to travel along them to meet the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.4] The same technique is widely used in farming, for example to breed cattle from selected bulls.
[bio23-s8.24.b] IVF takes fertilisation out of the body. Eggs and sperm are brought together in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is then placed in the uterus.
[bio23-s8.24.0] IVF means “in glass”, that is, fertilisation outside the body.
[bio23-s8.24.1] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs.
[bio23-s8.24.2] 2. The eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound.
[bio23-s8.24.3] 3. The eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place.
[bio23-s8.24.4] 4. The embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days, up to the blastocyst stage (a ball of cells ready to implant). There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.24.5] 5. Usually one, sometimes two, embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube.
[bio23-s8.24.6] 6. A pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted.
[bio23-s8.24.7] Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts.
[bio23-s8.25.b] IVF is used when simpler treatments will not work or are unsuitable. It was developed in the twentieth century and is now widely used.
[bio23-s8.25.0] IVF is used for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (checking embryos for a genetic condition before transfer). There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s8.25.1] History: the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe.
[bio23-s8.25.2] Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010.
[bio23-s8.25.3] Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.
[bio23-s8.26.b] ICSI is a version of IVF designed for severe male infertility. Instead of leaving sperm to fertilise the egg in the dish, the laboratory places one sperm inside the egg.
[bio23-s8.26.0] ICSI is the same as IVF except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope.
[bio23-s8.26.1] Because the sperm does not have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility.
[bio23-s8.26.2] ICSI is used for very low sperm count, sperm that cannot swim or cannot penetrate the egg, and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF.
[bio23-s8.26.3] ICSI is also used when sperm are taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle, for example after a vasectomy or a blockage.
[bio23-s8.26.4] ICSI is also used with frozen eggs, which are thawed before the sperm is injected.
[bio23-s8.27.b] A flow chart shows IVF and ICSI together, because they share every step except how the egg is fertilised.
[bio23-s8.27.0] Diagram: a flow chart of IVF with ICSI as a branch: stimulate ovaries → collect eggs → (IVF: mix eggs and sperm in a dish | ICSI: inject one sperm into each egg) → grow embryos for 2–5 days → transfer an embryo to the uterus → pregnancy test.
[bio23-s8.28.b] Discussing contraception means weighing benefits against drawbacks. The benefits range from the individual to the whole planet.
[bio23-s8.28.0] Contraception lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and to space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies.
[bio23-s8.28.1] It prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers.
[bio23-s8.28.2] Condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit.
[bio23-s8.28.3] Hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods, and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer.
[bio23-s8.28.4] At a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats. This is a sustainability link; there is more on this in The cross-cutting themes.
[bio23-s8.29.b] Contraception also has drawbacks, some medical, some personal, and one environmental.
[bio23-s8.29.0] Hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots.
[bio23-s8.29.1] No method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one.
[bio23-s8.29.2] User-dependent methods often fail in real life, because they must be used correctly every time.
[bio23-s8.29.3] Sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted.
[bio23-s8.29.4] Some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds.
[bio23-s8.29.5] Synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.
[bio23-s8.30.b] Treatments for infertility bring benefits that are both personal and medical. Each argument rests on the biology of the technique.
[bio23-s8.30.0] It allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress.
[bio23-s8.30.1] ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers.
[bio23-s8.30.2] Egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.30.3] Preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on.
[bio23-s8.30.4] Donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children.
[bio23-s8.30.5] Research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
[bio23-s8.31.b] Infertility treatments also raise health, cost and ethical concerns. Some are risks to mother and baby, others are questions of fairness and identity.
[bio23-s8.31.0] Stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother; this is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured.
[bio23-s8.31.1] Ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous.
[bio23-s8.31.2] Success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who cannot pay.
[bio23-s8.31.3] ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility, for example some changes on the Y chromosome, can be passed to a son.
[bio23-s8.31.4] Spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s8.31.5] Children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity.
[bio23-s8.31.6] Treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk, for example a higher chance of chromosome conditions.
[bio23-s8.32.b] Egg freezing gives women more control over timing but is not a guarantee. Weigh the benefits against the limits.
[bio23-s8.32.0] For: it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.32.1] For: it gives women more choice over when to have children.
[bio23-s8.32.2] Against: success is uncertain, and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages.
[bio23-s8.32.3] Against: it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs.
[bio23-s8.32.4] Against: it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio28-s1: Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism
[bio28-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a steady supply of oxygen and must get rid of the carbon dioxide it makes. The breathing system is the body's way of swapping these gases with the air.
[bio28-s1.0.0] Every cell respires aerobically (releases energy from food using oxygen), so every cell uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s1.0.1] The breathing system exchanges gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid, the fluid that bathes the cells): oxygen is taken into the blood, and carbon dioxide and some water vapour are removed.
[bio28-s1.0.2] Breathing, or ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. It is not respiration, which is the chemical release of energy inside cells, and gas exchange is not respiration either.
[bio28-s1.0.3] A large animal such as a human cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin, because its surface area is too small for its volume and the distances to its inner cells are too great.
[bio28-s1.0.4] So it needs a specialised exchange surface, the alveoli (tiny air sacs in the lungs), and a transport system, the blood, to carry gases to and from every cell.
[bio28-s1.1.b] The breathing tract is the pathway air follows from outside to the lungs. The first parts clean, warm and moisten the air and keep food out of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.0] Nose (nasal cavity): hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and microorganisms. The lining has a rich blood supply that warms the air, and the mucus moistens it, so the delicate alveoli do not dry out or chill.
[bio28-s1.1.1] Breathing through the mouth skips this filtering, warming and moistening, which is why nose breathing is better for the lungs.
[bio28-s1.1.2] Pharynx (throat): the passage behind the nose and mouth that is shared by air and food.
[bio28-s1.1.3] Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage (firm, flexible tissue) that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink pass into the oesophagus (the food tube) instead of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.4] Choking happens when food enters the trachea instead.
[bio28-s1.1.5] Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea. It contains the vocal cords, which vibrate as air passes over them, producing sound.
[bio28-s1.2.b] Below the larynx the airway divides again and again, like an upside-down tree, until it reaches the alveoli where gases actually cross into the blood.
[bio28-s1.2.0] Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation.
[bio28-s1.2.1] The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
[bio28-s1.2.2] Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium (a surface layer of cells carrying tiny hair-like cilia) with mucus-secreting goblet cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms.
[bio28-s1.2.3] The cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the body's first line of defence against infection.
[bio28-s1.2.4] Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one to each lung. They are also supported by cartilage.
[bio28-s1.2.5] Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow.
[bio28-s1.2.6] The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels). The alveoli are the site of gas exchange.
[bio28-s1.3.b] The lungs have no muscle of their own, so they depend on the chest wall and on the muscles around it. The pleural membranes are what link the lungs to the chest wall.
[bio28-s1.3.0] The two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (ribs, sternum and vertebrae) and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm.
[bio28-s1.3.1] Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move.
[bio28-s1.3.2] The fluid also makes the lungs cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
[bio28-s1.3.3] Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax.
[bio28-s1.3.4] Intercostal muscles: two sets, external and internal, lying between the ribs, which move the rib cage.
[bio28-s1.3.5] Diagram: the breathing system in a front view of the chest. Label the nasal cavity, pharynx, epiglottis, larynx, trachea with cartilage rings, two bronchi, bronchioles, lungs, pleural membranes, ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm and an outline of the heart. Add an enlarged inset of alveoli wrapped in capillaries.
[bio28-s1.4.b] Breathing in works by changing pressure. The muscles enlarge the thorax, the pressure in the lungs drops below the pressure outside, and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.0] Air always moves from a region of higher pressure to one of lower pressure. When the thorax volume increases, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure (the pressure of the air outside) and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.1] When the thorax volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs follow the chest wall because they have no muscle of their own.
[bio28-s1.4.2] Inhalation (inspiration) is an active process, which means it uses energy. 1. The brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles.
[bio28-s1.4.3] 2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards.
[bio28-s1.4.4] 3. The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and sternum upwards and outwards.
[bio28-s1.4.5] 4. The volume of the thorax increases.
[bio28-s1.4.6] 5. The lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.4.7] 6. Air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP (the cell's energy currency), so inhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.5.b] Breathing out at rest needs no muscle contraction at all: the body simply springs back. During exercise, however, extra muscles make exhalation active.
[bio28-s1.5.0] Exhalation (expiration) at rest is a passive process, which means it uses no energy. 1. The impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax.
[bio28-s1.5.1] 2. The diaphragm returns to its dome shape, pushed up by the abdominal organs, and the ribs move down and in under their own weight.
[bio28-s1.5.2] 3. The stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils, like a stretched elastic band springing back.
[bio28-s1.5.3] 4. The volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.5.4] 5. Air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used.
[bio28-s1.5.5] Forced exhalation, as in exercise, coughing, blowing or speaking loudly: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up.
[bio28-s1.5.6] This expels air faster and more completely, and because muscles contract, forced exhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.6.b] Putting the two halves of the breathing cycle side by side shows that every change in one is reversed in the other.
[bio28-s1.6.0] Diaphragm: it contracts and flattens in inhalation, but relaxes and domes upwards in exhalation at rest.
[bio28-s1.6.1] External intercostal muscles: they contract in inhalation but relax in exhalation. The ribs move up and out in inhalation, and down and in in exhalation.
[bio28-s1.6.2] Thorax volume increases in inhalation and decreases in exhalation. Lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure in inhalation and rises above it in exhalation, so air moves in, then out.
[bio28-s1.6.3] Inhalation is active, whereas exhalation at rest is passive.
[bio28-s1.6.4] The bell-jar model shows the volume and pressure principle: pulling the rubber sheet (the diaphragm) down inflates the balloons (the lungs). There is more on this in Models.
[bio28-s1.6.5] Diagram: two side-view sketches of the thorax, one at inhalation and one at exhalation, showing the diaphragm (flat or domed) and the ribs (raised or lowered), with arrows for rib movement and air flow. Label the diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, lungs and trachea.
[bio28-s1.7.b] Breathing continues while you sleep because the brain controls it automatically. The signal that adjusts it is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood.
[bio28-s1.7.0] Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain. It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
[bio28-s1.7.1] We can change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum (the thinking part of the brain), for example by holding our breath, speaking or singing. The automatic control takes over again afterwards.
[bio28-s1.7.2] The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises, as during exercise when muscles respire faster, the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.3] This removes more CO₂ and takes in more oxygen. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.4] An adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath, the tidal volume.
[bio28-s1.7.5] During hard exercise both the rate and the depth (the volume of air per breath) increase greatly.
[bio28-s1.8.b] Gas exchange is a simple physical process: oxygen and carbon dioxide each move down their own concentration gradient between the air in the alveolus and the blood in the capillary beside it.
[bio28-s1.8.0] Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used, so it is not active transport.
[bio28-s1.8.1] Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration than the deoxygenated blood arriving from the body in the pulmonary artery.
[bio28-s1.8.2] Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma (the liquid part of the blood), and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio28-s1.8.3] Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than the air in the alveoli. CO₂, carried mostly in the plasma, diffuses out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
[bio28-s1.8.4] The oxygenated blood leaves the lungs in the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio28-s1.8.5] The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air.
[bio28-s1.8.6] Blood flow also keeps the gradients steep, because it constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.
[bio28-s1.9.b] The alveoli are built so that diffusion is as fast as possible. Each feature either gives gases more room to cross, a shorter distance to travel, or a steeper gradient.
[bio28-s1.9.0] Very large total surface area: about 300 million alveoli in the two lungs give an exchange surface of roughly 70 m², so a large amount of gas can diffuse at once.
[bio28-s1.9.1] Very thin walls: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick and pressed close together, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio28-s1.9.2] Moist lining: gases dissolve in the moisture before they diffuse across the wall.
[bio28-s1.9.3] Dense capillary network: every alveolus is surrounded by capillaries with a continuous blood flow, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio28-s1.9.4] Narrow capillaries: they are just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, pressed against the wall. This shortens the distance further and slows the blood, giving time for exchange.
[bio28-s1.9.5] Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation, which helps push air out.
[bio28-s1.9.6] Diagram: a single alveolus with a capillary wrapped around it. Label alveolus wall (one cell thick), capillary wall (one cell thick), moist lining, red blood cell and plasma. Add arrows: O₂ into the blood, CO₂ into the alveolus, and blood flow from pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) to pulmonary vein (oxygenated).
[bio28-s1.10.b] The air we breathe out differs from the air we breathe in, because the body takes oxygen from it and adds carbon dioxide and water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.0] Inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with a variable amount of water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.1] Exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. It is saturated with water vapour and is warmer than inhaled air.
[bio28-s1.10.2] Nitrogen is not used by the body, so its proportion is the same in both.
[bio28-s1.10.3] Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths work in CPR (the emergency procedure that keeps a casualty's blood supplied with oxygen).
[bio28-s1.10.4] Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing that exhaled air contains more CO₂.
[bio28-s1.11.b] Damage to the breathing system shows why its structures matter: when cilia, alveoli or bronchioles stop working properly, gas exchange suffers.
[bio28-s1.11.0] Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production, so mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing (smoker's cough) and frequent chest infections.
[bio28-s1.11.1] Emphysema, mainly caused by smoking: if alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces, the surface area for gas exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless. This shows why the large surface area of the alveoli matters.
[bio28-s1.11.2] Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the amount of oxygen the blood can carry. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio28-s1.11.3] Asthma: the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts, and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that less air reaches the alveoli. Inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle.

CANDIDATES bio28-s2: CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing
[bio28-s2.0.b] Every living cell respires, so every cell keeps adding carbon dioxide to the blood. The body has to match its breathing to that supply, and it does so by monitoring CO₂ rather than oxygen.
[bio28-s2.0.0] Carbon dioxide is produced by every respiring cell. If it is not removed, it accumulates in the blood, where it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (carbonic acid, hydrogen ions and hydrogencarbonate ions).
[bio28-s2.0.1] The reaction is speeded up by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells. A rise in CO₂ therefore makes the blood more acidic, lowering its pH, because more hydrogen ions are released.
[bio28-s2.0.2] Blood pH is normally kept within a narrow range around 7.4, slightly alkaline. A fall in pH alters the shape of enzymes and other proteins and disturbs cell function, so the CO₂ level must be controlled. Breathing is the body's fastest way of adjusting it.
[bio28-s2.0.3] In a healthy person at sea level, the CO₂ level, not the oxygen level, is the main stimulus for breathing. The body is far more sensitive to a small rise in CO₂ than to a fall in O₂.
[bio28-s2.0.4] Oxygen receptors only strongly increase breathing when the oxygen level falls a lot, for example at high altitude. This is why the urge to breathe when you hold your breath comes from rising CO₂, not falling O₂.
[bio28-s2.1.b] A change in blood CO₂ has to be detected before breathing can respond. Special receptors in two sets of locations do this and report to the breathing centre in the brain.
[bio28-s2.1.0] A chemoreceptor is a receptor that detects the concentration of a chemical, here CO₂ and the hydrogen ions it produces.
[bio28-s2.1.1] Central chemoreceptors lie in the medulla oblongata (the lowest part of the brain, joining the spinal cord), close to the breathing centre. They are responsible for most of the response to a rise in CO₂.
[bio28-s2.1.2] CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the cerebrospinal fluid (the fluid surrounding the brain), where it forms carbonic acid and lowers the pH. The central receptors respond to this rise in hydrogen ions, so they detect CO₂ through its effect on pH.
[bio28-s2.1.3] Peripheral chemoreceptors lie in the carotid bodies, small structures in the wall of each carotid artery in the neck (the arteries that carry blood to the brain), and in the aortic bodies, in the wall of the arch of the aorta.
[bio28-s2.1.4] Peripheral chemoreceptors detect a rise in CO₂ (and H⁺) and also a large fall in O₂ in the arterial blood. They send impulses along sensory nerves to the breathing centre.
[bio28-s2.1.5] Diagram: outline of the head, neck and chest. Label the medulla oblongata (breathing centre and central chemoreceptors), carotid bodies on the carotid arteries, aortic bodies on the aortic arch, nerves to the breathing centre, and nerves from the breathing centre to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
[bio28-s2.2.b] Detection is only the start. The signal then passes through a chain from stimulus to response, which brings the CO₂ level back to normal.
[bio28-s2.2.0] 1. Stimulus: CO₂ in the blood rises, for example when muscles respire faster during exercise.
[bio28-s2.2.1] 2. Receptors: the central and peripheral chemoreceptors detect the rise in CO₂, through the fall in pH.
[bio28-s2.2.2] 3. Control centre: impulses reach the breathing centre in the medulla oblongata, which coordinates the response.
[bio28-s2.2.3] 4. Effectors (the muscles that carry out the response): the breathing centre sends impulses more often along motor nerves to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, and in heavy breathing to the abdominal muscles.
[bio28-s2.2.4] 5. Response: the muscles contract more strongly and more often, so breathing becomes deeper and faster.
[bio28-s2.2.5] 6. Return to normal: more CO₂ is exhaled from the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) and more oxygen is taken in, so the CO₂ level in the blood falls back towards normal and the pH rises.
[bio28-s2.2.6] 7. Reset: the receptors detect the return to normal, the breathing centre sends fewer impulses, and breathing returns to the resting rate.
[bio28-s2.3.b] The loop above is an example of negative feedback, the pattern the body uses to keep conditions steady. The same CO₂ signal also speeds up the heart so that the extra air is matched by faster blood flow.
[bio28-s2.3.0] This is negative feedback: the response, removing CO₂, cancels the change that caused it (the rise in CO₂), keeping the blood CO₂ level and pH near a set point.
[bio28-s2.3.1] It works in both directions: if CO₂ falls below normal, the breathing centre reduces the rate and depth of breathing, so CO₂ builds up again.
[bio28-s2.3.2] The cardiac centre in the medulla (the part of the brain that controls heart rate) responds to the same signals. A rise in CO₂ also increases the heart rate, so blood carries CO₂ to the lungs and oxygen to the muscles faster. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio28-s2.3.3] Diagram: a feedback loop flow chart. CO₂ rises, then chemoreceptors, breathing centre in the medulla, diaphragm and intercostal muscles, faster and deeper breathing, CO₂ falls, back to normal, with a loop arrow returning to the start. Draw a matching loop for CO₂ falling.
[bio28-s2.4.b] The same loop explains several everyday and exam situations. In each case, follow what happens to blood CO₂ and the signal it sends.
[bio28-s2.4.0] Exercise: muscles respire faster and release more CO₂ into the blood. The rise is detected and breathing rate and depth increase within seconds, removing CO₂ and supplying the extra oxygen.
[bio28-s2.4.1] Breathing stays fast for a time after exercise stops, while the extra CO₂ is removed and the oxygen debt from anaerobic respiration (lactic acid) is repaid. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s2.4.2] Holding the breath: CO₂ builds up in the blood and the signal from the chemoreceptors to the breathing centre becomes stronger and stronger. Eventually the automatic control overrides the voluntary control by the cerebrum, forcing a breath.
[bio28-s2.4.3] This is why a person cannot hold their breath until they lose consciousness at rest. The urge to breathe comes from rising CO₂, not from falling O₂.
[bio28-s2.4.4] Hyperventilation (deliberate fast, deep breathing) removes extra CO₂, so the CO₂ level falls below normal and the urge to breathe is reduced; the person may feel dizzy.
[bio28-s2.4.5] Before swimming underwater, hyperventilation is dangerous: the urge to breathe comes too late, so the oxygen level can fall far enough to cause blacking out before the swimmer feels the need to surface.
[bio28-s2.4.6] Breathing air with a raised CO₂ content, for example in a poorly ventilated, crowded room or when rebreathing air from a bag, makes breathing faster and deeper through the same loop.
[bio28-s2.4.7] Link to plants: carbon dioxide concentration is also a controlling factor in plants, where it acts on the stomata. That, and the comparison of the plant and human responses, is in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.

CANDIDATES bio29-s4: Blood
[bio29-s4.0.b] Blood is a tissue, like muscle or bone, but a liquid one. It is made of cells and cell fragments carried in a fluid, and each part has its own job.
[bio29-s4.0.0] Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
[bio29-s4.0.1] If blood, with a chemical added to stop it clotting, is spun in a centrifuge (a machine that spins samples so the heaviest parts sink), it separates into about 55% plasma, a straw-coloured liquid on top, and about 45% cells.
[bio29-s4.0.2] The cells form a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
[bio29-s4.0.3] Numbers in a healthy adult: red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. Red cells therefore greatly outnumber white cells, by several hundred to one.
[bio29-s4.0.4] All blood cells and platelets are made in the red bone marrow, found in spongy bone such as the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones. They come from stem cells (unspecialised cells that can become other cell types) that divide by mitosis.
[bio29-s4.0.5] Functions of blood: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature and water balance).
[bio29-s4.0.6] Diagram: a stained blood smear under a light microscope. Draw many red blood cells (pale centres, no nucleus), one or two white blood cells (larger, with a stained nucleus: a phagocyte with a lobed nucleus and a lymphocyte with a large round nucleus) and small platelets. Label each.
[bio29-s4.0.7] Diagram: draw a centrifuged tube labelled plasma, white cells and platelets layer, and red cells layer, with percentages.
[bio29-s4.1.b] Plasma is the liquid part of blood, and everything else in blood is carried in it or by it. Because it is mostly water, it can dissolve and transport a huge range of substances around the body.
[bio29-s4.1.0] Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it. Water is a good solvent, so many substances dissolve in it, and it also carries heat.
[bio29-s4.1.1] Dissolved substances transported in plasma: nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals); wastes (urea, from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; and salts, such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio29-s4.1.2] Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻). They form in red blood cells, by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻).
[bio29-s4.1.3] A small amount of carbon dioxide is simply dissolved in plasma, and some is carried by haemoglobin. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s4.1.4] Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors, for clotting; antibodies, for defence against pathogens; and albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
[bio29-s4.1.5] Plasma distributes heat from active organs, such as working muscles, around the body. Its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers (chemicals that resist changes in acidity) keep the blood pH near 7.4.
[bio29-s4.2.b] Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body. Almost every feature of their structure helps them do that one job well.
[bio29-s4.2.0] Red blood cells, or erythrocytes, are biconcave discs (round and thinner in the centre than at the edge), about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
[bio29-s4.2.1] The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface.
[bio29-s4.2.2] There is no nucleus, and no mitochondria, in the mature cell, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. The cell is also packed with haemoglobin, so each cell can carry a lot of oxygen.
[bio29-s4.2.3] The cell is small and flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. This shortens the distance oxygen must diffuse.
[bio29-s4.2.4] Because they have no mitochondria, red blood cells respire anaerobically (without oxygen), so they do not use up the oxygen they carry.
[bio29-s4.2.5] Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron, so each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is bright red.
[bio29-s4.2.6] In respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. The reaction is reversible. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio29-s4.3.b] Red blood cells wear out and must be constantly replaced. Problems with their number or with haemoglobin affect how much oxygen reaches the tissues.
[bio29-s4.3.0] Red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days. With no nucleus they cannot repair themselves, so they wear out.
[bio29-s4.3.1] Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen. The iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments, which are excreted in bile.
[bio29-s4.3.2] The kidney hormone EPO stimulates the production of red blood cells. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio29-s4.3.3] Anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin. A common cause is iron deficiency, because iron is needed to make haem.
[bio29-s4.3.4] Carbon monoxide, in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances, combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried and it can kill.
[bio29-s4.3.5] Sickle-cell anaemia is a further disorder of red blood cells, named here only.
[bio29-s4.4.b] White blood cells are the body's defenders against infection. They differ from red cells in several ways, which makes a good comparison to learn.
[bio29-s4.4.0] White blood cells, or leucocytes, defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, are larger and colourless (they have no haemoglobin), and are far fewer in number.
[bio29-s4.4.1] They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues, where infections often are.
[bio29-s4.4.2] There are two broad groups: phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells.
[bio29-s4.4.3] The number of white cells rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections. Leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells.
[bio29-s4.5.b] Platelets are not whole cells but fragments, and they are the first responders when a blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.5.0] Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus. They break off from large cells in the red bone marrow.
[bio29-s4.5.1] Platelets live about 7 to 10 days and start the clotting of blood.
[bio29-s4.5.2] Clotting matters because it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so that pathogens cannot enter. It is part of the first line of defence against infection.
[bio29-s4.6.b] Clotting is a chain of events in which one change triggers the next, ending in a mesh of protein threads that plugs the wound. Learn the stages in order.
[bio29-s4.6.0] 1. A blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.6.1] 2. Platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug.
[bio29-s4.6.2] 3. The platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors.
[bio29-s4.6.3] 4. A series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver with the help of vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin.
[bio29-s4.6.4] 5. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin.
[bio29-s4.6.5] 6. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot.
[bio29-s4.6.6] 7. At the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs. Calcium and vitamin K are both needed for clotting. Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing.
[bio29-s4.6.7] An unwanted clot inside a vessel, a thrombosis, can block an artery and cause a heart attack or stroke. Diagram: a flow chart of damage, platelets, clotting factors (with Ca²⁺ and vitamin K), prothrombin, thrombin, fibrinogen, fibrin mesh and clot. Add fibrin threads trapping red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.7.b] Your ABO blood group depends on molecules on the surface of your red blood cells. The plasma then carries antibodies matched to whichever molecules your own cells lack.
[bio29-s4.7.0] A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person's red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response. In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
[bio29-s4.7.1] The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen or antigens that a person's own red cells lack. This stops the body attacking its own cells.
[bio29-s4.7.2] Group A has antigen A on the red cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.3] Group AB has both antigens and no antibodies. Group O has no antigens and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.4] Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody, for example A cells meeting anti-A, the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps.
[bio29-s4.7.5] In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed, or burst. This can cause kidney failure and death.
[bio29-s4.7.6] Diagram: four red blood cells, one for each group, showing the antigens on the membrane (A, B, both, none), with the antibodies in the plasma written beside each. Add a sketch of agglutination, with antibodies linking red cells into a clump.
[bio29-s4.8.b] Transfusion works only if the donor's red cells do not meet an antibody in the recipient's plasma that matches their antigens. The rule follows from the ABO antigens and antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.0] What matters is the donor's red-cell antigens and the recipient's antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen against which their own plasma has antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.1] Group O red cells carry no antigens, so they can be given to anyone, and group O is called the universal donor. This applies to red cells: group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B.
[bio29-s4.8.2] Group AB people have no antibodies, so they can receive red cells of any ABO group, and group AB is the universal recipient. In practice, hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
[bio29-s4.8.3] Compatibility: group O can receive O only; group A can receive A or O; group B can receive B or O; group AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
[bio29-s4.8.4] Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and with anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only means group A; clumping with anti-B only means group B; clumping with both means AB; clumping with neither means O.
[bio29-s4.8.5] A third test, with anti-D, tests the Rhesus group.
[bio29-s4.9.b] The Rhesus factor is a second red-cell antigen system, on top of ABO. Unlike the ABO antibodies, the Rhesus antibody has to be learned by the immune system first.
[bio29-s4.9.0] The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+), and those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−).
[bio29-s4.9.1] A person's full blood group combines both systems, for example O negative or A positive.
[bio29-s4.9.2] Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present. A Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood, for example through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy.
[bio29-s4.9.3] A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
[bio29-s4.10.b] A Rhesus negative mother carrying a Rhesus positive baby can become sensitised to her baby's blood, and that may endanger a later baby. The problem is called haemolytic disease of the newborn, and it can be prevented.
[bio29-s4.10.0] 1. A Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby, inherited from a Rh+ father.
[bio29-s4.10.1] 2. Normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth, or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury, some fetal red cells enter the mother's blood.
[bio29-s4.10.2] 3. Her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells, and she is now sensitised. The first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made.
[bio29-s4.10.3] 4. In a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.10.4] 5. The baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage or death.
[bio29-s4.10.5] Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies, anti-D immunoglobulin, during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby, or after a miscarriage or injury.
[bio29-s4.10.6] These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells. The injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks.
[bio29-s4.10.7] This is artificial passive immunity (antibodies given ready-made, not made by the body). Every pregnant woman's blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason.
[bio29-s4.11.b] Knowing a person's blood group saves lives in several ways, from transfusions to pregnancy care. Each use below comes from knowing the groups.
[bio29-s4.11.0] Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient's ABO and Rhesus groups are tested, and the donor blood is cross-matched, meaning it is mixed with the patient's plasma to check that no clumping occurs.
[bio29-s4.11.1] This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and allows transfusions in surgery, after accidents, in childbirth, for anaemia and in cancer treatment.
[bio29-s4.11.2] Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
[bio29-s4.11.3] Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
[bio29-s4.11.4] Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks. Donors of all groups are needed, because blood can be stored only for a limited time.
[bio29-s4.11.5] In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service, and donated blood is screened for infections.
[bio29-s4.11.6] Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group and tissue type to reduce the chance of rejection.
[bio29-s4.11.7] Nature of Science: before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, which made safe transfusion possible.
[bio29-s4.11.8] In 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after the rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.

CANDIDATES bio8-s5: Vitamins
[bio8-s5.0.b] Your body needs a handful of chemicals in tiny amounts that it cannot build for itself. These are the vitamins, and without them key processes stop working properly.
[bio8-s5.0.0] Vitamins are organic compounds (carbon-based molecules made by living things) needed in very small amounts for normal metabolism, growth and health. The body cannot make them, or cannot make enough, so they must come from the diet.
[bio8-s5.0.1] Vitamins supply no energy and are not used to build tissues in bulk. This is what separates them from carbohydrates, lipids and proteins, which are needed in large amounts.
[bio8-s5.0.2] Many vitamins act as coenzymes (helper molecules that enzymes need) or help enzymes work. For example, the B vitamins help release energy from food in respiration.
[bio8-s5.0.3] Some vitamins are needed to make particular substances, such as vitamin C for collagen (a structural protein). Others act as antioxidants that protect cells from damage (vitamins C and E).
[bio8-s5.0.4] Vitamin D controls the absorption of minerals: it controls how much calcium is taken in from food.
[bio8-s5.0.5] A lack of a vitamin causes a deficiency disease with characteristic symptoms. The disease is cured or prevented by supplying the vitamin.
[bio8-s5.0.6] Vitamins are grouped by what they dissolve in. Water-soluble: vitamin C and the B group. Fat-soluble: vitamins A, D, E and K.
[bio8-s5.1.b] Whether a vitamin dissolves in water or in fat decides how it is absorbed, carried, stored and lost, and so how often you must eat it and whether it can build up to harmful levels. Compare the two groups point by point.
[bio8-s5.1.0] Absorption: water-soluble vitamins dissolve in the watery contents of the gut and are absorbed directly into the blood. Fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in dietary fat and are absorbed along with it.
[bio8-s5.1.1] So a very low-fat diet, or a condition that prevents fat absorption, can cause a deficiency of vitamin A, D, E or K, because these vitamins cannot be absorbed without fat.
[bio8-s5.1.2] Transport: water-soluble vitamins travel dissolved in the blood plasma (the watery liquid part of blood). Fat-soluble vitamins are carried with lipids.
[bio8-s5.1.3] Storage of water-soluble vitamins: any excess is excreted by the kidneys in urine and most are not stored. They must therefore be eaten regularly, ideally daily, and a deficiency develops within weeks or months.
[bio8-s5.1.4] Storage of fat-soluble vitamins: they are stored in the liver and fatty tissue, so the body can draw on these reserves and they need not be eaten every day.
[bio8-s5.1.5] Toxicity (harmfulness): because excess water-soluble vitamins are excreted, overdose is rare. Fat-soluble vitamins can build up in the liver to harmful levels if large supplements are taken.
[bio8-s5.1.6] Too much vitamin A is toxic, which is why pregnant women are advised not to eat liver or take vitamin A supplements.
[bio8-s5.1.7] Cooking: water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) out of food into the cooking water, and vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and by long storage. Vegetables lose vitamin C when boiled for a long time; steaming, microwaving or eating them raw keeps more. Fat-soluble vitamins are not lost into cooking water.
[bio8-s5.2.b] Vitamin C is the standard example of a water-soluble vitamin. Its main job is to help build collagen, so a shortage weakens the body's connective tissue.
[bio8-s5.2.0] Vitamin C, also called ascorbic acid, is water-soluble.
[bio8-s5.2.1] Roles: it is needed to make collagen, the protein of connective tissue in skin, gums, blood vessel walls, bones and scar tissue. It also helps wounds heal, acts as an antioxidant, helps the gut absorb iron from plant foods, and supports the immune system.
[bio8-s5.2.2] Sources: citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruit), kiwi fruit, strawberries, blackcurrants, peppers, broccoli, tomatoes and potatoes. It is best eaten fresh and raw, because it leaches into cooking water and is destroyed by heat and long storage.
[bio8-s5.2.3] Deficiency disease: scurvy. Without vitamin C, collagen is not made properly, so connective tissue weakens. This is why the symptoms involve gums, blood vessels and wounds.
[bio8-s5.2.4] Scurvy symptoms: swollen, bleeding gums and loose teeth; easy bruising and bleeding under the skin; wounds that heal slowly or reopen; tiredness and aching joints.
[bio8-s5.2.5] Untreated, scurvy can be fatal. It is cured quickly by vitamin C.
[bio8-s5.2.6] History (Health and Nature of Science link): scurvy killed many sailors on long voyages without fresh food. In 1747 James Lind, a Scottish naval surgeon, gave different treatments to groups of sailors with scurvy and found that those given oranges and lemons recovered. This was an early controlled trial.
[bio8-s5.3.b] Vitamin D is the standard example of a fat-soluble vitamin. Its main job is to help the gut take in calcium, which bones and teeth need to harden.
[bio8-s5.3.0] Vitamin D, also called calciferol, is fat-soluble.
[bio8-s5.3.1] Role: it increases the absorption of calcium, and phosphate, from food in the small intestine. It is therefore needed for the formation and hardening of bones and teeth. It also helps muscle function and the immune system.
[bio8-s5.3.2] Sources: the skin makes vitamin D when it is exposed to ultraviolet light in sunlight. Dietary sources are oily fish (e.g. salmon, mackerel and sardines), fish-liver oils such as cod-liver oil, egg yolk and liver, and fortified foods such as some milks, spreads and breakfast cereals.
[bio8-s5.3.3] Deficiency disease: rickets in children. Bones do not harden, so they stay soft and weak. The leg bones bend under the body's weight, giving bowed legs; growth is poor and the bones are painful.
[bio8-s5.3.4] In adults the equivalent condition is osteomalacia, which means softening of the bones. Low vitamin D also raises the risk of osteoporosis (thinning, brittle bones) in later life.
[bio8-s5.3.5] People at risk are those who get little sunlight: people living at northern latitudes like Ireland, where winter sunlight is too weak for the skin to make vitamin D, and people who stay indoors or cover their skin.

[bio8-s5.4.b] You may choose other vitamins to answer questions on the role, source and deficiency of a vitamin. These three are fat-soluble, so they are stored in the body and absorbed with dietary fat.
[bio8-s5.4.0] Vitamin A (retinol) is fat-soluble and is needed for vision in dim light and for healthy skin and linings. Deficiency causes night blindness.
[bio8-s5.4.1] Vitamin A sources: liver, dairy produce, eggs and oily fish. Carrots and other orange and dark green vegetables contain beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A.
[bio8-s5.4.2] Vitamin K is fat-soluble and is needed for blood clotting. Sources: green leafy vegetables. It is also made by bacteria in the large intestine.
[bio8-s5.4.3] Newborn babies have little vitamin K, so they are given it soon after birth to prevent bleeding.
[bio8-s5.4.4] Vitamin E is fat-soluble and is an antioxidant that protects cell membranes from damage. Sources: vegetable oils, nuts and seeds. No named deficiency disease is needed.
[bio8-s5.5.b] The B-group vitamins are water-soluble, so they are not stored well and must be eaten often. Several have a named deficiency disease and two are needed for healthy red blood cells.
[bio8-s5.5.0] The B-group vitamins are water-soluble and help enzymes release energy from food. Sources: wholegrain cereals, meat, eggs, dairy produce and beans.
[bio8-s5.5.1] Vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency causes beriberi; vitamin B3 (niacin) deficiency causes pellagra.
[bio8-s5.5.2] Folic acid (folate, a B vitamin) and vitamin B12 are needed to make red blood cells. A lack of either causes a form of anaemia (too few healthy red blood cells).
[bio8-s5.5.3] Vitamin B12 is found naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or supplements.
[bio8-s5.5.4] Women are advised to take folic acid before and in early pregnancy, as it reduces the risk of neural tube defects (faults in the development of the baby's spine and brain) such as spina bifida in the baby.
[bio8-s5.6.b] Some groups of people struggle to get enough of a vitamin from their normal diet. Two measures are used to protect them.
[bio8-s5.6.0] Food fortification, adding vitamins to foods such as breakfast cereals and milk, and taking supplements are used to prevent deficiency diseases in groups at risk. An example is the vitamin D supplement advised for babies in their first year.
