CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.

CANDIDATES bio24-s2: Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants
[bio24-s2.0.b] A flower's structure follows from how its pollen travels. Insects must be lured in and rewarded, so insect-pollinated flowers advertise; the wind needs no persuading, so wind-pollinated flowers drop the advertising. Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second, and the flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio24-s2.0.0] Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides (markings that lead the insect towards the nectar), to attract insects.
[bio24-s2.0.1] Wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, because there is no animal to attract and large petals would block pollen in the wind.
[bio24-s2.0.2] Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries (the glands that produce nectar, a sugary liquid), so the insect is rewarded for visiting.
[bio24-s2.0.3] Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries, because there is no visitor to reward.
[bio24-s2.0.4] Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters, so that insects can spot them.
[bio24-s2.0.5] Wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins (dangling spikes of tiny flowers) or spikes held high above the leaves, where the wind can reach them.
[bio24-s2.0.6] Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
[bio24-s2.0.7] Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
[bio24-s2.1.b] The anther (the pollen-producing part of the stamen, held up on a stalk called the filament) and the stigma (the sticky or feathery top of the carpel that receives pollen) are positioned to suit the carrier. Insects touch the flower's parts; the wind blows past them.
[bio24-s2.1.0] Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them, so pollen rubs onto its body.
[bio24-s2.1.1] Wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
[bio24-s2.1.2] Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them and pollen sticks to them.
[bio24-s2.1.3] Wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s2.1.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of a buttercup or wallflower half-flower (large petal, nectary, anther inside, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas, anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
[bio24-s2.2.b] The pollen itself also differs. Insects deliver pollen accurately, whereas the wind scatters it at random, and the grains are built for the different journeys.
[bio24-s2.2.0] Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower.
[bio24-s2.2.1] Wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species, so many grains are needed for a few to land.
[bio24-s2.2.2] Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs.
[bio24-s2.2.3] Wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump together.
[bio24-s2.2.4] Diagram: an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.
[bio24-s2.3.b] This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.
[bio24-s2.3.0] Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
[bio24-s2.3.1] Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
[bio24-s2.3.2] What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features (recorded in words) are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface.
[bio24-s2.3.3] Quantitative features (measured as numbers) are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
[bio24-s2.3.4] Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen, so every flower is at the same stage. Examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements, to reduce the effect of one unusual flower.
[bio24-s2.3.5] Measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification, so the two types can be compared fairly.
[bio24-s2.3.6] Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.3.7] Also needed: microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.
[bio24-s2.4.b] The method moves from the whole flower, to its separated parts, to the pollen under the microscope, and repeats everything for the second flower type.
[bio24-s2.4.0] 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
[bio24-s2.4.1] 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
[bio24-s2.4.2] 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
[bio24-s2.4.3] 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
[bio24-s2.4.4] 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
[bio24-s2.4.5] 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
[bio24-s2.4.6] 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power.
[bio24-s2.4.7] 8. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
[bio24-s2.5.b] Scalpels, pollen, some plants and glass slides each carry a risk, so the practical needs a precaution for each.
[bio24-s2.5.0] Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely, so nobody is cut.
[bio24-s2.5.1] Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
[bio24-s2.5.2] Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
[bio24-s2.5.3] Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
[bio24-s2.5.4] Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.
[bio24-s2.6.b] Recording results in a table puts the two flowers side by side, so each difference can be seen and then explained.
[bio24-s2.6.0] Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
[bio24-s2.6.1] Expected primary data (collected by you) for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.6.2] Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.7.b] A conclusion links what was seen to why it matters for pollination, and secondary data add evidence the class could not collect itself.
[bio24-s2.7.0] Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations above. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio24-s2.7.1] Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen.
[bio24-s2.7.2] Other secondary data: published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; and labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
[bio24-s2.7.3] Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio24-s2.8.b] Every practical has weaknesses. Each limitation below has a matching improvement, and you should be able to give both.
[bio24-s2.8.0] Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
[bio24-s2.8.1] Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
[bio24-s2.8.2] Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.8.3] Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes.
[bio24-s2.8.4] The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
[bio24-s2.8.5] Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective, because different people judge differently; have two people record them.
[bio24-s2.8.6] Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio7-s2: Microscopy
[bio7-s2.0.b] Microscopes do two different jobs: they make things look bigger, and they let you see fine detail. Students often mix these up, but only the second one limits what a light microscope can show.
[bio7-s2.0.0] Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the real object, so a ×100 image is 100 times the real size.
[bio7-s2.0.1] Resolution (also called resolving power) is the ability to show two points that are close together as separate points. It decides how much detail can be seen.
[bio7-s2.0.2] Magnifying an image beyond the resolution limit only makes it bigger and blurrier, not more detailed, because the two close points have already merged into one blur.
[bio7-s2.0.3] Total magnification of a light microscope = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification. For example, a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.
[bio7-s2.1.b] The light (optical) microscope passes light through a thin specimen and uses glass lenses to enlarge the image. These parts form the image and bring it into focus.
[bio7-s2.1.0] Eyepiece (ocular) lens: the lens you look through. It magnifies the image from the objective, usually ×10.
[bio7-s2.1.1] Objective lenses: mounted on a revolving nosepiece so they can be swapped. They are usually ×4 (scanning), ×10 (low power) and ×40 (high power), sometimes ×100 (oil immersion). They produce the first magnified image.
[bio7-s2.1.2] Stage with clips: the platform that holds the slide in place over the hole through which light passes.
[bio7-s2.1.3] Coarse focus knob: moves the stage (or tube) a long way quickly, so it is used for focusing at low power only.
[bio7-s2.1.4] Fine focus knob: moves the stage or tube a small distance to give a sharp image. It is the only focus used at high power.
[bio7-s2.2.b] Light has to reach the specimen in the right amount and direction, and the instrument needs a stable frame. These parts do that.
[bio7-s2.2.0] Light source: a built-in lamp, or a mirror that reflects light up through the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.1] Condenser: a lens under the stage that focuses light onto the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.2] Diaphragm (iris): adjusts the amount of light reaching the specimen. Less light often improves contrast for pale, unstained cells, which are nearly transparent and look washed out in bright light.
[bio7-s2.2.3] Arm and base: support the microscope. The arm is used for carrying it.
[bio7-s2.2.4] Diagram: a light microscope labelled eyepiece, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source (or mirror), coarse focus knob, fine focus knob, arm and base.
[bio7-s2.3.b] The method is built to protect the slide and the lenses. You always start on the lowest power, which has the widest view and the most room between lens and slide.
[bio7-s2.3.0] 1. Carry the microscope with one hand on the arm and the other under the base, so its weight is supported.
[bio7-s2.3.1] 2. Switch on the light and turn the lowest-power objective into place.
[bio7-s2.3.2] 3. Clip the slide on the stage with the specimen over the hole, so light can pass through it.
[bio7-s2.3.3] 4. Looking from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide without touching it. Watching from the side stops you grinding the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.3.4] 5. Looking through the eyepiece, focus by moving the objective and slide apart until the image is clear.
[bio7-s2.3.5] 6. Use the fine focus to sharpen the image, and the diaphragm to adjust the light.
[bio7-s2.3.6] 7. Move the part to be studied to the centre of the field of view (the circle of specimen you can see), so it stays in view when you increase magnification.
[bio7-s2.3.7] 8. Turn the next objective into place and use only the fine focus.
[bio7-s2.3.8] 9. Record the total magnification with every drawing.
[bio7-s2.4.b] The image behaves in predictable ways as you change objective, and one safety rule matters for your eyes.
[bio7-s2.4.0] The image is inverted, meaning upside down and reversed left to right. This means moving the slide to the left moves the image to the right.
[bio7-s2.4.1] At higher magnification the field of view is smaller and the image is dimmer, so more light is needed.
[bio7-s2.4.2] At high power the objective is very close to the slide, which is why the coarse focus must not be used: it could crash the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.4.3] Never use a mirror to reflect direct sunlight into the microscope, as it can damage the eye.
[bio7-s2.5.b] To view a specimen with light passing through it, you mount it on a glass slide. The technique below is the general method that any slide uses.
[bio7-s2.5.0] A wet mount: the specimen is placed in a drop of water or stain on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip (a thin square of glass).
[bio7-s2.5.1] The specimen must be very thin, ideally one layer of cells thick, so that light can pass through it and the cells do not overlap.
[bio7-s2.5.2] Stains add contrast to transparent structures by colouring some parts more than others. Iodine solution is used for plant cells: it stains the nucleus yellow-brown and turns starch blue-black. Methylene blue is used for animal cells: it stains the nucleus dark blue.
[bio7-s2.5.3] Lowering the coverslip: touch one edge of the coverslip to the liquid at an angle of about 45° and lower it slowly with a mounted needle. This pushes air out ahead of it and avoids air bubbles, which appear as black-edged circles and can hide cells.
[bio7-s2.5.4] The coverslip keeps the specimen flat, stops it drying out, and protects the objective lens from the liquid. Excess liquid is removed by touching filter paper or a paper towel to the edge.
[bio7-s2.5.5] Stain can also be added after the coverslip is on. Put a drop at one edge and draw it under the coverslip with filter paper held at the opposite edge (irrigation). There is more on the full procedures in Investigation: plant and animal cells.
[bio7-s2.6.b] A light microscope cannot resolve anything much smaller than 0.2 µm. Electron microscopes get round this by using electrons instead of light, so they show far finer detail.
[bio7-s2.6.0] An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by electromagnets instead of glass lenses. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, so resolution is far better.
[bio7-s2.6.1] The specimen is in a vacuum, because air would scatter the electrons, so it must be dead. The image is viewed on a screen and recorded digitally. It is black and white, and colour is added afterwards (false colour).
[bio7-s2.6.2] Transmission electron microscope (TEM): electrons pass through an extremely thin, stained section of a specimen. It gives a flat (2D) image of internal structure at the highest magnifications, e.g. mitochondria, ribosomes, nuclear pores and viruses.
[bio7-s2.6.3] Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a beam of electrons scans across the surface of a specimen coated with a very thin layer of metal, e.g. gold. Electrons bouncing off it form an image. It gives a 3D image of surfaces, e.g. the surface of cells, pollen grains and insects.
[bio7-s2.6.4] The SEM has lower resolution than the TEM and shows no internal structure unless the specimen is broken open.
[bio7-s2.6.5] The first electron microscope was built in Germany in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska.
[bio7-s2.7.b] The two types differ in how they work, what they can resolve and what they demand of the specimen. Compare them point by point.
[bio7-s2.7.0] Radiation: light for the light microscope, electrons for the electron microscope. Lenses: glass for the light microscope, electromagnets for the electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.1] Maximum useful magnification: about ×1000–1500 for the light microscope, compared with hundreds of thousands of times or more for electron microscopes.
[bio7-s2.7.2] Resolution: about 0.2 µm (200 nm) for the light microscope, compared with less than 1 nm for the TEM, roughly a thousand times better.
[bio7-s2.7.3] So the light microscope shows the nucleus, cell wall, vacuole and chloroplasts. Ribosomes, nuclear pores, the inner structure of mitochondria and viruses are too small and need an electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.4] Specimens: the light microscope can view living or dead specimens, with colour visible. The electron microscope needs dead specimens in a vacuum, and its images are black and white.
[bio7-s2.7.5] Preparation and cost: light microscopes are simple, quick, cheap and portable. Electron microscopes need complex preparation (thin sectioning, metal coating) that can create artefacts, structures that are not really in the living cell. They are very expensive, large, and need trained operators.
[bio7-s2.8.b] Microscopy has a long history and is still developing. Newer techniques tag, scan or freeze specimens to reveal what older methods could not.
[bio7-s2.8.0] Robert Hooke described and named “cells” from thin slices of cork in his book Micrographia (1665).
[bio7-s2.8.1] Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using his own single-lens microscopes, was the first to see and describe bacteria and other single-celled organisms, in the 1670s.
[bio7-s2.8.2] Fluorescence microscopy: specific molecules are tagged with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins that glow when lit with a particular colour of light. This shows exactly where a protein is in a living cell.
[bio7-s2.8.3] Green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a jellyfish, earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.
[bio7-s2.8.4] Confocal laser scanning microscopy: a laser scans one thin plane of the specimen at a time, giving sharp images that a computer combines into a 3D model.
[bio7-s2.8.5] Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy overcomes the light microscope’s normal resolution limit. It earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William Moerner.
[bio7-s2.8.6] Cryo-electron microscopy: samples are flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice instead of being dried and stained, so molecules keep their natural shape. It shows the structure of proteins and viruses in near-atomic detail.
[bio7-s2.8.7] Cryo-electron microscopy earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson.
[bio7-s2.8.8] Cryo-electron microscopy was used to show the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which helped vaccine design.
[bio7-s2.9.b] Microscopes are everyday tools in medicine, research and forensics, and modern imaging adds automation.
[bio7-s2.9.0] Uses in health and technology: examining blood smears and biopsies to diagnose disease, including spotting cancer cells; identifying pathogens; research into how cells and viruses work; and forensic science, e.g. comparing hairs and fibres.
[bio7-s2.9.1] Digital cameras and computer image analysis now allow images to be measured, shared and analysed automatically.

CANDIDATES bio7-s4: Investigation: plant and animal cells
[bio7-s4.0.b] This investigation compares plant cells with animal cells. You gather first-hand (primary) data with a light microscope, then add second-hand (secondary) data from scanning electron microscope images. A fair comparison needs a clear aim, a prediction and tight control of variables.
[bio7-s4.0.0] Aim: to observe and compare the structures of plant cells (onion epidermis, the thin outer skin layer) and animal cells (human cheek cells) using a light microscope, and to use scanning electron microscope (SEM) images to see structures the light microscope cannot show.
[bio7-s4.0.1] Hypothesis (a testable prediction): plant cells will show a cell wall and a large vacuole, which animal cells lack. Both will show a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus.
[bio7-s4.0.2] Independent variable (the one thing you change): the type of cell, plant or animal.
[bio7-s4.0.3] Dependent variable (what you observe or measure): the structures observed, whether present or absent, and their shape and arrangement.
[bio7-s4.0.4] Controlled variables (kept the same so the comparison is fair): the same microscope and total magnification (×100 and ×400), the same light setting, a fresh thin specimen one cell layer thick, and the same volume of stain and staining time.
[bio7-s4.0.5] Control: an unstained wet mount (specimen in a drop of water) of each cell type, viewed in the same way. It shows what the stain adds, meaning which structures become visible, and that the structures seen are in the cells and not produced by the stain.
[bio7-s4.1.b] You need a short list of apparatus, and several of the items carry real hazards: sharp blades, stains, thin glass and body cells. Good practice is part of the method, so know the precaution for each hazard.
[bio7-s4.1.0] Equipment: light microscope; clean glass slides and coverslips; onion; scalpel or knife and white tile; forceps; mounted needle; dropper (pipette); filter paper or paper towel; iodine solution; methylene blue stain; sterile cotton buds; beaker of disinfectant for used buds; distilled water; gloves and safety glasses; pencil and paper for drawings.
[bio7-s4.1.1] Use only your own cheek cells, with a new sterile cotton bud. Put used buds, and slides after use, into disinfectant to prevent spreading infection, and wash hands afterwards.
[bio7-s4.1.2] Iodine solution and methylene blue stain skin and clothes and can irritate eyes, so wear gloves and safety glasses and wipe up spills.
[bio7-s4.1.3] Cut away from your fingers on a white tile with the scalpel, and carry scalpels pointing down so the blade cannot injure anyone if you trip.
[bio7-s4.1.4] Hold slides and coverslips by their edges. Coverslips are very thin and break easily, so dispose of broken glass in the glass bin, not the ordinary bin, where it could cut someone.
[bio7-s4.1.5] Keep water away from the microscope's electric lamp and cable to avoid electric shock. Never point a microscope mirror at direct sunlight, as this can damage the eye.
[bio7-s4.2.b] Onion epidermis peels off in a layer only one cell thick, so light passes through it and the cells can be seen clearly. The steps below prepare a stained slide and an unstained control. There is more on lowering the coverslip and focusing in Microscopy.
[bio7-s4.2.0] 1. Place a few drops of water in the centre of a clean slide.
[bio7-s4.2.1] 2. Cut an onion into quarters and remove one fleshy scale leaf.
[bio7-s4.2.2] 3. Snap the scale leaf and use forceps to peel the thin, transparent epidermis from its inner (concave) surface.
[bio7-s4.2.3] 4. Cut a piece about 1 cm square and lay it flat in the water, unfolding it with the mounted needle so there are no folds or overlaps.
[bio7-s4.2.4] 5. Add one or two drops of iodine solution and leave it for a few minutes to stain.
[bio7-s4.2.5] 6. Lower a coverslip at 45° with the mounted needle, which avoids trapping air bubbles, then blot excess liquid from the edges with filter paper.
[bio7-s4.2.6] 7. Prepare an unstained slide the same way, using water only, as the control.
[bio7-s4.2.7] 8. Examine under low power (×100), then high power (×400). Make a labelled pencil drawing of a few cells and record the total magnification.
[bio7-s4.3.b] Cheek cells are collected by scraping the lining of your mouth, then stained so they show up. Because they come from a person, hygiene steps are built into the method. There is also an alternative for schools that do not use cheek cells.
[bio7-s4.3.0] 1. Gently rub the inside of your own cheek with a clean, sterile cotton bud.
[bio7-s4.3.1] 2. Smear the bud across the centre of a clean slide in a thin layer.
[bio7-s4.3.2] 3. Immediately put the used bud into the beaker of disinfectant to prevent spreading infection.
[bio7-s4.3.3] 4. Add one or two drops of methylene blue and leave for one to two minutes.
[bio7-s4.3.4] 5. Lower a coverslip at 45° and blot the excess with filter paper.
[bio7-s4.3.5] 6. Prepare an unstained slide with a drop of water, as the control.
[bio7-s4.3.6] 7. Examine under low power, then high power. Look for flat, separate cells, avoiding clumps, which are hard to interpret. Make a labelled pencil drawing and record the magnification.
[bio7-s4.3.7] Alternative where a school does not use cheek cells: a prepared (commercial) slide of human cheek cells or another animal tissue, examined in the same way.
[bio7-s4.4.b] Onion bulb cells have no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), so this optional extension uses a leaf instead.
[bio7-s4.4.0] Mount a single leaf of a moss or of Elodea (Canadian pondweed) in a drop of water, without stain. These leaves are only one or two cells thick, so light passes through them.
[bio7-s4.4.1] The green chloroplasts are visible without any stain, which onion bulb cells do not have.
[bio7-s4.5.b] The stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm stand out, and the shape of the cells tells you whether they are plant or animal. You should be able to describe what each slide looks like and explain why.
[bio7-s4.5.0] Onion epidermis: cells are regular, brick-shaped or rectangular, and fitted closely together in rows. Each cell is outlined by a clear, rigid cell wall.
[bio7-s4.5.1] In onion cells the nucleus is stained yellow-brown by iodine and is often seen near the edge of the cell. The cytoplasm stains pale yellow and forms a thin layer pushed to the edge by a large central vacuole (a fluid-filled sac), which looks clear.
[bio7-s4.5.2] Onion cells have no chloroplasts, because the bulb grows underground and does not photosynthesise.
[bio7-s4.5.3] Cheek cells: cells are flat and irregular in shape, rounded or many-sided, lying singly or in small overlapping groups. Each has a cell membrane but no cell wall.
[bio7-s4.5.4] In cheek cells the nucleus is small, central and stained dark blue, and the cytoplasm stains pale blue. There is no large vacuole and no chloroplasts.
[bio7-s4.5.5] Unstained controls: the cells are almost transparent. The onion cell walls are faintly visible but the nucleus is hard to see, and the cheek cells are barely visible. So stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm visible by adding contrast.
[bio7-s4.5.6] Structures the light microscope cannot show: ribosomes, the internal detail of mitochondria, and nuclear pores. Mitochondria are too small to see clearly at ×400.
[bio7-s4.5.7] Diagram: pencil drawings of (i) a few onion epidermis cells labelled cell wall, cell membrane (pressed against the wall), cytoplasm, vacuole and nucleus, and (ii) two or three cheek cells labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus, each with the total magnification (e.g. ×400) written beside it.
[bio7-s4.6.b] Secondary data is information collected by someone else, here scanning electron microscope (SEM) images. They reveal detail the light microscope cannot, but they have limits of their own. You also need to be able to work out real cell size from an image.
[bio7-s4.6.0] Sources: published SEM images (micrographs) of onion epidermis, of cheek (epithelial) cells, and of other cells, from textbooks or reputable scientific image libraries. Each has a scale bar or magnification. Record the source of each image so others can check it.
[bio7-s4.6.1] What SEM images add: a three-dimensional view of the cell surface, at much higher magnification and resolution (the ability to separate fine detail) than the light microscope.
[bio7-s4.6.2] For example, SEM images show the regular, box-like shape of the onion cells and the outlines of their walls, and the flat, thin, overlapping shape of cheek cells and the texture of their surfaces.
[bio7-s4.6.3] Limits of SEM images: they show surfaces only, not internal organelles, which need TEM (transmission electron microscope) images. They show dead, metal-coated cells, so preparation can create artefacts (features that are not really in the living cell). Any colour is false colour added afterwards.
[bio7-s4.6.4] Measuring size from an image: actual size = image size ÷ magnification. Measure the image with a ruler in mm and convert to µm (1 mm = 1000 µm).
[bio7-s4.6.5] Worked example: an onion cell measures 40 mm long on an image magnified ×200. 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2 mm = 200 µm.
[bio7-s4.6.6] With a scale bar, measure the bar, then calculate: actual size = (image size of the cell ÷ image length of the bar) × the length the bar represents.
[bio7-s4.6.7] Comparing the data: the light microscope shows living-cell features in colour (with stain) and at low cost. The SEM confirms the shapes and surfaces in 3D at much greater detail. Using both gives a fuller and more reliable picture than either alone.
[bio7-s4.7.b] Your results can now be compared with the hypothesis. The differences between the two cell types fit what each cell does, so you should be able to link structure to function.
[bio7-s4.7.0] Both cell types have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus, so both are eukaryotic (their DNA is enclosed in a nucleus).
[bio7-s4.7.1] The onion cells have a cell wall, which gives their fixed, regular shape and support, and a large vacuole, which keeps the cell turgid (firm and full of water). The cheek cells have neither, so they have a flexible, irregular shape. This supports the hypothesis.
[bio7-s4.7.2] The cheek cells are lining (epithelial) cells: flat and thin, forming a protective layer that is continually rubbed off and replaced, which is why they come away easily.
[bio7-s4.7.3] The onion epidermis is a protective outer layer of tightly fitting cells with strong walls.
[bio7-s4.7.4] The onion has no chloroplasts because the bulb is a food store, not a photosynthetic organ.
[bio7-s4.8.b] Slide preparation is fiddly, and small mistakes give misleading images. For each problem, know what you would see and how to fix it.
[bio7-s4.8.0] Air bubbles (black-edged circles) can be mistaken for cells or can hide them. Lower the coverslip at 45°.
[bio7-s4.8.1] Folded or overlapping tissue, or a thick smear, makes cells hard to see because light cannot pass through. Use a flat piece of epidermis one cell layer thick, and a thin smear.
[bio7-s4.8.2] Too much stain darkens everything and hides detail; too little gives poor contrast. Use one or two drops and blot the excess.
[bio7-s4.8.3] The specimen drying out under the lamp distorts the cells. Add a drop of water at the edge of the coverslip.
[bio7-s4.8.4] Poor focus or wrong light settings: focus with the fine focus at high power and adjust the diaphragm (which controls how much light reaches the specimen).
[bio7-s4.8.5] Drawings depend on the observer, so compare them with other students' slides and with the SEM images. Examine several cells and several fields of view, not just one, so your conclusion does not rest on a single unusual cell.
