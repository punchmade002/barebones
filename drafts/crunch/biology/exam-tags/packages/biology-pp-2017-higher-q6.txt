CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio23-s2: The female reproductive system
[bio23-s2.0.b] The female reproductive system does more than make eggs: it also receives sperm, provides the place where an egg is fertilised, and then looks after the developing baby. Knowing the route an egg and sperm each take makes the position of every organ easier to remember.
[bio23-s2.0.0] The system produces eggs (ova; singular ovum), the female gametes (sex cells), and the hormones oestrogen and progesterone (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio23-s2.0.1] It receives sperm during intercourse, is the site of fertilisation (the joining of egg and sperm), and holds, nourishes and protects the developing baby until birth.
[bio23-s2.0.2] Its organs lie in the lower abdomen (pelvis), where the pelvic bones protect them, except the external parts (the vulva).
[bio23-s2.0.3] Route of an egg: ovary → fallopian tube (where it may be fertilised) → uterus.
[bio23-s2.0.4] Route of sperm after intercourse: vagina → cervix → uterus → fallopian tube, so the two meet in the fallopian tube.
[bio23-s2.1.b] The vulva is the external female genitalia, the only part of the system you can see from outside the body. Its parts protect the openings to the inside and provide sexual sensation.
[bio23-s2.1.0] Vulva: the collective name for the external female genitals, including the labia, the clitoris and the openings of the urethra and the vagina, so it is the outer area while the vagina is the tube inside.
[bio23-s2.1.1] Labia: two pairs of folds of skin around the openings.
[bio23-s2.1.2] The outer labia (labia majora) are fleshier and covered with pubic hair after puberty; the inner labia (labia minora) are thinner.
[bio23-s2.1.3] The labia protect the openings of the vagina and urethra from infection and damage, because the folds close over them like a cover.
[bio23-s2.1.4] Clitoris: a small, very sensitive organ at the front of the vulva where the inner labia meet, with many nerve endings, which is why it is so sensitive.
[bio23-s2.1.5] The clitoris contains erectile tissue that fills with blood during arousal; its function is sexual pleasure. It develops in the embryo from the same tissue as the penis.
[bio23-s2.1.6] In females the urethra (the tube from the bladder) opens separately, in front of the vaginal opening, and carries urine only; it is not part of the reproductive system.
[bio23-s2.1.7] This is the main contrast with the male urethra, which carries semen as well as urine. There is more on this in The male reproductive system.
[bio23-s2.2.b] The vagina is the passage that links the outside of the body to the cervix. It has three jobs, receiving sperm, acting as the birth canal and letting menstrual blood out, and its structure suits each of them.
[bio23-s2.2.0] Vagina: a muscular, elastic tube about 7–10 cm long, leading from the vulva to the cervix.
[bio23-s2.2.1] The vagina receives the penis during intercourse; semen is deposited at its top, near the cervix.
[bio23-s2.2.2] The vagina is the birth canal, which stretches greatly during birth because its wall is muscular and elastic.
[bio23-s2.2.3] Menstrual blood leaves the body through the vagina, because it is the passage that runs from the cervix to the outside.
[bio23-s2.2.4] The vaginal lining is moist and kept acidic by harmless bacteria (lactobacilli), which helps prevent infection by pathogens (disease-causing microbes); this is an innate defence, one present from birth rather than learned. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio23-s2.3.b] The cervix is a narrow gateway between the vagina and the uterus. It controls what gets through, and it opens wide only when a baby is born.
[bio23-s2.3.0] Cervix: the narrow neck at the lower end of the uterus, opening into the top of the vagina through a small canal. It is part of the uterus, not part of the vagina.
[bio23-s2.3.1] Glands in the cervix make mucus. For most of the cycle the mucus is thick and blocks sperm and pathogens, so it keeps the uterus protected.
[bio23-s2.3.2] Around ovulation (release of the egg) the mucus becomes thin, clear and slippery, letting sperm pass towards the egg. There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s2.3.3] During pregnancy a thick mucus plug seals the cervix, so the baby in the uterus stays protected from infection.
[bio23-s2.3.4] In labour the cervix widens (dilates) to about 10 cm to let the baby out.
[bio23-s2.3.5] Cells from the cervix are sampled in cervical screening, a test for early signs of cancer, because the cervix is easy to reach from the vagina.
[bio23-s2.4.b] The uterus is where a pregnancy develops. Its thick muscular wall does the pushing, while its lining, the endometrium, prepares each month to receive an embryo.
[bio23-s2.4.0] Uterus (womb): a hollow, pear-shaped organ, about the size of a fist when not pregnant, with a very thick wall of smooth muscle.
[bio23-s2.4.1] The embryo (the earliest stage of the developing baby) implants (attaches) in the uterus and the foetus develops there.
[bio23-s2.4.2] The muscular wall stretches enormously during pregnancy to hold the growing foetus and contracts powerfully to push the baby out in labour.
[bio23-s2.4.3] Endometrium: the inner lining of the uterus, rich in blood vessels and glands, which supply the embryo with nourishment.
[bio23-s2.4.4] Each month the endometrium thickens under the influence of ovarian hormones, ready to receive an embryo.
[bio23-s2.4.5] If no embryo implants, the outer layer of the endometrium breaks down and is shed with blood in menstruation (the monthly period). There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s2.4.6] If an embryo implants, the endometrium stays and helps form the mother's part of the placenta (the organ that feeds the foetus).
[bio23-s2.4.7] Only the endometrium is shed in menstruation, not the muscular wall, which is the bulk of the uterus.
[bio23-s2.5.b] The fallopian tubes connect the ovaries to the uterus and are where the egg meets sperm. An egg cannot swim, so the tube has to move it along.
[bio23-s2.5.0] Fallopian tubes (oviducts): two narrow tubes, about 10 cm long, one from each side of the top of the uterus towards an ovary.
[bio23-s2.5.1] The open end near the ovary is funnel-shaped with finger-like projections that sweep the released egg into the tube.
[bio23-s2.5.2] Cilia (tiny hairs) lining the tube beat, and waves of muscle contraction (peristalsis) move the egg, or the early embryo, towards the uterus, because the egg cannot swim.
[bio23-s2.5.3] Fertilisation normally happens in the fallopian tube, because this is where the egg and the sperm travelling up from the uterus meet.
[bio23-s2.5.4] Ectopic pregnancy: if an embryo implants in the fallopian tube instead of the uterus, the tube cannot stretch to hold it and may rupture, which is a medical emergency.
[bio23-s2.5.5] A damaged tube, for example one scarred by infection, raises the risk of an ectopic pregnancy. There is more on this in Fertility control and infertility.
[bio23-s2.6.b] The ovaries are the organs that make eggs and the female sex hormones. They sit beside the uterus but are not attached to the tubes, which affects how the egg is collected.
[bio23-s2.6.0] Ovaries: two almond-sized organs, one on each side of the uterus, close to but not joined to the open end of each fallopian tube.
[bio23-s2.6.1] The ovaries contain follicles, each holding an immature egg. Usually one follicle matures and releases its egg each month, from puberty to menopause; this is ovulation.
[bio23-s2.6.2] The ovaries also secrete oestrogen and progesterone. There is more on this in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio23-s2.6.3] Because the ovary is not attached to the tube, the egg must be swept in by the funnel.
[bio23-s2.7.b] Exams ask you to draw and label the system or to identify parts on a diagram. Practise two views: the front view shows the organs side by side, and the side view shows their position relative to the bladder and rectum.
[bio23-s2.7.0] Diagram: front view of the female reproductive system showing two ovaries, two fallopian tubes with funnels and finger-like projections, the uterus with its thick muscular wall and the endometrium lining it, the cervix and the vagina. Label ovary, fallopian tube (oviduct), uterus, endometrium, muscle wall of uterus, cervix, vagina.
[bio23-s2.7.1] Diagram: side view (longitudinal section) showing the uterus between the bladder in front and the rectum behind, with the vagina, cervix and external parts. Label the ovary, fallopian tube, uterus, endometrium, cervix, vagina, vulva, labia, clitoris, bladder and urethra.
[bio23-s2.8.b] Here is every part in turn, from outside to inside, in the form: where it is, what it does, and how its structure suits that job.
[bio23-s2.8.0] Vulva – external genitals – collective name for the external parts that protect the openings and give sexual sensation.
[bio23-s2.8.1] Labia – around the openings of the vagina and urethra – protect them from infection and damage – folds of skin, the outer pair fleshier.
[bio23-s2.8.2] Clitoris – front of the vulva where the inner labia meet – sexual pleasure – many nerve endings and erectile tissue.
[bio23-s2.8.3] Vagina – from the vulva to the cervix – receives the penis, is the birth canal, and carries menstrual blood – muscular, elastic and stretchy, with an acidic lining.
[bio23-s2.8.4] Cervix – neck of the uterus – controls entry of sperm and pathogens, and dilates in labour – mucus changes through the cycle and the canal is narrow.
[bio23-s2.8.5] Uterus – between the bladder and rectum – holds and nourishes the foetus – thick smooth muscle that stretches and contracts.
[bio23-s2.8.6] Endometrium – inner lining of the uterus – receives the embryo and is shed if there is no pregnancy – rich in blood vessels and glands, so it can nourish an embryo.
[bio23-s2.8.7] Fallopian tube – from the uterus towards each ovary – carries the egg to the uterus and is the site of fertilisation – funnel with finger-like projections, cilia and muscle.
[bio23-s2.8.8] Ovary – one on each side of the uterus, near the fallopian tube – produces eggs and oestrogen and progesterone – contains follicles, each holding an immature egg.
[bio23-s2.9.b] The male and female systems do matching jobs, making gametes and hormones, but they differ in where the gonads sit and how the urethra is used.
[bio23-s2.9.0] Gonads (organs that make gametes): testes in the male; ovaries in the female, so each system has its own gamete factory.
[bio23-s2.9.1] Gamete produced: sperm, made continuously from puberty in the male; one egg a month from puberty to menopause in the female. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s2.9.2] Main hormones: testosterone in the male; oestrogen and progesterone in the female. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s2.9.3] Gonad position: the testes lie outside the body in the scrotum; the ovaries lie inside the pelvis.
[bio23-s2.9.4] Urethra: shared with the reproductive system in the male, carrying urine and semen; separate in the female, carrying urine only, so it is not a reproductive organ in females.

CANDIDATES bio23-s3: Gametes and fertilisation
[bio23-s3.0.b] Gametes are the cells that carry genes from one generation to the next, and they are made by a special kind of cell division. Knowing where this division happens, and which cells do it, is the starting point for sperm and egg production.
[bio23-s3.0.0] A gamete is a haploid sex cell, meaning it has only one chromosome of each pair. In humans the haploid number is 23 (n = 23).
[bio23-s3.0.1] Sperm and eggs are made by meiosis (the cell division that halves the chromosome number), so each gamete carries one chromosome of each pair, and each gamete is genetically different from the others. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio23-s3.0.2] At fertilisation two haploid gametes fuse to give a diploid zygote (the first cell of a new individual, with both chromosomes of each pair) with 46 chromosomes (2n = 46).
[bio23-s3.0.3] Meiosis happens only in the gonads (the organs that make gametes): in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes) for sperm, and in the ovaries for eggs.
[bio23-s3.0.4] The cells that undergo meiosis are diploid. Every other cell in the body divides by mitosis, which keeps the chromosome number the same.
[bio23-s3.1.b] Unlike eggs, sperm are made continuously, in huge numbers, from puberty onwards. The process happens in the testes and finishes in a storage tube beside them.
[bio23-s3.1.0] Sperm production begins at puberty and then continues every day throughout a man's life, though it declines in old age.
[bio23-s3.1.1] Many millions of sperm are made each day in the walls of the seminiferous tubules, from diploid cells that first multiply by mitosis (which keeps a supply of cells available) and then divide by meiosis.
[bio23-s3.1.2] Each cell that completes meiosis gives four sperm, all of equal size.
[bio23-s3.1.3] Half the sperm carry an X chromosome and half carry a Y, because the dividing cell has one X and one Y. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio23-s3.1.4] The new sperm move into the epididymis (a coiled tube on the back of each testis), where they are stored and mature, becoming able to swim.
[bio23-s3.1.5] It takes about two to three months for a sperm to be produced and matured, so a change in lifestyle or health affects sperm only after some weeks.
[bio23-s3.1.6] Production needs the lower temperature of the scrotum (the sac that holds the testes outside the body) and the hormones FSH and testosterone.
[bio23-s3.2.b] Egg production runs on a very different timetable from sperm production. A girl's eggs begin forming before she is born, and she never makes more.
[bio23-s3.2.0] Egg production starts before birth: while a girl is a foetus (an unborn baby), her ovaries form all the immature eggs she will ever have, each inside a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac of cells that nourishes the egg).
[bio23-s3.2.1] Each of these eggs begins meiosis but stops part-way.
[bio23-s3.2.2] A baby girl is born with about one to two million immature eggs. Most degenerate (break down), and only about 400–500 are ever released in her life.
[bio23-s3.2.3] No new eggs are made after birth, which is why the supply is fixed.
[bio23-s3.2.4] From puberty, usually one follicle each month develops fully and releases its egg at ovulation (the release of an egg from the ovary). The monthly cycle is dealt with in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s3.2.5] Meiosis continues around ovulation but is completed only if a sperm enters the egg, so an egg that is never fertilised never finishes meiosis.
[bio23-s3.3.b] Meiosis in the ovary does not split the cell evenly, and the eggs it makes are as old as the woman herself. Both facts matter for the early embryo and for fertility.
[bio23-s3.3.0] Division in egg production is unequal: almost all the cytoplasm goes to one cell, the egg, and the other products are tiny cells called polar bodies, which degenerate.
[bio23-s3.3.1] So one cell undergoing meiosis gives one egg, not four.
[bio23-s3.3.2] This unequal split gives the egg a large store of cytoplasm, food reserves and organelles (the small working structures inside a cell) to support the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.3.3] Because the eggs are as old as the woman, they age with her. In older women more eggs have errors in chromosome separation, which is why the risk of conditions such as Down syndrome rises with the mother's age. There is more on this in Mutations.
[bio23-s3.3.4] Fertility also falls with age because of these egg changes.
[bio23-s3.4.b] A sperm has one job, to carry the father's haploid nucleus to the egg. Every part of its structure helps it do that, so it is small, streamlined and powered for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.0] A sperm is a very small, mobile cell, about 0.05 mm long.
[bio23-s3.4.1] The head contains the haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes) and, at its tip, the acrosome, a cap containing enzymes that digest a path through the layers around the egg.
[bio23-s3.4.2] The middle piece is packed with mitochondria that release energy (ATP) by aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen) to power the tail.
[bio23-s3.4.3] The tail is a flagellum (a whip-like tail) that beats to make the sperm swim.
[bio23-s3.4.4] A sperm has very little cytoplasm, which keeps it small and streamlined for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.5] Diagram: a sperm cell. Label the head, acrosome, nucleus, middle piece, mitochondria and tail.
[bio23-s3.5.b] The egg has a different job from the sperm. It cannot move, so its structure is built to supply and protect the new life once a sperm arrives.
[bio23-s3.5.0] The egg (ovum) is one of the largest cells in the body, about 0.1 mm across, just visible to the naked eye. It cannot move by itself.
[bio23-s3.5.1] It contains a haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes, always including an X), a cell membrane, and a large volume of cytoplasm holding food reserves and many mitochondria, which feed and power the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.5.2] Around the egg is a thick, clear, jelly-like coat called the zona pellucida, and outside that an outer layer of follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.5.3] Diagram: an egg, drawn much larger than the sperm. Label the nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, zona pellucida (jelly coat) and follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.6.b] Sperm and eggs are both haploid gametes, but their differences follow from their jobs: the sperm travels and delivers, and the egg stores food and waits. Compare them in pairs, one feature at a time.
[bio23-s3.6.0] Size: sperm are tiny, about 0.05 mm long, while the egg is far larger, about 0.1 mm across, with a far greater volume.
[bio23-s3.6.1] Movement: the sperm swims with its tail, but the egg cannot swim and is moved along the fallopian tube by cilia (tiny hair-like structures lining the tube).
[bio23-s3.6.2] Numbers: there are millions of sperm per ejaculation, but usually one egg a month.
[bio23-s3.6.3] Cytoplasm and food: sperm have almost none, but the egg is a large store.
[bio23-s3.6.4] Sex chromosome: a sperm carries an X or a Y, but the egg always carries an X.
[bio23-s3.6.5] Production: sperm are made continuously from puberty, four per meiosis, while eggs begin to form before birth, one per meiosis.
[bio23-s3.7.b] Fertilisation needs sperm and egg to meet in the right place at the right time. Only a tiny fraction of sperm survive the journey, and the timing is tight because both gametes live for a limited time.
[bio23-s3.7.0] During intercourse semen (the fluid containing the sperm) is ejaculated into the top of the vagina, near the cervix (the neck of the uterus).
[bio23-s3.7.1] Sperm swim through the cervical mucus, which is easiest to pass around ovulation when it is thin, then through the uterus and into the fallopian tubes, helped by contractions of the uterus.
[bio23-s3.7.2] Only a few hundred of the millions of sperm reach the egg. Most die in the acidic vagina, are blocked by mucus, or go into the tube with no egg.
[bio23-s3.7.3] Sperm can survive up to about five days in the female tract.
[bio23-s3.7.4] An egg survives only about 12–24 hours after ovulation.
[bio23-s3.7.5] So intercourse in the five days before ovulation, or on the day of ovulation, can lead to fertilisation, because live sperm and a live egg are then present together.
[bio23-s3.8.b] Fertilisation is more than a sperm touching an egg. It is the fusion of two nuclei, and it happens in a set order that stops more than one sperm getting in.
[bio23-s3.8.0] Fertilisation is the fusion of the haploid nucleus of a sperm with the haploid nucleus of an egg to form a diploid zygote.
[bio23-s3.8.1] In humans it normally takes place in the upper part of a fallopian tube, within a day of ovulation.
[bio23-s3.8.2] 1. Sperm reach the egg and push between the follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.8.3] 2. The acrosome of a sperm releases its enzymes, which digest a path through the zona pellucida. This is called the acrosome reaction.
[bio23-s3.8.4] 3. The membrane of one sperm fuses with the egg's membrane, and the sperm's nucleus enters the egg. The tail is left outside, so the sperm's mitochondria stay out.
[bio23-s3.8.5] 4. At once the egg's outer layer changes and the zona pellucida hardens, so no other sperm can enter. This ensures the zygote receives only one set of chromosomes from the father.
[bio23-s3.8.6] 5. The egg completes its meiosis.
[bio23-s3.8.7] 6. The sperm nucleus and egg nucleus fuse, giving a zygote with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent.
[bio23-s3.9.b] Fertilisation settles several things at once: the child's sex, where its mitochondria come from, and the start of a new genetically unique individual.
[bio23-s3.9.0] At fertilisation the sex of the child is decided by whether the sperm carries an X or a Y, because the egg always carries an X. The zygote receives its mitochondria from the egg only. There is more on this in Sex determination and in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio23-s3.9.1] Fertilisation restores the diploid number, which would otherwise halve in every generation.
[bio23-s3.9.2] It combines genetic material from two parents, so the offspring is genetically unique. Together with meiosis, this is a source of variation.
[bio23-s3.9.3] Fertilisation starts the development of the embryo. The zygote begins to divide by mitosis as it moves down the tube. There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s3.9.4] Diagram: a sequence showing a sperm entering an egg (the acrosome digesting the zona pellucida), the sperm nucleus inside the egg, and the two nuclei fusing to form the zygote. Label the sperm nucleus, egg nucleus, zona pellucida, zygote, n and 2n.
[bio23-s3.10.b] Twins can arise in two different ways, and the difference explains why some twins are genetically identical and others are not.
[bio23-s3.10.0] Identical twins come from one zygote that splits into two embryos early in development, so they have the same genes.
[bio23-s3.10.1] Non-identical (fraternal) twins come from two eggs released at the same time and fertilised by two different sperm, so they are no more alike genetically than other brothers and sisters.

CANDIDATES bio23-s4: The menstrual cycle
[bio23-s4.0.b] Once a month, the female reproductive system gets ready for a possible pregnancy and, if none begins, starts over. This repeating pattern is the menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s4.0.0] The menstrual cycle is the series of changes in the ovary (where eggs are stored) and the uterus (the womb) that happens about once a month in a woman from puberty to menopause, except during pregnancy.
[bio23-s4.0.1] Its purpose is to release an egg and to prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) to receive an embryo, so a fertilised egg has somewhere to implant and grow.
[bio23-s4.0.2] The cycle lasts about 28 days on average, but a cycle of about 21 to 35 days is normal, and its length varies between women and from month to month.
[bio23-s4.0.3] Day 1 is the first day of bleeding, so every day number in the cycle is counted from the start of a period.
[bio23-s4.0.4] Two sets of changes run side by side: the ovarian cycle, in which a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac holding an egg) develops, releases its egg and becomes the corpus luteum, and the uterine cycle, in which the endometrium is shed, rebuilt and thickened.
[bio23-s4.0.5] Hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood) coordinate the two sets of changes, which is why the ovary and uterus stay in step with each other.
[bio23-s4.0.6] The first period (menarche) occurs during puberty, and the cycles end at the menopause. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.1.b] The cycle starts with the period, the phase where the lining built up in the last cycle is lost. Counting from day 1, it is the first of the four phases.
[bio23-s4.1.0] Menstruation lasts about days 1–5 of a typical 28-day cycle, although the typical days given for each phase are only approximate.
[bio23-s4.1.1] If no egg was fertilised in the previous cycle, the thickened outer layer of the endometrium is no longer needed, so it breaks down and is shed through the cervix (the neck of the uterus) and vagina.
[bio23-s4.1.2] The shed lining leaves with a small amount of blood. This is a period, and the shedding lasts about 3–7 days.
[bio23-s4.2.b] After the old lining is lost, the body prepares a new egg and rebuilds the lining. This phase is named after the follicles in the ovary that grow during it.
[bio23-s4.2.0] The follicular phase runs from day 1 to about day 13, so it overlaps with menstruation.
[bio23-s4.2.1] In the ovary, several follicles start to develop, and usually one grows into a large, mature, fluid-filled follicle containing the egg, called a Graafian follicle.
[bio23-s4.2.2] In the uterus, after menstruation ends, the endometrium is repaired and grows thicker from about day 6, rebuilding the lining that was lost.
[bio23-s4.2.3] Near the end of this phase the cervical mucus (the fluid made at the cervix) becomes thin, clear and slippery, which lets sperm through.
[bio23-s4.3.b] Ovulation is the high point of the ovarian cycle: the mature egg leaves the ovary.
[bio23-s4.3.0] Ovulation happens at about day 14: the Graafian follicle bursts at the surface of the ovary and releases its egg, which is swept into the fallopian tube (the tube that carries the egg towards the uterus).
[bio23-s4.3.1] This is the most fertile time, because the egg survives only about 12–24 hours, so sperm must already be present or arrive within that short window. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s4.4.b] After the egg is released, the ovary and the uterus prepare for a possible embryo. What happens next depends on whether an embryo implants.
[bio23-s4.4.0] The luteal phase lasts about days 15–28. The empty follicle turns into a yellow mass of cells, the corpus luteum (Latin for “yellow body”).
[bio23-s4.4.1] The endometrium thickens further, becoming spongy, with more blood vessels and glands, so that it is ready for an embryo to implant (embed in the lining).
[bio23-s4.4.2] If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down after about 10–14 days and the endometrium is shed, so the next menstruation begins on day 1 of a new cycle.
[bio23-s4.4.3] If an embryo implants, the corpus luteum stays active, so the lining is kept and there is no period. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.5.b] Not every woman ovulates on day 14. The timing depends on a fixed gap before the next period, not a fixed day number.
[bio23-s4.5.0] Ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period starts, because the luteal phase is fairly constant at about 14 days.
[bio23-s4.5.1] Differences in cycle length come mostly from the follicular phase, since that is the phase that varies in length.
[bio23-s4.5.2] So in a 35-day cycle ovulation is about day 21, not day 14, because 35 minus 14 gives day 21.
[bio23-s4.6.b] Here is each phase with its days, what the ovary is doing and what the endometrium is doing, for a typical 28-day cycle.
[bio23-s4.6.0] Menstruation: days 1–5; ovary: the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down; endometrium: shed.
[bio23-s4.6.1] Follicular phase: days 1–13; ovary: a follicle develops into the Graafian follicle; endometrium: repaired and growing thicker from about day 6.
[bio23-s4.6.2] Ovulation: about day 14; ovary: the Graafian follicle bursts and releases the egg; endometrium: at the thickness built up in the follicular phase.
[bio23-s4.6.3] Luteal phase: days 15–28; ovary: the corpus luteum forms and then breaks down if there is no pregnancy; endometrium: thickens further, becoming spongy, and is shed if no embryo implants.
[bio23-s4.7.b] A timeline puts the ovary and the uterus side by side, so you can see how the two cycles line up day by day.
[bio23-s4.7.0] Diagram: a timeline, day 0 to day 28. Above: the ovary (follicle growing, Graafian follicle, ovulation at day 14, corpus luteum forming then breaking down). Below: endometrium thickness (shed days 1–5, thickening to day 28). Label the four phases along the timeline.
[bio23-s4.8.b] Four hormones coordinate the cycle. Knowing which organ makes each one is the key to explaining the rest of this topic.
[bio23-s4.8.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone) are made by the pituitary gland (a small gland at the base of the brain). Oestrogen is made mainly by the developing follicle, and in smaller amounts by the corpus luteum. Progesterone is made by the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s4.9.b] The cycle restarts when progesterone falls, and the follicular phase is then driven by FSH and oestrogen. Each hormone's level explains what happens in the ovary and uterus.
[bio23-s4.9.0] Menstruation: oestrogen and progesterone are both low, because the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down.
[bio23-s4.9.1] The fall in progesterone is what causes the endometrium to break down. Low oestrogen alone is not the cause.
[bio23-s4.9.2] Low progesterone no longer holds back the pituitary, so FSH begins to rise.
[bio23-s4.9.3] Follicular phase: FSH stimulates follicles to develop, and one becomes the Graafian follicle. The growing follicle secretes oestrogen, so oestrogen rises steadily.
[bio23-s4.9.4] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium and makes the cervical mucus thin.
[bio23-s4.9.5] Rising oestrogen at first inhibits (holds back) FSH, so FSH falls back and usually only one follicle completes development.
[bio23-s4.10.b] Ovulation is triggered by a sudden surge of LH, which is itself set off by high oestrogen.
[bio23-s4.10.0] When oestrogen reaches a high peak (around day 12–13), it stimulates the pituitary to release a large surge of LH, with a smaller rise in FSH.
[bio23-s4.10.1] The LH surge causes the Graafian follicle to burst and release its egg about a day later (ovulation, about day 14).
[bio23-s4.10.2] Oestrogen falls just after ovulation.
[bio23-s4.11.b] After ovulation the corpus luteum takes over, and progesterone rises. Whether the cycle ends or a pregnancy continues depends on whether the corpus luteum survives.
[bio23-s4.11.0] Luteal phase: LH causes the empty Graafian follicle to develop into the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s4.11.1] The corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and some oestrogen), so progesterone rises to a peak around day 21.
[bio23-s4.11.2] Progesterone maintains and further thickens the endometrium, making it ready for implantation, and makes the cervical mucus thick again.
[bio23-s4.11.3] High progesterone, with oestrogen, inhibits FSH and LH, so no new follicle develops and no further egg is released while the corpus luteum is active. FSH and LH stay low.
[bio23-s4.11.4] End of the cycle: without pregnancy, low LH means the corpus luteum is no longer maintained. It breaks down after about 10–14 days, so progesterone and oestrogen fall.
[bio23-s4.11.5] The endometrium can no longer be maintained and is shed (menstruation). The fall removes the inhibition of FSH, which rises and starts a new cycle.
[bio23-s4.11.6] If an embryo implants, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so progesterone stays high and menstruation does not happen. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.12.b] This summary gives the level of each hormone in each phase, with the state of the ovary.
[bio23-s4.12.0] Menstruation: FSH rising, LH low, oestrogen low, progesterone low; a new follicle begins to grow.
[bio23-s4.12.1] Follicular phase: FSH high then falling, LH low, oestrogen rising, progesterone low; a follicle grows to the Graafian follicle.
[bio23-s4.12.2] Ovulation: LH surge, a small FSH peak, oestrogen at its peak then falling, progesterone low; the Graafian follicle bursts.
[bio23-s4.12.3] Luteal phase: FSH and LH low, progesterone high with a peak about day 21, oestrogen at a moderate second, smaller peak, both falling at the end; the corpus luteum active, then breaking down.
[bio23-s4.13.b] The ovary and the pituitary signal each other in a loop, and that feedback is what keeps the cycle going from one month to the next.
[bio23-s4.13.0] The cycle is controlled by feedback, meaning each hormone's level changes the release of the next.
[bio23-s4.13.1] Negative feedback: progesterone, and moderate oestrogen, inhibits the pituitary's release of FSH and LH. This keeps FSH and LH low in the luteal phase, and their fall lets the corpus luteum break down. There is more on negative feedback in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio23-s4.13.2] Positive feedback: high oestrogen just before ovulation causes more LH to be released, giving the LH surge.
[bio23-s4.13.3] Each hormone controls the next: FSH → follicle grows → oestrogen → LH surge → ovulation and corpus luteum → progesterone → FSH and LH inhibited → corpus luteum breaks down → progesterone falls → menstruation and FSH rises.
[bio23-s4.13.4] Technology link: home ovulation test kits detect the LH surge in urine, showing that ovulation is likely within about a day or two.
[bio23-s4.13.5] Technology link: the combined contraceptive pill gives steady oestrogen and progesterone, which by negative feedback keep FSH and LH low, so no follicle matures and no LH surge occurs.
[bio23-s4.13.6] Diagram: this sequence as a loop (a flow chart) linking the pituitary, the ovary and the endometrium, with “+” on the oestrogen-to-LH arrow and “−” on the progesterone-to-FSH/LH arrow.
[bio23-s4.14.b] Exams show graphs of the four hormones across a 28-day cycle. You need to recognise each curve and explain every change by its cause.
[bio23-s4.14.0] How to read the standard graphs of a 28-day cycle: FSH has a rise early in the cycle and a small peak at day 14.
[bio23-s4.14.1] LH is low except for a sharp, tall peak just before day 14.
[bio23-s4.14.2] Oestrogen rises through the follicular phase to a peak just before the LH surge, drops after ovulation, then has a lower second rise in the luteal phase.
[bio23-s4.14.3] Progesterone is low until ovulation, rises to a peak around day 21, then falls to day 28.
[bio23-s4.14.4] Exam answers explain each change by its cause, for example: progesterone rises after day 14 because the corpus luteum secretes it.
[bio23-s4.14.5] Diagram: two graphs on one 28-day time axis, one for FSH and LH (pituitary), one for oestrogen and progesterone (ovarian), lined up with the ovary events and endometrium thickness from the timeline. Label the LH surge, oestrogen peak, progesterone peak and ovulation.

CANDIDATES bio23-s5: Pregnancy
[bio23-s5.0.b] Pregnancy starts with a single fertilised cell and, within about a week, a ball of cells that must attach itself to the wall of the uterus. The journey and the attachment (implantation) happen in a set order and in set places.
[bio23-s5.0.0] Fertilisation takes place in the fallopian tube (the tube that carries the egg from the ovary towards the uterus) and gives a diploid zygote (a cell with a full set of chromosomes), the first cell of the new individual. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s5.0.1] The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis (division that makes identical cells) as cilia (tiny moving hairs lining the tube) and muscle contractions of the tube wall carry it along towards the uterus.
[bio23-s5.0.2] The cells do not grow between divisions, so they become smaller with each division and the ball stays about the size of the egg.
[bio23-s5.0.3] After about five days the ball reaches the uterus, the muscular organ in which the baby will develop, as a small ball of cells.
[bio23-s5.0.4] Implantation: about 6–7 days after fertilisation the ball of cells sinks into the thick, spongy endometrium (the lining of the uterus) and embeds itself there, with enzymes from its outer cells digesting a way in. From implantation the woman is pregnant.
[bio23-s5.1.b] Once the ball of cells is embedded, the endometrium has to stay in place, or it would be shed in a period and the pregnancy would be lost. A hormone from the embryo makes sure it stays.
[bio23-s5.1.0] Implantation needs an endometrium kept thick by progesterone, a hormone made by the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after the egg is released).
[bio23-s5.1.1] After implantation, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so the endometrium is kept and menstruation (the monthly period) stops.
[bio23-s5.1.2] A missed period is often the first sign of pregnancy, and pregnancy tests work by detecting this hormone, hCG, in urine. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s5.1.3] If the ball of cells implants in the fallopian tube instead of the uterus, it is an ectopic pregnancy.
[bio23-s5.2.b] Soon after implantation the embryo needs a way to get food and oxygen from its mother and to get rid of its wastes. The placenta is the organ that does this, and it is built jointly by the embryo and the mother.
[bio23-s5.2.0] After implantation, the outer cells of the embryo grow many finger-like projections (villi) into the endometrium.
[bio23-s5.2.1] The villi, together with the mother's endometrium and the blood spaces around them, form the placenta, a disc-shaped organ attached to the wall of the uterus.
[bio23-s5.2.2] The placenta is therefore made by both the embryo (the villi) and the mother (the endometrium).
[bio23-s5.2.3] The placenta is fully formed by about the end of the third month (12 weeks) and keeps growing until birth.
[bio23-s5.3.b] The foetus (the developing baby) floats in the uterus, away from the placenta. The umbilical cord is the link between them, and it carries blood in two directions.
[bio23-s5.3.0] The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta.
[bio23-s5.3.1] The cord contains two umbilical arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) with wastes from the foetus to the placenta.
[bio23-s5.3.2] The cord contains one umbilical vein, which carries oxygenated blood with nutrients from the placenta to the foetus.
[bio23-s5.3.3] The vein carries oxygenated blood, the reverse of the adult pattern for most arteries and veins, because arteries are defined by carrying blood away from the heart and veins by carrying it towards the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio23-s5.4.b] Mother and foetus swap materials at the placenta, but their blood never meets. Everything that is exchanged has to cross a very thin barrier, and the placenta's structure is built to make that crossing quick.
[bio23-s5.4.0] The mother's and the foetus's blood do not mix. They are separated by a thin barrier of the villi, only a few cells thick.
[bio23-s5.4.1] This barrier protects the foetus from the mother's high blood pressure and from her immune cells, and it matters because their blood groups may differ. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio23-s5.4.2] The villi give a very large surface area, and the barrier is very thin, so the distance for diffusion is short.
[bio23-s5.4.3] The foetus's capillaries (tiny blood vessels) in the villi lie close to pools of the mother's blood, and blood flows on both sides, which keeps the concentration gradients high so diffusion carries on.
[bio23-s5.4.4] Substances cross by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), and some, such as glucose and amino acids, cross by facilitated diffusion or active transport.
[bio23-s5.5.b] Before birth the foetus's own lungs, gut and kidneys cannot do their jobs, so the placenta does them. It also protects and controls the pregnancy.
[bio23-s5.5.0] It supplies oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals and water to the foetus, so it acts as the foetus's lungs and gut.
[bio23-s5.5.1] It removes carbon dioxide and urea, which pass into the mother's blood to be excreted by her lungs and kidneys, so it acts as the foetus's kidneys.
[bio23-s5.5.2] It passes antibodies from mother to foetus, giving passive natural immunity (protection received ready-made, not made by the baby's own body) for the first months after birth. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s5.5.3] It secretes hormones, taking over the production of progesterone and oestrogen from the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s5.5.4] It forms a barrier to many pathogens (disease-causing organisms) and to the mother's blood cells.
[bio23-s5.6.b] The placenta is a barrier to many things, but not to everything. Some harmful substances in the mother's blood can cross to the foetus, which is why what a mother drinks, smokes, takes or catches matters.
[bio23-s5.6.0] Alcohol crosses and can cause foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, with damage to the brain and growth.
[bio23-s5.6.1] Nicotine and carbon monoxide from smoking reduce the oxygen supply to the foetus, and are linked to low birth weight and premature birth.
[bio23-s5.6.2] Many drugs, legal and illegal, cross the placenta; the thalidomide case is a well-known example. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio23-s5.6.3] Some viruses cross the placenta. Rubella (German measles) can cause deafness, blindness and heart defects if caught in early pregnancy, and HIV can also cross.
[bio23-s5.6.4] This is why antenatal advice (advice given to women during pregnancy) matters.
[bio23-s5.7.b] The foetus also needs protection from knocks and from drying out or overheating. A fluid-filled sac around it provides that.
[bio23-s5.7.0] The amnion is a thin membrane that forms a fluid-filled sac, the amniotic sac, around the embryo.
[bio23-s5.7.1] Amniotic fluid cushions the foetus against shocks, keeps its temperature steady, lets it move and so develop its muscles and bones, and prevents it sticking to the membranes.
[bio23-s5.7.2] Foetal cells in the fluid can be sampled for testing by amniocentesis. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s5.8.b] A labelled drawing of the foetus in the uterus pulls together the placenta, cord and amnion in one picture.
[bio23-s5.8.0] Diagram: a foetus in the uterus, showing the uterus wall and endometrium, the placenta attached to the uterus wall, the umbilical cord with its two arteries and one vein, the amnion and amniotic fluid, and the cervix with its mucus plug. Label each.
[bio23-s5.9.b] Over about nine months one cell becomes a baby with every tissue and organ. This follows a timetable, and the early weeks are the most delicate.
[bio23-s5.9.0] The baby develops by repeated mitosis (increasing cell number), cell growth, and cell differentiation (cells becoming specialised for different jobs), so that one zygote gives rise to all the tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.9.1] Length of pregnancy (gestation): about 38 weeks from fertilisation, which in practice is counted as about 40 weeks from the first day of the woman's last period, because the date of fertilisation is not known.
[bio23-s5.9.2] The pregnancy is divided into three trimesters of about three months each.
[bio23-s5.9.3] In the first two months all the main organs form, and the heart begins beating by the end of the first month.
[bio23-s5.9.4] By about 12 weeks the baby is recognisably human with all organs present, though tiny.
[bio23-s5.9.5] During the rest of pregnancy the organs grow and mature, the baby moves (felt by the mother from about 16–20 weeks), and fat is laid down under the skin in the last weeks.
[bio23-s5.9.6] The lungs are among the last organs to mature, which is why babies born very early have difficulty breathing. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s5.9.7] The first two months are when the baby is most at risk from harmful substances, because that is when the organs are forming; damage then can cause structural birth defects.
[bio23-s5.10.b] The ball of cells on its way to the uterus passes through two named stages. The names describe what the ball looks like at each point.
[bio23-s5.10.0] Zygote: the single diploid cell formed at fertilisation.
[bio23-s5.10.1] Morula: after about 3–4 days of division the zygote has become a solid ball of about 16 or more cells, still inside the zona pellucida (the protective coat around the egg), looking like a mulberry, which is the meaning of "morula".
[bio23-s5.10.2] The morula is still in the fallopian tube or just entering the uterus.
[bio23-s5.10.3] Blastocyst: by about day 5 the cells have rearranged into a hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity.
[bio23-s5.10.4] The blastocyst has two parts: an outer layer of cells, the trophoblast, which will form the embryo's part of the placenta and its membranes; and a small cluster inside, the inner cell mass, which will form the embryo itself.
[bio23-s5.10.5] The blastocyst hatches out of the zona pellucida and is the stage that implants, trophoblast first.
[bio23-s5.10.6] The cells of the inner cell mass can develop into any type of body cell, which is why they are embryonic stem cells. Their use and the ethical questions they raise are in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s5.10.7] Diagram: the sequence zygote → 2 cells → 4 cells → morula → blastocyst; the blastocyst in section (trophoblast, inner cell mass, fluid-filled cavity); and a side view of tube and uterus marking fertilisation, morula (both in the tube) and blastocyst implanting in the endometrium.
[bio23-s5.11.b] Soon after implantation the cells of the embryo sort themselves into three layers. Each layer goes on to form a particular set of tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.11.0] Germ layers: during the third week after fertilisation the cells of the inner cell mass move and arrange themselves into three layers, the germ layers, each of which gives rise to particular tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.11.1] Ectoderm (outer layer) forms the epidermis of the skin, hair and nails, the whole nervous system (brain, spinal cord and nerves), and parts of the eye.
[bio23-s5.11.2] Mesoderm (middle layer) forms muscles, bones and cartilage, the heart, blood and blood vessels, the kidneys and the reproductive organs.
[bio23-s5.11.3] Endoderm (inner layer) forms the lining of the digestive system and of the breathing system (trachea, bronchi and lungs), and the liver and pancreas.
[bio23-s5.11.4] Memory aid: ecto = outer skin and nerves; meso = movement, blood and support; endo = inner linings.
[bio23-s5.12.b] From implantation to birth the developing baby has two names. The boundary between them is when the organs have finished forming.
[bio23-s5.12.0] Embryo: the developing individual from implantation, about the second week after fertilisation, until the end of the eighth week, during which all the main organs form (organ formation).
[bio23-s5.12.1] By the end of week 8 the embryo is about 3 cm long and has all its major organs.
[bio23-s5.12.2] Foetus: from the ninth week until birth. No new organs form, but the existing ones grow, mature and begin to work.
[bio23-s5.12.3] The distinction: embryo means organs forming; foetus means organs present and growing.
[bio23-s5.12.4] Summary sequence: zygote (day 0, fallopian tube) → morula (days 3–4) → blastocyst (about day 5; implants about days 6–7) → germ layers form (week 3) → embryo (to the end of week 8; organs form) → foetus (week 9 to birth; growth and maturation).
[bio23-s5.13.b] Labour is the end of pregnancy. It starts when the uterus, which has held the baby for about nine months, begins to push it out.
[bio23-s5.13.0] Labour is the process by which the baby, then the placenta, is pushed out of the uterus.
[bio23-s5.13.1] Labour usually begins at about 40 weeks of pregnancy, when the baby is normally head down.
[bio23-s5.13.2] Hormonal changes start it, and the hormone oxytocin drives the contractions (tightening of the uterus muscle) by positive feedback (a change that leads to more of the same change). There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s5.14.b] The first stage prepares the way out. The cervix (the narrow neck at the bottom of the uterus), which stayed closed during pregnancy, has to open wide enough for the baby's head.
[bio23-s5.14.0] The muscle of the uterus wall begins to contract regularly, and the contractions become stronger and more frequent.
[bio23-s5.14.1] The contractions pull the cervix open until it is fully dilated, about 10 cm wide.
[bio23-s5.14.2] The mucus plug (the plug of mucus that sealed the cervix) comes away, and the amnion usually bursts, releasing the amniotic fluid; this is what is meant by "the waters break".
[bio23-s5.14.3] Stage 1 is the longest stage, often many hours in a first birth.
[bio23-s5.15.b] In the second stage the baby is pushed out through the birth canal. As soon as it is born it must switch from relying on the placenta to doing things for itself.
[bio23-s5.15.0] Strong contractions of the uterus, helped by the mother pushing with her abdominal muscles, push the baby, usually head first, out through the cervix and the vagina, the birth canal. This stage is also called expulsion.
[bio23-s5.15.1] The umbilical cord is then clamped and cut; the stump later dries and falls off, leaving the navel.
[bio23-s5.15.2] At birth the baby must start breathing for itself: the first breaths inflate the lungs, and its circulation changes so that blood goes to the lungs for oxygen instead of relying on the placenta.
[bio23-s5.16.b] Labour is not over when the baby is born: the placenta still has to come out. Sometimes a vaginal birth is not safe, and the baby is delivered by surgery instead.
[bio23-s5.16.0] Shortly after the birth, further contractions separate the placenta and membranes from the uterus wall and push them out; this is the afterbirth.
[bio23-s5.16.1] Contraction of the uterus also closes the blood vessels where the placenta was attached, which limits bleeding.
[bio23-s5.16.2] A Caesarean section is the delivery of the baby through a surgical cut in the abdomen and uterus, used when a vaginal birth would be unsafe for the mother or baby, for example a baby lying the wrong way, or a placenta blocking the cervix.

CANDIDATES bio24-s1: Flower structure and pollination
[bio24-s1.0.b] Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers, which produce the gametes (sex cells) and bring about pollination (the transfer of pollen) and fertilisation (the joining of male and female gametes). Three structures carry the process from gamete to new plant: the flower, the seed and the fruit.
[bio24-s1.0.0] The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle, the tip of the flower stalk. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
[bio24-s1.0.1] The flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination. It is the only one of the three structures that exists before fertilisation, because the seed and fruit develop from flower parts afterwards.
[bio24-s1.0.2] The seed, a fertilised and developed ovule, contains the embryo (a new plant) with a food store and a protective coat. The fruit, a developed ovary, protects the seeds and helps to disperse them, that is, spread them away from the parent plant.
[bio24-s1.0.3] Seed detail is in Seeds; dispersal is in Fruit and seed dispersal.
[bio24-s1.0.4] Many flowers are bisexual (hermaphrodite), meaning they have both male and female parts in the same flower, e.g. buttercup.
[bio24-s1.0.5] Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel, whose male flowers hang in catkins and whose small female flowers have red stigmas. In some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly.
[bio24-s1.1.b] A flower is built in rings, listed here from the outside inwards. The outer parts hold the flower together, protect it and, in many species, attract the animals that carry its pollen.
[bio24-s1.1.0] Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached, so it holds the flower together. For example, in strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
[bio24-s1.1.1] Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud, and some photosynthesise (make food using light).
[bio24-s1.1.2] Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, which attracts insects.
[bio24-s1.1.3] Many insect-pollinated petals have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent, because there is no need to attract animals.
[bio24-s1.1.4] Nectary: a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects so that they keep coming back. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
[bio24-s1.2.b] The male part of the flower is the stamen. Its job is to make pollen, the package that carries the male gametes, and to hold it where a pollinator can pick it up.
[bio24-s1.2.0] Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament, so that the pollen is held up where it can be picked up. The stamens together form the androecium.
[bio24-s1.2.1] Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis (a cell division that halves the chromosome number), so the pollen grains are haploid, with one set of chromosomes. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
[bio24-s1.2.2] Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position, where insects brush against it or out in the wind. It also carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand (a thin tube of transport tissue).
[bio24-s1.2.3] Diagram: a stamen drawn on its own. Labels: anther, filament.
[bio24-s1.3.b] The female part of the flower is the carpel. It catches pollen, provides a route for the male gametes to reach the egg cell, and protects the ovules that become seeds.
[bio24-s1.3.0] Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
[bio24-s1.3.1] Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers, so pollen carried on an insect adheres, and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses, so it can catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s1.3.2] Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube (the thin tube a pollen grain grows) grows down through it to the ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.3] Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them, so the developing seeds are not exposed. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
[bio24-s1.3.4] Ovule: a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis, so it is haploid. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
[bio24-s1.3.5] Diagram: a carpel drawn on its own. Labels: stigma, style, ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.6] Diagram: a half-flower (vertical section) of an insect-pollinated flower such as buttercup or wallflower. Labels: receptacle, sepal, petal, nectary, stamen (anther, filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), ovule.
[bio24-s1.4.b] Pollination must happen before fertilisation can take place. Flowers can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated, and several features favour cross-pollination.
[bio24-s1.4.0] Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. It is only the arrival of pollen: fertilisation, the fusion of gamete nuclei in the ovule, comes later.
[bio24-s1.4.1] Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
[bio24-s1.4.2] Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation. The species is better able to adapt to change, such as new diseases or climate, and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding (breeding between close relatives).
[bio24-s1.4.3] Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but it gives less variation. It suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
[bio24-s1.4.4] Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly).
[bio24-s1.4.5] In primrose two flower forms exist: pin flowers, with a long style and low anthers, and thrum flowers, with a short style and high anthers. Insects carry pollen between the two forms, so the pollen reaches a different plant.
[bio24-s1.4.6] Agents of pollination (the carriers of pollen): insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak and birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. There is more on this in Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants.
[bio24-s1.5.b] The flower's structure matches the agent of pollination, whether an animal or the wind. Each type of flower is built to make the transfer of pollen as reliable as possible.
[bio24-s1.5.0] In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them.
[bio24-s1.5.1] Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
[bio24-s1.5.2] Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip, having learned the flower they are faithful to, so pollen is carried to the right species.
[bio24-s1.5.3] Plant and pollinator both benefit, because the plant is pollinated and the insect is fed: this is a mutualism (a relationship in which both partners gain). There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio24-s1.5.4] In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma. There is nothing to attract animals, so there are no showy petals, scent or nectar.
[bio24-s1.5.5] Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries and oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease.
[bio24-s1.5.6] Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre. It encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators, e.g. by leaving wildflowers and mowing less often.
[bio24-s1.5.7] Health link: wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.
[bio24-s1.6.b] Once pollen reaches a stigma of the right species, a sequence of events leads to fertilisation. Afterwards the flower parts change into the seed and fruit.
[bio24-s1.6.0] Fertilisation in five steps: 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma.
[bio24-s1.6.1] 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes.
[bio24-s1.6.2] 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it.
[bio24-s1.6.3] 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes, the first cell of the new plant).
[bio24-s1.6.4] 5. The zygote divides by mitosis (ordinary cell division that makes identical cells) to form the embryo.
[bio24-s1.6.5] Double fertilisation: a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store.
[bio24-s1.6.6] After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed, and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa, the seed coat. The ovary becomes the fruit, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp). The petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.
[bio24-s1.6.7] Diagram: a carpel in section, with a pollen grain on the stigma, the pollen tube growing down the style and entering an ovule through the micropyle. Labels: pollen grain, pollen tube, style, ovary, ovule, egg cell, micropyle.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.
