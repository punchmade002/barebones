CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.

CANDIDATES bio28-s2: CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing
[bio28-s2.0.b] Every living cell respires, so every cell keeps adding carbon dioxide to the blood. The body has to match its breathing to that supply, and it does so by monitoring CO₂ rather than oxygen.
[bio28-s2.0.0] Carbon dioxide is produced by every respiring cell. If it is not removed, it accumulates in the blood, where it reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions: CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻ (carbonic acid, hydrogen ions and hydrogencarbonate ions).
[bio28-s2.0.1] The reaction is speeded up by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in red blood cells. A rise in CO₂ therefore makes the blood more acidic, lowering its pH, because more hydrogen ions are released.
[bio28-s2.0.2] Blood pH is normally kept within a narrow range around 7.4, slightly alkaline. A fall in pH alters the shape of enzymes and other proteins and disturbs cell function, so the CO₂ level must be controlled. Breathing is the body's fastest way of adjusting it.
[bio28-s2.0.3] In a healthy person at sea level, the CO₂ level, not the oxygen level, is the main stimulus for breathing. The body is far more sensitive to a small rise in CO₂ than to a fall in O₂.
[bio28-s2.0.4] Oxygen receptors only strongly increase breathing when the oxygen level falls a lot, for example at high altitude. This is why the urge to breathe when you hold your breath comes from rising CO₂, not falling O₂.
[bio28-s2.1.b] A change in blood CO₂ has to be detected before breathing can respond. Special receptors in two sets of locations do this and report to the breathing centre in the brain.
[bio28-s2.1.0] A chemoreceptor is a receptor that detects the concentration of a chemical, here CO₂ and the hydrogen ions it produces.
[bio28-s2.1.1] Central chemoreceptors lie in the medulla oblongata (the lowest part of the brain, joining the spinal cord), close to the breathing centre. They are responsible for most of the response to a rise in CO₂.
[bio28-s2.1.2] CO₂ diffuses from the blood into the cerebrospinal fluid (the fluid surrounding the brain), where it forms carbonic acid and lowers the pH. The central receptors respond to this rise in hydrogen ions, so they detect CO₂ through its effect on pH.
[bio28-s2.1.3] Peripheral chemoreceptors lie in the carotid bodies, small structures in the wall of each carotid artery in the neck (the arteries that carry blood to the brain), and in the aortic bodies, in the wall of the arch of the aorta.
[bio28-s2.1.4] Peripheral chemoreceptors detect a rise in CO₂ (and H⁺) and also a large fall in O₂ in the arterial blood. They send impulses along sensory nerves to the breathing centre.
[bio28-s2.1.5] Diagram: outline of the head, neck and chest. Label the medulla oblongata (breathing centre and central chemoreceptors), carotid bodies on the carotid arteries, aortic bodies on the aortic arch, nerves to the breathing centre, and nerves from the breathing centre to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles.
[bio28-s2.2.b] Detection is only the start. The signal then passes through a chain from stimulus to response, which brings the CO₂ level back to normal.
[bio28-s2.2.0] 1. Stimulus: CO₂ in the blood rises, for example when muscles respire faster during exercise.
[bio28-s2.2.1] 2. Receptors: the central and peripheral chemoreceptors detect the rise in CO₂, through the fall in pH.
[bio28-s2.2.2] 3. Control centre: impulses reach the breathing centre in the medulla oblongata, which coordinates the response.
[bio28-s2.2.3] 4. Effectors (the muscles that carry out the response): the breathing centre sends impulses more often along motor nerves to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, and in heavy breathing to the abdominal muscles.
[bio28-s2.2.4] 5. Response: the muscles contract more strongly and more often, so breathing becomes deeper and faster.
[bio28-s2.2.5] 6. Return to normal: more CO₂ is exhaled from the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) and more oxygen is taken in, so the CO₂ level in the blood falls back towards normal and the pH rises.
[bio28-s2.2.6] 7. Reset: the receptors detect the return to normal, the breathing centre sends fewer impulses, and breathing returns to the resting rate.
[bio28-s2.3.b] The loop above is an example of negative feedback, the pattern the body uses to keep conditions steady. The same CO₂ signal also speeds up the heart so that the extra air is matched by faster blood flow.
[bio28-s2.3.0] This is negative feedback: the response, removing CO₂, cancels the change that caused it (the rise in CO₂), keeping the blood CO₂ level and pH near a set point.
[bio28-s2.3.1] It works in both directions: if CO₂ falls below normal, the breathing centre reduces the rate and depth of breathing, so CO₂ builds up again.
[bio28-s2.3.2] The cardiac centre in the medulla (the part of the brain that controls heart rate) responds to the same signals. A rise in CO₂ also increases the heart rate, so blood carries CO₂ to the lungs and oxygen to the muscles faster. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio28-s2.3.3] Diagram: a feedback loop flow chart. CO₂ rises, then chemoreceptors, breathing centre in the medulla, diaphragm and intercostal muscles, faster and deeper breathing, CO₂ falls, back to normal, with a loop arrow returning to the start. Draw a matching loop for CO₂ falling.
[bio28-s2.4.b] The same loop explains several everyday and exam situations. In each case, follow what happens to blood CO₂ and the signal it sends.
[bio28-s2.4.0] Exercise: muscles respire faster and release more CO₂ into the blood. The rise is detected and breathing rate and depth increase within seconds, removing CO₂ and supplying the extra oxygen.
[bio28-s2.4.1] Breathing stays fast for a time after exercise stops, while the extra CO₂ is removed and the oxygen debt from anaerobic respiration (lactic acid) is repaid. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s2.4.2] Holding the breath: CO₂ builds up in the blood and the signal from the chemoreceptors to the breathing centre becomes stronger and stronger. Eventually the automatic control overrides the voluntary control by the cerebrum, forcing a breath.
[bio28-s2.4.3] This is why a person cannot hold their breath until they lose consciousness at rest. The urge to breathe comes from rising CO₂, not from falling O₂.
[bio28-s2.4.4] Hyperventilation (deliberate fast, deep breathing) removes extra CO₂, so the CO₂ level falls below normal and the urge to breathe is reduced; the person may feel dizzy.
[bio28-s2.4.5] Before swimming underwater, hyperventilation is dangerous: the urge to breathe comes too late, so the oxygen level can fall far enough to cause blacking out before the swimmer feels the need to surface.
[bio28-s2.4.6] Breathing air with a raised CO₂ content, for example in a poorly ventilated, crowded room or when rebreathing air from a bag, makes breathing faster and deeper through the same loop.
[bio28-s2.4.7] Link to plants: carbon dioxide concentration is also a controlling factor in plants, where it acts on the stomata. That, and the comparison of the plant and human responses, is in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.

CANDIDATES bio29-s3: Heartbeat
[bio29-s3.0.b] The heart does not wait for instructions to beat. A small patch of muscle in its own wall sets the pace and sends a wave of electrical signals that makes the chambers contract in the right order.
[bio29-s3.0.0] A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles (the two lower chambers) into the arteries.
[bio29-s3.0.1] An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
[bio29-s3.0.2] Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid.
[bio29-s3.0.3] The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium (an upper chamber), near where the superior vena cava (the large vein from the upper body) enters.
[bio29-s3.0.4] The SA node produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
[bio29-s3.1.b] The impulse from the SA node does not reach every part at once. It travels in a set route with a built-in pause, which makes the atria contract first and the ventricles second.
[bio29-s3.1.0] 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.1] 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles, so the ventricles cannot contract too early.
[bio29-s3.1.2] 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum (the wall between the two sides of the heart). The AV node delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.3] 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex (the pointed lower tip of the heart), and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
[bio29-s3.1.4] 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery, the two arteries leaving the heart. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.
[bio29-s3.1.5] Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.
[bio29-s3.2.b] The SA node sets a basic rhythm, but the body's needs change from moment to moment. Nerves and a hormone adjust the rate the SA node sets, so the heart can speed up or slow down.
[bio29-s3.2.0] The rate is changed to match the body's needs. It rises during exercise, because the muscles then need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂, which the faster-flowing blood delivers and carries away.
[bio29-s3.2.1] Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata (part of the brainstem) sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down.
[bio29-s3.2.2] The medulla responds to information from receptors, which detect changes in the body. For example, a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate, and a rise in blood pressure slows it.
[bio29-s3.2.3] Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction.
[bio29-s3.2.4] Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
[bio29-s3.2.5] Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart.
[bio29-s3.2.6] The artificial pacemaker detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.
[bio29-s3.3.b] Each time the ventricles contract, they force a surge of blood into the arteries. You can feel this surge as the pulse, which gives an easy way to measure heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.0] The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.1] The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe.
[bio29-s3.3.2] Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries (the tiny vessels between arteries and veins) before the blood reaches them.
[bio29-s3.3.3] Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes, so the reading is a resting one.
[bio29-s3.3.4] 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
[bio29-s3.3.5] 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
[bio29-s3.3.6] 4. Repeat three times and take the mean, which reduces the effect of any one miscount.
[bio29-s3.3.7] If asked what the pulse is, say it is a wave in the artery wall, not blood flowing past your finger.
[bio29-s3.4.b] Exercise makes the muscles need more oxygen, so the heart speeds up. Measuring the pulse before and after exercise shows this, and how quickly it recovers shows how fit someone is.
[bio29-s3.4.0] This is a standard class investigation with primary data (data you collect yourself). Measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value.
[bio29-s3.4.1] The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people.
[bio29-s3.4.2] Fitter people also tend to have a lower resting pulse, because each beat pumps more blood.
[bio29-s3.4.3] Independent variable (the one you change): activity level (rest, or after exercise). Dependent variable (the one you measure): pulse rate in beats per minute.
[bio29-s3.4.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.
[bio29-s3.5.b] Blood only flows round the body because the ventricles push it with force. That force, pressing on the walls of the vessels, is blood pressure, and when it stays too high it harms the body.
[bio29-s3.5.0] Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.5.1] Pressure is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles (small arteries) and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae.
[bio29-s3.5.2] Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
[bio29-s3.5.3] Pressure is given as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.4] Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.5] Hypertension usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
[bio29-s3.5.6] Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet, being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.5.7] High blood pressure is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.
[bio29-s3.6.b] The heart works non-stop, so its muscle needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose. It cannot get these from the blood inside its chambers, so it has a separate circulation of its own.
[bio29-s3.6.0] The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
[bio29-s3.6.1] The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve (the valve at the exit of the left ventricle). They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle.
[bio29-s3.6.2] These small arteries supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration (energy release using oxygen) that keeps it working continuously.
[bio29-s3.6.3] Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.
[bio29-s3.6.4] Diagram: a front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins. Label each.
[bio29-s3.7.b] The coronary arteries are narrow, so damage to them has serious effects. When they narrow or block, the heart muscle is starved of oxygen.
[bio29-s3.7.0] Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
[bio29-s3.7.1] If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction.
[bio29-s3.7.2] Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after a heart attack can break down the clot and restore blood flow to the starved muscle, so they limit the damage (see 'Industrial enzymes').
[bio29-s3.7.3] Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol, high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.7.4] Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight, which each remove a risk factor.
[bio29-s3.7.5] Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter (a thin tube) is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open.
[bio29-s3.7.6] Alternatively, the narrowed artery can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.
