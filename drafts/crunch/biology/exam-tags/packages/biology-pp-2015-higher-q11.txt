CANDIDATES bio13-s1: Enzymes as catalysts
[bio13-s1.0.b] Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions, and left alone most of them would be far too slow to keep a cell alive. Catalysts speed reactions up, and enzymes are the catalysts that living things make for themselves.
[bio13-s1.0.0] A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by it, so it can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.0.1] A catalyst does not change which products form or how much product is made in the end; it only makes the reaction reach that point faster.
[bio13-s1.0.2] An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a catalyst made by a living cell.
[bio13-s1.0.3] Almost all enzymes are globular proteins (proteins folded into a compact, rounded ball), folded into a precise three-dimensional shape. That shape decides what the enzyme can do. There is more on protein structure in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s1.0.4] Because enzymes are proteins, they are made at ribosomes (the tiny structures in a cell where proteins are built), following the instructions in a gene (the length of DNA that codes for the protein).
[bio13-s1.1.b] Because an enzyme is a protein that is reused rather than consumed, it has a set of properties that follow from that.
[bio13-s1.1.0] Enzymes are needed only in small amounts, because each molecule is reused many times; catalase, among the fastest, breaks down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second.
[bio13-s1.1.1] Enzymes are unchanged at the end of the reaction, which is why the same molecule can start work on another substrate straight away.
[bio13-s1.1.2] Enzymes are specific: each acts on one reaction or one type of reaction, because only a substrate of the right shape fits the enzyme's active site (the region where the substrate binds).
[bio13-s1.1.3] Enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH, and enzymes are denatured (their shape is changed so that they stop working) by heat and by extremes of pH. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio13-s1.2.b] Enzyme names usually give away the job the enzyme does. Enzymes also work in different places, depending on whether their job is inside or outside the cell.
[bio13-s1.2.0] Most enzyme names end in -ase, and many are named after their substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on), so the name tells you the job: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins, sucrase on sucrose.
[bio13-s1.2.1] Some older names, such as pepsin and trypsin, don't follow this rule, so don't assume that every enzyme name ends in -ase.
[bio13-s1.2.2] Most enzymes work inside the cell that made them (intracellular), for example the enzymes of respiration (releasing energy from food) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and of photosynthesis (making food using light) in chloroplasts.
[bio13-s1.2.3] Some enzymes are secreted to work outside the cell (extracellular), for example digestive enzymes released into the gut and the enzymes that fungi and bacteria release onto their food. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio13-s1.3.b] Even a reaction that releases energy overall needs a push to get started. An enzyme works by making that push smaller, not by adding energy.
[bio13-s1.3.0] Activation energy is the minimum energy that reacting molecules need before a reaction can start: the energy needed to break or strain existing bonds.
[bio13-s1.3.1] Think of activation energy as an energy hill that must be climbed even when the reaction releases energy overall.
[bio13-s1.3.2] Without a catalyst, most reactions of metabolism (the chemical reactions of life) have such a high activation energy that, at body temperature (37 °C), they would happen far too slowly to keep a cell alive.
[bio13-s1.3.3] Heating would supply the energy, but high temperatures would damage cells and their proteins, so heating is not an option for living things.
[bio13-s1.3.4] An enzyme provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at the temperature of the cell, and the reaction is many times faster.
[bio13-s1.3.5] The overall energy change of the reaction (energy released or taken in) stays the same; only the hill to be climbed gets lower.
[bio13-s1.3.6] Diagram: energy-profile graph, energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis. Reactants high on the left, products lower on the right.
[bio13-s1.3.7] Draw two humps, a tall one labelled without enzyme and a lower one labelled with enzyme. Mark each activation energy with a vertical arrow from the reactants to the top of its hump.
[bio13-s1.4.b] Every enzyme reaction has two partners: the enzyme and the molecule it works on. One small region of the enzyme does the work, and its shape is what makes the enzyme selective.
[bio13-s1.4.0] The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on; the products are the molecules made.
[bio13-s1.4.1] Example: catalase acts on its substrate hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
[bio13-s1.4.2] The active site is a small region of the enzyme, a pocket or groove on its surface, where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place.
[bio13-s1.4.3] The active site's shape, and the chemical charges in it, are complementary to the substrate, meaning the two fit together like matching pieces.
[bio13-s1.4.4] The active site is a part of the enzyme, not of the substrate. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site, not the same as it.
[bio13-s1.5.b] Once the substrate meets the active site, the reaction follows three steps. Together they make the active-site (lock-and-key) model of enzyme action.
[bio13-s1.5.0] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex (the enzyme and substrate joined together).
[bio13-s1.5.1] Enzyme and substrate molecules meet by random movement, so anything that increases collisions (more substrate, more enzyme, higher temperature) increases the rate, up to a point.
[bio13-s1.5.2] 2. While the substrate is held in the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, for example by straining the bonds that must break, or by holding two substrate molecules close together in the right position to react.
[bio13-s1.5.3] The substrate is then converted to products, forming an enzyme–product complex.
[bio13-s1.5.4] 3. The products have a different shape, no longer fit the active site, and are released.
[bio13-s1.5.5] The active site is then free and unchanged, ready to bind another substrate molecule, which is why one enzyme molecule can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.5.6] In summary: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products.
[bio13-s1.5.7] Diagram: three stages in a row: enzyme with active site and complementary substrate approaching; enzyme–substrate complex; unchanged enzyme with two products leaving. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex and products. A matching diagram for a joining reaction is optional.
[bio13-s1.6.b] The same active-site mechanism works whichever way a reaction runs. Metabolism has reactions that break molecules down and reactions that build them up, and enzymes catalyse both.
[bio13-s1.6.0] In catabolic reactions one substrate is split into smaller products, for example amylase splits starch into maltose.
[bio13-s1.6.1] In anabolic reactions two or more substrates are joined into a larger product. For example, DNA polymerase joins nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA) into a new DNA strand when DNA is copied.
[bio13-s1.6.2] Another anabolic example is ATP synthase, which joins ADP and phosphate into ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy.
[bio13-s1.7.b] A cell contains thousands of different molecules but does not react them all at random. Enzymes are selective: each one picks out its own substrate, and that selectivity comes from the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s1.7.0] Specificity means that each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with the complementary shape fits its active site.
[bio13-s1.7.1] This is what selective catalysts means: amylase digests starch but not protein or fat; pepsin digests protein but not starch.
[bio13-s1.7.2] The lock-and-key model is the simplest explanation: the substrate (the key) fits only the active site (the lock) of the right shape.
[bio13-s1.7.3] The lock-and-key model was later refined to the induced-fit model, in which the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio13-s1.7.4] Because enzymes are specific, a cell needs thousands of different enzymes, one for each of its reactions, and it can control a reaction by making, or not making, the one enzyme that catalyses it.
[bio13-s1.7.5] Anything that changes the shape of the active site stops the substrate fitting, so the enzyme stops working: for example high temperature or extreme pH (denaturation).
[bio13-s1.7.6] A change in the enzyme's amino-acid sequence (the order of the amino acids that make up the protein), caused by a mutation in its gene, also changes the active site and stops the substrate fitting.
[bio13-s1.8.b] Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in a living thing. Enzymes make these reactions possible, and they usually work in sequences called pathways.
[bio13-s1.8.0] Every reaction of metabolism is catalysed by its own specific enzyme.
[bio13-s1.8.1] Without enzymes, the reactions that keep cells alive (respiration, photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA copying, digestion) would be far too slow at the temperatures and pH inside living things.
[bio13-s1.8.2] Most processes are metabolic pathways: a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme, in which the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next.
[bio13-s1.8.3] Respiration and photosynthesis are such pathways. There is more on this in Respiration and Photosynthesis.
[bio13-s1.8.4] Diagram: a pathway written as A → B → C → D, with enzyme 1, enzyme 2 and enzyme 3 written over the arrows.
[bio13-s1.9.b] Because every step in a pathway needs its own enzyme, a cell controls its chemistry by controlling its enzymes.
[bio13-s1.9.0] A cell controls which reactions happen, and how fast, by switching on or off the genes for particular enzymes and by activating or inhibiting enzymes already made.
[bio13-s1.9.1] If one enzyme in a pathway is missing, the pathway stops at that step: its substrate builds up and the later products are not made.
[bio13-s1.9.2] Many enzymes need a non-protein helper to work: a mineral ion (for example magnesium for many enzymes that use ATP) or a coenzyme made from a vitamin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio13-s1.10.b] Because each reaction depends on one enzyme, blocking or losing that enzyme has serious effects. Some poisons act this way, and some inherited or common conditions come from a missing enzyme.
[bio13-s1.10.0] Some drugs and poisons work by blocking an enzyme: for example some insecticides and nerve agents inhibit the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter (a chemical messenger between nerve cells). There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.
[bio13-s1.10.1] Lactose intolerance: many adults make little lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose (milk sugar), so undigested lactose passes to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing bloating, wind, cramps and diarrhoea.
[bio13-s1.10.2] Lactose-free milk is made using lactase, which digests the lactose in the milk before it is drunk.
[bio13-s1.10.3] Phenylketonuria (PKU): an inherited lack of the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine into another amino acid (tyrosine), so phenylalanine builds up in the blood and damages the developing brain.
[bio13-s1.10.4] PKU is detected by the heel-prick test given to newborn babies in Ireland, and is controlled by a diet low in phenylalanine.

CANDIDATES bio27-s1: The digestive system
[bio27-s1.0.b] Humans can't make their own food, so every meal has to be taken in, broken down and moved into the body before cells can use it. This happens in four stages: ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.
[bio27-s1.0.0] Humans are heterotrophs, meaning they obtain food from other organisms rather than making it. They are also omnivores: they eat both plant and animal material.
[bio27-s1.0.1] Ingestion is taking food into the body through the mouth, which is where every meal enters the gut.
[bio27-s1.0.2] Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. It is both mechanical (physical breakdown into smaller pieces) and chemical (hydrolysis, the splitting of large molecules by enzymes).
[bio27-s1.0.3] Digestion is necessary because large molecules such as starch, proteins and fats cannot pass through the wall of the gut or through cell membranes. It converts them to small soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.
[bio27-s1.0.4] Cells then use the absorbed molecules to build the body's own molecules or for respiration. This use of absorbed molecules is called assimilation: for example, amino acids are built into proteins and glucose is respired to release energy.
[bio27-s1.0.5] Absorption is the passage of the small soluble products of digestion, together with water, vitamins and minerals, through the wall of the gut into the blood or lymph. It happens mainly in the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.0.6] Egestion is the removal of undigested and unabsorbed material, mainly dietary fibre, as faeces through the anus. This material is left over because the body cannot digest or absorb it.
[bio27-s1.0.7] Egestion is not excretion. Faeces were never part of the body's metabolism (the chemical reactions inside cells), except for bile pigments, whereas excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells.
[bio27-s1.1.b] The alimentary canal, or gut, is the pathway food follows through the body. Its muscular wall and lining are built to move food along and to protect the gut itself.
[bio27-s1.1.0] The alimentary canal, or gut, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. Food passes in order through the mouth, pharynx (throat), oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus.
[bio27-s1.1.1] The gut wall contains two layers of smooth muscle, circular and longitudinal, which move food along by peristalsis (waves of muscle contraction that push food onwards).
[bio27-s1.1.2] The inner lining of the gut makes mucus. Mucus lubricates the food so it slides along, and protects the lining from digestive enzymes and acid, which would otherwise damage the gut's own cells.
[bio27-s1.1.3] Sphincters are rings of muscle that control movement between parts of the gut, for example at the entrance and exit of the stomach (the exit one is the pyloric sphincter) and at the anus.
[bio27-s1.2.b] The first three organs prepare food and carry it onwards. The mouth breaks it up and starts starch digestion, the oesophagus transports it, and the stomach stores and churns it while starting protein digestion.
[bio27-s1.2.0] The mouth is where food is ingested and chewed by the teeth, then mixed with saliva from three pairs of salivary glands. The tongue shapes it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. Starch digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.1] The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries food by peristalsis from the pharynx, down through the chest behind the trachea (windpipe) and through the diaphragm (the sheet of muscle below the lungs), to the stomach. No digestion or absorption happens in it.
[bio27-s1.2.2] During swallowing the epiglottis (a flap) covers the entrance to the trachea, so food goes down the oesophagus and not into the lungs.
[bio27-s1.2.3] The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the upper left of the abdomen, just below the diaphragm. It stores food for a few hours, which means a meal can be eaten quickly and released to the intestine gradually.
[bio27-s1.2.4] The stomach churns food with gastric juice (acid and a protease, an enzyme that digests protein) into a thick liquid called chyme. The acid kills most bacteria in food, and protein digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.5] The pyloric sphincter at the stomach's exit releases chyme a little at a time into the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.3.b] After the stomach, chyme enters the small intestine, where digestion is finished and most absorption happens. What is left passes to the large intestine, which recovers water and stores the waste until it leaves the body.
[bio27-s1.3.0] The small intestine is the longest part of the gut, coiled in the abdomen. Its first part, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas, and most chemical digestion happens there.
[bio27-s1.3.1] The later parts, the jejunum and the ileum, complete digestion and absorb most digested food and water through millions of villi (tiny finger-like projections of the lining that give a large surface for absorption).
[bio27-s1.3.2] The large intestine is wider but shorter than the small intestine; "large" refers to its width, not its length. Its parts are the caecum, with the appendix attached, the colon and the rectum.
[bio27-s1.3.3] The appendix is a small dead-end tube with no digestive role in humans, so food is not digested in it.
[bio27-s1.3.4] The colon absorbs water and some minerals from the remaining material, and is home to many bacteria. It is not an excretory organ, because what leaves is egested, not excreted.
[bio27-s1.3.5] The rectum is the last part of the large intestine, which stores faeces until egestion, so waste does not have to leave the body continuously.
[bio27-s1.3.6] The anus is the opening at the end of the gut, closed by sphincter muscles. The outer sphincter is under voluntary control, so egestion can be delayed until it is convenient.
[bio27-s1.4.b] Some organs help digestion without food ever entering them. They make secretions that reach the gut through ducts (narrow tubes), so they are called associated organs.
[bio27-s1.4.0] The salivary glands are three pairs, around the mouth and below the jaw, releasing saliva into the mouth through ducts. Saliva contains water, mucus and the enzyme salivary amylase.
[bio27-s1.4.1] The liver is the largest internal organ, in the upper right of the abdomen just below the diaphragm. Its digestive role is to make bile.
[bio27-s1.4.2] The liver also receives the absorbed products of digestion through the hepatic portal vein (the vein carrying blood from the gut to the liver), stores glycogen and deaminates amino acids (removes their nitrogen-containing part).
[bio27-s1.4.3] The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile. It squeezes bile through the bile duct into the duodenum when fatty food arrives there. It makes no bile itself.
[bio27-s1.4.4] The pancreas is a soft, pinkish gland behind the stomach, lying in the curve of the duodenum. It makes pancreatic juice, which contains enzymes for starch, protein and fat and sodium hydrogencarbonate, and this flows through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum.
[bio27-s1.4.5] The pancreas is also an endocrine gland (a ductless gland that releases hormones into the blood) making insulin, so it has both ducted (exocrine) and ductless (endocrine) roles.
[bio27-s1.4.6] The liver, gallbladder and pancreas are associated organs: food never passes through them, and their secretions reach the gut through ducts. Food does pass through the mouth, stomach and intestines.
[bio27-s1.5.b] A labelled diagram shows how the gut and its associated organs sit in relation to each other. Getting the positions right matters as much as the labels.
[bio27-s1.5.0] Diagram: alimentary canal and associated glands, front view, as one continuous tube. Labels: mouth, salivary glands, tongue, oesophagus, diaphragm, liver (above stomach, body's right), gallbladder (under liver), bile duct, stomach, pyloric sphincter, pancreas (in duodenum's loop), pancreatic duct, duodenum, ileum, caecum, appendix, colon, rectum, anus.
[bio27-s1.6.b] Following one mouthful from start to finish pulls the stages of nutrition together. Each part of the gut has its own main job.
[bio27-s1.6.0] Mouth: ingestion; mechanical and chemical digestion begin.
[bio27-s1.6.1] Oesophagus: transport of food to the stomach, because the mouth and stomach are too far apart for food to move unaided.
[bio27-s1.6.2] Stomach: storage and churning; protein digestion begins.
[bio27-s1.6.3] Duodenum: most chemical digestion, with bile and pancreatic juice.
[bio27-s1.6.4] Ileum: digestion is completed and absorption takes place.
[bio27-s1.6.5] Colon: absorption of water, so the remaining waste becomes more solid.
[bio27-s1.6.6] Rectum: storage of faeces.
[bio27-s1.6.7] Anus: egestion, the removal of faeces from the body.

CANDIDATES bio27-s2: Mechanical breakdown
[bio27-s2.0.b] Before food can be absorbed it has to be broken down in two ways. Mechanical breakdown makes the pieces smaller; chemical breakdown then splits the molecules themselves. This subsection is about the first kind.
[bio27-s2.0.0] Mechanical (physical) digestion breaks food into smaller pieces, or fat into smaller droplets, without changing the molecules: no chemical bonds are broken and no new substances are formed. Chemical digestion, done by enzymes (biological catalysts that speed up reactions), does break bonds. There is more on this in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.0.1] Smaller pieces have a much larger total surface area, so enzymes can act on more of the food at once and digestion is faster. Think of how sugar dissolves faster as powder than as a lump.
[bio27-s2.0.2] Mechanical breakdown also makes food soft and moist enough to swallow, and mixes it with digestive juices (the watery secretions that contain enzymes), so the enzymes reach all of the food.
[bio27-s2.0.3] The four mechanisms are chewing by the teeth (mouth), peristalsis (the whole gut), churning (stomach), and emulsification of fats by bile salts (duodenum, the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s2.1.b] Digestion starts in the mouth, where the teeth cut and crush food into small pieces. Each type of tooth has a shape that suits its job.
[bio27-s2.1.0] Chewing (mastication) breaks food into small pieces and mixes it with saliva. The tongue rolls the moistened food into a bolus (a soft ball of food) for swallowing, and mucus in saliva lubricates the bolus so it slides down easily.
[bio27-s2.1.1] Incisors are at the front of the mouth. They are chisel-shaped with sharp, flat edges, which suits them for biting and cutting food.
[bio27-s2.1.2] Canines, one on each side of the incisors in each jaw, are pointed, so they grip and tear food. Human canines are small compared with those of carnivores (meat-eating animals).
[bio27-s2.1.3] Premolars and molars at the back are broad with ridged surfaces (cusps), for crushing and grinding food. Premolars have two cusps; molars are larger with more cusps.
[bio27-s2.1.4] The jaw moves sideways as well as up and down, so the broad surfaces of the molars can grind food rather than only crush it.
[bio27-s2.1.5] Diagram: (a) the four types of teeth side by side, labelled incisor, canine, premolar and molar, with their roles; (b) a vertical section of a molar labelled crown, root, enamel, dentine, pulp cavity, nerves and blood vessels, gum, cement and jawbone.
[bio27-s2.2.b] The teeth in your mouth can be summarised in a short code called the dental formula. Knowing how a tooth is built also shows why it is hard-wearing yet sensitive.
[bio27-s2.2.0] The dental formula gives the number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper jaw over one half of the lower jaw, in the order incisors, canines, premolars, molars.
[bio27-s2.2.1] The adult human dental formula is 2.1.2.3 over 2.1.2.3, so there are 16 teeth on each side of the mouth (8 in each quarter) and 32 teeth in total.
[bio27-s2.2.2] Child's milk (deciduous) teeth have the formula 2.1.0.2 over 2.1.0.2, giving 20 teeth in total. They have no premolars and only two molars in each quarter, so the adult set has 12 more teeth.
[bio27-s2.2.3] The third molars are the wisdom teeth. They appear last, often in the late teens or later, or not at all.
[bio27-s2.2.4] A tooth has a crown above the gum and a root fixed in the jawbone. Enamel, the hardest substance in the body, covers the crown, so it can withstand biting and chewing.
[bio27-s2.2.5] Dentine, a bone-like layer, lies beneath the enamel. The pulp cavity in the centre contains nerves and blood vessels, which is why a damaged tooth hurts. Cement holds the root in its socket.
[bio27-s2.3.b] Enamel is very hard, but acid can still dissolve it. Tooth decay is the result of bacteria turning the sugar in the mouth into acid.
[bio27-s2.3.0] Bacteria in plaque (a sticky film on the teeth) feed on sugars and release acid. The acid dissolves enamel and then dentine, forming a cavity.
[bio27-s2.3.1] Eating and drinking sugary food less often gives the bacteria less to feed on, so less acid is made.
[bio27-s2.3.2] Brushing with fluoride toothpaste removes plaque and hardens enamel. Drinking fluoridated water (water with fluoride added) also hardens enamel, so it resists the acid. There is more on fluoride in Minerals.
[bio27-s2.3.3] Regular dental check-ups let decay be found and treated early, before the cavity reaches the pulp cavity.
[bio27-s2.4.b] Food has to travel the whole length of the gut, even though the gut has no hands or pump. Peristalsis does this with waves of muscle, and it also helps break the food up.
[bio27-s2.4.0] Peristalsis is a wave of muscular contraction that passes along the wall of the gut, pushing food along. It happens from the oesophagus (the food pipe) to the rectum, and also in the ureters (the tubes from the kidneys to the bladder).
[bio27-s2.4.1] The gut wall has an inner layer of circular muscle and an outer layer of longitudinal muscle. Both are smooth, involuntary muscle, which means they work without conscious control.
[bio27-s2.4.2] 1. The circular muscle just behind the bolus contracts, narrowing the gut and squeezing the food forwards.
[bio27-s2.4.3] 2. The circular muscle just in front of the bolus relaxes, widening the gut to receive it. The wave then moves along, carrying the food with it.
[bio27-s2.4.4] Peristalsis is automatic (involuntary) once food is swallowed, and it works against gravity: a person can swallow while lying down or upside down, and the food still reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s2.4.5] Peristalsis also mixes food with digestive juices and breaks it up, so it is part of mechanical breakdown. In the small intestine it keeps moving the contents past the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s2.4.6] Dietary fibre adds bulk that the muscles can grip and push against, so a high-fibre diet keeps peristalsis effective and prevents constipation. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio27-s2.4.7] Diagram: a section of the gut showing a bolus with contracted circular muscle (narrow) behind it and relaxed muscle (wide) in front, with an arrow for the direction of movement; label circular muscle, longitudinal muscle, bolus, contraction and relaxation.
[bio27-s2.5.b] The stomach is a muscular bag that holds food for a few hours. Its walls churn the food, which is the third mechanical process after chewing and peristalsis.
[bio27-s2.5.0] The stomach wall has three layers of smooth muscle: longitudinal, circular and an oblique (diagonal) layer. Having muscle running in three directions means their contractions churn the food, breaking it up and mixing it thoroughly with gastric juice (the stomach's digestive secretion).
[bio27-s2.5.1] After a few hours of churning, food becomes chyme, a thick, creamy liquid. The sphincters (rings of muscle that close an opening) at the entrance and exit stay closed during churning, so food is held in the stomach.
[bio27-s2.5.2] The pyloric sphincter, at the exit of the stomach, relaxes now and then to let small amounts of chyme into the duodenum. This means the small intestine is never overloaded and has time to digest and neutralise each portion.
[bio27-s2.5.3] The stomach also has a chemical role, using acid and pepsin. There is more on this in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.6.b] Fat does not mix with water, so it forms large droplets in the watery gut. Bile salts solve this by breaking fat into tiny droplets, a mechanical step that gives the fat-digesting enzyme far more surface to work on.
[bio27-s2.6.0] Fats and oils do not mix with the watery contents of the gut, so they form large droplets. Lipase (the fat-digesting enzyme, which works in water) can therefore reach only the droplet surfaces.
[bio27-s2.6.1] Bile salts are made by the liver and enter the duodenum in bile. Each bile-salt molecule has one end that attracts water (hydrophilic) and one end that attracts fat (hydrophobic).
[bio27-s2.6.2] The bile salts gather on the surface of fat droplets with the fat-loving end inwards. With the churning of the gut they break the large droplets into many tiny droplets and stop them joining together again.
[bio27-s2.6.3] This is emulsification, and the mixture of tiny droplets in water is an emulsion.
[bio27-s2.6.4] Emulsification is mechanical: the fat molecules are unchanged and no bonds are broken. It greatly increases the surface area of fat exposed to lipase, so fat is digested much faster.
[bio27-s2.6.5] Bile contains no enzymes. Its other roles, neutralising stomach acid and excreting bile pigments (waste products), are in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.6.6] Washing-up liquid emulsifies grease in the same way, which is why bile salts are often compared to a detergent.
[bio27-s2.6.7] Diagram: one large fat droplet, then, after bile salts, many small droplets each coated with bile-salt molecules, labelled large surface area for lipase.

CANDIDATES bio27-s3: Chemical breakdown
[bio27-s3.0.b] Food is made of large molecules that are too big to pass into the blood. Chemical digestion cuts them into small units using enzymes (biological catalysts made by the body).
[bio27-s3.0.0] Chemical digestion is the hydrolysis of large food molecules into their small, soluble units by digestive enzymes: each bond is broken by adding a molecule of water, which is what hydrolysis means.
[bio27-s3.0.1] Carbohydrates become simple sugars, proteins become amino acids, and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. These small units are soluble and small enough to be absorbed.
[bio27-s3.0.2] Each digestive enzyme is specific to one type of substrate (the molecule it acts on) and works best at its optimum pH, the pH of the part of the gut where it acts.
[bio27-s3.0.3] The gut changes pH along its length: neutral in the mouth, acid in the stomach and alkaline in the small intestine. This suits the different enzymes working in each place.
[bio27-s3.0.4] Most digestive enzymes are released from glands into the gut cavity, so digestion there is extracellular, meaning it happens outside cells.
[bio27-s3.1.b] Digestion begins in the mouth, where food is mixed with saliva. Saliva moistens the food and also starts the digestion of starch.
[bio27-s3.1.0] Saliva is produced by the three pairs of salivary glands and released into the mouth.
[bio27-s3.1.1] The pH in the mouth is about 7 (neutral to slightly alkaline), which is the optimum for salivary amylase, the enzyme in saliva.
[bio27-s3.1.2] Salivary amylase digests starch to maltose (a sugar made of two glucose units). The product of an amylase is maltose.
[bio27-s3.1.3] Food is in the mouth only briefly, so only a little starch is digested there. Stomach acid denatures (destroys the shape of) the amylase soon after it reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s3.1.4] Saliva also contains water, which dissolves food so that it can be tasted and moistens it, and mucus, which lubricates swallowing.
[bio27-s3.1.5] Saliva also contains lysozyme, an enzyme that kills some bacteria.
[bio27-s3.2.b] In the stomach, food meets a strongly acidic fluid called gastric juice. It begins protein digestion and also protects the body from bacteria that arrive in food.
[bio27-s3.2.0] Gastric juice is produced by gastric glands in the stomach lining and released into the stomach. It contains hydrochloric acid, pepsin (released as inactive pepsinogen) and mucus.
[bio27-s3.2.1] The pH in the stomach is about 2 (strongly acidic), which is the optimum for pepsin.
[bio27-s3.2.2] Hydrochloric acid kills most bacteria in food, which is a defence against infection. It also provides the acid pH that pepsin needs.
[bio27-s3.2.3] Hydrochloric acid converts inactive pepsinogen to active pepsin, and it stops salivary amylase, which cannot work at this pH.
[bio27-s3.2.4] Pepsin is a protease (a protein-digesting enzyme): it hydrolyses peptide bonds inside protein chains, breaking proteins into shorter chains called polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.2.5] The product of a protease is polypeptides. Complete protein digestion needs further enzymes in the small intestine to give amino acids.
[bio27-s3.2.6] Pepsin is made in the inactive form pepsinogen so that it does not digest the gastric gland cells that make it. It becomes active only in the stomach cavity.
[bio27-s3.2.7] A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus barrier fails, the lining is damaged, causing a stomach ulcer.
[bio27-s3.3.b] Fat digestion needs help before enzymes can act, and the acid from the stomach has to be dealt with. Bile does both jobs when food enters the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s3.3.0] Bile is produced by the liver, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, and released through the bile duct into the duodenum when chyme (the partly digested, acidic food mixture leaving the stomach), especially fatty chyme, arrives.
[bio27-s3.3.1] Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments and sodium hydrogencarbonate. It contains no enzymes.
[bio27-s3.3.2] Bile salts emulsify fats, which means they break large fat droplets into tiny droplets. This is a physical change, not a chemical one. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s3.3.3] Sodium hydrogencarbonate is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach. This raises the pH to suit the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes and stops the action of pepsin.
[bio27-s3.3.4] Bile pigments are excretory wastes from the breakdown of haemoglobin from old red blood cells in the liver. They give faeces their brown colour.
[bio27-s3.4.b] The pancreas adds a second fluid to the duodenum, one that carries most of the enzymes needed to digest starch, protein and fat. Because the duodenum is alkaline, these enzymes work best there.
[bio27-s3.4.0] Pancreatic juice is produced by the pancreas and released through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. It contains sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralises acid, and three enzymes: pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.4.1] Pancreatic amylase digests the remaining starch (the starch not digested in the mouth) to maltose.
[bio27-s3.4.2] Trypsin is a protease, released in an inactive form and activated in the duodenum. It digests proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptides.
[bio27-s3.4.3] Lipase digests fats and oils (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. The product of a lipase is fatty acids and glycerol, not fatty acids alone.
[bio27-s3.4.4] Lipase acts on the tiny droplets made by emulsification, because they give it a much larger surface to work on.
[bio27-s3.4.5] The pH in the duodenum and small intestine is about 8 (slightly alkaline), because of the sodium hydrogencarbonate in bile and pancreatic juice. This suits pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.5.b] The last stage of digestion is done by enzymes made by the cells lining the small intestine. They finish breaking down the sugars and peptides that earlier enzymes produced.
[bio27-s3.5.0] The cells lining the villi (the tiny finger-like projections of the small intestine) make enzymes that complete digestion.
[bio27-s3.5.1] Many of these enzymes are held in the membranes of the lining cells, so the last step of digestion happens right at the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s3.5.2] Maltase digests maltose to two glucose molecules. Sucrase digests sucrose to glucose and fructose. Lactase digests lactose to glucose and galactose.
[bio27-s3.5.3] Peptidases digest peptides to amino acids.
[bio27-s3.5.4] These enzymes work at a pH of about 8, the same alkaline conditions as the duodenum.
[bio27-s3.5.5] Lactose intolerance: people who stop making lactase after childhood cannot digest lactose, so bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, causing bloating, wind and diarrhoea. There is more on this in Variation and Diet.
[bio27-s3.6.b] This table pulls together where each enzyme is made, where it acts, its pH and its products. Each line has the same pattern so you can compare them quickly.
[bio27-s3.6.0] Salivary amylase – made in salivary glands – acts in mouth – pH about 7 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.1] Pepsin (protease) – made in gastric glands of stomach wall – acts in stomach – pH about 2 – protein → polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.6.2] Pancreatic amylase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.3] Trypsin (protease) – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – proteins and polypeptides → peptides.
[bio27-s3.6.4] Lipase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.6.5] Maltase – made in the wall of the small intestine – acts there – pH about 8 – maltose → glucose.
[bio27-s3.6.6] Peptidases – made in the wall of the small intestine – act there – pH about 8 – peptides → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.6.7] Bile salts (not an enzyme) – made in liver, stored in gallbladder – act in duodenum – emulsify fats.
[bio27-s3.7.b] Putting the enzymes in order shows how each nutrient is broken down step by step along the gut. You can then use these flow models to predict what goes wrong when part of the system fails.
[bio27-s3.7.0] Carbohydrate model: starch is digested by salivary amylase in the mouth (pH 7) to maltose. The remaining starch is digested by pancreatic amylase in the duodenum (pH 8) to maltose. Maltose is digested by maltase in the small intestine wall to glucose.
[bio27-s3.7.1] Sucrose and lactose are digested by sucrase and lactase in the small intestine wall. Cellulose is not digested, because humans make no enzyme for it, so it passes on as fibre.
[bio27-s3.7.2] Protein model: protein → (pepsin, stomach, pH 2) → polypeptides → (trypsin, duodenum, pH 8) → shorter peptides → (peptidases, small intestine wall) → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.7.3] Fat model: large fat droplets → (bile salts, duodenum: emulsification, a physical change) → tiny droplets → (lipase from the pancreas, duodenum, pH 8) → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.7.4] Fat is digested only in the small intestine, after emulsification.
[bio27-s3.7.5] Using the models to predict: if the bile duct is blocked, for example by gallstones, fats are poorly emulsified and digested, and undigested fat appears in the faeces.
[bio27-s3.7.6] If the pancreas releases too little juice, as in cystic fibrosis, digestion of all three nutrients is reduced, which is why patients take enzyme capsules. There is more on this in Industrial enzymes.
[bio27-s3.7.7] Diagram: a flow chart with three rows (carbohydrate, protein, fat), each running left to right through mouth, stomach, duodenum and small intestine wall, showing enzyme or bile salts, pH and product at each step; or an alimentary canal (whole gut) outline with enzymes and pH at each site.
[bio27-s3.8.b] Visking tubing is a simple physical model of the small intestine. It shows why food must be digested into small molecules before it can pass into the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.0] Visking (dialysis) tubing is a selectively permeable membrane: it lets small molecules (water, glucose and maltose) through but not large ones (starch). It models the wall of the small intestine, and the water around it models the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.1] Method, step 1: tie one end of two lengths of Visking tubing, after soaking them in water.
[bio27-s3.8.2] Method, step 2: fill tube A with starch solution plus amylase solution. Fill tube B, the control (the comparison with no enzyme), with the same volume of starch solution plus water (or boiled amylase).
[bio27-s3.8.3] Method, step 3: tie the open ends, rinse the outsides, and place each tube in a separate boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C.
[bio27-s3.8.4] Method, step 4: at the start, and again after about 30 minutes, test samples of the water outside each tube with iodine solution (for starch) and with Benedict's solution in a hot water bath (for reducing sugar), using the food tests.
[bio27-s3.8.5] Expected results: the water outside both tubes stays yellow-brown with iodine, so no starch passes out.
[bio27-s3.8.6] After 30 minutes the water outside tube A turns brick red with Benedict's solution, showing reducing sugar, while the water outside tube B stays blue. Inside tube A, a test would show less starch and much reducing sugar.
[bio27-s3.8.7] Conclusion: starch molecules are too large to pass through the membrane. Amylase digests starch to maltose, which is small enough to pass through. This models why food must be digested before it can be absorbed into the blood. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio27-s3.8.8] Safety: wear safety glasses; Benedict's solution is an irritant; take care with hot water; heat the tubes in a water bath, never directly.
[bio27-s3.9.b] A model is useful only if you know where it differs from the real gut. The tubing copies the barrier but little else.
[bio27-s3.9.0] The tubing has no villi or microvilli, so its surface area is far smaller than the gut's.
[bio27-s3.9.1] No blood flows past to carry products away and keep the concentration gradient (the difference in concentration that drives diffusion).
[bio27-s3.9.2] The real gut absorbs some products by active transport (using energy to move molecules), which tubing cannot do.
[bio27-s3.9.3] Only one enzyme and one nutrient are modelled, at a constant pH, whereas the gut uses a sequence of enzymes and pH values.
[bio27-s3.9.4] The tubing does not move food by peristalsis (the waves of muscle contraction that push food along the gut).
[bio27-s3.9.5] Diagram: a boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C, with a knotted length of Visking tubing containing starch and amylase hanging in it; label Visking tubing, starch and amylase, distilled water, water bath at 37 °C; show the control tube beside it.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio27-s5: Diet
[bio27-s5.0.b] Food has to supply energy and raw materials for growth and repair. A balanced diet does this without leaving the body short of anything or overloaded with anything.
[bio27-s5.0.0] A balanced diet contains all the nutrients (carbohydrate, protein, lipid, vitamins and minerals), together with water and dietary fibre, in the right amounts and proportions to meet a person's needs, with enough energy but not too much.
[bio27-s5.0.1] Balanced means right proportions, not equal amounts from each food group. A person needs far more starchy food and vegetables than fats, for example.
[bio27-s5.0.2] Malnutrition is any diet that does not meet the body's needs. Undernutrition is too little energy or too little of a nutrient, e.g. iron-deficiency anaemia or rickets (a bone disease caused by too little vitamin D).
[bio27-s5.0.3] Overnutrition is too much energy or too much of a nutrient, e.g. obesity (a body mass well above the healthy range, from long-term excess energy) or too much salt.
[bio27-s5.0.4] Energy balance: if the energy taken in from food equals the energy used in metabolism (the body's chemical reactions) and activity, body mass stays the same.
[bio27-s5.0.5] Excess energy is stored as fat, causing weight gain. Too little energy means the body uses its glycogen (the short-term carbohydrate store), then fat, and eventually muscle protein, so weight is lost.
[bio27-s5.1.b] The Food Pyramid turns the idea of a balanced diet into a picture of how much of each food group to eat. The wider the shelf, the more servings you should have from it.
[bio27-s5.1.0] Ireland's Food Pyramid (Department of Health, 2016) is a guide to a balanced diet, in which most servings come from the lower shelves.
[bio27-s5.1.1] The lower shelves are vegetables, salad and fruit (5 to 7 servings a day), and wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[bio27-s5.1.2] Milk, yoghurt and cheese (3 servings a day, or 5 for ages 9 to 18, because teenagers are growing fast and need more calcium) and meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (2 servings) are in the middle.
[bio27-s5.1.3] Fats, spreads and oils are for very small amounts, and the top shelf, foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, is not for every day.
[bio27-s5.2.b] Body mass index (BMI) gives a quick number for whether a person's mass is suitable for their height. It is a screening tool and you may be asked to calculate and interpret it.
[bio27-s5.2.0] Body mass index (BMI) is mass in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres.
[bio27-s5.2.1] For adults, the World Health Organization classes 18.5 to 24.9 as a healthy weight, 25 to 29.9 as overweight and 30 or more as obese; below 18.5 is underweight.
[bio27-s5.2.2] BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle, so a muscular athlete can have a high BMI even with little body fat. It also does not show where fat is stored.
[bio27-s5.2.3] Children and teenagers are still growing, so they are assessed with growth charts for their age and sex instead of the adult categories.
[bio27-s5.2.4] Worked example: 70 kg ÷ (1.75 m × 1.75 m) = 70 ÷ 3.06 = 22.9, a healthy weight.
[bio27-s5.3.b] A balanced diet is not the same for everyone. What a person needs depends on their health, their age, their gender and how active they are, and each factor has a biological reason.
[bio27-s5.3.0] Health: illnesses change needs. People with coeliac disease (an immune reaction to gluten, a protein in wheat) must avoid gluten, and people with type 1 diabetes match carbohydrate intake to their insulin. There is more on coeliac disease in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio27-s5.3.1] People with high blood pressure are advised to eat less salt, and people with anaemia need more iron to make haemoglobin; people recovering from injury or surgery need more protein for repair, and people with lactose intolerance avoid or limit lactose (milk sugar) and need other calcium sources.
[bio27-s5.3.2] Age, children and teenagers: growing children need more protein, calcium and vitamin D for bones, and more energy per kilogram of body mass than adults. Babies' needs are met by breast milk or formula.
[bio27-s5.3.3] The teenage growth spurt raises energy, protein, calcium and iron needs, which is why the Food Pyramid gives 5 dairy servings for ages 9 to 18.
[bio27-s5.3.4] Age, older people: metabolic rate and activity usually fall, so less energy is needed. Protein (to limit muscle loss), calcium and vitamin D (to limit bone loss and osteoporosis, brittle bones), fibre (constipation is more common) and water (the sense of thirst weakens with age) remain important.
[bio27-s5.3.5] Gender: on average males have more muscle and a larger body, so they need more energy and protein. Females who menstruate lose iron in blood each month, so they need more iron. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise the need for energy, protein, calcium and iron.
[bio27-s5.3.6] Folic acid (a B vitamin) is advised before and in early pregnancy, and pregnant women should avoid alcohol and liver, which is high in vitamin A.
[bio27-s5.3.7] Activity: active people use more energy, so they need more starchy carbohydrate (builds glycogen stores), protein for muscle repair, and water and salt to replace sweat; inactive (sedentary) people use less energy, so they need less, and eating as though active leads to weight gain.
[bio27-s5.4.b] Eating from every food group is not enough on its own. Eating a range of different foods is what makes sure nothing is missed.
[bio27-s5.4.0] No single food contains every nutrient in the right proportions, so a varied diet from all the food groups is the most reliable way to get all the vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids and essential fatty acids (the ones the body cannot make for itself).
[bio27-s5.4.1] Variety within groups also matters because different fruits and vegetables supply different vitamins, minerals and fibre types, e.g. citrus fruit for vitamin C, green leafy vegetables for iron, calcium and vitamin K, and carrots for beta-carotene.
[bio27-s5.4.2] Combining plant proteins, e.g. beans with cereal, supplies all the essential amino acids, because each food makes up for what the other lacks. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio27-s5.5.b] Water makes up most of the body and is constantly being lost, so a diet is only balanced if it also replaces water.
[bio27-s5.5.0] Water is lost every day in urine, sweat, breath and faeces, and must be replaced from drinks and food. Needs rise in hot weather, during exercise, with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, and when breastfeeding. There is more on the roles of water in Water.
[bio27-s5.5.1] Signs of dehydration (too little water in the body) are thirst, dark-yellow urine in small volumes, headache, tiredness and poor concentration. The urine is dark and scant because the hormone ADH makes the kidneys hold on to water at Higher Level.
[bio27-s5.5.2] Severe dehydration is dangerous, especially in babies and older people.
[bio27-s5.5.3] Water is the best drink. Sugar-sweetened drinks supply water but also free sugars (sugars added to foods and drinks, explained under high-sugar diets below).
[bio27-s5.6.b] Fibre is the part of plant food that passes through you without being digested, yet it is essential for a healthy gut. It supplies almost no energy and is not absorbed.
[bio27-s5.6.0] Dietary fibre, or roughage, is plant material that human enzymes cannot digest, mainly cellulose from plant cell walls. There is more on cellulose in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio27-s5.6.1] Sources of fibre are wholegrain cereals and wholemeal bread, brown rice, vegetables, fruit (especially with skins), beans, peas and lentils, and nuts and seeds. There is no fibre in meat, fish, eggs or dairy produce.
[bio27-s5.6.2] Fibre adds bulk and absorbs water, so faeces are soft and large. This gives the gut muscles something to push against, keeps peristalsis (the waves of muscle contraction that move food along the gut) effective and prevents constipation.
[bio27-s5.6.3] Fibre makes food more filling and slows the absorption of sugar, which helps control body mass and blood glucose.
[bio27-s5.6.4] High-fibre diets are linked to a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer.
[bio27-s5.6.5] Bacteria in the colon ferment some fibre, which supports a healthy gut microbiome (the community of microorganisms living in the gut).
[bio27-s5.6.6] Raising fibre intake suddenly can cause wind and bloating, and fibre needs extra water to work. Very large amounts of bran can reduce the absorption of some minerals such as iron and calcium.
[bio27-s5.7.b] Newspapers and adverts make many claims about food. Researching a claim in steps lets you judge whether the evidence is reliable before you accept it.
[bio27-s5.7.0] 1. Pose a specific, testable question, e.g. 'Does a vegan diet supply enough iron for teenage girls?'
[bio27-s5.7.1] 2. Find several reliable sources, such as public health bodies, peer-reviewed reviews (checked by other scientists before publication) and textbooks. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio27-s5.7.2] 3. Check who funded and wrote each source, and whether it sells a product, because money can bias what is reported.
[bio27-s5.7.3] 4. Judge the type of evidence. Most diet evidence comes from observational studies of large populations, which show links (correlations) but not cause, because people who choose a diet often differ in other ways; vegetarians, for example, on average smoke less and exercise more.
[bio27-s5.7.4] Controlled trials, where researchers assign the diets, give stronger evidence of cause but are usually short and small.
[bio27-s5.7.5] 5. Explain the biological mechanism behind each claim, naming the nutrient and the body process.
[bio27-s5.7.6] 6. Reach a balanced, justified conclusion for a particular group of people.
[bio27-s5.7.7] Example of funding bias: a 1967 review of diet and heart disease, by Harvard scientists in the New England Journal of Medicine, played down sugar and blamed dietary fat. In 2016 it emerged that the Sugar Research Foundation, the sugar industry's trade group, had paid for it without disclosure.
[bio27-s5.8.b] Some people leave animal foods out of their diet. The different types are defined by exactly which foods they exclude, and each has benefits and risks.
[bio27-s5.8.0] A vegetarian diet excludes meat and fish; most vegetarians eat eggs and dairy produce (lacto-ovo vegetarians). A vegan diet excludes all animal products, including eggs, dairy produce and honey. Pescatarians eat fish but not meat.
[bio27-s5.8.1] Vegetarian and vegan diets are usually higher in fibre, fruit and vegetables and lower in saturated fat, so on average blood cholesterol and BMI are lower. Observational studies link them to a lower risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
[bio27-s5.8.2] They avoid processed and red meat, which are linked to bowel cancer.
[bio27-s5.8.3] Producing plant food generally uses less land and water and releases less greenhouse gas than producing meat, which makes these diets more sustainable. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio27-s5.8.4] Ethical reasons include animal welfare.
[bio27-s5.9.b] Leaving out animal foods removes some useful nutrients. These risks call for planning, not avoidance, because each can be replaced by other foods or supplements.
[bio27-s5.9.0] Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods (with nutrients added) or supplements to prevent anaemia and nerve damage. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s5.9.1] Iron from plants is less easily absorbed than iron from meat, so vegans should eat iron-rich plants (beans, lentils, green leafy vegetables and fortified cereals) with vitamin C, which helps absorption.
[bio27-s5.9.2] Vegans must get calcium from fortified plant milks, tofu and green vegetables instead of dairy produce.
[bio27-s5.9.3] Iodine comes mainly from milk and seafood, so vegans may be short of it.
[bio27-s5.9.4] The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids are found mainly in oily fish, so vegans and vegetarians who avoid fish get little of them.
[bio27-s5.9.5] Plant proteins must be combined to supply all the essential amino acids, though this is easily done, e.g. beans with cereal.
[bio27-s5.9.6] Teenagers, pregnant women and young children have the highest needs, so a vegan diet for them needs careful planning.
[bio27-s5.10.b] Evidence about these diets mostly comes from large observational studies. Reading the results carefully means knowing what they can and cannot show.
[bio27-s5.10.0] A large UK study of people's diets and health over many years, EPIC-Oxford, found that vegetarians had a lower rate of heart disease but a slightly higher rate of stroke than meat eaters, and that vegans had more bone fractures.
[bio27-s5.10.1] Observational findings like these show links that may have other causes, because people who choose these diets also differ in other ways.
[bio27-s5.10.2] Judgement: a well-planned vegetarian or vegan diet can meet all needs, but it must replace the nutrients that meat, fish and dairy produce supply.
[bio27-s5.11.b] Not all sugar in food is the same. The recommendation applies only to free sugars, so you need to know exactly which sugars count.
[bio27-s5.11.0] Free sugars are sugars added to food and drink, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice. The sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk is not counted as free sugar.
[bio27-s5.11.1] The World Health Organization (2015) recommends that free sugars make up less than 10% of daily energy intake, and ideally less than 5%.
[bio27-s5.12.b] Eating a lot of free sugar harms health in several ways, but a fair evaluation also gives the case for sugar. The amount and the frequency are what matter.
[bio27-s5.12.0] Tooth decay: plaque bacteria on teeth ferment sugar to acid, which damages tooth enamel. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s5.12.1] Excess energy from sugar is stored as fat, causing weight gain and obesity, which raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and some cancers. Obesity is the main link between sugar and type 2 diabetes.
[bio27-s5.12.2] Sugary foods often supply energy with few other nutrients ('empty' energy) and replace more nutritious foods, and sugary drinks add energy without making a person feel full.
[bio27-s5.12.3] Sugar triggers dopamine release (a brain chemical linked to reward), which can drive overeating. There is more on this in Dopamine and endorphins.
[bio27-s5.12.4] Counter-arguments: glucose is the brain's main fuel, and sugar is a quick energy source, useful for example during long endurance exercise or for a person with diabetes whose blood glucose is too low.
[bio27-s5.12.5] Sugar in whole fruit comes with fibre and vitamins and is not the problem; the risk comes from the amount and how often it is eaten.
[bio27-s5.12.6] Policy link: Ireland introduced a tax on sugar-sweetened drinks in 2018.
[bio27-s5.12.7] Judgement: small amounts of free sugar are harmless; frequent, large amounts harm teeth and raise the risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[bio27-s5.13.b] Fat is a necessary nutrient, but the type and the amount matter. Too much saturated fat is the main concern.
[bio27-s5.13.0] Saturated fats, found in butter, lard, fatty meat and cheese, raise the type of blood cholesterol (LDL) that is deposited in artery walls, narrowing them (atherosclerosis) and raising the risk of heart attack and stroke.
[bio27-s5.13.1] Artificial trans fats, once common in processed foods, are worse than saturated fats.
[bio27-s5.13.2] Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated oils (olive oil, nuts and oily fish) lowers this risk.
[bio27-s5.13.3] Counter-argument: fat is needed for energy, insulation, cell membranes, essential fatty acids and the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K, so a very low-fat diet can cause deficiencies. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s5.13.4] Fat has about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate, so fatty food makes weight gain easy.
[bio27-s5.14.b] People such as body-builders eat extra protein hoping to build muscle. The body does not simply store extra protein as muscle.
[bio27-s5.14.0] Extra protein beyond needs is not stored as muscle. Excess amino acids are deaminated (the nitrogen part is removed) and the urea is excreted, and the rest is used for energy or stored as fat. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio27-s5.14.1] Muscle growth depends on training, with enough protein and energy.
[bio27-s5.14.2] High-protein diets based on processed and red meat bring more saturated fat, and they are often low in fibre.
[bio27-s5.15.b] Processed foods have been changed by manufacture, such as being cooked, refined or packaged. Many fast and processed foods have a poor nutrient profile.
[bio27-s5.15.0] Many processed and fast foods are high in fat, sugar and salt and low in fibre, vitamins and minerals.
[bio27-s5.15.1] Implications include constipation, a higher risk of bowel cancer, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and obesity, and high salt intake linked to high blood pressure. There is more on salt in Minerals.
[bio27-s5.15.2] Counter-point: some processed foods are useful and nutritious, e.g. frozen vegetables, tinned beans and fortified cereals, so the label, not the word processed, is what matters.
[bio27-s5.16.b] Some people cut out dairy produce or gluten. For some this is medically necessary, but for others it is a choice that has costs.
[bio27-s5.16.0] Dairy-free and gluten-free diets are necessary for people with milk allergy, lactose intolerance (where lactose-free milk is another option) or coeliac disease.
[bio27-s5.16.1] For other people they bring no proven benefit and need care, since dairy produce is the main calcium and iodine source in many Irish diets, and gluten-free products are often lower in fibre and more expensive.
[bio27-s5.17.b] Some people try to lose weight fast by skipping meals or by eating very little (crash diets). This has several biological drawbacks.
[bio27-s5.17.0] Skipping meals and very low-energy (crash) diets run down glycogen stores, leading to tiredness and poor concentration.
[bio27-s5.17.1] The body breaks down muscle protein as well as fat, nutrients are likely to be missed, and weight lost quickly is often regained.

CANDIDATES bio31-s7: Energy and nutrient transfer
[bio31-s7.0.b] Every ecosystem sorts its organisms by how they get energy and nutrients. Producers bring energy in, consumers pass it along, and decomposers return the nutrients to the soil or water.
[bio31-s7.0.0] Producers are autotrophs: organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly by photosynthesis, using light energy. They include green plants, algae, phytoplankton (microscopic floating algae) and photosynthetic bacteria.
[bio31-s7.0.1] Producers are the entry point of energy and matter into every food chain, because no other group can turn light energy and inorganic substances into organic food. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio31-s7.0.2] Consumers are heterotrophs, meaning they cannot make their own food and obtain energy and nutrients by eating other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers, e.g. rabbits, slugs, caterpillars and limpets.
[bio31-s7.0.3] Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, e.g. thrushes, frogs and crabs. Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers, e.g. sparrowhawks, foxes and herons.
[bio31-s7.0.4] A top (apex) consumer is at the end of a chain and is not normally eaten. Carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat plants and animals, e.g. badgers, foxes and humans.
[bio31-s7.0.5] Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, feed on dead organisms and waste. They secrete enzymes onto the dead matter and absorb the products (saprophytic nutrition), and so release minerals and CO₂ for producers to reuse.
[bio31-s7.0.6] Detritivores are animals that eat dead organic matter (detritus), e.g. earthworms and woodlice. They break it into smaller pieces, which gives decomposers a larger surface to act on, so decomposition is faster. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio31-s7.1.b] A food chain shows who eats whom, and each step is a trophic (feeding) level. Following the chain shows how energy and nutrients are passed on from one organism to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.0] A trophic (feeding) level is the position an organism occupies in a food chain, according to how it obtains its energy. Producers are trophic level 1, primary consumers level 2, secondary consumers level 3 and tertiary consumers level 4.
[bio31-s7.1.1] A food chain is a sequence of organisms in which each is eaten by the next, showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one trophic level to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.2] Arrows point from the organism that is eaten towards the organism that eats it, which is the direction of energy flow ("is eaten by").
[bio31-s7.1.3] Irish examples, one per ecosystem. Grassland: grass → rabbit → fox. Hedgerow or woodland: oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk. Rocky shore: seaweed → periwinkle → crab → herring gull.
[bio31-s7.1.4] Two more Irish examples. Freshwater: phytoplankton → water flea → minnow → heron (or pike). Sea: phytoplankton → zooplankton → herring → seal.
[bio31-s7.1.5] Grazing food chains start with living producers. Detritus (decomposer) food chains start with dead organic matter, e.g. dead leaves → earthworm → blackbird. Much of the energy in a woodland passes through detritus chains.
[bio31-s7.1.6] Food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels, because so much energy is lost at each level that too little is left to support a further level.
[bio31-s7.2.b] Real organisms rarely eat just one thing or get eaten by just one thing. A food web joins the separate food chains of an ecosystem into one network, which is why it is a more realistic picture.
[bio31-s7.2.0] A food web is a set of interlinked food chains in an ecosystem. It is more realistic than a single chain because most organisms eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator.
[bio31-s7.2.1] An organism may also feed at more than one trophic level, as an omnivore does, or as a fox does when it eats rabbits and also beetles.
[bio31-s7.2.2] Example grassland and hedgerow web to draw: grass → rabbit, grass → slug and grass → caterpillar; grass seeds → wood mouse; slug → thrush and caterpillar → thrush; rabbit → fox; wood mouse → fox and wood mouse → barn owl; thrush → sparrowhawk.
[bio31-s7.2.3] Decomposers (bacteria, fungi and earthworms) feed on dead material from every level of this web, because every organism eventually dies or produces waste.
[bio31-s7.2.4] Food webs show interdependence: a change in one population affects many others, directly through feeding and indirectly through competition for the same food. For example, the fox and the barn owl both eat wood mice, so they compete.
[bio31-s7.2.5] Diagram: the food web drawn with arrows pointing towards the consumer, organisms placed in rows by trophic level, and decomposers shown at the side receiving arrows from all levels.
[bio31-s7.3.b] Energy enters an ecosystem once, as sunlight, and then dwindles as it is passed along a food chain. Most of it is lost at every step, and this explains both the shape of food chains and how we should feed people.
[bio31-s7.3.0] Energy enters ecosystems as light. Producers capture only a small fraction of the light that reaches them, around 1–2%, and store it as chemical energy in organic molecules. The rest is reflected, passes through leaves, or is lost as heat.
[bio31-s7.3.1] On average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to, and stored in, the next. The figure varies, roughly from 5% to 20%, so about 90% is lost at each transfer.
[bio31-s7.3.2] Respiration releases energy for movement, growth, active transport and keeping warm, and this ends up as heat lost to the surroundings. Birds and mammals lose the most, because they keep a constant body temperature.
[bio31-s7.3.3] Roots, bark, wood, bones and fur are left uneaten. Undigested food is egested as faeces, e.g. cellulose eaten by herbivores, and some energy is lost in excretory products such as urea. The energy in uneaten parts, faeces and dead bodies passes to decomposers, not to the next level.
[bio31-s7.3.4] Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuous input of energy from the Sun.
[bio31-s7.3.5] Worked example (illustrative, using the 10% figure, not measured data): grass in a field stores 10 000 kJ, the rabbits that eat it store about 1 000 kJ, and the foxes about 100 kJ. A fourth level would store about 10 kJ, too little to support a population.
[bio31-s7.3.6] So there are fewer top predators, they often need large territories, and they are especially vulnerable to habitat loss.
[bio31-s7.3.7] Consequence for food production: eating plants directly, a shorter chain, uses the energy captured by crops more efficiently than feeding crops to animals and then eating the animals. A given area of land can therefore feed more people on plant-based food.
[bio31-s7.4.b] Matter, unlike energy, passes along a food chain in the form of the food organisms eat. Some is lost at each level, but the ecosystem as a whole does not lose it, because decomposers send it back to producers.
[bio31-s7.4.0] Nutrients (matter) pass between trophic levels when one organism eats another. The carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids of the food are digested, absorbed and built into the consumer's own body (assimilated).
[bio31-s7.4.1] The elements involved are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (in all biomolecules), nitrogen (in proteins and nucleic acids), phosphorus (in DNA, ATP and phospholipids), and minerals such as calcium and iron.
[bio31-s7.4.2] The amount of matter passed on also decreases at each level. Much of the food eaten is respired (carbon leaves as CO₂, hydrogen as water), egested in faeces, or excreted (nitrogen as urea or ammonia). So biomass (the mass of living material) also decreases up a food chain.
[bio31-s7.4.3] Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the ecosystem. Decomposers break down dead bodies, faeces and urine, releasing CO₂ and minerals such as nitrate, phosphate and ammonium that producers absorb and reuse, so nutrients cycle.
[bio31-s7.4.4] Contrast: energy flows one way through the ecosystem and is lost as heat, whereas nutrients cycle and are reused again and again.
[bio31-s7.4.5] Persistent substances that are not broken down or excreted, such as the insecticide DDT and mercury, pass up the food chain with the food and build up in the bodies of organisms at each level. They reach the highest concentrations in top consumers, which is called biomagnification.
[bio31-s7.4.6] Biomagnification is how DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey, which sit at the top of food chains and so carry the highest doses.
[bio31-s7.4.7] Diagram: a flow diagram of one food chain, with energy arrows getting narrower at each level, heat-loss arrows from every level, and a separate loop of nutrient arrows from all levels to decomposers and back to producers.
[bio31-s7.5.b] A food chain or web is a model: a simplified picture that lets you work out what happens when one population goes up or down. Following a set method keeps the prediction logical.
[bio31-s7.5.0] The method has five steps. 1. Find the organisms that eat the changed species (its predators).
[bio31-s7.5.1] 2. Find what the changed species eats (its food).
[bio31-s7.5.2] 3. Find its competitors, the species that share its food.
[bio31-s7.5.3] 4. Predict the direction of each change, giving reasons.
[bio31-s7.5.4] 5. Note other effects, e.g. on plants and habitat.
[bio31-s7.5.5] Worked prediction using the food web above, if a disease such as myxomatosis (a viral disease of rabbits) kills most of the rabbits: (a) grass is grazed less and grows taller, providing more food and cover for slugs, caterpillars and wood mice.
[bio31-s7.5.6] Continued: (b) foxes lose their main prey, so they eat more wood mice and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds, and fox numbers may fall. (c) Wood mice decline from the extra fox predation, so barn owls, which compete with foxes for wood mice, have less food.
[bio31-s7.5.7] Real outcomes depend on how flexible each predator's diet is, because a predator that can switch to other food will be affected less.
[bio31-s7.6.b] Changes spread through a web both when a species is removed and when one is added. Because the model is simplified, its predictions have limits.
[bio31-s7.6.0] Removing a top predator can cause a trophic cascade, a chain of effects passing down the food web. In Ireland, with no wolves, deer numbers increase, they browse young trees, and the woodland cannot regenerate, which affects every species that depends on the woodland.
[bio31-s7.6.1] Adding a species also spreads through a web. An invasive filter-feeder such as the zebra mussel removes phytoplankton at the base of a lake food web, reducing food for zooplankton and for the fish that eat them. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio31-s7.6.2] Food chains and webs simplify real feeding, because many links are left out and diets change with the season and with age.
[bio31-s7.6.3] They usually show no numbers or amounts of energy, which pyramids do, and decomposers are often left out. There is more on models in general in Models.

CANDIDATES bio33-s4: The microbiome
[bio33-s4.0.b] Your body, and every patch of soil, is home to huge numbers of tiny organisms that live together rather than alone. Biologists treat that whole community as one thing, called a microbiome.
[bio33-s4.0.0] A microbiome is the whole community of microorganisms (bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists and viruses) living together in a particular environment, such as the human gut, the skin or the soil. It is a community of many kinds, not a single species and not just bacteria.
[bio33-s4.0.1] A microbiome also includes the genes of these microorganisms and the products they make.
[bio33-s4.0.2] The organisms themselves are often called the microbiota; many people use the two words for the same thing, so don't be thrown if a question uses either.
[bio33-s4.0.3] A microbiome is an ecological community (different species living and interacting in one place): its members compete, feed on each other's products and interact with their host or surroundings.
[bio33-s4.0.4] The make-up of a microbiome changes with conditions such as food, pH, oxygen, temperature and water, because each species thrives only in certain conditions.
[bio33-s4.0.5] A diverse microbiome, with many species, is generally more stable and recovers better after a disturbance, because if one species is lost others can often do its job.
[bio33-s4.0.6] Most microorganisms are not pathogens (disease-causing): the majority in a healthy microbiome are harmless (commensals, which gain without harming the host) or beneficial (mutualists, where both sides gain). There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio33-s4.1.b] A microbiome is hard to study because you can't simply grow its members in a dish. Scientists read the DNA of the whole community at once instead.
[bio33-s4.1.0] Most of these microorganisms cannot be grown on agar (the jelly used to culture microbes in the laboratory), so culturing would miss most of the community.
[bio33-s4.1.1] Microbiomes are therefore studied mainly by extracting all the DNA from a sample, e.g. faeces or soil.
[bio33-s4.1.2] The extracted DNA is sequenced to identify which organisms and genes are present, so the community can be described without growing any of it. There is more on this in DNA sequencing.
[bio33-s4.2.b] Humans are not microbe-free. Several parts of the body carry their own microbiomes, and the biggest by far is in the gut.
[bio33-s4.2.0] Humans carry microbiomes in the gut, mouth, nose, skin and vagina.
[bio33-s4.2.1] The largest is in the gut, and most of the gut's microorganisms live in the large intestine (colon), where food moves slowly and there is almost no oxygen, so most are anaerobic bacteria (bacteria that live without oxygen).
[bio33-s4.2.2] A 2016 estimate found that the body contains about as many bacterial cells (about 38 trillion) as human cells (about 30 trillion), a ratio of roughly 1 : 1.
[bio33-s4.2.3] The older, often-quoted figure of ten bacteria for every human cell is now thought to be an overestimate.
[bio33-s4.2.4] The gut contains hundreds of species, and each person's combination is different.
[bio33-s4.3.b] A baby starts life with almost no microbiome and builds one from its first contacts. How it is born and fed shapes which microorganisms arrive first.
[bio33-s4.3.0] A baby's gut is colonised (first settled by microorganisms) at birth and soon after.
[bio33-s4.3.1] Babies born vaginally pick up microorganisms from the mother's vagina and gut.
[bio33-s4.3.2] Babies born by caesarean section are first colonised mostly by skin and hospital microorganisms, so their early gut microbiome is different.
[bio33-s4.3.3] Breast milk contains sugars (oligosaccharides) that the baby cannot digest but that feed beneficial gut bacteria such as Bifidobacterium.
[bio33-s4.3.4] The microbiome changes with diet, weaning (the switch from milk to solid food), antibiotics, environment (e.g. pets, farm animals, siblings) and age, becoming more stable in childhood.
[bio33-s4.3.5] The host provides a warm, moist, food-rich, stable home, and the microorganisms provide digestion, vitamins and protection.
[bio33-s4.3.6] This mutualism (a relationship in which both partners benefit) is an example of how organisms depend on each other.
[bio33-s4.4.b] Humans can't digest everything they eat, but gut bacteria can. They turn part of our undigested food into useful products.
[bio33-s4.4.0] Human enzymes cannot digest dietary fibre, e.g. cellulose and other plant cell-wall polysaccharides, and resistant starch, so it reaches the large intestine undigested.
[bio33-s4.4.1] There, gut bacteria ferment the fibre anaerobically (break it down without oxygen), using their own enzymes.
[bio33-s4.4.2] The main products of fermentation are short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs): acetate, propionate and butyrate.
[bio33-s4.4.3] The colon absorbs the SCFAs: butyrate is the main energy source of the cells lining the colon, which keeps the lining healthy.
[bio33-s4.4.4] The other SCFAs are carried to the liver and body cells and used for energy.
[bio33-s4.4.5] Gases, namely CO₂, hydrogen and, in some people, methane made by archaea, are by-products of fermentation.
[bio33-s4.5.b] Fermentation is not the only service gut bacteria provide. They also make vitamins and handle substances our own enzymes can't.
[bio33-s4.5.0] Gut bacteria make vitamin K, needed for blood clotting, and some B vitamins, part of which the body absorbs.
[bio33-s4.5.1] Gut bacteria also break down some substances that human enzymes cannot, e.g. some plant chemicals and some drugs.
[bio33-s4.5.2] Gut bacteria convert bile salts (made by the liver to help digest fats), which affects how fats are absorbed.
[bio33-s4.5.3] Herbivores depend even more on their microbiome: cattle, sheep and rabbits rely on microorganisms to digest cellulose, which their own enzymes cannot do.
[bio33-s4.5.4] The large intestine, where most of this happens, also absorbs water from the remaining food. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio33-s4.6.b] A gut full of harmless bacteria leaves little room for invaders. This crowding-out effect protects the host and is called colonisation resistance.
[bio33-s4.6.0] Protection against pathogens is called colonisation resistance: the normal gut microbiota occupy the space and attachment sites on the gut lining and use up the nutrients that pathogens would need.
[bio33-s4.6.1] Some gut microorganisms make acids that lower the pH, and some make antibacterial proteins (bacteriocins) that kill competing bacteria.
[bio33-s4.6.2] The skin and vaginal microbiota protect in the same way.
[bio33-s4.6.3] In the vagina, Lactobacillus bacteria make lactic acid, keeping the pH low, which stops many pathogens growing. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio33-s4.7.b] Colonisation resistance only works while the normal bacteria are present. If they are wiped out, harmful microorganisms can move in.
[bio33-s4.7.0] When antibiotics kill many normal gut bacteria, pathogens can take over: e.g. the bacterium Clostridioides difficile can multiply and cause severe diarrhoea.
[bio33-s4.7.1] The yeast Candida can also overgrow when the normal microbiota are reduced, causing thrush. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s4.7.2] Repeated C. difficile infection can be treated by faecal microbiota transplant, which restores a healthy microbiome from a donor.
[bio33-s4.8.b] The gut microbiome also talks to the immune system. Chemical signals from the microorganisms help the body learn what to attack and what to ignore.
[bio33-s4.8.0] The gut wall contains a large part of the body's immune tissue, so the microorganisms are in constant contact with immune cells.
[bio33-s4.8.1] Molecules from gut microorganisms, e.g. parts of their cell walls and SCFAs such as butyrate, are detected by immune cells in the gut lining, which then send signals.
[bio33-s4.8.2] In early life these signals 'train' the immune system to tolerate harmless microorganisms and food while still responding to pathogens.
[bio33-s4.8.3] E.g. butyrate promotes suppressor T cells (also called regulatory T cells), which damp down inflammation. There is more on this in White blood cells.
[bio33-s4.9.b] The microbiome also helps keep the gut wall intact. Evidence for its role in immunity is real, but much of it shows links rather than proof of cause.
[bio33-s4.9.0] The microbiome helps maintain the gut barrier: it stimulates the lining to make mucus and keeps its cells tightly joined.
[bio33-s4.9.1] This stops bacteria and their products leaking into the blood and triggering inflammation.
[bio33-s4.9.2] Germ-free mice, raised completely free of microorganisms, have poorly developed gut immune tissue, which suggests the microbiome is needed for normal immune development.
[bio33-s4.9.3] A less diverse or altered gut microbiome has been linked to inflammatory bowel disease, allergies, asthma and some autoimmune diseases (where the immune system attacks the body's own cells), which fits the hygiene hypothesis. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio33-s4.9.4] These links are mostly correlations: it is often not known whether the altered microbiome causes the disease or results from it.
[bio33-s4.10.b] Metabolic health is about how the body handles energy. The gut microbiome influences it in three main ways.
[bio33-s4.10.0] Metabolic health means healthy body mass, blood glucose, blood fats and blood pressure, without obesity or type 2 diabetes.
[bio33-s4.10.1] First, it affects how much energy is extracted from food: more efficient fermentation of fibre releases more energy as SCFAs.
[bio33-s4.10.2] Second, signals from SCFAs affect appetite hormones and how the body stores fat and controls blood glucose.
[bio33-s4.10.3] Third, inflammation from a leaky gut barrier (one that lets bacteria and their products into the blood) is linked to insulin resistance, where cells respond poorly to insulin and blood glucose stays high.
[bio33-s4.11.b] Animal experiments suggest the microbiome can affect body fat, but human evidence is weaker. Diet is a major factor in shaping the microbiome itself.
[bio33-s4.11.0] In a 2006 study, germ-free mice given gut microorganisms from obese mice gained more body fat than germ-free mice given microorganisms from lean mice, on the same diet.
[bio33-s4.11.1] This shows the microbiome can affect fat storage, at least in mice, because diet was the same in both groups.
[bio33-s4.11.2] In humans, people with obesity or type 2 diabetes often have a less diverse gut microbiome, but this is a correlation, partly explained by differences in diet.
[bio33-s4.11.3] A diet high in varied fibre from vegetables, fruit, pulses and whole grains feeds fibre-fermenting bacteria and is linked to a more diverse microbiome and more SCFAs.
[bio33-s4.11.4] A diet high in sugar, fat and processed food and low in fibre is linked to lower diversity.
[bio33-s4.12.b] A healthy microbiome is balanced, and that balance can be lost. Some everyday habits protect it.
[bio33-s4.12.0] Dysbiosis is an imbalance in the microbiome, e.g. a loss of diversity or of beneficial species, or overgrowth of harmful ones.
[bio33-s4.12.1] Causes of dysbiosis include antibiotics, which kill beneficial as well as harmful bacteria, a low-fibre diet, infection, stress and some medicines.
[bio33-s4.12.2] Eat plenty of varied fibre to promote a healthy gut microbiome, because fibre feeds the fermenting bacteria.
[bio33-s4.12.3] Eat fermented foods containing live microorganisms, e.g. live yogurt or kefir.
[bio33-s4.12.4] Take antibiotics only when needed and complete the course as prescribed; this also reduces antibiotic resistance. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s4.13.b] Two similar-sounding words describe two different ways of supporting gut bacteria: adding live microorganisms, or feeding the ones already there.
[bio33-s4.13.0] Probiotics are live microorganisms taken in food or supplements, e.g. Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species, to benefit health.
[bio33-s4.13.1] Prebiotics are food components that humans cannot digest but that feed beneficial gut bacteria, e.g. some fibres in onions, garlic, leeks, bananas and oats.
[bio33-s4.13.2] Evidence that probiotics help is good for some conditions, e.g. reducing diarrhoea caused by antibiotics, but weak or mixed for many claims.
[bio33-s4.13.3] Most probiotics do not permanently change the microbiome.
[bio33-s4.14.b] A gram of fertile soil holds hundreds of millions to billions of bacteria, so its microorganisms cycle many nutrients, and carbon is the first example.
[bio33-s4.14.0] A single gram of fertile soil can contain hundreds of millions to billions of bacteria, many metres of fungal hyphae (thread-like fungal filaments) and thousands of species.
[bio33-s4.14.1] Together these microorganisms cycle many nutrients, not just one.
[bio33-s4.14.2] Carbon: bacteria and fungi decompose dead plants, animals and dung, respiring the organic carbon and releasing CO₂.
[bio33-s4.14.3] Part of the dead matter is turned into stable humus, which stores carbon in the soil for many years. There is more on this in The carbon cycle.
[bio33-s4.14.4] Fungi are the main decomposers of wood and of the tough lignin and cellulose of dead plants.
[bio33-s4.15.b] Plants need nitrogen in a form they can absorb, and soil microorganisms supply it at several steps.
[bio33-s4.15.0] Decomposers release ammonium from proteins in dead matter.
[bio33-s4.15.1] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria, both free-living and in legume root nodules, add nitrogen from the air.
[bio33-s4.15.2] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, the main form plants absorb.
[bio33-s4.15.3] Denitrifying bacteria in waterlogged soils convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, and some nitrous oxide, N₂O, is released. There is more on this in The nitrogen cycle.
[bio33-s4.16.b] Soil microorganisms also free up phosphorus and sulfur for plants, and they control whether soil gives off or takes up methane.
[bio33-s4.16.0] Phosphorus: some soil bacteria and fungi release phosphate from organic matter and from insoluble minerals.
[bio33-s4.16.1] Mycorrhizal fungi (fungi living in partnership with plant roots) carry phosphate and water to plant roots in exchange for sugars. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio33-s4.16.2] Sulfur: decomposers release sulfur from proteins as sulfate, which plants absorb.
[bio33-s4.16.3] Methane: in waterlogged soils and bogs, where there is no oxygen, methane-producing archaea release methane.
[bio33-s4.16.4] In drier, aerated soils other bacteria oxidise methane, so soils can be a source or a sink of methane.
[bio33-s4.17.b] Beyond cycling nutrients, soil microorganisms shape the soil itself and affect how well plants grow. Farming methods can help or harm them.
[bio33-s4.17.0] Soil structure: fungal hyphae bind soil particles together, and bacteria make sticky polysaccharides, forming crumbs, called aggregates, with air spaces between them.
[bio33-s4.17.1] This structure improves drainage and aeration and reduces erosion.
[bio33-s4.17.2] A healthy, diverse soil microbiome suppresses some plant diseases, by competition and by antibiotic production.
[bio33-s4.17.3] Some bacteria and fungi promote plant growth, e.g. nitrogen fixers and mycorrhizae.
[bio33-s4.17.4] Intensive farming, with heavy use of artificial fertiliser and pesticides, frequent ploughing and compaction, can reduce soil microbial diversity.
[bio33-s4.17.5] Practices such as adding manure or compost, growing clover, reduced ploughing and crop rotation support soil microbial diversity. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio33-s4.18.b] Soil microorganisms respire faster when it is warmer. Because soils hold so much carbon, this can feed back into climate warming.
[bio33-s4.18.0] A rise in temperature speeds up the enzyme-controlled respiration of soil microorganisms, up to their optimum (the temperature at which their enzymes work best), so they decompose soil organic matter faster and release more CO₂.
[bio33-s4.18.1] Long-term field experiments have measured extra loss of soil carbon as CO₂ from warmed plots: forest soil plots are warmed by buried heating cables and compared with unheated plots.
[bio33-s4.18.2] Soils store more carbon than the atmosphere and all plants together, so even a small proportional loss matters.
[bio33-s4.18.3] Extra CO₂ released by warming adds to the greenhouse effect, which causes more warming, which speeds decomposition further.
[bio33-s4.18.4] This positive feedback (a change that drives more of the same change) is a major uncertainty in climate predictions.
[bio33-s4.19.b] Two kinds of waterlogged or frozen ground hold carbon that microorganisms can't normally break down. Warming can release it.
[bio33-s4.19.0] In the Arctic, frozen soils, called permafrost, store huge amounts of undecomposed organic matter.
[bio33-s4.19.1] As permafrost thaws, microorganisms decompose this matter, releasing CO₂ and, in waterlogged thawed soil, methane, a more powerful greenhouse gas.
[bio33-s4.19.2] Irish bogs store carbon as peat, because waterlogging stops decomposition.
[bio33-s4.19.3] Warmer, drier summers, and draining, lower the water table and let air in, so aerobic microorganisms decompose the peat and release CO₂.
[bio33-s4.19.4] Warmer waterlogged peat also releases more methane. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s4.20.b] Warming and drought also change nutrient cycling and which microorganisms dominate. How big the long-term effect will be is still being researched.
[bio33-s4.20.0] Warming can speed up the release of ammonium and nitrate, giving plants more nutrients in the short term but causing more leaching (washing out by water) of nitrate.
[bio33-s4.20.1] Warming can also increase the nitrous oxide, a greenhouse gas, released by nitrifying and denitrifying bacteria.
[bio33-s4.20.2] Drought reduces microbial activity, and rewetting dry soil causes a sudden burst of CO₂ release.
[bio33-s4.20.3] Warming and drought change which species dominate, e.g. the balance of bacteria and fungi, which can change how fast nutrients are cycled.
[bio33-s4.20.4] In some long-term experiments the extra CO₂ release declined after some years, because the easily decomposed carbon was used up or the microbial community adjusted (acclimatised).
[bio33-s4.20.5] How large the long-term feedback will be is still being researched.
[bio33-s4.21.b] Aim: to show that soil microorganisms release CO₂ by respiration, and that they do so faster when warmer.
[bio33-s4.21.0] Independent variable: temperature, e.g. 5 °C, 15 °C and 25 °C. The dependent variable is the time the indicator takes to change colour. Equal masses of the same fresh soil keep other variables constant.
[bio33-s4.21.1] 1. Put equal masses, e.g. 20 g, of the same fresh soil into sealed containers, each with a small open tube of hydrogencarbonate indicator (or limewater) suspended above the soil, not touching it.
[bio33-s4.21.2] 2. Set up a control with the same mass of soil that has been sterilised by heating in an oven, so its microorganisms are dead.
[bio33-s4.21.3] 3. Keep sets at different temperatures, e.g. 5 °C, 15 °C and 25 °C, in the dark.
[bio33-s4.21.4] 4. Record the time for the indicator to change from red to yellow (more CO₂), or for the limewater to turn milky.
[bio33-s4.21.5] Expected result: the colour changes fastest at the highest temperature, and not, or very little, in the sterilised control. The control shows the CO₂ comes from living microorganisms.
[bio33-s4.21.6] Keep the apparatus in the dark, so that no photosynthesis by algae or seedlings removes CO₂.
[bio33-s4.21.7] Safety: wash hands after handling soil.
[bio33-s4.22.b] Two skills matter for microbiome questions: reading the CO₂ indicator, and judging published studies.
[bio33-s4.22.0] Hydrogencarbonate indicator colours: red (orange-red) at normal air CO₂ levels, yellow when CO₂ rises, and purple when CO₂ falls.
[bio33-s4.22.1] Secondary data to explore include published results of microbiome sequencing studies, e.g. comparisons of the gut microbiomes of people on different diets, or of babies born vaginally and by caesarean section.
[bio33-s4.22.2] Long-term soil-warming experiments and soil carbon measurements are also secondary data to explore.
[bio33-s4.22.3] When evaluating these data, check the sample size, whether the study was in humans or animals, and whether it shows correlation or cause. There is more on this in Evaluating evidence.
