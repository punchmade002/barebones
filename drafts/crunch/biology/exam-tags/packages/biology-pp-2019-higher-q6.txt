PART (a) (8 marks) subsections: bio28-s1, bio29-s5
MODEL ANSWER: The pulmonary artery.

PART (b) (6 marks) subsections: bio28-s1
MODEL ANSWER: 1. The alveolus wall is very thin (one cell thick), so the diffusion distance is short.
2. The alveoli have a very large surface area (about 300 million of them), so a lot of gas can diffuse at once.
3. The capillary wall is one cell thick and in close contact with the alveolus, so the gases have only a short distance to diffuse.

PART (c) (6 marks) subsections: bio28-s1, bio24-s2, bio33-s2
MODEL ANSWER: Name: Asthma.
Cause: an allergen such as pollen or mould spores triggers it; the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts and the lining swells and makes extra mucus, narrowing the airways.
Treatment: an inhaler, which delivers a drug that relaxes the smooth muscle in the bronchioles.

CANDIDATES bio24-s2: Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants
[bio24-s2.0.b] A flower's structure follows from how its pollen travels. Insects must be lured in and rewarded, so insect-pollinated flowers advertise; the wind needs no persuading, so wind-pollinated flowers drop the advertising. Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second, and the flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio24-s2.0.0] Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides (markings that lead the insect towards the nectar), to attract insects.
[bio24-s2.0.1] Wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, because there is no animal to attract and large petals would block pollen in the wind.
[bio24-s2.0.2] Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries (the glands that produce nectar, a sugary liquid), so the insect is rewarded for visiting.
[bio24-s2.0.3] Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries, because there is no visitor to reward.
[bio24-s2.0.4] Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters, so that insects can spot them.
[bio24-s2.0.5] Wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins (dangling spikes of tiny flowers) or spikes held high above the leaves, where the wind can reach them.
[bio24-s2.0.6] Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
[bio24-s2.0.7] Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
[bio24-s2.1.b] The anther (the pollen-producing part of the stamen, held up on a stalk called the filament) and the stigma (the sticky or feathery top of the carpel that receives pollen) are positioned to suit the carrier. Insects touch the flower's parts; the wind blows past them.
[bio24-s2.1.0] Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them, so pollen rubs onto its body.
[bio24-s2.1.1] Wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
[bio24-s2.1.2] Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them and pollen sticks to them.
[bio24-s2.1.3] Wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s2.1.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of a buttercup or wallflower half-flower (large petal, nectary, anther inside, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas, anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
[bio24-s2.2.b] The pollen itself also differs. Insects deliver pollen accurately, whereas the wind scatters it at random, and the grains are built for the different journeys.
[bio24-s2.2.0] Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower.
[bio24-s2.2.1] Wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species, so many grains are needed for a few to land.
[bio24-s2.2.2] Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs.
[bio24-s2.2.3] Wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump together.
[bio24-s2.2.4] Diagram: an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.
[bio24-s2.3.b] This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.
[bio24-s2.3.0] Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
[bio24-s2.3.1] Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
[bio24-s2.3.2] What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features (recorded in words) are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface.
[bio24-s2.3.3] Quantitative features (measured as numbers) are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
[bio24-s2.3.4] Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen, so every flower is at the same stage. Examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements, to reduce the effect of one unusual flower.
[bio24-s2.3.5] Measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification, so the two types can be compared fairly.
[bio24-s2.3.6] Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.3.7] Also needed: microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.
[bio24-s2.4.b] The method moves from the whole flower, to its separated parts, to the pollen under the microscope, and repeats everything for the second flower type.
[bio24-s2.4.0] 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
[bio24-s2.4.1] 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
[bio24-s2.4.2] 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
[bio24-s2.4.3] 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
[bio24-s2.4.4] 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
[bio24-s2.4.5] 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
[bio24-s2.4.6] 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power.
[bio24-s2.4.7] 8. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
[bio24-s2.5.b] Scalpels, pollen, some plants and glass slides each carry a risk, so the practical needs a precaution for each.
[bio24-s2.5.0] Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely, so nobody is cut.
[bio24-s2.5.1] Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
[bio24-s2.5.2] Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
[bio24-s2.5.3] Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
[bio24-s2.5.4] Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.
[bio24-s2.6.b] Recording results in a table puts the two flowers side by side, so each difference can be seen and then explained.
[bio24-s2.6.0] Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
[bio24-s2.6.1] Expected primary data (collected by you) for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.6.2] Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.7.b] A conclusion links what was seen to why it matters for pollination, and secondary data add evidence the class could not collect itself.
[bio24-s2.7.0] Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations above. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio24-s2.7.1] Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen.
[bio24-s2.7.2] Other secondary data: published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; and labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
[bio24-s2.7.3] Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio24-s2.8.b] Every practical has weaknesses. Each limitation below has a matching improvement, and you should be able to give both.
[bio24-s2.8.0] Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
[bio24-s2.8.1] Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
[bio24-s2.8.2] Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.8.3] Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes.
[bio24-s2.8.4] The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
[bio24-s2.8.5] Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective, because different people judge differently; have two people record them.
[bio24-s2.8.6] Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

CANDIDATES bio28-s1: Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism
[bio28-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a steady supply of oxygen and must get rid of the carbon dioxide it makes. The breathing system is the body's way of swapping these gases with the air.
[bio28-s1.0.0] Every cell respires aerobically (releases energy from food using oxygen), so every cell uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s1.0.1] The breathing system exchanges gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid, the fluid that bathes the cells): oxygen is taken into the blood, and carbon dioxide and some water vapour are removed.
[bio28-s1.0.2] Breathing, or ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. It is not respiration, which is the chemical release of energy inside cells, and gas exchange is not respiration either.
[bio28-s1.0.3] A large animal such as a human cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin, because its surface area is too small for its volume and the distances to its inner cells are too great.
[bio28-s1.0.4] So it needs a specialised exchange surface, the alveoli (tiny air sacs in the lungs), and a transport system, the blood, to carry gases to and from every cell.
[bio28-s1.1.b] The breathing tract is the pathway air follows from outside to the lungs. The first parts clean, warm and moisten the air and keep food out of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.0] Nose (nasal cavity): hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and microorganisms. The lining has a rich blood supply that warms the air, and the mucus moistens it, so the delicate alveoli do not dry out or chill.
[bio28-s1.1.1] Breathing through the mouth skips this filtering, warming and moistening, which is why nose breathing is better for the lungs.
[bio28-s1.1.2] Pharynx (throat): the passage behind the nose and mouth that is shared by air and food.
[bio28-s1.1.3] Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage (firm, flexible tissue) that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink pass into the oesophagus (the food tube) instead of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.4] Choking happens when food enters the trachea instead.
[bio28-s1.1.5] Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea. It contains the vocal cords, which vibrate as air passes over them, producing sound.
[bio28-s1.2.b] Below the larynx the airway divides again and again, like an upside-down tree, until it reaches the alveoli where gases actually cross into the blood.
[bio28-s1.2.0] Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation.
[bio28-s1.2.1] The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
[bio28-s1.2.2] Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium (a surface layer of cells carrying tiny hair-like cilia) with mucus-secreting goblet cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms.
[bio28-s1.2.3] The cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the body's first line of defence against infection.
[bio28-s1.2.4] Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one to each lung. They are also supported by cartilage.
[bio28-s1.2.5] Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow.
[bio28-s1.2.6] The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels). The alveoli are the site of gas exchange.
[bio28-s1.3.b] The lungs have no muscle of their own, so they depend on the chest wall and on the muscles around it. The pleural membranes are what link the lungs to the chest wall.
[bio28-s1.3.0] The two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (ribs, sternum and vertebrae) and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm.
[bio28-s1.3.1] Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move.
[bio28-s1.3.2] The fluid also makes the lungs cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
[bio28-s1.3.3] Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax.
[bio28-s1.3.4] Intercostal muscles: two sets, external and internal, lying between the ribs, which move the rib cage.
[bio28-s1.3.5] Diagram: the breathing system in a front view of the chest. Label the nasal cavity, pharynx, epiglottis, larynx, trachea with cartilage rings, two bronchi, bronchioles, lungs, pleural membranes, ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm and an outline of the heart. Add an enlarged inset of alveoli wrapped in capillaries.
[bio28-s1.4.b] Breathing in works by changing pressure. The muscles enlarge the thorax, the pressure in the lungs drops below the pressure outside, and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.0] Air always moves from a region of higher pressure to one of lower pressure. When the thorax volume increases, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure (the pressure of the air outside) and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.1] When the thorax volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs follow the chest wall because they have no muscle of their own.
[bio28-s1.4.2] Inhalation (inspiration) is an active process, which means it uses energy. 1. The brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles.
[bio28-s1.4.3] 2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards.
[bio28-s1.4.4] 3. The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and sternum upwards and outwards.
[bio28-s1.4.5] 4. The volume of the thorax increases.
[bio28-s1.4.6] 5. The lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.4.7] 6. Air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP (the cell's energy currency), so inhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.5.b] Breathing out at rest needs no muscle contraction at all: the body simply springs back. During exercise, however, extra muscles make exhalation active.
[bio28-s1.5.0] Exhalation (expiration) at rest is a passive process, which means it uses no energy. 1. The impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax.
[bio28-s1.5.1] 2. The diaphragm returns to its dome shape, pushed up by the abdominal organs, and the ribs move down and in under their own weight.
[bio28-s1.5.2] 3. The stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils, like a stretched elastic band springing back.
[bio28-s1.5.3] 4. The volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.5.4] 5. Air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used.
[bio28-s1.5.5] Forced exhalation, as in exercise, coughing, blowing or speaking loudly: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up.
[bio28-s1.5.6] This expels air faster and more completely, and because muscles contract, forced exhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.6.b] Putting the two halves of the breathing cycle side by side shows that every change in one is reversed in the other.
[bio28-s1.6.0] Diaphragm: it contracts and flattens in inhalation, but relaxes and domes upwards in exhalation at rest.
[bio28-s1.6.1] External intercostal muscles: they contract in inhalation but relax in exhalation. The ribs move up and out in inhalation, and down and in in exhalation.
[bio28-s1.6.2] Thorax volume increases in inhalation and decreases in exhalation. Lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure in inhalation and rises above it in exhalation, so air moves in, then out.
[bio28-s1.6.3] Inhalation is active, whereas exhalation at rest is passive.
[bio28-s1.6.4] The bell-jar model shows the volume and pressure principle: pulling the rubber sheet (the diaphragm) down inflates the balloons (the lungs). There is more on this in Models.
[bio28-s1.6.5] Diagram: two side-view sketches of the thorax, one at inhalation and one at exhalation, showing the diaphragm (flat or domed) and the ribs (raised or lowered), with arrows for rib movement and air flow. Label the diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, lungs and trachea.
[bio28-s1.7.b] Breathing continues while you sleep because the brain controls it automatically. The signal that adjusts it is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood.
[bio28-s1.7.0] Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain. It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
[bio28-s1.7.1] We can change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum (the thinking part of the brain), for example by holding our breath, speaking or singing. The automatic control takes over again afterwards.
[bio28-s1.7.2] The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises, as during exercise when muscles respire faster, the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.3] This removes more CO₂ and takes in more oxygen. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.4] An adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath, the tidal volume.
[bio28-s1.7.5] During hard exercise both the rate and the depth (the volume of air per breath) increase greatly.
[bio28-s1.8.b] Gas exchange is a simple physical process: oxygen and carbon dioxide each move down their own concentration gradient between the air in the alveolus and the blood in the capillary beside it.
[bio28-s1.8.0] Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used, so it is not active transport.
[bio28-s1.8.1] Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration than the deoxygenated blood arriving from the body in the pulmonary artery.
[bio28-s1.8.2] Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma (the liquid part of the blood), and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio28-s1.8.3] Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than the air in the alveoli. CO₂, carried mostly in the plasma, diffuses out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
[bio28-s1.8.4] The oxygenated blood leaves the lungs in the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio28-s1.8.5] The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air.
[bio28-s1.8.6] Blood flow also keeps the gradients steep, because it constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.
[bio28-s1.9.b] The alveoli are built so that diffusion is as fast as possible. Each feature either gives gases more room to cross, a shorter distance to travel, or a steeper gradient.
[bio28-s1.9.0] Very large total surface area: about 300 million alveoli in the two lungs give an exchange surface of roughly 70 m², so a large amount of gas can diffuse at once.
[bio28-s1.9.1] Very thin walls: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick and pressed close together, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio28-s1.9.2] Moist lining: gases dissolve in the moisture before they diffuse across the wall.
[bio28-s1.9.3] Dense capillary network: every alveolus is surrounded by capillaries with a continuous blood flow, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio28-s1.9.4] Narrow capillaries: they are just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, pressed against the wall. This shortens the distance further and slows the blood, giving time for exchange.
[bio28-s1.9.5] Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation, which helps push air out.
[bio28-s1.9.6] Diagram: a single alveolus with a capillary wrapped around it. Label alveolus wall (one cell thick), capillary wall (one cell thick), moist lining, red blood cell and plasma. Add arrows: O₂ into the blood, CO₂ into the alveolus, and blood flow from pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) to pulmonary vein (oxygenated).
[bio28-s1.10.b] The air we breathe out differs from the air we breathe in, because the body takes oxygen from it and adds carbon dioxide and water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.0] Inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with a variable amount of water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.1] Exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. It is saturated with water vapour and is warmer than inhaled air.
[bio28-s1.10.2] Nitrogen is not used by the body, so its proportion is the same in both.
[bio28-s1.10.3] Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths work in CPR (the emergency procedure that keeps a casualty's blood supplied with oxygen).
[bio28-s1.10.4] Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing that exhaled air contains more CO₂.
[bio28-s1.11.b] Damage to the breathing system shows why its structures matter: when cilia, alveoli or bronchioles stop working properly, gas exchange suffers.
[bio28-s1.11.0] Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production, so mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing (smoker's cough) and frequent chest infections.
[bio28-s1.11.1] Emphysema, mainly caused by smoking: if alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces, the surface area for gas exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless. This shows why the large surface area of the alveoli matters.
[bio28-s1.11.2] Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the amount of oxygen the blood can carry. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio28-s1.11.3] Asthma: the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts, and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that less air reaches the alveoli. Inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle.

CANDIDATES bio29-s5: Blood vessels
[bio29-s5.0.b] Human blood never leaves a set of tubes called blood vessels, which is why the system is described as closed. The vessels differ in structure, and the differences make sense once you know the circuit and the three-layered wall that most of them share.
[bio29-s5.0.0] Blood flows in a closed circuit: heart, arteries, arterioles (the smallest arteries), capillaries, venules (the smallest veins), veins, and back to the heart.
[bio29-s5.0.1] Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood towards the heart. Capillaries link them inside the organs, and it is across capillary walls that materials are exchanged with the cells.
[bio29-s5.0.2] The walls of arteries and veins have three layers. The inner layer is the endothelium, one cell thick and smooth, which reduces friction as blood slides past. The middle layer is smooth muscle and elastic fibres.
[bio29-s5.0.3] The outer layer is tough connective tissue containing collagen fibres, which protects the vessel and stops it over-stretching. The space in the centre, through which blood flows, is the lumen.
[bio29-s5.0.4] Capillaries have only the endothelium, with no muscle, elastic or connective layers. This is why they can act as exchange surfaces, as the capillary block below shows.
[bio29-s5.1.b] Blood leaves the heart in surges at high pressure, and arteries have to carry it to the organs without bursting. Each feature of an artery's structure helps it cope with that pressure and keep the blood moving.
[bio29-s5.1.0] Role: arteries carry blood away from the heart to the organs, at high pressure. All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery, which takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
[bio29-s5.1.1] Thick wall, with a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic fibres, to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the ventricles (the heart's lower pumping chambers) without bursting.
[bio29-s5.1.2] Elastic fibres let the wall stretch as each surge of blood enters, then recoil between beats. This pushes the blood on, keeps the pressure up between beats and smooths the flow. The stretching is felt as the pulse.
[bio29-s5.1.3] Narrow lumen relative to the thickness of the wall, which helps keep the pressure high. In section, an artery keeps a round shape, because its thick wall holds it open.
[bio29-s5.1.4] No valves, except the semilunar valves (pocket-shaped valves) where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart. The high pressure keeps blood moving forwards, so valves are not needed along the length.
[bio29-s5.1.5] Blood flows fast and in spurts, and a cut artery spurts bright red blood. Bright red is the colour of oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s5.1.6] Arterioles are the smallest arteries, leading into the capillary networks. Their walls have a high proportion of smooth muscle, which contracts to narrow them (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to widen them (vasodilation).
[bio29-s5.1.7] By narrowing or widening, arterioles control how much blood flows to each organ, for example more to the muscles in exercise and more to the skin when hot.
[bio29-s5.2.b] By the time blood has passed through the capillaries its pressure is low, so veins must return it to the heart without a strong push behind it. Their structure reduces resistance, and valves and the squeezing of nearby muscles keep blood moving one way.
[bio29-s5.2.0] Role: veins carry blood from the organs back towards the heart, at low pressure. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary veins, which bring oxygenated blood from the lungs.
[bio29-s5.2.1] Thin wall, with a thin middle layer and few elastic fibres, because the pressure is low and there is no surge to withstand. The wall is easily squashed, so in section a vein often looks flattened or irregular.
[bio29-s5.2.2] Wide lumen, which reduces resistance to the slow-moving blood, so it can flow with little pressure behind it.
[bio29-s5.2.3] Valves: pocket-shaped, or semilunar, valves are found especially in the veins of the limbs and let blood flow only towards the heart. When blood starts to flow backwards, it fills the pockets, which close the vein.
[bio29-s5.2.4] Skeletal muscle pump: contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins that run between them, pushing blood along, and the valves prevent backflow. This is why standing still for a long time makes blood pool in the legs.
[bio29-s5.2.5] Breathing movements lower the pressure in the thorax (the chest cavity) during inhalation, which draws blood towards the heart. Veins next to arteries are also squeezed by the arterial pulse.
[bio29-s5.2.6] Venules are small veins that collect blood from capillary networks and join to form veins.
[bio29-s5.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a vein showing an open valve, with blood flowing towards the heart, and a closed valve, with its pockets filled and blocking backflow. Add arrows, and label the wall, lumen and valve.
[bio29-s5.3.b] Capillaries are where the circulatory system does its real work, because this is where materials pass between the blood and the cells. Everything about their structure makes that exchange quick and gives it time to happen.
[bio29-s5.3.0] Role: the exchange of materials between the blood and the body cells. Capillaries form dense networks, called capillary beds, in every tissue, so that no cell is far from one.
[bio29-s5.3.1] The wall is only one cell thick, consisting of endothelium only, with no muscle or elastic layers, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio29-s5.3.2] The lumen is very narrow, only wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, which brings them close to the wall.
[bio29-s5.3.3] Capillaries are so numerous that their total cross-section is large, so blood flows slowly through them, giving time for exchange.
[bio29-s5.3.4] Leaky: there are small gaps between the endothelial cells, so plasma fluid and small dissolved molecules can pass out, and white blood cells can squeeze through. Red blood cells and large plasma proteins cannot.
[bio29-s5.3.5] Pressure falls as blood flows through the capillaries, so blood leaving them enters the venules at low pressure. That is why veins have no pulse.
[bio29-s5.4.b] Cells do not touch the blood directly. Instead, part of the plasma leaks out of the capillaries and bathes the cells, and exchange takes place through this fluid.
[bio29-s5.4.0] At the arterial end of a capillary bed (the end nearer the arteriole), the blood pressure is higher and forces plasma out through the capillary wall into the spaces between the cells. Plasma is water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts and oxygen.
[bio29-s5.4.1] This fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes every cell and forms the cells' internal environment. Red blood cells and plasma proteins stay in the capillary because they are too large to pass through the gaps.
[bio29-s5.4.2] Substances pass between tissue fluid and cells by diffusion and active transport. Oxygen and nutrients enter the cells; CO₂, urea and other wastes leave them.
[bio29-s5.4.3] At the venous end the pressure is lower, and the plasma proteins left in the blood draw water back in by osmosis. So most of the tissue fluid, now carrying CO₂ and wastes, returns to the capillary.
[bio29-s5.4.4] The small amount of excess fluid is drained away by lymph vessels and returned to the blood.
[bio29-s5.4.5] Diagram: a capillary bed between an arteriole and a venule, surrounded by body cells. Add arrows for fluid leaving at the arterial end and returning at the venous end, and for O₂ and nutrients entering the cells and CO₂ and wastes leaving them.
[bio29-s5.5.b] Each vessel's structure matches its job, so the clearest way to revise is to compare the three feature by feature. Learn the pairing of each feature with its reason, because exam questions ask you to relate structure to role.
[bio29-s5.5.0] Direction of flow: arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it towards the heart; capillaries link arterioles to venules within an organ.
[bio29-s5.5.1] Pressure: high and pulsing in arteries; falling through the capillaries; low and steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.2] Wall: thick, muscular and elastic in arteries to withstand pressure; thin, with little muscle and elastic tissue, in veins; one cell thick, endothelium only, in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.3] Lumen: narrow relative to the wall in arteries; wide in veins; just wide enough for one red blood cell in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.4] Valves: none in arteries, apart from at the heart; present in veins; none in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.5] Speed of flow: fast in arteries; slow in capillaries; slow but steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.6] Oxygen content: arteries usually carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood, except in the pulmonary circuit, and capillaries are where exchange happens. This is not how the vessels are defined; they are defined by direction of flow.
[bio29-s5.5.7] Exchange: no exchange with the tissues through the thick walls of arteries and veins; all exchange happens in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.8] Diagram: transverse sections of an artery (round, thick wall, narrow lumen), a vein (thin wall, same three layers, wide lumen, often flattened) and a capillary (ring of one or two endothelial cells round one red blood cell). Label each layer and the lumen.

CANDIDATES bio33-s2: Investigation: growth of microorganisms
[bio33-s2.0.b] Before testing what helps or harms microorganisms, you need to know what counts as growth and how to put a number on it. Most investigations in this subsection measure growth in one of the ways below; the antibacterial test measures the width of a clear zone instead.
[bio33-s2.0.0] Growth of a microbial population means an increase in the number of cells (bacteria and yeasts dividing) or in the size of the mycelium (the mass of thread-like filaments that makes up a mould), not the growth of one cell.
[bio33-s2.0.1] A colony is a visible mass of cells on a solid medium (a jelly-like surface the cells grow on), grown by repeated division from a single cell or spore. Counting colonies therefore estimates how many living cells were on the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.2] Bacterial colonies are usually small, round, shiny or matt spots; mould colonies are fuzzy and spread outwards. This difference in appearance helps you tell which organism has grown.
[bio33-s2.0.3] On plates, count the colonies on each plate if they are separate, or measure the diameter of a mould colony with a ruler under the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.4] Alternatively, estimate the percentage of the plate's surface covered, using a grid of squares drawn on the base. This works when colonies have merged and cannot be counted.
[bio33-s2.0.5] In liquid broth cultures, measure the cloudiness (turbidity) with a colorimeter, an instrument that measures how much light passes through. Cloudier broth has more cells and absorbs more light.
[bio33-s2.0.6] For yeast, count cells with a microscope and a counting chamber (a slide marked with a grid of known volume), or measure the CO₂ produced.
[bio33-s2.0.7] Microorganisms need a source of nutrients (for energy and raw materials), water, a suitable temperature and pH and, for most, oxygen. Each of these is one factor tested later in this subsection.
[bio33-s2.0.8] Harmful chemicals and high external solute concentrations (a lot of dissolved salt or sugar outside the cells) inhibit growth, so these are tested too.
[bio33-s2.1.b] Microorganisms are everywhere: in the air, on skin and on benches. Aseptic technique is how you grow only the organism you chose, without letting it or any contaminant spread.
[bio33-s2.1.0] Aseptic technique is the set of procedures that keep unwanted microorganisms out of cultures, so that only the intended organism grows and results are valid.
[bio33-s2.1.1] Aseptic technique also keeps the cultured microorganisms from escaping onto people and surfaces, for safety.
[bio33-s2.1.2] Any culture may contain or become contaminated with harmful microorganisms, so every culture is treated as a possible pathogen (a microorganism that causes disease).
[bio33-s2.1.3] Before starting: tie back long hair, cover cuts with a waterproof plaster, wash hands with soap and water, and wipe the bench with disinfectant. Each step reduces the microorganisms that could get into the culture or onto you.
[bio33-s2.1.4] Do not eat, drink or touch the face during the practical, because this could carry cultured microorganisms into your mouth or eyes.
[bio33-s2.1.5] Sterilise all equipment and media before use: nutrient agar, broths and glassware are heated in an autoclave (or pressure cooker) at 121 °C for 15 minutes, which kills all microorganisms, including bacterial endospores (tough resting cells that survive ordinary heat).
[bio33-s2.1.6] Plastic Petri dishes (the shallow lidded dishes that hold the agar) are bought pre-sterilised and sealed, because plastic would melt in an autoclave.
[bio33-s2.2.b] Once equipment is sterile, the risk is airborne microorganisms and anything that touches the culture. These bench habits deal with both.
[bio33-s2.2.0] Inoculating loop (a wire loop used to transfer microorganisms): heat it in the hottest part of a Bunsen flame until it glows red-hot, before and after every use, to kill any microorganisms on it.
[bio33-s2.2.1] Let the loop cool for a few seconds, without putting it down, before touching the culture, so that the heat does not kill the microorganisms being transferred.
[bio33-s2.2.2] Work close to a lit Bunsen burner: the rising hot air carries airborne microorganisms upwards and away from the open plates and bottles.
[bio33-s2.2.3] When opening a culture bottle or tube, hold the cap in the little finger and never put it down on the bench, because the bench could contaminate the inside of the cap.
[bio33-s2.2.4] Pass the neck of the bottle briefly through the flame after opening and before closing it, to kill microorganisms at the mouth and create an upward air current.
[bio33-s2.2.5] Open a Petri dish lid only slightly, tilted up like a clam shell, and for as short a time as possible, so that airborne microorganisms do not land on the agar.
[bio33-s2.3.b] After inoculating (putting the microorganism onto the plate), the plate must be closed, labelled, incubated (kept warm to let growth happen) and finally disposed of safely. Each rule here has a reason.
[bio33-s2.3.0] After inoculating, secure the lid to the base with two or four short strips of sticky tape, not sealed all the way round, so that oxygen can still enter. Sealing the lid fully would make conditions anaerobic (without oxygen), which would favour the growth of harmful anaerobic bacteria.
[bio33-s2.3.1] Label the base, not the lid (which could be swapped), around the edge with name, date and treatment.
[bio33-s2.3.2] Incubate plates upside down, lid at the bottom, so that condensation forming on the lid does not drip onto the agar and spread the colonies, and the agar does not dry out.
[bio33-s2.3.3] Incubate school cultures at about 25 °C, never at 37 °C, because body temperature favours the growth of microorganisms that are pathogenic to humans.
[bio33-s2.3.4] Never open plates after incubation: examine and count colonies through the closed lid. Never culture samples from the human body, toilets or animal waste, because these are likely to contain pathogens.
[bio33-s2.3.5] Disposal: put used plates and cultures into an autoclave bag and autoclave them (121 °C, 15 minutes) before putting them in the bin, or soak them in strong disinfectant. Disinfect the bench again and wash hands thoroughly at the end.
[bio33-s2.3.6] Spills: cover with paper towels soaked in disinfectant, leave for the time stated on the disinfectant, then clear up wearing gloves, and tell the teacher.
[bio33-s2.3.7] Ethanol, used to sterilise a glass spreader, is highly flammable: keep the beaker of ethanol away from the Bunsen flame. Pass the spreader through the flame only briefly, to burn off the ethanol, holding it pointing down.
[bio33-s2.4.b] A culture medium is the food and water the microorganisms grow on or in. Choosing the right one, and a safe organism, is the first step of every investigation.
[bio33-s2.4.0] A culture medium supplies microorganisms with nutrients and water. Nutrient agar contains agar, a jelly extracted from seaweed, which sets solid and which most microorganisms cannot digest, so the surface stays firm while they grow.
[bio33-s2.4.1] Nutrient agar also contains nutrients such as meat or yeast extract and peptone (partly digested protein), giving amino acids, sugars, vitamins and minerals.
[bio33-s2.4.2] Nutrient broth is the same as nutrient agar without the agar, so it is liquid.
[bio33-s2.4.3] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds grow better on malt agar or potato dextrose agar, which are sugar-rich and slightly acidic.
[bio33-s2.4.4] Suitable microorganisms are baker's yeast suspended in sterile water or sugar solution, moulds such as Rhizopus from bread, or non-pathogenic bacterial cultures supplied for schools.
[bio33-s2.4.5] The source of the microorganism must be known, so the risk is low.
[bio33-s2.5.b] A poured plate is a layer of sterile agar in a Petri dish. How you then put the microorganisms on it depends on what you want to see: separate colonies or an even covering.
[bio33-s2.5.0] Pouring a plate, step 1: melt the sterile agar in its bottle in a boiling water bath.
[bio33-s2.5.1] Step 2: cool the agar to about 50 °C, which is still liquid and cool enough not to kill an organism added to it (agar sets as it cools below about 40 °C).
[bio33-s2.5.2] Step 3: flame the neck of the bottle, to kill microorganisms at the mouth.
[bio33-s2.5.3] Step 4: lift the Petri dish lid slightly and pour in enough agar to cover the base (about 15–20 cm³).
[bio33-s2.5.4] Step 5: replace the lid, swirl gently and leave on a level surface to set.
[bio33-s2.5.5] Streaking: draw the flamed, cooled loop, dipped in the culture, across the agar in a zigzag. This thins the cells out along the line, so they form separate colonies.
[bio33-s2.5.6] Spreading: spread a set volume (e.g. 0.1 cm³) of a culture or diluted suspension evenly with a sterile glass spreader to make a lawn (growth covering the whole plate). A lawn is needed for the antibacterial test. There is more on this in the antibacterial and antifungal investigation below.
[bio33-s2.5.7] Diagram: a Petri dish after inoculation, shown upside down in the incubator. Labels: base labelled with name, date and treatment, lid held with two short strips of tape. Beside it, a flamed inoculating loop held in a Bunsen flame.
[bio33-s2.6.b] All six factor investigations below follow one plan: change one thing, keep everything else the same, and compare against controls. Learn this plan once and you can design any of them.
[bio33-s2.6.0] Aim: to investigate how a named factor affects the growth of a named microorganism. Each investigation changes one factor, the independent variable, and measures growth after a fixed time; the dependent variable is colony count, colony diameter, percentage cover or turbidity.
[bio33-s2.6.1] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the same microorganism, culture and volume of inoculum (the amount of culture added) on every plate; the same medium and volume of agar; and the same incubation time.
[bio33-s2.6.2] Also controlled, except when it is the factor tested: the same temperature, pH, nutrient level and water availability, and the same method of measuring.
[bio33-s2.6.3] Uninoculated control: an uninoculated plate (sterile medium only) is incubated with the others. No growth on it shows that the medium and technique were sterile, so any growth on the test plates came from the inoculum.
[bio33-s2.6.4] A reference plate with normal conditions (e.g. normal pH, no added salt, or a water disc in the antibacterial test) is compared with the tested conditions, so you can tell what the factor changed.
[bio33-s2.6.5] Replicates: set up at least three plates for each value of the factor and calculate the mean, so that one contaminated or unusual plate does not mislead. Worked example: colony counts of 42, 38 and 46 at 25 °C give a mean of 126 ÷ 3 = 42 colonies.
[bio33-s2.6.6] Record results in a table: value of the factor, growth on each replicate, and the mean. Plot a bar chart for categories or a line graph for a continuous factor, with mean growth on the y-axis.
[bio33-s2.6.7] Conclusion: describe the trend in the mean results and say whether they support the hypothesis. Explain the results using the biology given for each factor, and compare them with secondary data.
[bio33-s2.7.b] Temperature changes how fast the chemical reactions inside cells run. This investigation compares growth at several temperatures, and tests high temperatures by heating the culture before plating.
[bio33-s2.7.0] Hypothesis: “Growth increases with temperature up to an optimum, then decreases.” The optimum is the temperature at which growth is fastest.
[bio33-s2.7.1] Independent variable: temperature, i.e. about 4 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C and 25 °C, plus 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C for the heated cultures.
[bio33-s2.7.2] Step 1: inoculate identical plates with the same volume of the same culture, e.g. yeast.
[bio33-s2.7.3] Step 2: incubate sets of three at about 4 °C (refrigerator), 15 °C, 20 °C (room) and 25 °C (incubator) for the same time, e.g. 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.7.4] Step 3: count the colonies or measure colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.7.5] Step 4: to test high temperatures safely, heat tubes of the culture in water baths at 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C (or boil one) for 10 minutes before plating them. Heating the culture first avoids incubating dangerously warm plates.
[bio33-s2.7.6] Step 5: incubate all the plates at 25 °C, so that the only difference between them is the heat treatment.
[bio33-s2.8.b] The results fit a pattern: slow growth when cold, faster when warmer, and death when too hot. The explanation comes from enzymes, the proteins that control the reactions in cells.
[bio33-s2.8.0] Expected result: little or no growth at 4 °C. Growth increases with temperature over the range tested. Cultures heated to about 60 °C or more grow poorly or not at all.
[bio33-s2.8.1] Explanation: growth depends on enzyme-controlled reactions, and at low temperatures molecules move slowly, so reactions and growth are slow.
[bio33-s2.8.2] Cold does not kill the cells: growth resumes when they are warmed, which is why refrigerated food still goes off slowly and frozen food starts to go off when thawed.
[bio33-s2.8.3] Above the optimum, enzymes and other proteins are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working), so cells die. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio33-s2.8.4] Bacterial endospores survive higher temperatures than active cells, so heating that kills the active cells may leave endospores alive.
[bio33-s2.9.b] pH measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is (7 is neutral). This investigation grows bacteria on agar made up at different pH values to find the range they prefer.
[bio33-s2.9.0] Hypothesis: “Bacteria grow best at about neutral pH and poorly in strongly acidic or alkaline conditions.”
[bio33-s2.9.1] Independent variable: the pH of the agar or broth, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11.
[bio33-s2.9.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar (or broth) at different pH values, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11, by adding buffer solutions (which hold the pH steady) or dilute acid or alkali, checked with a pH meter, before pouring.
[bio33-s2.9.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of culture.
[bio33-s2.9.4] Step 3: incubate all at the same temperature for the same time.
[bio33-s2.9.5] Step 4: measure growth.
[bio33-s2.10.b] Bacteria and fungi prefer different pH ranges, and the reason lies in the proteins that run the cell. The same effect is used to preserve food.
[bio33-s2.10.0] Expected result: most bacteria grow best near neutral pH (about pH 6–8) and little in strongly acidic conditions.
[bio33-s2.10.1] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds tolerate, and often grow best in, slightly acidic conditions (about pH 5–6). Repeating the investigation with a fungus and a bacterium shows this difference.
[bio33-s2.10.2] Explanation: pH outside the optimum changes the charges on enzymes and other proteins, so their shape changes and they work slowly or are denatured.
[bio33-s2.10.3] Acid conditions are used to preserve food, e.g. pickling in vinegar, because few microorganisms grow well at low pH.
[bio33-s2.10.4] Yogurt keeps longer because the lactic acid made by its bacteria lowers the pH.
[bio33-s2.11.b] Nutrients are the food a microorganism uses for energy and to build new cells. This investigation compares media with and without nutrients, or with increasing amounts of sugar.
[bio33-s2.11.0] Hypothesis: “Microorganisms grow more when more nutrients are available, until another factor limits growth.”
[bio33-s2.11.1] Independent variable: the nutrient content of the medium, e.g. plain agar compared with nutrient agar, or glucose at 0%, 1%, 2% and 5% for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.2] Step 1: compare plain agar (agar and water only, no nutrients) with nutrient agar; or use broths (or agar) with increasing concentrations of glucose, e.g. 0%, 1%, 2%, 5%, for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of yeast culture.
[bio33-s2.11.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together at the same temperature.
[bio33-s2.11.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or percentage cover.
[bio33-s2.12.b] More food means more growth, but only up to a point. The results also show a link forward to the solute investigation, because very high sugar is harmful.
[bio33-s2.12.0] Expected result: little or no growth on plain agar, because there is nothing for the cells to use.
[bio33-s2.12.1] Growth increases with nutrient concentration and then levels off, as another factor, such as space or the build-up of wastes, becomes limiting (the one that stops growth rising further).
[bio33-s2.12.2] At very high sugar concentrations, growth falls again. There is more on this in Investigation: effect of external solute concentration.
[bio33-s2.12.3] Explanation: nutrients supply the energy, released by respiration of sugars, and the raw materials for new cells, i.e. sugars, amino acids, minerals such as nitrate and phosphate, and vitamins.
[bio33-s2.12.4] Variation: starch agar shows that only microorganisms that secrete amylase (the enzyme that digests starch) can use starch.
[bio33-s2.12.5] Flooding the plate with iodine after incubation leaves a clear zone around such colonies, where the starch has been digested. The rest of the plate turns dark because iodine stains starch.
[bio33-s2.13.b] This investigation uses bread and mould instead of agar plates. It tests whether mould needs moisture by comparing dried bread with moistened bread.
[bio33-s2.13.0] Hypothesis: “Moulds grow only on food that is moist.”
[bio33-s2.13.1] Independent variable: the water content of the bread, dried compared with moistened.
[bio33-s2.13.2] Step 1: take slices of the same bread (without preservatives, which would inhibit growth); dry some fully in an oven at a low temperature and moisten others with the same volume of sterile water.
[bio33-s2.13.3] Step 2: expose each slice to the air of the same room for the same time, e.g. 30 minutes, so that mould spores land on it.
[bio33-s2.13.4] Step 3: seal each in a clear plastic bag or sealed dish and label it.
[bio33-s2.13.5] Step 4: keep all in the same warm, dark place, about 25 °C, for 5–7 days.
[bio33-s2.13.6] Step 5: estimate the percentage cover of mould on each, using a transparent grid of squares held over the bag, e.g. 37 of 100 squares covered = 37%.
[bio33-s2.13.7] Do not open the bags, because the spores released could be breathed in.
[bio33-s2.14.b] Water is essential for life, and microorganisms are no exception. The result explains why drying is an old way of keeping food.
[bio33-s2.14.0] Expected result: mould grows on the moist bread and not, or very little, on the dried bread.
[bio33-s2.14.1] Explanation: microorganisms need water because their enzymes work only in solution, nutrients are absorbed in solution after extracellular digestion (digestion outside the cell), and water is needed for cells to grow and divide.
[bio33-s2.14.2] Dried foods, e.g. dried fruit, pasta, powdered milk and cereals, keep for a long time for this reason.
[bio33-s2.14.3] Safety: mould spores can trigger allergies and asthma, so keep the bags sealed throughout and dispose of them unopened.
[bio33-s2.15.b] A solute is something dissolved in water, such as salt or sugar. This investigation adds increasing amounts of solute to the agar to see what happens to growth.
[bio33-s2.15.0] Hypothesis: “Growth decreases as the concentration of salt (or sugar) outside the cells increases.”
[bio33-s2.15.1] Independent variable: the concentration of sodium chloride (salt), e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10% or 20%, or of sucrose (sugar).
[bio33-s2.15.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar containing increasing concentrations of sodium chloride (e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%) or of sucrose.
[bio33-s2.15.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of bacterial culture.
[bio33-s2.15.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together for the same time.
[bio33-s2.15.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.16.b] Salt and sugar slow growth because of what they do to water movement across the cell surface. This is the basis of salting and sugaring food.
[bio33-s2.16.0] Expected result: growth decreases as the solute concentration rises, with little or no growth at the highest concentrations.
[bio33-s2.16.1] Explanation: when the solution outside a cell has a higher solute concentration, and so lower water concentration, than the cytoplasm, water leaves the cell by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio33-s2.16.2] The cell then loses water, its enzymes cannot work, and it stops growing or dies.
[bio33-s2.16.3] In a very dilute solution water enters the cell, but the bacterial or fungal cell wall stops the cell bursting.
[bio33-s2.16.4] This is the basis of preserving food with salt (salted fish and bacon) and sugar (jam). There is more on this in Osmosis in food preservation and plant health.
[bio33-s2.17.b] Some chemicals stop microorganisms growing. Knowing which type acts on which group, and what the investigation sets out to show, comes before the method.
[bio33-s2.17.0] Antibacterial chemicals kill bacteria or stop them growing: antibiotics (e.g. penicillin, streptomycin), antiseptics (used on living tissue, e.g. on skin) and disinfectants (used on surfaces and objects).
[bio33-s2.17.1] Antifungal chemicals, e.g. the antifungal creams used for athlete's foot, or fungicides, act on fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.2] Antibiotics do not affect fungi, and antifungals generally do not affect bacteria, because the two groups have different structures. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.3] Hypothesis: “Different antiseptics (or antibiotics) inhibit the growth of a bacterium to different extents.”
[bio33-s2.17.4] Independent variable: the antibacterial or antifungal chemical on each disc; dependent variable: the diameter of the clear zone around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.b] In the disc diffusion method, paper discs soaked in chemicals are placed on a lawn of bacteria. Wherever the chemical stops growth, a clear zone appears around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.0] Step 1: spread a set volume of the culture evenly over a nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader, to make a lawn.
[bio33-s2.18.1] Step 2: using sterile forceps (flamed and cooled), dip paper discs of the same size (e.g. 6 mm, cut with a hole punch and sterilised) in each chemical for the same time, drain them, and place them on the lawn, well spaced.
[bio33-s2.18.2] Step 3: include a control disc dipped in sterile distilled water. Ready-made antibiotic discs, or a multi-disc with several antibiotics on one ring, can be used instead.
[bio33-s2.18.3] Step 4: tape and label the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.4] Step 5: incubate upside down at about 25 °C for 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.18.5] Step 6: measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc with a ruler on the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.6] For an antifungal chemical, make a lawn of yeast on malt agar and use discs of the antifungal, with a water control and, to show the difference in antibacterial and antifungal action, an antibiotic disc.
[bio33-s2.18.7] Diagram: a lawn plate with four discs. Labels: each disc's chemical, clear zones of inhibition of different sizes, and no zone around the water control.
[bio33-s2.19.b] The clear zones are the result. Their size tells you how well each chemical works, and the way they form explains why.
[bio33-s2.19.0] Expected result: a clear zone, the zone of inhibition, forms around discs whose chemical stops the bacterium growing.
[bio33-s2.19.1] There is no clear zone around the water control, which shows that the disc and the water alone do not stop growth.
[bio33-s2.19.2] The larger the zone, the more effective the chemical is against that organism at that concentration. In other words, the bacterium is more sensitive to it.
[bio33-s2.19.3] On the yeast lawn, the antifungal gives a clear zone and the antibiotic gives none.
[bio33-s2.19.4] The chemical diffuses outwards from the disc into the agar, so its concentration falls with distance; bacteria cannot grow where the concentration is high enough to kill or inhibit them.
[bio33-s2.19.5] A zone's size also depends on how fast the chemical diffuses, so it is a guide to strength, not an exact measure.
[bio33-s2.19.6] A bacterium that grows right up to an antibiotic disc is resistant to that antibiotic.
[bio33-s2.19.7] In hospitals the same test is used to find which antibiotic will work against the bacterium from a patient. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s2.20.b] Comparing zone areas, not just diameters, is fairer. Here is the calculation for a clear zone of diameter 18 mm around a 6 mm disc.
[bio33-s2.20.0] Radius = 9 mm; area of the zone = π r² = 3.14 × 9 × 9 ≈ 254 mm².
[bio33-s2.20.1] Subtracting the disc (radius 3 mm, area ≈ 28 mm²) gives a clear area of about 226 mm², which is the area where bacteria were actually stopped from growing.
[bio33-s2.20.2] Areas let zones be compared fairly, because doubling the diameter quadruples the area.
[bio33-s2.21.b] Your own results are primary data. A good conclusion also checks them against data collected by other people.
[bio33-s2.21.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, e.g. other class groups, textbooks, published research or food-safety guidance.
[bio33-s2.21.1] Conclusions must be supported by both primary and secondary data: compare your own results with the class's pooled results and with published findings.
[bio33-s2.21.2] Published findings include that most human pathogens grow best near body temperature and neutral pH, and that most bacteria grow little below refrigerator temperature.
[bio33-s2.22.b] Each factor you investigated is used to keep food safe. Preserving food means stopping microorganisms growing, either by killing them or by making conditions unsuitable.
[bio33-s2.22.0] Temperature: refrigeration and freezing slow or stop growth but do not kill microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.1] Heat: pasteurisation, heating milk briefly to a temperature below boiling and then cooling it quickly, kills most microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.2] Sterilisation: canning and ultra-heat treatment (UHT) heat food enough to sterilise it, and sealing keeps microorganisms out.
[bio33-s2.22.3] Water: drying removes water, which microorganisms need for their enzymes to work.
[bio33-s2.22.4] Solute: salting and sugaring use high solute concentration, which draws water out of microbial cells.
[bio33-s2.22.5] pH: pickling uses low pH.
[bio33-s2.23.b] The remaining factors give further preservation methods and wider uses in hygiene and in industry.
[bio33-s2.23.0] Chemicals: chemical preservatives inhibit growth.
[bio33-s2.23.1] Oxygen: vacuum packing removes oxygen, slowing aerobic microorganisms (those that need oxygen to grow).
[bio33-s2.23.2] Health and hygiene: antiseptics on wounds and skin, disinfectants on surfaces, and aseptic technique in surgery. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s2.23.3] Industry: bioreactors (vessels in which microorganisms are grown on a large scale) are kept at the optimum conditions found by such investigations to maximise growth.
[bio33-s2.24.b] Every fault below can make results unreliable. Each has a simple fix, so learn each error together with its fix.
[bio33-s2.24.0] Contamination by airborne or skin microorganisms gives colonies of a different appearance; check with the uninoculated control, and repeat with better aseptic technique.
[bio33-s2.24.1] Unequal inoculum, i.e. different volumes or numbers of cells, on different plates; use a measured volume from a well-mixed culture.
[bio33-s2.24.2] Colonies that grow together cannot be counted; dilute the culture so that colonies are separate, or measure percentage cover instead.
[bio33-s2.24.3] Agar or broth that is not at the intended pH or concentration; check with a pH meter and measure accurately.
[bio33-s2.24.4] Temperature not constant, e.g. the “room temperature” plates are near a radiator.
[bio33-s2.24.5] Discs not equally soaked, or chemicals not of known concentration; soak all discs for the same time.
[bio33-s2.24.6] Too few replicates; use at least three and calculate means.
