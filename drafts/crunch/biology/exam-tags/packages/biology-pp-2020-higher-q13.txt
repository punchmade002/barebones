CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio26-s1: The urinary system
[bio26-s1.0.b] Every cell produces waste as it carries out its chemical reactions, and some of that waste is harmful if it stays in the body. The kidneys are the main organs that clear it away, and they do a second job at the same time: keeping the body's fluids balanced.
[bio26-s1.0.0] Excretion is the removal of the waste products of metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells). Faeces are not excreted: they are undigested food that was never absorbed, so passing them out is egestion.
[bio26-s1.0.1] The kidneys excrete urea, excess water, excess salts and excess hydrogen ions in urine. Other organs help: the lungs excrete carbon dioxide, and sweat removes some water, salts and a little urea.
[bio26-s1.0.2] Urea is made in the liver from excess amino acids (the units of proteins) by deamination, the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of an amino acid. It is carried dissolved in the blood plasma to the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.0.3] Urea is toxic if it builds up, so it must be removed continuously rather than in occasional bursts.
[bio26-s1.0.4] Transport between environments: blood (the internal environment) carries wastes to the kidneys, the kidneys transfer them from the blood into urine, and urine is passed out of the body (the external environment).
[bio26-s1.0.5] At the same time the kidneys return useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most of the water and most of the salts) to the blood, so nutrients are not lost in the urine.
[bio26-s1.0.6] The kidneys have two roles: excretion (removing urea and other wastes), and osmoregulation and homeostasis, which regulate the volume, water content, salt concentration and pH of the body fluids.
[bio26-s1.0.7] Both roles use the same two processes: filtration of the blood (squeezing small molecules out of it), then reabsorption (taking back what the body needs). What is not reabsorbed is urine.
[bio26-s1.1.b] The kidneys sit well protected at the back of the body and are supplied directly by a large artery. That position and supply let them filter a large volume of blood at high pressure.
[bio26-s1.1.0] The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs at the back of the abdomen, one on each side of the vertebral column (backbone), just below the diaphragm. The lowest ribs partly protect them.
[bio26-s1.1.1] The right kidney is slightly lower than the left because the liver lies above it and takes up space.
[bio26-s1.1.2] Each kidney is embedded in a layer of fat that cushions it against physical damage and helps hold it in place, and is covered by a tough outer membrane, the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.1.3] The adrenal glands (small hormone-producing glands) sit on top of the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.1.4] Each kidney receives blood from a renal artery, a short wide branch of the aorta (the body's main artery), so blood arrives at high pressure and in large volume, which suits filtration.
[bio26-s1.1.5] Filtered blood leaves by the renal vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart).
[bio26-s1.1.6] Blood in the renal vein has less urea, less oxygen and less glucose than blood in the renal artery, and more carbon dioxide because the kidney cells respire. It is the blood vessel with the lowest urea concentration in the body.
[bio26-s1.1.7] Diagram: front view of the urinary system. Label two kidneys, renal arteries from the aorta, renal veins to the vena cava, ureters, bladder, sphincter muscles and urethra. The adrenal glands on top of the kidneys may be shown.
[bio26-s1.2.b] Once urine has formed it must be carried away, stored and released at a suitable moment. Three structures do this in turn: the ureters carry it, the bladder stores it and the urethra lets it out.
[bio26-s1.2.0] There are two ureters: narrow muscular tubes, one from the renal pelvis (the funnel-shaped collecting space inside each kidney) down to the bladder.
[bio26-s1.2.1] The ureter walls contain smooth muscle that moves urine by peristalsis (waves of contraction), so urine reaches the bladder whatever the body position, not just by gravity.
[bio26-s1.2.2] The urinary bladder is a hollow muscular bag in the lower abdomen (pelvis) that stores urine.
[bio26-s1.2.3] The bladder wall is elastic and folded, so it can stretch to hold a large volume and contract to empty.
[bio26-s1.2.4] Two rings of sphincter muscle (muscle that closes an opening) at the bladder outlet keep it closed: an inner one that works automatically (involuntary), and an outer one under conscious (voluntary) control, which young children learn to control.
[bio26-s1.2.5] The urethra is the single tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
[bio26-s1.2.6] In males the urethra is longer, runs through the penis and also carries semen. In females it is short and opens just in front of the vagina.
[bio26-s1.3.b] Cut a kidney lengthways and you can see that it is not uniform inside. The three regions each have a different role in making and collecting urine.
[bio26-s1.3.0] A kidney cut lengthways shows three regions: cortex, medulla and renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.3.1] Cortex: the dark outer layer, under the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.3.2] Medulla: the paler inner layer, divided into cone-shaped renal pyramids.
[bio26-s1.3.3] Renal pelvis: a funnel-shaped space on the inner (concave) side that collects urine and leads into the ureter. No filtration happens in the pelvis and urine is not changed there, so it is only a collecting space.
[bio26-s1.4.b] The real work of the kidney is done by microscopic tubes called nephrons. Each one first filters the blood, then takes back what the body needs, and the leftover liquid becomes urine.
[bio26-s1.4.0] Each kidney contains about one million nephrons, microscopic tubules that are the kidney's filtering units, so together they give a huge filtering surface. Each runs from the cortex down into the medulla and back.
[bio26-s1.4.1] The nephron tubules drain into collecting ducts that run through the pyramids and open into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.4.2] Filtration takes place in the cortex: blood is forced at high pressure through knots of capillaries (glomeruli) into the cup-shaped tops of the nephrons (Bowman's capsules).
[bio26-s1.4.3] Small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea) are filtered out of the blood. Blood cells and plasma proteins are too large, so they stay in the blood. The filtered liquid is called the filtrate.
[bio26-s1.4.4] Reabsorption (taking useful substances back into the blood) takes place in the cortex, in the coiled tubules, and in the medulla, in the loops of Henle and the collecting ducts.
[bio26-s1.4.5] All the glucose and amino acids, most of the salts and most of the water are taken back into the blood. The remaining liquid, carrying urea and excess salts and water, is urine.
[bio26-s1.4.6] Diagram: longitudinal section of a kidney. Label renal capsule, cortex, medulla, renal pyramid, renal pelvis, ureter, renal artery and renal vein. Draw one nephron from cortex into medulla to show where filtration and reabsorption happen.
[bio26-s1.5.b] Urine is what is left of the filtrate once the body has taken back what it needs. Its volume and concentration change from hour to hour, which is how the kidneys keep the water content of the blood steady.
[bio26-s1.5.0] Urine is mostly water, with urea, salts (mainly sodium chloride) and other wastes dissolved in it.
[bio26-s1.5.1] Healthy urine contains no protein and no glucose, because protein is not filtered out of the blood and all the glucose is reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.5.2] After drinking a lot of water, the kidneys produce a large volume of dilute (pale) urine, which gets rid of the extra water.
[bio26-s1.5.3] When water is short (little drinking, heavy sweating in exercise or hot weather, or diarrhoea), a small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine is produced, so the body saves water.
[bio26-s1.5.4] The control is by the hormone ADH. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation; here you only need the effect on urine.
[bio26-s1.6.b] Water is not the only thing the kidneys balance. They also adjust salts and acidity, and together this keeps the body's internal conditions steady.
[bio26-s1.6.0] Salt balance: after a salty meal the kidneys excrete more salt, and when salt is short they reabsorb more.
[bio26-s1.6.1] Keeping the salt and water concentration of the blood steady stops body cells from gaining or losing water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio26-s1.6.2] pH: the kidneys remove excess hydrogen ions from the blood in urine, helping to keep blood pH at about 7.4. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio26-s1.6.3] This is homeostasis: the kidneys keep the internal environment constant whatever is eaten or drunk, much as a thermostat holds a room at one temperature. This works by negative feedback, which is taught in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio26-s1.7.b] Urine is made all the time, but it is passed out only now and then. This sequence shows how it travels and why release can be delayed.
[bio26-s1.7.0] 1. Urine forms continuously in the nephrons and drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.7.1] 2. Peristalsis in the ureter carries it to the bladder in small spurts.
[bio26-s1.7.2] 3. The bladder fills and its wall stretches. Stretch receptors in the wall send nerve impulses that give the urge to urinate.
[bio26-s1.7.3] 4. To urinate, the bladder's muscular wall contracts and the sphincters relax. The outer sphincter relaxes under voluntary control, which is learned after early childhood.
[bio26-s1.7.4] 5. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.
[bio26-s1.8.b] Each part of the urinary system has features that suit its job. Examiners ask you to link structure to function, so learn these as pairs.
[bio26-s1.8.0] Fat layer around each kidney: protects it from physical damage.
[bio26-s1.8.1] Renal artery branching directly from the aorta: delivers high-pressure blood for filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.2] About one million nephrons: a huge filtering surface.
[bio26-s1.8.3] Cortex full of capillary knots: the site of filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.4] Long tubules and loops in the medulla: the site of reabsorption.
[bio26-s1.8.5] Funnel-shaped renal pelvis: collects urine.
[bio26-s1.8.6] Muscular ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristalsis.
[bio26-s1.8.7] Elastic bladder with sphincters: stores urine and allows controlled release. The urethra is the exit for urine from the body.
[bio26-s1.9.b] Because healthy urine contains no glucose or protein, finding either one in a urine sample tells a doctor that something is wrong.
[bio26-s1.9.0] Glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes, because blood glucose is so high that not all of it can be reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.9.1] Protein in urine is a sign of damage to the kidney's filters, because healthy filters do not let protein through.
[bio26-s1.9.2] Urine tests are also used to detect drugs, for example in sport.
[bio26-s1.10.b] If the kidneys stop working, the body can no longer clear its wastes or balance its fluids. There are two treatments: a machine that does the kidney's job, or a replacement kidney.
[bio26-s1.10.0] If the kidneys stop working, urea, salts and water build up in the blood and the person will die without treatment.
[bio26-s1.10.1] Treatment is by dialysis or by a kidney transplant.
[bio26-s1.10.2] A transplant gives a working kidney, but the patient must take drugs to stop the immune system rejecting it, because the body treats the new kidney as foreign. There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio26-s1.11.b] A dialysis machine copies the kidney's filtering. It uses diffusion across a membrane to clean the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.0] The patient's blood is pumped through a machine where it flows past a selectively permeable membrane (one that lets only some substances through), with dialysis fluid on the other side.
[bio26-s1.11.1] Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid, because their concentration is higher in the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.2] The fluid contains glucose and salts at normal blood concentrations, so these are not lost from the blood. Blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane.
[bio26-s1.11.3] Dialysis must be repeated several times a week, because it does not work continuously the way healthy kidneys do.

CANDIDATES bio26-s2: The nephron
[bio26-s2.0.b] Urine is made by tiny tubes called nephrons, so you need to know the parts of one in the order the fluid flows through them. Each part has its own job, and its position in the kidney is part of the answer.
[bio26-s2.0.0] A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney. It is the kidney's functional unit (the smallest part that does the kidney's job), where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one cell thick throughout.
[bio26-s2.0.1] Bowman's capsule is the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex (the outer layer of the kidney), wrapped around the glomerulus (a capillary knot). Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the capsule's inner space.
[bio26-s2.0.2] The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the first, long, coiled (convoluted) section after the capsule, in the cortex. Proximal means nearer the capsule.
[bio26-s2.0.3] The loop of Henle is a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla (the inner part of the kidney), with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
[bio26-s2.0.4] The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) is the second coiled section, back in the cortex. Distal means further from the capsule. It empties into a collecting duct.
[bio26-s2.0.5] The collecting duct is a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla, through a pyramid, and opens into the renal pelvis (the funnel that collects urine).
[bio26-s2.0.6] The syllabus does not list the collecting duct, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH. Some textbooks do not count it as part of the nephron.
[bio26-s2.1.b] A nephron works because blood is brought right up against it. The blood vessels are arranged so that pressure builds up for filtering, and so that the tubule is wrapped in a second set of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) to take back what it reabsorbs.
[bio26-s2.1.0] A branch of the renal artery (the artery that brings blood to the kidney) divides into arterioles (small arteries). The afferent arteriole (afferent means carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
[bio26-s2.1.1] The capillaries of the glomerulus rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent means carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. The afferent is the wider one, so do not say the efferent is wider.
[bio26-s2.1.2] Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus, like water backing up behind a narrowed nozzle. This high pressure drives filtration.
[bio26-s2.1.3] The glomerulus is unusual because it is a capillary network lying between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule (a small vein) as capillaries normally do.
[bio26-s2.1.4] The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries, so they must be close to the tubule.
[bio26-s2.1.5] These capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration, because the cells need ATP for active transport, and they carry away the cells' carbon dioxide.
[bio26-s2.1.6] The capillaries join into venules, then into a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
[bio26-s2.1.7] Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below; mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).
[bio26-s2.2.b] The first step in making urine is filtration. It happens in the glomerulus and capsule, where pressure forces fluid out of the blood without checking whether the body needs it.
[bio26-s2.2.0] Filtration, also called ultrafiltration or filtration under pressure, is the forcing of small molecules out of the blood by the high pressure in the glomerulus. They pass through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
[bio26-s2.2.1] Substances that pass into the filtrate (the fluid that enters the capsule) are water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea (the waste made from breaking down amino acids), vitamins and some hormones.
[bio26-s2.2.2] Substances that are not filtered and stay in the blood are red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
[bio26-s2.2.3] Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances such as glucose and amino acids are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
[bio26-s2.2.4] Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood); this is why reabsorption is essential.
[bio26-s2.2.5] Structure suits filtration: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arteriole raise the pressure, and the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous, so fluid passes through easily.
[bio26-s2.2.6] The many capillaries in each glomerulus, with about a million glomeruli in the kidney, give a large filtering surface, so a large volume of fluid can be filtered every day.
[bio26-s2.3.b] Filtration lets everything small through, so the body has to take back what it wants. Most of this reabsorption happens in the PCT, whose structure is built for the job.
[bio26-s2.3.0] Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
[bio26-s2.3.1] The PCT is the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here.
[bio26-s2.3.2] Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, which uses ATP (the cell's energy currency) to move them into the cells. Water follows by osmosis, and some urea diffuses back into the blood.
[bio26-s2.3.3] Water is always reabsorbed passively by osmosis; it is never actively pumped. Only salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.3.4] The PCT is long and coiled, which gives time and surface area for reabsorption. Its cells have microvilli (tiny finger-like projections) on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area.
[bio26-s2.3.5] The PCT cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. The tubule is closely surrounded by capillaries, and its wall is one cell thick, so the distance to the blood is short.
[bio26-s2.3.6] The carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.
[bio26-s2.4.b] After the PCT, the rest of the nephron fine-tunes the filtrate. The loop of Henle makes the medulla salty, and this lets the collecting duct take back more water.
[bio26-s2.4.0] The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty, with the highest solute concentration deep in the medulla.
[bio26-s2.4.1] The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
[bio26-s2.4.2] In the DCT more salts and water are reabsorbed, with the amounts adjusted to the body's needs.
[bio26-s2.4.3] The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption.
[bio26-s2.4.4] In the collecting duct, as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood, so the urine becomes more concentrated. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH, a hormone. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation.
[bio26-s2.4.5] What remains in the collecting duct is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s2.5.b] Comparing the composition of blood plasma, glomerular filtrate and urine shows what each part of the nephron has done. Each difference between them has one explanation.
[bio26-s2.5.0] Protein is present in plasma but absent from the filtrate and urine, because it is too large to be filtered.
[bio26-s2.5.1] Glucose and amino acids are present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration, because filtration lets them through freely. They are absent from urine, because all of them are reabsorbed in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.5.2] Urea is at the same concentration in plasma and filtrate, because it is freely filtered. It is much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
[bio26-s2.5.3] Salts are similar in plasma and filtrate. In urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
[bio26-s2.5.4] Skill: given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference. Absent from the filtrate means not filtered (too large); absent from the urine means reabsorbed; more concentrated in the urine means water was reabsorbed and the substance was not.
[bio26-s2.6.b] Following the fluid from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, each part of the nephron does one main job.
[bio26-s2.6.0] Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule: filtration under pressure.
[bio26-s2.6.1] Proximal convoluted tubule: reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and most water.
[bio26-s2.6.2] Loop of Henle: makes the medulla salty; water is reabsorbed from the descending limb.
[bio26-s2.6.3] Distal convoluted tubule: adjusts salts and water, and secretes hydrogen ions, potassium ions and some drugs.
[bio26-s2.6.4] Collecting duct: water is reabsorbed under ADH control.
[bio26-s2.6.5] Renal pelvis: receives the urine that is left.

CANDIDATES bio26-s3: ADH and osmoregulation
[bio26-s3.0.b] The water content of the body is never constant on its own: we take water in and lose it all day. Osmoregulation (keeping the water and solute balance of the body fluids steady) stops these swings from harming our cells.
[bio26-s3.0.0] Osmoregulation in humans is mainly done by the kidneys, which change how much water is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood) from the collecting ducts, the final tubes that carry urine out of the kidney. The variable being controlled is the concentration of the blood plasma.
[bio26-s3.0.1] Water is gained from drink, food and respiration, and lost in urine, sweat, breath (as water vapour) and faeces. Gains and losses change from hour to hour, so the kidney must adjust the urine to match.
[bio26-s3.0.2] If the blood becomes too concentrated, body cells lose water by osmosis (the movement of water from a dilute region to a more concentrated one) and shrink. If it becomes too dilute, cells gain water and swell. Either upsets cell function, especially in the brain.
[bio26-s3.1.b] The kidney needs a message telling it how much water to save. That message is a hormone, ADH, which changes how leaky the collecting duct wall is to water.
[bio26-s3.1.0] ADH stands for antidiuretic hormone. Diuresis is the production of a large volume of dilute urine, so ADH works against it and reduces urine volume. ADH is released from the pituitary gland.
[bio26-s3.1.1] The target cells (the cells that respond to the hormone) line the collecting ducts and the last part of the distal convoluted tubule.
[bio26-s3.1.2] ADH binds to receptors on these cells, which causes water channel proteins, called aquaporins, to be inserted into their membranes. This makes the duct wall more permeable to water.
[bio26-s3.1.3] The collecting duct passes through the salty medulla (the inner region of the kidney), so water moves out of the more permeable duct by osmosis into the medulla and is carried away in the blood capillaries.
[bio26-s3.1.4] More ADH means more water reabsorbed, less water left in the duct, and a small volume of concentrated urine.
[bio26-s3.1.5] Less ADH means fewer water channels, so the duct wall is less permeable and less water is reabsorbed. A large volume of dilute urine is the result.
[bio26-s3.1.6] ADH changes water reabsorption only. It does not change the amount of salt or urea (the waste made from the breakdown of proteins) excreted. There is more on channel proteins in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio26-s3.2.b] When the blood becomes too concentrated, the body responds in a fixed sequence that saves water and brings the concentration back to normal. Follow the steps in order.
[bio26-s3.2.0] 1. Stimulus: the water concentration of the blood falls (its solute concentration rises), for example after heavy sweating, not drinking enough, eating salty food, or diarrhoea.
[bio26-s3.2.1] 2. Receptors: osmoreceptors, special cells in the hypothalamus of the brain that detect the concentration of the blood, notice the change.
[bio26-s3.2.2] 3. The hypothalamus causes the pituitary gland to release more ADH into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.3] 4. ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys.
[bio26-s3.2.4] 5. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.5] 6. A small volume of concentrated, dark-yellow urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.2.6] 7. The water concentration of the blood rises back towards normal, and ADH release falls again. This is negative feedback, because the response reverses the original change.
[bio26-s3.2.7] At the same time the hypothalamus triggers thirst, so the person drinks more. Drinking is the only way to replace lost water, because the kidneys can only reduce further loss.
[bio26-s3.3.b] The same system works in reverse when the blood becomes too dilute. The kidney then lets more water go, so the concentration returns to normal.
[bio26-s3.3.0] 1. Stimulus: after drinking a large volume of water, the water concentration of the blood rises (it becomes more dilute).
[bio26-s3.3.1] 2. The hypothalamus detects the change.
[bio26-s3.3.2] 3. The pituitary releases less ADH.
[bio26-s3.3.3] 4. The collecting ducts become less permeable to water, because fewer water channels are present in their walls.
[bio26-s3.3.4] 5. Less water is reabsorbed, so more stays in the duct.
[bio26-s3.3.5] 6. A large volume of dilute, pale urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.3.6] 7. The blood's water concentration falls back to normal and ADH release returns to its usual level.
[bio26-s3.4.b] Everyday situations change the concentration of the blood, and ADH release changes with them. Each example below follows the same chain from blood concentration to urine volume.
[bio26-s3.4.0] On a hot day or during exercise, much water is lost in sweat, so more ADH is released and the urine is small in volume and dark.
[bio26-s3.4.1] A high salt intake raises the solute concentration of the blood, so more ADH is released, more water is reabsorbed and a smaller volume of urine is produced. The extra salt is excreted in this smaller volume, and the person also feels thirsty.
[bio26-s3.4.2] Alcohol reduces the release of ADH, so the collecting ducts reabsorb less water and a large volume of dilute urine is produced. This causes dehydration (loss of body water), which contributes to the headache and thirst of a hangover.
[bio26-s3.5.b] The two feedback responses can be drawn as one negative-feedback loop around the normal blood water concentration. Drawing it shows that every change is pushed back towards normal.
[bio26-s3.5.0] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop for water balance, with normal blood water concentration at the centre. One branch shows the water concentration falling, the hypothalamus detecting it, the pituitary releasing more ADH, the collecting ducts becoming more permeable, more water being reabsorbed, a small volume of concentrated urine being produced, and the concentration returning to normal. The other branch shows the water concentration rising, less ADH, less permeable collecting ducts, less water reabsorbed, a large volume of dilute urine, and a return to normal. Optionally add a small sketch of a collecting duct passing through the salty medulla with arrows for water leaving by osmosis.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio5-s1: Characteristics of living things
[bio5-s1.0.b] Biology is the study of living things, called organisms. No single feature separates living from non-living, so the specification lists eleven characteristics: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis. Together they define life.
[bio5-s1.0.0] An organism shows all eleven characteristics at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once. A dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but it is still alive.
[bio5-s1.0.1] Some non-living things show one or two characteristics. A crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells.
[bio5-s1.0.2] A fire seems to 'use oxygen', 'release energy' and 'spread', but it has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions, so it is not alive.
[bio5-s1.0.3] Something is alive only if it shows the full set, not just one or two features. Viruses are the main borderline case. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio5-s1.1.b] Living things are not random mixtures of chemicals: they are built in an ordered way, and the basic building block is the cell. Two of the eleven characteristics, organisation and cellular basis, describe this.
[bio5-s1.1.0] Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms (those made of many cells), cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs.
[bio5-s1.1.1] Even a single cell has organised parts called organelles (small structures inside a cell, each with its own job). There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio5-s1.1.2] Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life, so it is the basic unit of structure and function.
[bio5-s1.1.3] Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process. Multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells, each adapted to one job.
[bio5-s1.1.4] All cells share a cell membrane, cytoplasm (the jelly-like fluid inside the cell), ribosomes (which make proteins) and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
[bio5-s1.1.5] All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division. New cells are never made from non-living material.
[bio5-s1.1.6] These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century. Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
[bio5-s1.2.b] Every organism needs a supply of food to stay alive and to build and repair its body. Nutrition is how it gets that food, and organisms fall into two groups depending on whether they make it or take it in.
[bio5-s1.2.0] Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
[bio5-s1.2.1] Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances (ones not made by living things, such as carbon dioxide and water). Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio5-s1.2.2] A few bacteria are chemosynthetic: instead of light, they use energy from chemical reactions to make their food, so they can live where there is no sunlight.
[bio5-s1.2.3] Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals.
[bio5-s1.2.4] Fungi and many bacteria are also heterotrophs: they absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio5-s1.3.b] Food is only useful once its stored energy can be released in a form cells can use. Respiration does this inside every living cell, and it is easily confused with breathing.
[bio5-s1.3.0] Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food, mainly glucose, inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy), which powers the cell's activities.
[bio5-s1.3.1] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
[bio5-s1.3.2] Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio5-s1.3.3] In human muscle, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid. In yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[bio5-s1.3.4] Respiration is not the same as breathing. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange), while respiration is a chemical process in cells.
[bio5-s1.3.5] Plants respire all the time, day and night, because every living cell needs energy continuously. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio5-s1.4.b] Living cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Metabolism is the name for all of them, and they fall into two types: those that build up and those that break down.
[bio5-s1.4.0] Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction).
[bio5-s1.4.1] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose made from carbon dioxide and water) and protein synthesis from amino acids.
[bio5-s1.4.2] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, and the digestion of starch to maltose.
[bio5-s1.4.3] In short, anabolic means building up and using energy, while catabolic means breaking down and releasing energy. There is more on this in Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life.
[bio5-s1.5.b] Chemical reactions in cells make some products that the body cannot use. Excretion removes them before they cause harm.
[bio5-s1.5.0] Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism, which are substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed. If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
[bio5-s1.5.1] Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs. Urea, made in the liver from excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in urine. Excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
[bio5-s1.5.2] Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata (the tiny pores in leaves). Some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
[bio5-s1.5.3] Excretion is not egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.b] Cells work properly only when the conditions around them stay within narrow limits, even if the outside world changes. Homeostasis is the control that keeps them steady.
[bio5-s1.6.0] Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, such as body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance and pH, despite changes outside or inside the organism.
[bio5-s1.6.1] It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH, so a large change would slow or stop the reactions of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.2] For example, human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C, and insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. There is more on the mechanism, negative feedback, in Homeostasis.
[bio5-s1.7.b] Organisms must cope with a changing environment and must also produce new individuals, because every organism eventually dies. Response and reproduction cover these two needs.
[bio5-s1.7.0] Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
[bio5-s1.7.1] Animals respond quickly through the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio5-s1.7.2] Plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure, e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short, which reduces water loss. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio5-s1.7.3] Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
[bio5-s1.7.4] Asexual reproduction: one parent and no gametes (sex cells). The offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria and budding in yeast.
[bio5-s1.7.5] Sexual reproduction: two gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote (the first cell of the new organism). The offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans and flowering plants.
[bio5-s1.8.b] Organisms get bigger as they develop, and they pass their features on to the next generation. Growth and heredity describe these two processes.
[bio5-s1.8.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism, the building reactions of metabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
[bio5-s1.8.1] Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips. Most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
[bio5-s1.8.2] Heredity: the passing of genetic information, carried by genes made of DNA, from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. 'Like produces like', e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
[bio5-s1.8.3] Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits (its characteristics). Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance.
[bio5-s1.9.b] Here is the full set in one place for quick revision. An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life.
[bio5-s1.9.0] Organisation: highly ordered structure, from cells up to organ systems.
[bio5-s1.9.1] Response: detecting a stimulus and reacting to it.
[bio5-s1.9.2] Respiration: releasing energy from food inside cells.
