CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio15-s1: Respiration as a catabolic process
[bio15-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a constant supply of usable energy. Respiration is the process that gets it, by breaking down food molecules and transferring the energy they hold to ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio15-s1.0.0] Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food, mainly glucose, in every living cell. The energy released is transferred to ATP, so respiration releases energy rather than making it.
[bio15-s1.0.1] Respiration is catabolic, meaning a large molecule such as glucose is broken down into smaller molecules and energy is released. The products are CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂, depending on the conditions.
[bio15-s1.0.2] Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process, because it builds small molecules up into large ones and takes in energy, whereas respiration breaks large ones down.
[bio15-s1.0.3] The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once, as in burning. This is why much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. Energy not trapped as ATP is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
[bio15-s1.0.4] The ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport (moving substances across a membrane using energy) and building large molecules.
[bio15-s1.0.5] Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed (built into sugar).
[bio15-s1.0.6] Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake of carbon by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle. There is more on this in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio15-s1.0.7] Glucose is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down). Glycogen (in animals) and starch (in plants) are first broken down to glucose, and fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired.
[bio15-s1.1.b] Aerobic (with oxygen) respiration breaks glucose down completely, releasing energy. Knowing where the glucose and oxygen come from, and where the carbon dioxide and water go, shows how organisms and their surroundings are linked.
[bio15-s1.1.0] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
[bio15-s1.1.1] Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Its chemicals are those of the photosynthesis equation reversed, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
[bio15-s1.1.2] Source of glucose in plants: photosynthesis, or stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant). In animals: digested food absorbed into the blood, or glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
[bio15-s1.1.3] Source of oxygen in humans: the air, taken in through the lungs and carried to the cells by red blood cells, which is how it reaches every cell.
[bio15-s1.1.4] Source of oxygen in plants: the air, entering through stomata (leaf pores) and lenticels (pores in woody stems); soil air, taken in by the roots; and the plant's own photosynthesis. In aquatic organisms it is oxygen dissolved in water.
[bio15-s1.1.5] Fate of carbon dioxide: it diffuses out of cells. In humans it is carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
[bio15-s1.1.6] Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted. Some is breathed out as water vapour.
[bio15-s1.2.b] When oxygen is scarce or absent, cells can still release some energy from glucose, but not nearly as much. The products depend on the organism.
[bio15-s1.2.0] Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. Because the glucose is only partly broken down, much of the energy stays in the products, so much less energy is released per glucose and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.2.1] In animals, such as human muscle during intense exercise, and in some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
[bio15-s1.2.2] In yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen such as roots in waterlogged soil, the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
[bio15-s1.2.3] Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. In baking, yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise and the ethanol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and wine-making, yeast's ethanol is the useful product. In making yogurt and cheese, bacteria's lactic acid is the useful product.
[bio15-s1.2.4] In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire.
[bio15-s1.2.5] Oxygen debt: after exercise, extra oxygen is needed to remove the lactic acid. It is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose, and the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
[bio15-s1.2.6] Obligate anaerobes respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen, for example the bacteria that cause tetanus. Yeast can respire either way, so it is a facultative anaerobe.
[bio15-s1.3.b] Respiration happens in two stages in different parts of the cell. Stage 1 is the same whether or not oxygen is present, and what happens next depends on oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid inside the cell) of every cell and does not need oxygen. 1. Glucose, with 6 carbon atoms, is split into two molecules of pyruvate, each with 3 carbon atoms. 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
[bio15-s1.3.1] With oxygen, stage 2 takes place in the mitochondria (the organelles where aerobic respiration is completed). 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water.
[bio15-s1.3.2] 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.3] Without oxygen, pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (in animals and some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast and plants). No further ATP is made, so anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s1.3.4] Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria, so their aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane.
[bio15-s1.3.5] Diagram: flow diagram. Glucose (cytoplasm), then glycolysis (small ATP), then 2 pyruvate. Oxygen present: mitochondrion, then CO₂, water, large ATP. No oxygen: cytoplasm, then lactic acid (animals) or ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1, stage 2.
[bio15-s1.4.b] Aerobic and anaerobic respiration start the same way but differ in how far glucose is broken down and how much ATP results. Respiration also contrasts with photosynthesis: respiration is catabolic and happens in all living cells, while photosynthesis is anabolic and happens only in cells with chloroplasts.
[bio15-s1.4.0] Oxygen: needed in aerobic respiration; not needed in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.1] Location: aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria; anaerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm only.
[bio15-s1.4.2] Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O, in aerobic respiration; incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂, in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.3] Energy: a large amount of ATP in aerobic respiration; a small amount, only the ATP made in glycolysis, in anaerobic respiration, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio15-s1.4.4] Stages: stage 1 followed by stage 2 in aerobic respiration; stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.5] Respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂. It happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
[bio15-s1.4.6] Photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂. It happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only in the light.

CANDIDATES bio22-s1: Pathogens
[bio22-s1.0.b] Some organisms and particles make us ill, and the biology of disease starts with naming them. A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host, the organism it lives in or on.
[bio22-s1.0.0] An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, which is why such diseases can spread through a population.
[bio22-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful, so bacteria and fungi should not be lumped together as 'germs'. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s1.0.2] Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: they invade and destroy host cells or tissues, they release toxins, or they take nutrients from the host.
[bio22-s1.0.3] Invasion means the pathogen destroys cells: viruses burst the cells they reproduce in, and the malaria parasite bursts red blood cells.
[bio22-s1.0.4] Toxins are poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work; the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms.
[bio22-s1.0.5] In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage, for example inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia.
[bio22-s1.1.b] A pathogen only survives if it can keep moving from host to host. So each one is adapted to its host and, often, to a carrier that helps it spread.
[bio22-s1.1.0] Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host.
[bio22-s1.1.1] Many pathogens need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease: the mosquito for malaria and aphids (small sap-sucking insects) for many plant viruses.
[bio22-s1.1.2] The routes by which pathogens pass between hosts are called routes of transmission. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.2.b] A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is neither a cell nor a virus. It is the odd one out among pathogens, because a protein alone causes the disease.
[bio22-s1.2.0] The name comes from proteinaceous infectious particle, a term coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
[bio22-s1.2.1] A prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein, the prion protein, which is found on nerve cells, especially in the brain.
[bio22-s1.2.2] When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction.
[bio22-s1.2.3] Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so that the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope. This is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathy.
[bio22-s1.2.4] Prion diseases take years to appear, are always fatal and have no cure.
[bio22-s1.3.b] Prion diseases have been found in cattle, sheep and humans. Because prions are very hard to destroy, prevention depends on keeping infected material out of the food chain.
[bio22-s1.3.0] Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, mad cow disease) in cattle spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals.
[bio22-s1.3.1] Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans is linked to eating BSE-infected beef and was first described in the UK in 1996.
[bio22-s1.3.2] Scrapie affects sheep. Kuru affected the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
[bio22-s1.3.3] Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants.
[bio22-s1.3.4] The immune system does not attack prions, because they are the body's own protein in a different shape, so the body has no defence against them.
[bio22-s1.3.5] Prevention therefore keeps prions out of the food chain: meat-and-bone meal is banned from the feed of farm animals, and the brain and spinal cord of cattle are removed from food as specified risk material.
[bio22-s1.4.b] A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat. It is an obligate intracellular parasite, meaning it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s1.4.0] Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work.
[bio22-s1.4.1] Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins (molecules on the cell surface that it fits onto), so it infects only certain hosts and tissues: HIV infects helper T cells and influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways.
[bio22-s1.4.2] Human viral diseases include influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) and AIDS (HIV).
[bio22-s1.4.3] Animal viral diseases include foot-and-mouth disease, which affects cattle, sheep and pigs, and avian influenza, which affects birds.
[bio22-s1.4.4] Plant viral disease: tobacco mosaic virus infects tobacco and tomatoes.
[bio22-s1.4.5] Antibiotics don't affect viruses, because viruses have no cell structures for the drugs to target and reproduce inside host cells.
[bio22-s1.5.b] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes with no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, about 1–10 µm long. Only some bacteria are pathogens, but those that are can multiply very quickly inside the body.
[bio22-s1.5.0] Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission (one cell splitting into two) in host tissues, so numbers rise fast.
[bio22-s1.5.1] Most bacteria cause harm by releasing toxins; some invade and destroy tissue.
[bio22-s1.5.2] Most bacterial diseases can be treated with antibiotics, which target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s1.5.3] Antibiotic-resistant strains (bacteria no longer killed by the drug) are increasing, for example MRSA.
[bio22-s1.6.b] Each bacterial disease has a named bacterium and a usual route of spread. Learn them as pairs, and note the plant and cattle examples as well as the human ones.
[bio22-s1.6.0] Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spreads in airborne droplets and mainly affects the lungs.
[bio22-s1.6.1] Food poisoning is caused by Salmonella, which spreads through contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs.
[bio22-s1.6.2] Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae in water contaminated with faeces, and causes severe diarrhoea.
[bio22-s1.6.3] Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani. Its spores in soil enter a wound, and its toxin causes muscle spasms, known as lockjaw.
[bio22-s1.6.4] Lyme disease is caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and is passed on by tick bites, so the tick is the vector.
[bio22-s1.6.5] In plants, fire blight of apple and pear trees is caused by Erwinia amylovora.
[bio22-s1.6.6] In cattle, bovine TB is caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
[bio22-s1.7.b] Fungi are eukaryotes (their cells have a nucleus) with cell walls of chitin. Most are made of thread-like hyphae, and some, such as yeasts, are single cells. Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts.
[bio22-s1.7.0] Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, and the fungus absorbs the products.
[bio22-s1.7.1] Fungi spread by spores, which are often carried by wind, water or contact.
[bio22-s1.7.2] Athlete's foot and ringworm are skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (the protein in skin); ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
[bio22-s1.7.3] Thrush is caused by the yeast Candida albicans in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check.
[bio22-s1.7.4] Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
[bio22-s1.7.5] Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.
[bio22-s1.8.b] Fungi infect plants and animals as well as people. The examples below affect trees, cereal crops and amphibians.
[bio22-s1.8.0] Ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, is killing ash trees across Europe.
[bio22-s1.8.1] Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, is spread by elm bark beetles, which act as vectors.
[bio22-s1.8.2] Cereal rusts such as wheat rust are caused by Puccinia species.
[bio22-s1.8.3] Chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.9.b] Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are neither animals, plants nor fungi (kingdom Protista). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host. Malaria is one example.
[bio22-s1.9.0] Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, which is passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector.
[bio22-s1.9.1] The parasite reproduces first in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia (too few red blood cells).
[bio22-s1.9.2] Malaria is controlled with insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining of standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs such as artemisinin. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.10.b] Other protists cause gut and blood diseases in people, and one protist-like organism caused a famine in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.10.0] Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, which spreads in water or food contaminated with faeces.
[bio22-s1.10.1] African sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei, which is spread by tsetse flies, the vector.
[bio22-s1.10.2] Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus, being thread-like and spread by spores, but it is classed with the protists.
[bio22-s1.10.3] Its spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather, and it rots leaves and tubers.
[bio22-s1.10.4] Potato late blight caused the Irish Great Famine from 1845, because potatoes were the main crop and the disease destroyed them. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.11.b] A parasite lives in or on another organism, its host, feeding on it and harming it. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods. Endoparasites live inside the host.
[bio22-s1.11.0] Tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers.
[bio22-s1.11.1] Humans become infected with tapeworms by eating undercooked infected pork or beef.
[bio22-s1.11.2] Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) is a flatworm of sheep and cattle that damages the liver.
[bio22-s1.11.3] The liver fluke needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.11.4] Onchocerca volvulus is a roundworm spread by blackflies that causes river blindness. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.12.b] Ectoparasites live on the outside of the host. Worms inside the body are treated with drugs, and prevention targets how the parasite reaches us.
[bio22-s1.12.0] Head lice are ectoparasites that feed on blood from the scalp.
[bio22-s1.12.1] Ticks feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio22-s1.12.2] Parasitic worms are treated with anthelmintic drugs that kill them, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s1.12.3] Prevention: cook meat thoroughly, maintain sanitation, and control the second host or vector, because breaking the life cycle stops the parasite spreading.
[bio22-s1.13.b] The six types of pathogen differ in whether they are cells, what genetic material they carry, their size and how they are treated. The treatment follows from the structure, which is why antibiotics work only on bacteria.
[bio22-s1.13.0] Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment.
[bio22-s1.13.1] Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; treated with antivirals, not antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.2] Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; treated with antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.3] Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; from single cells to large networks of hyphae; treated with antifungals.
[bio22-s1.13.4] Protist: eukaryotic and mostly single-celled; DNA; treated with antiprotozoal drugs such as antimalarials.
[bio22-s1.13.5] Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; from millimetres to metres in size; treated with anthelmintics.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio31-s9: Pyramids of biomass
[bio31-s9.0.b] Counting organisms ignores how big they are. A pyramid of biomass fixes this by measuring how much living material there is at each trophic level (feeding level) of a food chain. There is more on how energy and nutrients pass between levels in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio31-s9.0.0] Biomass is the total mass of living material, usually measured as dry mass (the mass after all water is removed), in a given area or volume at a given time. It is measured in grams per square metre (g/m²) for land, or grams per cubic metre (g/m³) for water.
[bio31-s9.0.1] A pyramid of biomass shows the total dry mass of the organisms at each trophic level of a food chain or web, at one time, per unit area.
[bio31-s9.0.2] Each level is a horizontal bar whose width is proportional to its biomass, with the producers (organisms that make their own food) at the base.
[bio31-s9.0.3] Dry mass is used because the water content of organisms varies greatly, e.g. lettuce leaves are mostly water and wood much less, and it changes from hour to hour. Fresh (wet) mass would therefore be misleading.
[bio31-s9.0.4] Pyramids of biomass are almost always upright, because biomass decreases at each trophic level. Only part of the food eaten becomes the consumer's body; the rest is respired (lost as CO₂ and water), egested (passed out as undigested faeces) or excreted (waste made by the body's own chemistry).
[bio31-s9.1.b] Biomass cannot be read off an organism directly, because most of its fresh mass is water. It is measured by drying a sample until only the organic material is left.
[bio31-s9.1.0] 1. Sample the organisms at each trophic level in a measured area, e.g. a quadrat (a square frame of known size laid on the ground).
[bio31-s9.1.1] 2. Dry them in an oven at about 80–100 °C, which is below the temperature at which organic matter burns, so the water is driven off but the organisms' tissue is not destroyed.
[bio31-s9.1.2] 3. Weigh them, return them to the oven and reweigh until the mass stays constant, which means all the water has gone.
[bio31-s9.1.3] 4. Scale up to the whole area, because the sample covers only a small part of it.
[bio31-s9.1.4] Drying kills the organisms, so only small samples are taken, and the biomass of large organisms such as trees is estimated from their measurements.
[bio31-s9.2.b] Putting a pyramid of numbers beside a pyramid of biomass for the same food chain shows why the two can look so different. A pyramid of numbers counts individuals; a pyramid of biomass counts the mass of material.
[bio31-s9.2.0] In the chain oak tree → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawk, the oak is a single organism, so the numbers pyramid has a narrow base.
[bio31-s9.2.1] That one tree holds far more material than all the animals it supports, so the biomass pyramid is upright with the widest bar for the tree.
[bio31-s9.2.2] Diagram: a pyramid of numbers beside a pyramid of biomass for oak tree → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawk. The numbers pyramid has a narrow base for the one tree; the biomass pyramid is upright, with the widest bar for the tree.
[bio31-s9.3.b] Pyramids of biomass are almost always upright, but not always. In some aquatic ecosystems the base can be narrower than the level above it, which is called an inverted pyramid.
[bio31-s9.3.0] In some aquatic ecosystems, at certain times, the biomass of the producers (phytoplankton, tiny floating photosynthetic organisms) is smaller than that of the primary consumers (zooplankton, tiny floating animals) that eat them, so the pyramid is inverted at the base.
[bio31-s9.3.1] Reason: phytoplankton are tiny, reproduce very rapidly and are eaten almost as fast as they are produced. At any one moment the standing crop, the biomass present, is therefore small, even though over a year they produce far more biomass than the zooplankton.
[bio31-s9.3.2] In other words, the pyramid measures the biomass present at one moment, not the rate at which it is produced. A small, fast-turning-over base can still support a larger mass of consumers.
[bio31-s9.3.3] Diagram: an inverted pyramid of biomass for phytoplankton → zooplankton, with the producer bar narrower than the primary consumer bar, labelled.
[bio31-s9.4.b] Both pyramids are tools for studying how a food chain is built, so they share many features. They differ in what they count, and that decides which gives the truer picture.
[bio31-s9.4.0] Similarities: both show the trophic levels of a food chain or web as stacked bars with the producers at the base. Both are usually upright, both are measured at one time and change with the seasons, and both rely on sampling.
[bio31-s9.4.1] Similarities: both are models that show why there are fewer, or less mass of, top consumers, and both usually leave out decomposers (organisms that break down dead material).
[bio31-s9.4.2] What is measured: the number of individuals for pyramids of numbers, against the total dry mass per unit area for pyramids of biomass.
[bio31-s9.4.3] Size of organisms: pyramids of numbers ignore it, so one tree counts as one. Pyramids of biomass take it into account, so a large tree counts for its full mass. A biomass pyramid therefore gives a truer picture of how much food is available at each level.
[bio31-s9.4.4] Shape: pyramids of numbers are often inverted or spindle-shaped where one large producer feeds many herbivores or where parasites are involved. Pyramids of biomass are almost always upright on land, including for tree-based chains and parasite chains, and are inverted only in some aquatic ecosystems at some times.
[bio31-s9.5.b] Neither pyramid is perfect. The practical difficulties, and the things neither shows, decide which one is more useful for a given study of an ecosystem.
[bio31-s9.5.0] Ease of collecting data: counting is quicker and does not harm the organisms, for pyramids of numbers. Drying and weighing is slow and destructive, so it needs small samples, and large organisms must be estimated, for pyramids of biomass.
[bio31-s9.5.1] Drawing to scale: very large differences in numbers, e.g. millions of grass plants and one fox, make pyramids of numbers hard to draw to scale. Biomass values are usually less extreme, so pyramids of biomass are easier to draw to scale.
[bio31-s9.5.2] What neither shows well is the rate of production over time. Biomass shows only the standing crop, so short-lived, fast-reproducing organisms are under-represented.
[bio31-s9.5.3] What neither shows well is the energy content. A gram of fat contains about twice the energy of a gram of carbohydrate or protein, so equal biomass does not mean equal energy.
[bio31-s9.5.4] Judgement: pyramids of numbers are useful for a quick, non-destructive survey of feeding relationships, especially where the organisms are similar in size. Pyramids of biomass better show the amount of material passing up a food chain and the effect of energy loss, so they are more useful for comparing ecosystems.
[bio31-s9.5.5] A pyramid of energy, which shows the energy flowing through each level over a period of time and is never inverted, overcomes the limitations of both.
[bio31-s9.6.b] Working through numbers shows how the same food chain gives two different pyramids. The data below are invented for practice, with three trophic levels in one area.
[bio31-s9.6.0] Sample data (invented for practice) for a woodland food chain in one area: oak trees 1 (total dry biomass 2 000 kg); caterpillars 5 000 (total dry biomass 0.5 kg); blue tits 4 (total dry biomass 0.02 kg).
[bio31-s9.6.1] Pyramid of numbers: narrow base (1), very wide caterpillar bar (5 000), narrow top (4), so the pyramid is spindle-shaped, wide in the middle and narrow at both ends.
[bio31-s9.6.2] Pyramid of biomass: upright, decreasing from 2 000 kg to 0.5 kg to 0.02 kg (not to scale, because the values differ too much to draw accurately).
[bio31-s9.6.3] Interpretation: the tree is one organism but contains far more material than all the caterpillars, so the pyramid of biomass shows the food available at each level correctly.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

CANDIDATES bio34-s1: The carbon cycle
[bio34-s1.0.b] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the elements living things are built from have to be reused. Carbon is the clearest example: it moves round and round between organisms and their surroundings.
[bio34-s1.0.0] A biogeochemical cycle, or nutrient cycle, is the continuous movement of a chemical element between living things (bio) and the non-living environment (geo): the air, water, rocks and soil.
[bio34-s1.0.1] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the same atoms are used again and again. Energy, by contrast, flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.0.2] Carbon is the element at the centre of every biomolecule (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids; see Biomolecules and metabolism), so every organism needs a constant supply. Producers (organisms that make their own food, such as plants) take it in as CO₂; consumers get it already built into food.
[bio34-s1.0.3] A store (reservoir) is a place where carbon is held: the atmosphere (as CO₂, and a little methane, CH₄); living organisms (biomass, the mass of living material); dead organic matter in soil; the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate); rocks such as limestone and chalk (calcium carbonate); and fossil fuels.
[bio34-s1.0.4] A process, or flux, is whatever moves carbon from one store to another. Stores are the boxes of the cycle and processes are the arrows between them.
[bio34-s1.0.5] CO₂ makes up only about 0.04% of the air (over 420 parts per million), yet it is the source of carbon for almost all life on Earth, because photosynthesis starts from it.
[bio34-s1.1.b] Carbon dioxide leaves the air in only a few ways. By far the most important is photosynthesis, but dissolving in water and shell building also lock carbon away.
[bio34-s1.1.0] Photosynthesis by plants, algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating algae) fixes CO₂ into glucose and then into the rest of their biomass, so they act as carbon sinks (stores that take in more carbon than they release).
[bio34-s1.1.1] Photosynthesis is the only major route by which carbon enters living things, so nearly all the carbon in a food chain started as CO₂ taken in by a producer.
[bio34-s1.1.2] Dissolving: CO₂ dissolves in the surface of the oceans, lakes and rivers, and aquatic producers take it up for photosynthesis.
[bio34-s1.1.3] Shell and skeleton building: many marine organisms (e.g. corals, molluscs and some phytoplankton) combine dissolved carbonate with calcium to make calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.
[bio34-s1.1.4] When these organisms die, their shells and skeletons settle on the sea floor and, over millions of years, form limestone and chalk. These rocks are very long-term stores because the carbon stays locked in them for so long.
[bio34-s1.2.b] Once carbon is in a producer it does not stay there. It passes along the food chain and into the dead matter that every organism leaves behind.
[bio34-s1.2.0] Feeding: carbon in the producer's biomass passes to herbivores and then carnivores when they eat and digest it. At each step most of it is respired away (broken down to release energy, giving off CO₂) rather than built into the consumer's body.
[bio34-s1.2.1] Each step in a food chain is a trophic level (a feeding level). There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.2.2] Egestion and excretion: carbon leaves animals in faeces and in wastes such as urea, and enters the dead organic matter store, as do shed leaves and dead organisms. This store is the food supply for decomposers.
[bio34-s1.3.b] Carbon dioxide is put back into the air by respiration, by decomposers, by burning and by geological release. If it were not returned, producers would run out of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.3.0] Respiration by plants, animals and microorganisms breaks down glucose and releases CO₂. Details are in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio34-s1.3.1] Plants respire all the time, day and night, so the respiration arrow must start from plants as well as animals. Over a growing season photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the plant as a whole gains carbon.
[bio34-s1.3.2] Combustion: burning biomass (wood, peat, forest fires) or fossil fuels combines the carbon with oxygen and releases it as CO₂ very quickly.
[bio34-s1.3.3] Natural geological release: volcanoes release CO₂ from rocks, and the weathering of limestone releases some. This is small compared with the biological fluxes and with fossil-fuel burning.
[bio34-s1.4.b] Dead organisms, faeces and fallen leaves contain a lot of carbon. Decomposers are the living things that break this matter down and return its carbon to the air, so the cycle can keep turning.
[bio34-s1.4.0] Decomposers are bacteria and fungi. They feed saprophytically (on dead organic matter): they secrete enzymes that digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products, and respire them, releasing CO₂ and minerals.
[bio34-s1.4.1] Say that decomposers digest the matter and then respire the products. The CO₂ comes from their respiration, not from the matter breaking down into CO₂ directly.
[bio34-s1.4.2] Without decomposers, carbon would stay locked in dead matter and the CO₂ supply for photosynthesis would run out. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi, and soil microbiomes are covered in The microbiome.
[bio34-s1.4.3] Decomposers work fastest when it is warm, moist and well aerated, because they respire aerobically (using oxygen).
[bio34-s1.4.4] In cold, waterlogged, acidic or oxygen-poor places decomposition is very slow, so dead matter builds up, e.g. as peat in bogs.
[bio34-s1.4.5] Anaerobic decomposition: where there is no oxygen (waterlogged soils, bogs, rice paddies, landfill, slurry tanks and the stomachs of cattle and sheep), some microorganisms break organic matter down to methane (CH₄) instead of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.4.6] The methane-producing microorganisms are archaea, a separate group of single-celled organisms from bacteria (see Domains of life). Methane in the air is slowly converted to CO₂.
[bio34-s1.5.b] Fossil fuels are a store of carbon that the natural cycle leaves almost untouched. People now burn them, which is why they matter so much to the carbon cycle.
[bio34-s1.5.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) are the remains of organisms that lived millions of years ago and were buried before they could decompose fully.
[bio34-s1.5.1] Coal formed mainly from land plants in ancient swamps. Oil and natural gas formed mainly from tiny marine organisms (plankton) buried in sea-floor mud. Heat and pressure over millions of years changed them into fuels.
[bio34-s1.5.2] The carbon in fossil fuels was fixed by photosynthesis long ago, so they store ancient solar energy and carbon. Peat is an early stage of the same process, which is why it is sometimes counted with them.
[bio34-s1.5.3] In the natural cycle almost no carbon leaves this store.
[bio34-s1.5.4] Burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution moves carbon locked away for millions of years into the atmosphere in a few centuries, far faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can take it back, so atmospheric CO₂ rises. Consequences are in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.b] Whether a store helps to lower or raise atmospheric CO₂ depends on whether carbon is flowing in or out. Photosynthesis shows how plants act as sinks; here the sink and source distinction is applied to every store and to the overall balance of the cycle.
[bio34-s1.6.0] A store acts as a sink when its inputs exceed its outputs, so it gains carbon, and as a source when its outputs exceed its inputs, so it releases carbon.
[bio34-s1.6.1] The same store can switch between the two: a growing forest is a sink; a mature forest roughly balances; a burning or felled forest is a source. A healthy wet bog is a sink; a drained or burning bog is a source.
[bio34-s1.6.2] The main natural sinks on a carbon-cycle diagram are land plants (forests and grassland, with the soil carbon under them); algae and phytoplankton in the oceans (with dissolving CO₂ and shells sinking to the sea floor); bogs and other wetlands; and limestone and fossil fuels as very long-term stores.
[bio34-s1.6.3] These sinks are compared, and the strategies that use them are described, in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.4] Before large-scale fossil-fuel burning, the CO₂ taken up by photosynthesis and the oceans each year was roughly balanced by the CO₂ released by respiration, decomposition and natural fires. Atmospheric CO₂ therefore stayed nearly constant, at about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution.
[bio34-s1.6.5] Deforestation and fossil-fuel burning have tipped this balance, so more CO₂ is now added than removed.
[bio34-s1.6.6] Seasonal pattern: atmospheric CO₂ falls each northern summer, when photosynthesis exceeds respiration over the large land area of the northern hemisphere, and rises each winter, when respiration and decomposition exceed photosynthesis. This happens on top of the long-term rise.
[bio34-s1.7.b] A model represents the cycle in a diagram and in words, and is used to explain and predict. It is only as good as its limits allow.
[bio34-s1.7.0] Diagram: draw boxes for the stores: carbon dioxide in the air; green plants and algae; animals; dead organisms and wastes; decomposers (bacteria and fungi); fossil fuels; oceans.
[bio34-s1.7.1] Diagram arrows, each labelled with its process: photosynthesis (air → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (plants and animals → dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → decomposers); respiration (plants, animals and decomposers → air).
[bio34-s1.7.2] Diagram arrows, continued: fossilisation (dead matter → fossil fuels, over millions of years); combustion (fossil fuels and wood → air); dissolving (air ↔ oceans).
[bio34-s1.7.3] Prediction (a): cutting down and burning a forest removes photosynthesis and adds combustion and the decomposition of the remains, so atmospheric CO₂ rises.
[bio34-s1.7.4] Prediction (b): draining a bog lets air in, so aerobic decomposers break down the peat and release CO₂.
[bio34-s1.7.5] Prediction (c): if decomposers were killed (e.g. by soil made very acidic or polluted), dead matter would accumulate, minerals would not be returned to the soil, and plant growth would eventually fall.
[bio34-s1.7.6] Limitation: a diagram shows the processes but not how much carbon moves or how fast. It also treats each store as one box, although the oceans, soils and forests each hold carbon for very different lengths of time.
[bio34-s1.7.7] Limitation: it simplifies feedbacks, e.g. warming speeds up decomposition, which releases more CO₂. It is also a snapshot that hides seasonal and long-term change. There is more on this in Models.

CANDIDATES bio6-s3: Classification
[bio6-s3.0.b] There are so many kinds of living thing that biologists sort them into groups, the way a library sorts books into sections, shelves and titles. The groups sit inside one another in a fixed set of levels.
[bio6-s3.0.0] Classification is the grouping of organisms according to their shared characteristics. Taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
[bio6-s3.0.1] Organisms are placed in a hierarchy of ranks (taxa, the levels of grouping), from broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
[bio6-s3.0.2] Moving down the ranks, each group has fewer members, which share more features and are more closely related. This is why two species in the same genus are far more alike than two in the same kingdom.
[bio6-s3.0.3] Worked example, human: domain Eukarya, kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Primates, family Hominidae, genus Homo, species Homo sapiens.
[bio6-s3.1.b] The species is the smallest rank, so it needs a clear definition and a universal name. Every species gets a two-part scientific name, which is called binomial nomenclature.
[bio6-s3.1.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (young that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, a mule, is infertile, so they are different species.
[bio6-s3.1.1] Limitation of the species definition: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually (e.g. bacteria), because they do not interbreed at all, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding.
[bio6-s3.1.2] Binomial nomenclature: every species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name (capital first letter) followed by the species name (lower case), printed in italics or underlined when handwritten.
[bio6-s3.1.3] Examples of binomial names: Homo sapiens (human), Escherichia coli (a gut bacterium), Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould).
[bio6-s3.1.4] The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature; his Species Plantarum (1753) used it for all plants.
[bio6-s3.1.5] Species in the same genus are closely related, which is why they share the first part of their name. The lion (Panthera leo) and tiger (Panthera tigris) share the genus Panthera.
[bio6-s3.2.b] Before sorting living things into kingdoms, biologists ask one basic question about their cells: is there a nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) or not? The answer splits cellular life into two big groups.
[bio6-s3.2.0] The first division of cellular life is by cell type. Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (the small working parts of a cell). Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, and membrane-bound organelles. There is more on this in Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
[bio6-s3.2.1] Two kingdoms are prokaryotic, Bacteria and Archaea, each forming its own domain. Four kingdoms are eukaryotic, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, all in the domain Eukarya.
[bio6-s3.2.2] The distinction became clear only once the electron microscope showed cell interiors, because the details inside prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are too small to see with a light microscope.
[bio6-s3.3.b] Both prokaryotic kingdoms are single-celled with no nucleus, so cell structure alone does not separate them. One way of telling them apart is the chemistry of their cell walls, and another is rRNA. Each is described by cell type, number of cells, cell wall, nutrition and examples.
[bio6-s3.3.0] Bacteria: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls of peptidoglycan (a mesh of sugars and amino acids). They reproduce by binary fission (one cell splits into two).
[bio6-s3.3.1] Bacterial nutrition varies. Bacteria are autotrophic (make their own food), either photosynthetic or chemosynthetic, or heterotrophic (take in food made by others), either saprophytic (feeding on dead matter) or parasitic (feeding on a living host).
[bio6-s3.3.2] Bacteria examples: Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus (used to make yoghurt) and Streptococcus.
[bio6-s3.3.3] Archaea: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls that have no peptidoglycan. This difference in cell wall is one way of telling them apart from bacteria. Many live in extreme environments.
[bio6-s3.3.4] Archaea examples: methanogens (methane-producing archaea) in cattle digestive systems, and Halobacterium in salt lakes.
[bio6-s3.4.b] The four eukaryotic kingdoms all have a nucleus. They are separated by how many cells they have, what their cell walls are made of, how they get food and how they store carbohydrate.
[bio6-s3.4.0] Protista: eukaryotic and mostly unicellular, though some are multicellular algae such as seaweeds (kelp). They have no tissues (groups of similar cells doing one job) of the kind found in plants and animals. Some are photosynthetic (algae) and some are heterotrophic.
[bio6-s3.4.1] Protista includes eukaryotes that do not belong in the other three eukaryotic kingdoms. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (causes malaria) and Phytophthora infestans (causes potato blight, which caused the Great Famine in Ireland, 1845–1849).
[bio6-s3.4.2] Fungi: eukaryotic and mostly multicellular, made of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium (a tangled mass of threads), though yeasts are unicellular. They have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise, and their cell walls are made of chitin.
[bio6-s3.4.3] Fungi are heterotrophic: they secrete enzymes onto food and absorb the digested products, living as saprophytes or parasites. They store carbohydrate as glycogen and reproduce by spores. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), mushrooms and Penicillium.
[bio6-s3.4.4] Plantae: eukaryotic and multicellular, with cell walls of cellulose and chloroplasts (where photosynthesis happens). They are autotrophic by photosynthesis, store carbohydrate as starch, and mostly do not move. Examples: mosses, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants such as the daisy and oak.
[bio6-s3.4.5] Animalia: eukaryotic and multicellular, with no cell walls. They are heterotrophic, taking in (ingesting) food, and store carbohydrate as glycogen. Most can move and have nerves and muscles. Examples: sponges, earthworms, insects, fish and mammals.
[bio6-s3.5.b] Classification by kingdom and domain only works for things made of cells. Viruses fall outside it.
[bio6-s3.5.0] Viruses are not in any kingdom or domain, because they are not cells. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio6-s3.6.b] Grouping by appearance can mislead, so modern classification also asks which organisms share a recent ancestor. This evolutionary family-tree approach is called phylogeny.
[bio6-s3.6.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms. Phylogenetic classification groups organisms by how closely related they are by descent from common ancestors, rather than by appearance alone.
[bio6-s3.6.1] A phylogenetic tree shows these relationships. Each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor, and the closer two species' branches join, the more recent their common ancestor and the more closely related they are.
[bio6-s3.6.2] Species in the same genus share a very recent common ancestor; genera in the same family share an older one. This is how the ranks of the hierarchy reflect evolutionary history.
[bio6-s3.6.3] Example: the lion and tiger (genus Panthera) are more closely related to each other than either is to the domestic cat (Felis catus), but all three are in the cat family (Felidae).
[bio6-s3.6.4] The dog (Canis familiaris, also written Canis lupus familiaris) is in a different family, Canidae, and all four animals are in the order Carnivora.
[bio6-s3.6.5] Evidence used to work out phylogeny includes shared anatomical structures with the same basic plan, e.g. the five-digit limb of mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians; embryology (the study of early development); and fossils. There is more on this in Evolution: The Origins of Life.
[bio6-s3.6.6] The most powerful evidence is comparison of DNA base sequences and protein amino-acid sequences: the fewer the differences, the more closely related the organisms, because less time has passed for differences to build up.
[bio6-s3.6.7] Appearance can mislead. Whales and dolphins look like fish but are mammals: they breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young on milk.
[bio6-s3.6.8] Diagram: a phylogenetic tree for lion, tiger, domestic cat and dog, with the nodes for genus Panthera, family Felidae and order Carnivora labelled.
[bio6-s3.7.b] Classification is not fixed. Each time scientists gained a new tool for looking at living things, the groupings were redrawn, which is why you will meet different numbers of kingdoms in the history of the subject.
[bio6-s3.7.0] Early systems had two kingdoms, plants and animals. Linnaeus, in the 18th century, classified organisms by visible features.
[bio6-s3.7.1] Microscopes revealed single-celled organisms that fitted neither kingdom. In 1866 Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista.
[bio6-s3.7.2] In 1969 Robert Whittaker proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. Monera held all the prokaryotes.
[bio6-s3.7.3] Fungi were separated from plants in Whittaker's system because they have chitin walls, no chlorophyll and absorb their food.
[bio6-s3.7.4] rRNA sequencing (reading the sequence of a molecule found in ribosomes) by Carl Woese showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups. This gave three domains (1990).
[bio6-s3.7.5] The six kingdoms used in this course followed, with Monera split into Bacteria and Archaea. There is more on this in Domains of life.
[bio6-s3.7.6] Systems change because new technology provides new evidence, e.g. the light microscope, the electron microscope, biochemistry and DNA sequencing. Classification is revised to reflect evolutionary relationships more accurately. It is a model that scientists keep testing and improving.
[bio6-s3.8.b] DNA sequencing, reading the order of bases in an organism's DNA, is now the strongest tool for classification. It is still moving organisms between groups and adding new ones.
[bio6-s3.8.0] DNA sequencing and whole-genome comparisons are now the main evidence for classification, and have moved many organisms. Phytophthora (potato blight) was long classed as a fungus because it grows as threads. Its DNA and its cellulose cell walls show that it belongs with the protists (water moulds).
[bio6-s3.8.1] DNA evidence placed the giant panda in the bear family.
[bio6-s3.8.2] Environmental DNA sequencing, which sequences all the DNA in a soil or water sample, has revealed huge numbers of microorganisms that have never been grown in a laboratory. These add new branches to the tree of life.
[bio6-s3.8.3] Asgard archaea were first reported in 2015 from DNA in sediment near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vents in the Arctic Ocean. They share many genes with eukaryotes.
[bio6-s3.8.4] Some scientists argue that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, which would mean only two primary domains. This is still debated, so classification remains a work in progress.
[bio6-s3.8.5] DNA barcoding uses a short standard gene sequence to identify species quickly, e.g. to identify species from tiny samples or to detect mislabelled food.
[bio6-s3.9.b] A dichotomous key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by answering a series of either/or questions about what you can see. The worked key below identifies six invertebrates (animals without backbones).
[bio6-s3.9.0] A dichotomous key identifies an organism by a series of steps. Each step gives a choice between two contrasting statements (a couplet) about a visible feature. Each statement leads either to a name or to the number of the next couplet.
[bio6-s3.9.1] Worked example: a key to six invertebrates.
[bio6-s3.9.2] 1. Legs present → go to 2; legs absent → go to 4.
[bio6-s3.9.3] 2. Three pairs of legs → ladybird (an insect); more than three pairs of legs → go to 3.
[bio6-s3.9.4] 3. Four pairs of legs → spider; seven pairs of legs → woodlouse.
[bio6-s3.9.5] 4. Shell present → snail; no visible shell → go to 5.
[bio6-s3.9.6] 5. Body divided into rings (segments) → earthworm; body not divided into rings → slug.
[bio6-s3.10.b] To use a key, start at couplet 1 and follow the numbers until you reach a name. Practise tracing a route for each organism so you can do it in the exam.
[bio6-s3.10.0] Using the key: an animal with eight legs goes 1 (legs present) → 2 (more than three pairs) → 3 (four pairs) → spider. Eight legs means four pairs, which is why it ends at spider.
[bio6-s3.10.1] Write the route for one more organism: the slug goes 1 → 4 → 5 → slug, because it has no legs, no visible shell and a body that is not divided into rings.
[bio6-s3.11.b] You may have to build a key of your own, or use the features of the kingdoms to place an unknown organism. Both rely on picking features that give a clear yes or no.
[bio6-s3.11.0] To construct a key, list the organisms, choose a feature that splits them into two groups, and keep splitting each group in two until each organism is on its own.
[bio6-s3.11.1] Use features that are easy to observe and do not vary between individuals or seasons, e.g. number of legs, not size or colour, because size and colour change with age and season and would give unreliable answers.
[bio6-s3.11.2] A key for n organisms needs n − 1 couplets. For example, the six-invertebrate key above has five couplets.
[bio6-s3.11.3] To classify an unknown organism to a kingdom, use the features in order. Does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a bacterium or an archaeon, separated by cell-wall chemistry or rRNA.
[bio6-s3.11.4] If it has a nucleus, ask further questions. Is it multicellular with tissues? What is its cell wall made of? Is it autotrophic or heterotrophic?
[bio6-s3.11.5] Worked example: a newly found organism is eukaryotic, is made of hyphae with chitin walls, and absorbs food from dead leaves. It is in the kingdom Fungi.
[bio6-s3.12.b] Classification is more than tidy labelling. It makes biology easier to study, communicate and apply to health, farming and conservation.
[bio6-s3.12.0] It organises the enormous diversity of life into manageable groups, making it easier to study and to find information.
[bio6-s3.12.1] Scientific names are universal and unique, which avoids the confusion of common names. The robin of Europe (Erithacus rubecula) and the robin of North America (Turdus migratorius) are different species in different genera.
[bio6-s3.12.2] It shows evolutionary relationships, so features of a new organism can be predicted from its relatives. For example, relatives of a plant that produces a useful drug may produce similar compounds.
[bio6-s3.12.3] Health: correctly identifying a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) decides the treatment. A bacterial infection may respond to an antibiotic, but a fungal or viral one will not.
[bio6-s3.12.4] Agriculture and the environment: identifying pests, invasive species and endangered species is needed for control and conservation, and for measuring biodiversity (the variety of living things in an area).

CANDIDATES bio8-s3: Metabolic roles and nutritional sources
[bio8-s3.0.b] Carbohydrates do far more than supply energy. Glucose is fuel, a product of photosynthesis and a building block for storage molecules, and other carbohydrates give structure and carry sugar around organisms.
[bio8-s3.0.0] Respiratory and catabolic role (catabolic means breaking molecules down): glucose is the main respiratory substrate, the fuel respiration uses. It is broken down to release energy, which is trapped as ATP, the cell's energy carrier. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio8-s3.0.1] The brain relies mainly on glucose for its energy, so a steady supply of glucose in the blood matters.
[bio8-s3.0.2] Photosynthetic role: glucose is the product of photosynthesis, the anabolic process (anabolic means building molecules up) in which plants use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio8-s3.0.3] Almost all the carbohydrate in food chains therefore comes from photosynthesis, because plants are the producers that make it first and every consumer depends on them.
[bio8-s3.0.4] Anabolic and storage role: excess glucose is built up into storage polysaccharides (long chains of sugar units), which are starch in plants and glycogen in the liver and muscles of animals.
[bio8-s3.0.5] These stores are broken down again (catabolism) when glucose is needed, so the organism can release it back as fuel.
[bio8-s3.0.6] Structural role: cellulose forms plant cell walls, and chitin forms fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons (the hard outer body casing). The pentose (five-carbon) sugars ribose and deoxyribose are part of RNA and DNA nucleotides.
[bio8-s3.0.7] Transport role: plants move sugar from the leaves to other parts as sucrose, carried in the phloem (the tissue that transports food in plants).
[bio8-s3.1.b] Lipids (fats, oils and related molecules) are used for energy storage, but their uses go much further. They insulate, protect, build membranes, waterproof surfaces and act as regulators.
[bio8-s3.1.0] Respiratory and catabolic role: fats are respired to release energy, especially when glucose and glycogen run low.
[bio8-s3.1.1] A gram of fat releases about twice as much energy as a gram of carbohydrate or protein, so lipid is a compact long-term energy store.
[bio8-s3.1.2] Storage (anabolic): excess food is built into triglycerides (the main type of fat molecule) and stored as fat in adipose tissue, under the skin and around organs in animals.
[bio8-s3.1.3] Plants store oils in seeds, such as sunflower seeds, and in fruits, such as olives.
[bio8-s3.1.4] Insulation: fat under the skin reduces heat loss, as in the thick blubber of whales and seals. The myelin sheath around nerve fibres is rich in lipid and insulates them.
[bio8-s3.1.5] Protection: fat around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against physical damage.
[bio8-s3.1.6] Structural role: phospholipids form the bilayer (double layer) of all cell membranes, including the membranes inside chloroplasts and mitochondria.
[bio8-s3.1.7] Photosynthetic role: the light-absorbing pigments of photosynthesis are held in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast, which are built of phospholipids. Plants also convert some of the glucose made in photosynthesis into oils.
[bio8-s3.2.b] Two further roles of lipids come from their water-repelling nature and from their chemistry as signalling molecules.
[bio8-s3.2.0] Waterproofing: waxes, which are lipids, form the waxy cuticle on leaves, reducing water loss. They also waterproof feathers and fur.
[bio8-s3.2.1] Regulators: steroid hormones such as testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone are lipids, which means not all hormones are proteins.
[bio8-s3.2.2] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fat. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio8-s3.3.b] Proteins have many different jobs in the body, from speeding up reactions to defence, movement and transport. Each role below has a named protein you can give as an example.
[bio8-s3.3.0] Enzymes: almost all enzymes are proteins. They are biological catalysts that speed up the reactions of metabolism (all the chemical reactions in a cell) without being used up, for example amylase digests starch to maltose.
[bio8-s3.3.1] Hormones: some hormones are proteins, for example insulin, made by the pancreas, which lowers blood glucose. Other hormones are lipids.
[bio8-s3.3.2] Immunity: antibodies are proteins made by lymphocytes (B cells, a type of white blood cell). Each binds to a specific antigen (a marker molecule) on a pathogen (a disease-causing organism), marking it for destruction.
[bio8-s3.3.3] Transport of substances in the body: haemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen.
[bio8-s3.3.4] Movement: actin and myosin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they shorten to make the muscle contract.
[bio8-s3.3.5] Energy: protein is respired only when carbohydrate and fat are in short supply.
[bio8-s3.3.6] Excess amino acids (the units proteins are made of) cannot be stored. The liver removes their amino group (deamination), forming urea, which is excreted.
[bio8-s3.3.7] Growth and repair: proteins are the main raw material for new cells and tissues, so children, pregnant women and people recovering from injury need more.
[bio8-s3.4.b] Many proteins sit in the cell membrane (the phospholipid bilayer around every cell) and decide what crosses it and how cells respond to signals. The specification names four kinds.
[bio8-s3.4.0] Membrane proteins are embedded in the phospholipid bilayer. They control what enters and leaves cells and how cells communicate.
[bio8-s3.4.1] Channel proteins form water-filled pores through the membrane that let specific ions or molecules pass by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), for example the sodium and potassium channels of neurons, which allow nerve impulses.
[bio8-s3.4.2] Transporters (carrier proteins) bind a specific molecule and change shape to move it across the membrane.
[bio8-s3.4.3] Some transporters use energy from ATP to move substances against a concentration gradient (active transport), for example the sodium-potassium pump. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio8-s3.4.4] Receptors are proteins on the cell surface, or inside the cell, with a shape that fits one signalling molecule such as a hormone or neurotransmitter. When the signal binds, the cell responds, for example insulin receptors on liver and muscle cells.
[bio8-s3.4.5] This is why a hormone affects only its target cells: only cells with the matching receptor can respond.
[bio8-s3.4.6] Structural proteins give strength and support. Keratin forms hair, nails and the outer skin, and collagen forms skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone. Proteins also form the cytoskeleton, the internal scaffold of cells.
[bio8-s3.4.7] Diagram: a section of cell membrane (phospholipid bilayer) showing a channel protein, a carrier protein, and a receptor protein with its signalling molecule. Label each.
[bio8-s3.5.b] Hormones link the metabolic roles of proteins and lipids, because they are made from both. They let one part of the body control the activity of another.
[bio8-s3.5.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood to target cells, where it regulates their activity. Hormones regulate growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
[bio8-s3.5.1] Hormones are biomolecules from two groups: proteins, such as insulin, and steroid lipids, such as testosterone and oestrogen. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio8-s3.5.2] Hormones work only on cells with the matching receptor proteins, so only target cells respond.
[bio8-s3.5.3] Plants also make chemical regulators, called plant growth regulators, for example auxin.
[bio8-s3.6.b] Knowing the roles of each biomolecule is only half the story. You also need to name the foods that supply each one.
[bio8-s3.6.0] Carbohydrate sources, sugars: fruit, honey, jam, sweets and fizzy drinks, and lactose (milk sugar) in milk.
[bio8-s3.6.1] Carbohydrate sources, starch: bread, pasta, rice, potatoes and breakfast cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.2] Carbohydrate sources, fibre (cellulose): vegetables, fruit, wholegrain cereals and brown bread, and beans.
[bio8-s3.6.3] Lipid sources, fats (mostly saturated): butter, cream, cheese, lard and fatty meat.
[bio8-s3.6.4] Lipid sources, oils (mostly unsaturated): olive and sunflower oils, nuts and seeds, avocados, and oily fish such as salmon and mackerel.
[bio8-s3.6.5] Protein sources, animal: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Protein sources, plant: beans, peas, lentils, nuts, soya and tofu, and cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.6] Animal proteins contain all the essential amino acids (those the body cannot make). Many single plant foods lack one or more, so vegetarian and vegan diets combine a range of plant proteins, for example beans with cereal.
[bio8-s3.7.b] What we eat affects both our health and the environment, so food sources link back to the roles of each biomolecule.
[bio8-s3.7.0] Diets high in saturated fat are linked to raised blood cholesterol and heart disease.
[bio8-s3.7.1] Diets high in free sugars are linked to tooth decay, obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[bio8-s3.7.2] Producing plant protein generally needs less land and water, and causes lower greenhouse gas emissions, than producing meat. There is more on balanced diets in The Digestive System.
