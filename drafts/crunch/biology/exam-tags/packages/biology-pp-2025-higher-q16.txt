CANDIDATES bio16-s1: The cell cycle
[bio16-s1.0.b] Before you can follow the cell cycle you need to know what is being copied and shared out: the chromosomes. How many a cell has, and how they are arranged, decides whether it is diploid or haploid.
[bio16-s1.0.0] A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (the units of inheritance). There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio16-s1.0.1] Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin (DNA with its proteins). Before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio16-s1.0.2] Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent; the alleles (versions of a gene) on them may differ. Human body cells contain 23 such pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.3] Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, so that its chromosomes lie in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46. The 46 counts chromosomes, not pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.4] Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23. Haploid means one complete set, not half a chromosome.
[bio16-s1.0.5] When two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.
[bio16-s1.0.6] Haploid cells are made by meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number; mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s1.0.7] Each species has its own chromosome number: human 2n = 46, the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8, the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.
[bio16-s1.1.b] Cells don't just divide and stop: each one lives through a repeating sequence of events called the cell cycle. It shows how a cell prepares for division and then carries it out.
[bio16-s1.1.0] The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
[bio16-s1.1.1] It has three main activities, in this order. First, growth: the cell makes new proteins and organelles (the working parts of a cell) and gets bigger.
[bio16-s1.1.2] Second, DNA synthesis: every chromosome is copied, so the cell has two copies of its genetic information to hand out.
[bio16-s1.1.3] Third, division: the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells.
[bio16-s1.1.4] The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, about 90% of the cycle, so division is short.
[bio16-s1.1.5] In many human cells grown in the laboratory, one cycle takes about 24 hours.
[bio16-s1.1.6] Interphase is not a resting stage. The cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
[bio16-s1.1.7] One cycle, including division, produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s1.2.b] Not every cell divides at the same pace, and no cell divides without being told it is safe to. Whether a cell cycles depends on its job and on control checks built into the cycle.
[bio16-s1.2.0] Some cells leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, for example most mature nerve cells. Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide.
[bio16-s1.2.1] Other cells divide often throughout life, for example cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems, regions where growth happens).
[bio16-s1.2.2] The cycle is controlled. Proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on.
[bio16-s1.2.3] Signals from other cells (growth factors, chemical messages) tell a cell whether to divide. There is more on how loss of this control leads to cancer in Cancer.
[bio16-s1.2.4] Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle. Large sector: interphase (cell growth and DNA replication). Small sector: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis). Arrow for direction. Higher Level: also label G1, S, G2 and the four stages of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.3.b] Interphase is long and busy, and it has three parts: G1, S and G2. Together they prepare the cell so that division can be quick and accurate.
[bio16-s1.3.0] G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including the enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job.
[bio16-s1.3.1] S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere (the narrow point where they are held together).
[bio16-s1.3.2] G2 (second growth phase): further growth and more organelles, for example mitochondria. The proteins of the spindle (the fibres that later pull chromosomes apart) are made, and ATP is stored for division.
[bio16-s1.3.3] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), for example mature nerve cells.
[bio16-s1.3.4] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.
[bio16-s1.4.b] Mitosis is the division of the nucleus. It runs as one smooth process, but it is split into four stages to make it easier to describe, and each stage has a characteristic look and job.
[bio16-s1.4.0] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process, divided for convenience into four stages in this order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. It is followed by cytokinesis, which is division of the cytoplasm and not a stage of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.4.1] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear membrane breaks down, and spindle fibres (protein tubes called microtubules) begin to form.
[bio16-s1.4.2] In animal cells, two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them. Plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
[bio16-s1.4.3] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
[bio16-s1.4.4] Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first, which gives a V shape.
[bio16-s1.4.5] Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome, so each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio16-s1.4.6] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
[bio16-s1.4.7] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell, 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, shaded to show the two parents). Labels: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow (end of telophase).
[bio16-s1.5.b] You can tell which stage a cell is in just by looking at how its chromosomes are arranged under the microscope. Counting dividing cells then tells you how fast a tissue is dividing.
[bio16-s1.5.0] In stained cells from a growing root tip (for example onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase, seen as a nucleus with no visible chromosomes, because interphase is the longest part of the cycle.
[bio16-s1.5.1] Dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes: condensed and scattered for prophase; in a line across the middle for metaphase.
[bio16-s1.5.2] Two groups moving apart is anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, with new nuclei forming, is telophase.
[bio16-s1.5.3] The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing. For example, 24 dividing cells out of 200 gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%.
[bio16-s1.5.4] A high mitotic index shows a tissue that is dividing rapidly, such as a root tip or a tumour.
[bio16-s1.6.b] After the nucleus has divided, the cell still has to split in two. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and plant and animal cells do it differently because plant cells have a rigid wall.
[bio16-s1.6.0] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.1] In animal cells the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
[bio16-s1.6.2] In plant cells the rigid cell wall can't pinch in. Instead, vesicles (small membrane sacs) from the Golgi apparatus collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate.
[bio16-s1.6.3] The cell plate grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.4] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio22-s1: Pathogens
[bio22-s1.0.b] Some organisms and particles make us ill, and the biology of disease starts with naming them. A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host, the organism it lives in or on.
[bio22-s1.0.0] An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, which is why such diseases can spread through a population.
[bio22-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful, so bacteria and fungi should not be lumped together as 'germs'. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s1.0.2] Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: they invade and destroy host cells or tissues, they release toxins, or they take nutrients from the host.
[bio22-s1.0.3] Invasion means the pathogen destroys cells: viruses burst the cells they reproduce in, and the malaria parasite bursts red blood cells.
[bio22-s1.0.4] Toxins are poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work; the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms.
[bio22-s1.0.5] In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage, for example inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia.
[bio22-s1.1.b] A pathogen only survives if it can keep moving from host to host. So each one is adapted to its host and, often, to a carrier that helps it spread.
[bio22-s1.1.0] Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host.
[bio22-s1.1.1] Many pathogens need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease: the mosquito for malaria and aphids (small sap-sucking insects) for many plant viruses.
[bio22-s1.1.2] The routes by which pathogens pass between hosts are called routes of transmission. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.2.b] A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is neither a cell nor a virus. It is the odd one out among pathogens, because a protein alone causes the disease.
[bio22-s1.2.0] The name comes from proteinaceous infectious particle, a term coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
[bio22-s1.2.1] A prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein, the prion protein, which is found on nerve cells, especially in the brain.
[bio22-s1.2.2] When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction.
[bio22-s1.2.3] Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so that the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope. This is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathy.
[bio22-s1.2.4] Prion diseases take years to appear, are always fatal and have no cure.
[bio22-s1.3.b] Prion diseases have been found in cattle, sheep and humans. Because prions are very hard to destroy, prevention depends on keeping infected material out of the food chain.
[bio22-s1.3.0] Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, mad cow disease) in cattle spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals.
[bio22-s1.3.1] Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans is linked to eating BSE-infected beef and was first described in the UK in 1996.
[bio22-s1.3.2] Scrapie affects sheep. Kuru affected the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
[bio22-s1.3.3] Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants.
[bio22-s1.3.4] The immune system does not attack prions, because they are the body's own protein in a different shape, so the body has no defence against them.
[bio22-s1.3.5] Prevention therefore keeps prions out of the food chain: meat-and-bone meal is banned from the feed of farm animals, and the brain and spinal cord of cattle are removed from food as specified risk material.
[bio22-s1.4.b] A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat. It is an obligate intracellular parasite, meaning it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s1.4.0] Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work.
[bio22-s1.4.1] Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins (molecules on the cell surface that it fits onto), so it infects only certain hosts and tissues: HIV infects helper T cells and influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways.
[bio22-s1.4.2] Human viral diseases include influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) and AIDS (HIV).
[bio22-s1.4.3] Animal viral diseases include foot-and-mouth disease, which affects cattle, sheep and pigs, and avian influenza, which affects birds.
[bio22-s1.4.4] Plant viral disease: tobacco mosaic virus infects tobacco and tomatoes.
[bio22-s1.4.5] Antibiotics don't affect viruses, because viruses have no cell structures for the drugs to target and reproduce inside host cells.
[bio22-s1.5.b] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes with no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, about 1–10 µm long. Only some bacteria are pathogens, but those that are can multiply very quickly inside the body.
[bio22-s1.5.0] Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission (one cell splitting into two) in host tissues, so numbers rise fast.
[bio22-s1.5.1] Most bacteria cause harm by releasing toxins; some invade and destroy tissue.
[bio22-s1.5.2] Most bacterial diseases can be treated with antibiotics, which target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s1.5.3] Antibiotic-resistant strains (bacteria no longer killed by the drug) are increasing, for example MRSA.
[bio22-s1.6.b] Each bacterial disease has a named bacterium and a usual route of spread. Learn them as pairs, and note the plant and cattle examples as well as the human ones.
[bio22-s1.6.0] Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spreads in airborne droplets and mainly affects the lungs.
[bio22-s1.6.1] Food poisoning is caused by Salmonella, which spreads through contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs.
[bio22-s1.6.2] Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae in water contaminated with faeces, and causes severe diarrhoea.
[bio22-s1.6.3] Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani. Its spores in soil enter a wound, and its toxin causes muscle spasms, known as lockjaw.
[bio22-s1.6.4] Lyme disease is caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and is passed on by tick bites, so the tick is the vector.
[bio22-s1.6.5] In plants, fire blight of apple and pear trees is caused by Erwinia amylovora.
[bio22-s1.6.6] In cattle, bovine TB is caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
[bio22-s1.7.b] Fungi are eukaryotes (their cells have a nucleus) with cell walls of chitin. Most are made of thread-like hyphae, and some, such as yeasts, are single cells. Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts.
[bio22-s1.7.0] Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, and the fungus absorbs the products.
[bio22-s1.7.1] Fungi spread by spores, which are often carried by wind, water or contact.
[bio22-s1.7.2] Athlete's foot and ringworm are skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (the protein in skin); ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
[bio22-s1.7.3] Thrush is caused by the yeast Candida albicans in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check.
[bio22-s1.7.4] Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
[bio22-s1.7.5] Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.
[bio22-s1.8.b] Fungi infect plants and animals as well as people. The examples below affect trees, cereal crops and amphibians.
[bio22-s1.8.0] Ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, is killing ash trees across Europe.
[bio22-s1.8.1] Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, is spread by elm bark beetles, which act as vectors.
[bio22-s1.8.2] Cereal rusts such as wheat rust are caused by Puccinia species.
[bio22-s1.8.3] Chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.9.b] Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are neither animals, plants nor fungi (kingdom Protista). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host. Malaria is one example.
[bio22-s1.9.0] Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, which is passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector.
[bio22-s1.9.1] The parasite reproduces first in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia (too few red blood cells).
[bio22-s1.9.2] Malaria is controlled with insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining of standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs such as artemisinin. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.10.b] Other protists cause gut and blood diseases in people, and one protist-like organism caused a famine in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.10.0] Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, which spreads in water or food contaminated with faeces.
[bio22-s1.10.1] African sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei, which is spread by tsetse flies, the vector.
[bio22-s1.10.2] Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus, being thread-like and spread by spores, but it is classed with the protists.
[bio22-s1.10.3] Its spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather, and it rots leaves and tubers.
[bio22-s1.10.4] Potato late blight caused the Irish Great Famine from 1845, because potatoes were the main crop and the disease destroyed them. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.11.b] A parasite lives in or on another organism, its host, feeding on it and harming it. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods. Endoparasites live inside the host.
[bio22-s1.11.0] Tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers.
[bio22-s1.11.1] Humans become infected with tapeworms by eating undercooked infected pork or beef.
[bio22-s1.11.2] Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) is a flatworm of sheep and cattle that damages the liver.
[bio22-s1.11.3] The liver fluke needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.11.4] Onchocerca volvulus is a roundworm spread by blackflies that causes river blindness. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.12.b] Ectoparasites live on the outside of the host. Worms inside the body are treated with drugs, and prevention targets how the parasite reaches us.
[bio22-s1.12.0] Head lice are ectoparasites that feed on blood from the scalp.
[bio22-s1.12.1] Ticks feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio22-s1.12.2] Parasitic worms are treated with anthelmintic drugs that kill them, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s1.12.3] Prevention: cook meat thoroughly, maintain sanitation, and control the second host or vector, because breaking the life cycle stops the parasite spreading.
[bio22-s1.13.b] The six types of pathogen differ in whether they are cells, what genetic material they carry, their size and how they are treated. The treatment follows from the structure, which is why antibiotics work only on bacteria.
[bio22-s1.13.0] Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment.
[bio22-s1.13.1] Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; treated with antivirals, not antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.2] Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; treated with antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.3] Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; from single cells to large networks of hyphae; treated with antifungals.
[bio22-s1.13.4] Protist: eukaryotic and mostly single-celled; DNA; treated with antiprotozoal drugs such as antimalarials.
[bio22-s1.13.5] Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; from millimetres to metres in size; treated with anthelmintics.

CANDIDATES bio22-s2: Innate and adaptive immunity
[bio22-s2.0.b] Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection by pathogens (disease-causing organisms such as bacteria and viruses). The immune system does this by telling self from non-self, and it has two parts that work at different speeds.
[bio22-s2.0.0] The immune system consists of physical and chemical barriers, white blood cells (made from stem cells in the bone marrow), the lymphatic system (lymph nodes, spleen and thymus) and defence proteins in the blood, such as antibodies and complement.
[bio22-s2.0.1] The body's own cells carry their own marker molecules on their surface, which the immune system normally ignores, so foreign cells and molecules, which lack these markers, are attacked.
[bio22-s2.0.2] Innate immunity, also called non-specific immunity, is present from birth, acts against any pathogen in the same way and acts within minutes to hours.
[bio22-s2.0.3] Adaptive immunity, also called acquired or specific immunity, develops during life after exposure to a particular pathogen and acts against that pathogen only.
[bio22-s2.0.4] Adaptive immunity takes days to develop the first time, but it remembers the pathogen, which is why a later infection is dealt with much faster.
[bio22-s2.0.5] Innate defences are found in all animals and in plants; adaptive immunity, with antibodies and lymphocytes (the white blood cells that run it), is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.1.b] The best way to deal with a pathogen is to stop it getting in. Skin and the linings of the body's openings form physical barriers that block or trap microorganisms before they can cause infection.
[bio22-s2.1.0] Skin is a tough outer layer of dead, keratin-filled cells (keratin is a tough protein) that pathogens can't easily cross.
[bio22-s2.1.1] Oil (sebum) from skin glands makes the surface slightly acidic, which slows bacterial growth.
[bio22-s2.1.2] Breaks in the skin are sealed by blood clotting, which stops blood loss and blocks pathogen entry.
[bio22-s2.1.3] Mucous membranes line the breathing, digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts. Their sticky mucus traps microorganisms and dust, so these don't reach the cells beneath.
[bio22-s2.1.4] In the trachea and bronchi (the main airways to the lungs), cilia (tiny hair-like projections) beat the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.
[bio22-s2.1.5] Coughing and sneezing are reflexes (automatic responses) that expel irritants, so trapped microorganisms are blown out of the airways.
[bio22-s2.2.b] Some barriers work by chemistry rather than by physically blocking entry. Acids and enzymes kill or inhibit microorganisms, and harmless resident microorganisms add a living defence of their own.
[bio22-s2.2.0] Hydrochloric acid in the stomach, at about pH 2, kills most microorganisms swallowed with food.
[bio22-s2.2.1] Lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus, breaks down bacterial cell walls, so the bacteria burst or die.
[bio22-s2.2.2] The acidic conditions in the vagina inhibit many pathogens.
[bio22-s2.2.3] Harmless microorganisms living on the skin and in the gut and vagina, the normal microbiota, compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, which leaves pathogens less room to establish an infection. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s2.3.b] If a pathogen gets past the barriers, the innate response moves on to cells that destroy it. Phagocytes find the invader, swallow it and digest it in a process called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.0] Phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens, and the process is called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.1] Neutrophils are the most numerous white blood cells and the first to arrive at an infection. Macrophages, formed from monocytes (another type of white blood cell), are larger and longer-lived.
[bio22-s2.3.2] 1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals released by pathogens and damaged cells.
[bio22-s2.3.3] 2. It binds the pathogen.
[bio22-s2.3.4] 3. It surrounds the pathogen with extensions of its membrane and takes it into a vesicle (a small membrane-bound sac inside the cell).
[bio22-s2.3.5] 4. Digestive enzymes are released into the vesicle and break the pathogen down.
[bio22-s2.3.6] 5. The harmless products are absorbed or released.
[bio22-s2.3.7] Diagram: the stages of phagocytosis in four sketches: a phagocyte approaching a bacterium; membrane extensions surrounding it; the bacterium enclosed in a vesicle; the bacterium being digested. Label phagocyte, nucleus, bacterium, vesicle and digestive enzymes.
[bio22-s2.4.b] Inflammation and fever are whole-body or whole-area responses that bring more defences to an infection and make conditions harder for the pathogen. The signs you notice when you are ill are the immune system at work.
[bio22-s2.4.0] Inflammation: damaged cells and mast cells (cells in tissues that release signals when damaged) release histamine and other chemicals, which widen local blood vessels and make capillaries leakier.
[bio22-s2.4.1] More blood flow brings phagocytes and defence proteins to the site, and fluid leaks into the tissue, giving redness, heat, swelling and pain at the site.
[bio22-s2.4.2] Pus is a mixture of dead phagocytes, pathogens and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.4.3] Fever: chemicals released by white blood cells reset the temperature set point in the hypothalamus (the brain region that controls body temperature) upwards, raising body temperature. There is more on this in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio22-s2.4.4] A higher temperature slows the reproduction of many pathogens and speeds up the reactions of the immune system, so fever helps the body fight infection.
[bio22-s2.5.b] Besides phagocytes, the innate response uses proteins in the blood and released by infected cells. Plants have no adaptive immunity, so their innate defences have to do all the work.
[bio22-s2.5.0] Complement, a group of blood proteins, coats pathogens so that phagocytes engulf them more easily, and it can punch holes in bacterial membranes, bursting them.
[bio22-s2.5.1] Interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that make neighbouring cells resist virus replication, so the infection is slowed before it spreads.
[bio22-s2.5.2] Natural killer cells also act in the innate response, in addition to phagocytes and defence proteins.
[bio22-s2.5.3] Plants have innate defences only: physical barriers (cuticle, bark and cell walls), protective chemicals, and the death of infected cells to wall off a pathogen. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio22-s2.6.b] Adaptive immunity depends on recognising a particular pathogen. The molecules it recognises are called antigens, and the immune system's ability to tell them apart from the body's own molecules is what makes the response specific.
[bio22-s2.6.0] An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen or on a toxin it releases, that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s2.6.1] Antigens trigger a specific immune response, including antibody production.
[bio22-s2.6.2] Each pathogen carries several antigens, so more than one kind of antibody can be made against it. The body's own cell markers are self antigens, which are normally tolerated, so the body doesn't attack itself.
[bio22-s2.7.b] An antibody is the body's protein that matches one antigen. Its Y shape and the shape of its tips explain why each antibody works against only one antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.0] An antibody, also called an immunoglobulin, is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells, which are derived from B lymphocytes. It binds to one specific antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.1] An antibody has four polypeptide chains (chains of amino acids), two long heavy chains and two short light chains, held together by disulfide bonds.
[bio22-s2.7.2] The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ between antibodies and form two identical antigen-binding sites. The rest of the molecule is the constant region, which is the same in all antibodies of a class.
[bio22-s2.7.3] Specificity: the shape of the antigen-binding site is complementary to the shape of one antigen, so each antibody binds only that antigen, like a key in a lock. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio22-s2.7.4] Diagram: an antibody as a Y shape, labelled heavy chain, light chain, variable region, antigen-binding site (two), constant region and disulfide bonds, with an antigen fitting one binding site.
[bio22-s2.8.b] Once antibodies have bound to an antigen, they can stop the pathogen in four different ways. The Y shape matters again here, because the two arms bind the antigen while the stem is recognised by other defences.
[bio22-s2.8.0] Agglutination: because each antibody has two binding sites, antibodies link pathogens together into clumps that can't spread and are easily engulfed.
[bio22-s2.8.1] Neutralisation: antibodies cover the parts of viruses that attach to cells, or bind to toxins (antitoxins), so that they can't act.
[bio22-s2.8.2] Opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens, marking them for phagocytes, which have receptors for the antibody's constant region.
[bio22-s2.8.3] Complement activation: antibodies activate complement (the blood proteins of the innate response), which bursts bacterial cells.
[bio22-s2.9.b] The body can't know in advance which pathogen it will meet, so it keeps a huge variety of B cells ready. Only the ones that fit the invader are chosen to multiply, a process called clonal selection.
[bio22-s2.9.0] Lymphocytes are the white blood cells of adaptive immunity, and there are two main kinds.
[bio22-s2.9.1] B lymphocytes (B cells) produce antibodies. This is the antibody-mediated or humoral response, acting on pathogens and toxins in blood and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.9.2] T lymphocytes (T cells) act directly on infected body cells in the cell-mediated response (Higher Level).
[bio22-s2.9.3] Clonal selection: the body has millions of different B cells, each with one type of antibody fixed in its membrane as a receptor, made before any infection.
[bio22-s2.9.4] When a pathogen enters, only the B cells whose receptors fit its antigen bind to it. These are selected and, with chemical signals from helper T cells, divide rapidly by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells.
[bio22-s2.9.5] The clone differentiates (becomes specialised) into plasma cells and memory B cells.
[bio22-s2.9.6] Plasma cells secrete large amounts of the matching antibody into the blood, thousands of molecules per second, for a few days, and then die.
[bio22-s2.9.7] Memory cells stay in the body for years, ready to respond to the same antigen.
[bio22-s2.10.b] The first time you meet a pathogen, the response is slow. A second meeting is much faster, because memory cells are already there. This difference is the reason you rarely get the same infection twice.
[bio22-s2.10.0] Primary response, on first exposure: there is a delay of several days, often about a week, while matching B cells are selected and cloned. Antibody levels rise slowly and stay fairly low.
[bio22-s2.10.1] During the primary response the person may become ill, because the pathogen multiplies before enough antibodies are made.
[bio22-s2.10.2] Secondary response, on later exposure to the same antigen: memory cells recognise it at once and quickly produce many plasma cells, so antibodies appear within a few days, reach a much higher level and last longer.
[bio22-s2.10.3] The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear, so the person is immune. This is the basis of vaccination. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s2.10.4] Diagram: a graph of antibody concentration (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis), with a first exposure giving a small, slow primary peak and a second exposure giving a faster, much taller, longer secondary peak. Label both exposures, the lag phase, and the primary and secondary responses.
[bio22-s2.11.b] Antibodies work in blood and tissue fluid, but some pathogens hide inside body cells. The cell-mediated response uses T cells to find and deal with those infected cells, and it begins with the cell showing that it is infected.
[bio22-s2.11.0] The cell-mediated response is the part of adaptive immunity carried out by T lymphocytes acting directly on cells, not by antibodies.
[bio22-s2.11.1] It is needed because antibodies can't reach pathogens inside body cells, such as viruses and some bacteria, for example the TB bacterium, which lives inside macrophages.
[bio22-s2.11.2] It also destroys cancer cells and cells of transplanted organs, which is why transplant patients take drugs that suppress the immune system.
[bio22-s2.11.3] Antigen presentation: an infected body cell displays fragments of the pathogen's antigens on its surface, held in its self-marker proteins (MHC proteins).
[bio22-s2.11.4] Macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells that have engulfed a pathogen display its antigens in the same way.
[bio22-s2.11.5] T cells can't recognise free antigens, so this presentation is how they detect infection.
[bio22-s2.12.b] Helper T cells act as the managers of adaptive immunity. Once one is activated, it signals to the other cells that need to act, so it links the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.12.0] Activation of helper T cells: a helper T cell whose receptor fits the displayed antigen binds to the antigen-presenting cell and is activated. It divides to form a clone of helper T cells, as B cells do.
[bio22-s2.12.1] Helper T cells coordinate the response by releasing chemical signals called cytokines.
[bio22-s2.12.2] Cytokines stimulate the matching killer T cells to divide.
[bio22-s2.12.3] Cytokines also stimulate the matching B cells to divide into plasma cells and memory cells, because B cells need this help to respond fully.
[bio22-s2.12.4] Cytokines also attract and activate macrophages, which increases phagocytosis at the infection.
[bio22-s2.12.5] Helper T cells therefore link the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.13.b] After helper T cells give the signal, the killer T cells destroy infected cells, the B cells add antibodies, and suppressor T cells end the response. Memory cells are left behind for next time.
[bio22-s2.13.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) of the clone bind to body cells displaying the antigen and kill them, destroying the pathogens inside before they can multiply. There is more on this in White blood cells.
[bio22-s2.13.1] When the infection is cleared, suppressor T cells switch off the response by stopping the activity of killer T cells and B cells, so that healthy tissue isn't damaged.
[bio22-s2.13.2] Memory T cells remain, so a second infection triggers a faster, larger cell-mediated response.
[bio22-s2.13.3] Role of B lymphocytes: B cells act as antigen-presenting cells, taking in the antigen they bind and displaying it to helper T cells.
[bio22-s2.13.4] The antibodies released by the plasma cells of B cells mark infected cells and pathogens for destruction.
[bio22-s2.13.5] Diagram: draw a flow diagram in this order: pathogen; antigen presented by macrophage or infected cell; helper T cell activated and cloned; cytokines; killer T cells cloned, infected cells killed; B cells cloned into plasma cells, antibodies; memory T and B cells formed; suppressor T cells end response.
[bio22-s2.14.b] The two systems differ in speed, specificity and memory, but they are not separate. A comparison of the two is a standard exam question, so learn each as a pair.
[bio22-s2.14.0] Presence: innate immunity is present from birth, while adaptive immunity develops after exposure to a pathogen.
[bio22-s2.14.1] Specificity: innate immunity is non-specific, giving the same response to every pathogen, while adaptive immunity is specific, with each response against one antigen.
[bio22-s2.14.2] Memory: innate immunity gives the same response each time, while adaptive immunity has memory, so a second response is faster and stronger.
[bio22-s2.14.3] Speed: innate immunity acts within minutes to hours, while adaptive immunity takes days on first exposure.
[bio22-s2.14.4] Mechanisms: innate immunity uses barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages), inflammation, fever, complement, interferons and natural killer cells; adaptive immunity uses B and T lymphocytes and antibodies.
[bio22-s2.14.5] Distribution: innate defences are found in all animals and in plants, while adaptive immunity is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.14.6] The two systems work together: macrophages of the innate system present antigens to start the adaptive response, and antibodies of the adaptive system make phagocytosis and complement more effective.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio22-s5: White blood cells
[bio22-s5.0.b] The immune system is run by several different kinds of white blood cell, and they all start from the same place. Knowing where each type comes from and matures explains how the body learns to attack invaders but not itself.
[bio22-s5.0.0] White blood cells (leucocytes) are the cells of the immune system. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, which holds the genes they need to make the proteins for their defensive jobs.
[bio22-s5.0.1] All white blood cells develop from stem cells (cells that can divide and become different cell types) in the red bone marrow, the soft tissue inside some bones.
[bio22-s5.0.2] They travel in the blood and lymph (the fluid that drains from tissues), and many leave the capillaries to enter the tissues, because that is where pathogens (disease-causing organisms) are found.
[bio22-s5.0.3] There are two broad groups. Phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages) engulf pathogens, taking them into the cell.
[bio22-s5.0.4] The second group is the lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells). These are small cells with a large round nucleus filling most of the cell and little cytoplasm.
[bio22-s5.0.5] B lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow. T lymphocytes leave the marrow and mature in the thymus, a gland in the chest behind the breastbone (T for thymus).
[bio22-s5.0.6] During maturation, lymphocytes that would attack the body's own cells are removed, so mature lymphocytes respond only to non-self antigens (molecules on a pathogen's surface that the immune system recognises as foreign).
[bio22-s5.1.b] Monocytes are the white blood cells that travel in the blood and then settle in tissues as macrophages. Macrophages are the body's general-purpose cleaners and also the link between the fast, general response and the specific one.
[bio22-s5.1.0] Monocytes are the largest white blood cells, with a large kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus, which makes them easy to tell from lymphocytes under the microscope.
[bio22-s5.1.1] They circulate in the blood for a few days, then move into tissues and develop into macrophages, which can live for months.
[bio22-s5.1.2] Macrophages carry out phagocytosis (engulfing and digesting) of pathogens, dead cells and debris. There is more on the stages in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.1.3] Macrophages are antigen-presenting cells: they display fragments of engulfed pathogens on their surface to activate helper T cells, so they link innate immunity (the fast, non-specific response) and adaptive immunity (the slower, specific one).
[bio22-s5.1.4] Macrophages release chemicals that cause inflammation and fever and attract other white blood cells to the site of infection.
[bio22-s5.1.5] Diagram: a monocyte (large cell, kidney-shaped nucleus) beside a lymphocyte (small cell, large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm). Label nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane.
[bio22-s5.2.b] Natural killer (NK) cells are a fast first line of attack against body cells that have gone wrong, either because a virus is hiding inside them or because they have become cancerous. They check cells for a missing marker rather than for a particular pathogen.
[bio22-s5.2.0] Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes of the innate system. They are not specific to one antigen and need no earlier exposure, so they act within hours of an infection, before B and T cells have responded.
[bio22-s5.2.1] Healthy body cells display normal self-marker (MHC) proteins on their surface, which tell the immune system the cell belongs to the body.
[bio22-s5.2.2] Many virus-infected cells and cancer cells display fewer of these markers, or display stress molecules instead. NK cells kill cells showing this missing self pattern.
[bio22-s5.2.3] NK cells also kill cells coated with antibodies, the proteins that bind to antigens.
[bio22-s5.2.4] NK cells release perforin, a protein that makes pores in the target cell's membrane, and enzymes called granzymes that enter through the pores and trigger the cell to self-destruct (apoptosis, programmed cell death).
[bio22-s5.2.5] The dying cell breaks into fragments that macrophages engulf, so the virus inside is destroyed without spreading.
[bio22-s5.2.6] NK cells also release chemicals that activate macrophages, which helps clear the fragments.
[bio22-s5.3.b] B lymphocytes are the cells behind antibodies. Each B cell is committed to one antibody shape, so the body needs a huge range of B cells to cover many different antigens.
[bio22-s5.3.0] B lymphocytes carry antibody molecules in their membrane as receptors, which let them bind a matching antigen. Each B cell and its descendants make only one antibody shape.
[bio22-s5.3.1] B lymphocytes are responsible for antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity, meaning protection by antibodies carried in body fluids. Clonal selection, plasma cells and memory B cells are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.3.2] Plasma cells, the antibody-secreting form of B cells, are larger than resting B cells, with extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum (a network of membranes studded with ribosomes) to make and secrete large amounts of antibody protein.
[bio22-s5.3.3] Antibodies act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, in blood, tissue fluid and mucus, not on pathogens hidden inside cells, because antibodies cannot get into body cells.
[bio22-s5.4.b] T lymphocytes are the cells that control and carry out cell-mediated immunity, the response that deals with infected body cells. Helper T cells are the coordinators that most of the adaptive response depends on.
[bio22-s5.4.0] All T cells have T-cell receptors on their surface that recognise one antigen, but only when it is displayed on a cell's surface (presented), never as a free molecule.
[bio22-s5.4.1] T cells don't make antibodies. That is the job of plasma cells, which come from B cells.
[bio22-s5.4.2] Helper T cells carry the surface marker CD4.
[bio22-s5.4.3] Helper T cells are the coordinators of the adaptive response, signalling with cytokines (chemical messengers between immune cells). Which cells they activate, and in what order, is taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.4.4] Almost every adaptive response depends on helper T cells, so HIV, which infects and destroys CD4 helper T cells, leaves the body unable to fight many infections.
[bio22-s5.5.b] Killer T cells deal with threats that antibodies cannot reach, because the pathogen is hidden inside a body cell. They destroy the whole infected cell.
[bio22-s5.5.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) carry the surface marker CD8.
[bio22-s5.5.1] Killer T cells bind to body cells displaying their matching antigen, such as virus-infected cells, cancer cells and cells of transplanted tissue.
[bio22-s5.5.2] They kill these cells by releasing perforin and granzymes, which triggers apoptosis (the cell's programmed self-destruction).
[bio22-s5.5.3] Killer T cells use the same killing method as NK cells, but only against cells showing their one specific antigen, whereas NK cells look for missing self-markers.
[bio22-s5.5.4] Each killer T cell can kill many target cells in turn.
[bio22-s5.6.b] An immune response needs brakes, and it needs a record of past infections. Suppressor T cells provide the brakes, and memory T cells provide the record that makes a second infection easier to fight.
[bio22-s5.6.0] Suppressor T cells, now usually called regulatory T cells, are the brakes of the immune system.
[bio22-s5.6.1] Suppressor T cells end a response once an infection is cleared, so the body is not damaged by an immune response that carries on. The details are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.6.2] They also suppress lymphocytes that react to the body's own antigens, helping to prevent autoimmune disease (the body attacking its own tissues) and allergy.
[bio22-s5.6.3] Memory T cells form from the clone of activated T cells (the identical cells that divide from one activated T cell) and remain in the blood and lymph tissue for years or decades.
[bio22-s5.6.4] On re-exposure to the same antigen, memory T cells quickly produce new helper and killer T cells, giving a faster, stronger cell-mediated response. Memory B cells do the same for antibodies.
[bio22-s5.6.5] Memory cells are why vaccination works: the vaccine exposes you to the antigen safely, so memory cells are ready if the real pathogen arrives.
[bio22-s5.7.b] The outcome asks you to compare the roles of different white blood cells. The first comparison is between the fast, general innate cells and the slower, specific adaptive cells, and then between the ways each one destroys pathogens.
[bio22-s5.7.0] Innate versus adaptive: monocytes, macrophages and NK cells are part of innate immunity, which is non-specific, acts quickly and has no memory.
[bio22-s5.7.1] B and T lymphocytes are adaptive: they are specific to one antigen, slower on first exposure, and they form memory cells.
[bio22-s5.7.2] Macrophages engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis. NK and killer T cells kill infected body cells by perforin and apoptosis.
[bio22-s5.7.3] B cells, as plasma cells, secrete antibodies. Helper T cells kill nothing themselves but signal to other cells.
[bio22-s5.7.4] Suppressor T cells regulate the response. Memory cells kill nothing but enable a rapid future response.
[bio22-s5.8.b] Cells also differ in what they attack and in how they recognise it. This explains why different cells are needed to cover both free pathogens and pathogens hidden inside body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.0] Where the target is: antibodies from B cells act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, because antibodies cannot enter body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.1] Killer T and NK cells act on pathogens inside body cells by killing the cell. Macrophages engulf free pathogens.
[bio22-s5.8.2] Recognition: B cells recognise free antigens with membrane antibodies, while T cells recognise only antigens presented on cell surfaces.
[bio22-s5.8.3] NK cells recognise missing or abnormal self-markers, not a specific antigen.
[bio22-s5.8.4] Macrophages recognise general features of pathogens and antibody-coated particles, so they need no specific receptor for each pathogen.
[bio22-s5.9.b] This list puts each cell side by side: whether it is innate or adaptive, whether it is specific, its main role, and where it matures. Use it to compare cells quickly.
[bio22-s5.9.0] Monocyte or macrophage: innate; non-specific; main role is phagocytosis and antigen presentation; matures in the bone marrow, then in tissues.
[bio22-s5.9.1] NK cell: innate; non-specific; kills infected and cancer cells; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.2] B lymphocyte: adaptive; specific; main role is antibodies (via plasma cells) and memory; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.3] Helper T cell: adaptive; specific; activates B cells, killer T cells and macrophages (CD4); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.4] Killer T cell: adaptive; specific; kills infected, cancer and transplant cells (CD8); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.5] Suppressor T cell: adaptive; ends responses and prevents attack on self; matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.6] Memory B and T cells: adaptive; specific; long-lived, giving a fast secondary response.
[bio22-s5.10.b] In a real infection the cells act as a team in sequence. Influenza, a virus that infects the cells lining the airways, shows how the innate and adaptive cells combine.
[bio22-s5.10.0] First, infected cells release interferons (chemical signals that warn neighbouring cells of a virus) and NK cells kill some of them within hours. Macrophages engulf viruses and present antigens, and helper T cells are activated.
[bio22-s5.10.1] Next, killer T cells destroy infected cells while plasma cells release antibodies that neutralise free viruses, so both hidden and free viruses are dealt with.
[bio22-s5.10.2] Finally, suppressor T cells end the response and memory cells remain. Because flu antigens change, memory cells may not recognise next year's strain.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio28-s1: Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism
[bio28-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a steady supply of oxygen and must get rid of the carbon dioxide it makes. The breathing system is the body's way of swapping these gases with the air.
[bio28-s1.0.0] Every cell respires aerobically (releases energy from food using oxygen), so every cell uses oxygen and produces carbon dioxide. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio28-s1.0.1] The breathing system exchanges gases between the external environment (the air) and the internal environment (the blood and tissue fluid, the fluid that bathes the cells): oxygen is taken into the blood, and carbon dioxide and some water vapour are removed.
[bio28-s1.0.2] Breathing, or ventilation, is the movement of air into and out of the lungs. It is not respiration, which is the chemical release of energy inside cells, and gas exchange is not respiration either.
[bio28-s1.0.3] A large animal such as a human cannot get enough oxygen by diffusion through its skin, because its surface area is too small for its volume and the distances to its inner cells are too great.
[bio28-s1.0.4] So it needs a specialised exchange surface, the alveoli (tiny air sacs in the lungs), and a transport system, the blood, to carry gases to and from every cell.
[bio28-s1.1.b] The breathing tract is the pathway air follows from outside to the lungs. The first parts clean, warm and moisten the air and keep food out of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.0] Nose (nasal cavity): hairs and sticky mucus trap dust and microorganisms. The lining has a rich blood supply that warms the air, and the mucus moistens it, so the delicate alveoli do not dry out or chill.
[bio28-s1.1.1] Breathing through the mouth skips this filtering, warming and moistening, which is why nose breathing is better for the lungs.
[bio28-s1.1.2] Pharynx (throat): the passage behind the nose and mouth that is shared by air and food.
[bio28-s1.1.3] Epiglottis: a flap of cartilage (firm, flexible tissue) that closes over the entrance to the larynx and trachea during swallowing, so food and drink pass into the oesophagus (the food tube) instead of the airway.
[bio28-s1.1.4] Choking happens when food enters the trachea instead.
[bio28-s1.1.5] Larynx (voice box): at the top of the trachea. It contains the vocal cords, which vibrate as air passes over them, producing sound.
[bio28-s1.2.b] Below the larynx the airway divides again and again, like an upside-down tree, until it reaches the alveoli where gases actually cross into the blood.
[bio28-s1.2.0] Trachea (windpipe): a tube held open by C-shaped rings of cartilage, so it does not collapse when the pressure inside falls during inhalation.
[bio28-s1.2.1] The rings are incomplete at the back, where the trachea lies against the oesophagus, so the oesophagus can expand as food is swallowed.
[bio28-s1.2.2] Lining of the trachea and bronchi: ciliated epithelium (a surface layer of cells carrying tiny hair-like cilia) with mucus-secreting goblet cells. Mucus traps dust and microorganisms.
[bio28-s1.2.3] The cilia beat in waves that sweep the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out. This is part of the body's first line of defence against infection.
[bio28-s1.2.4] Bronchi: the trachea divides into two bronchi (singular bronchus), one to each lung. They are also supported by cartilage.
[bio28-s1.2.5] Bronchioles: each bronchus branches repeatedly into thousands of narrower bronchioles. They have no cartilage; their walls contain smooth muscle, which can narrow or widen them and so change air flow.
[bio28-s1.2.6] The finest bronchioles end in clusters of alveoli (air sacs): tiny thin-walled sacs, each surrounded by a network of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels). The alveoli are the site of gas exchange.
[bio28-s1.3.b] The lungs have no muscle of their own, so they depend on the chest wall and on the muscles around it. The pleural membranes are what link the lungs to the chest wall.
[bio28-s1.3.0] The two lungs are spongy, elastic organs in the thorax (chest cavity), protected by the rib cage (ribs, sternum and vertebrae) and separated from the abdomen by the diaphragm.
[bio28-s1.3.1] Each lung is enclosed by two pleural membranes with a thin layer of pleural fluid between them. The fluid reduces friction as the lungs move.
[bio28-s1.3.2] The fluid also makes the lungs cling to the chest wall, so they expand when the thorax expands.
[bio28-s1.3.3] Diaphragm: a dome-shaped sheet of muscle and tendon forming the floor of the thorax.
[bio28-s1.3.4] Intercostal muscles: two sets, external and internal, lying between the ribs, which move the rib cage.
[bio28-s1.3.5] Diagram: the breathing system in a front view of the chest. Label the nasal cavity, pharynx, epiglottis, larynx, trachea with cartilage rings, two bronchi, bronchioles, lungs, pleural membranes, ribs, intercostal muscles, diaphragm and an outline of the heart. Add an enlarged inset of alveoli wrapped in capillaries.
[bio28-s1.4.b] Breathing in works by changing pressure. The muscles enlarge the thorax, the pressure in the lungs drops below the pressure outside, and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.0] Air always moves from a region of higher pressure to one of lower pressure. When the thorax volume increases, the pressure inside the lungs falls below atmospheric pressure (the pressure of the air outside) and air flows in.
[bio28-s1.4.1] When the thorax volume decreases, the pressure rises above atmospheric pressure and air flows out. The lungs follow the chest wall because they have no muscle of their own.
[bio28-s1.4.2] Inhalation (inspiration) is an active process, which means it uses energy. 1. The brain sends nerve impulses to the breathing muscles.
[bio28-s1.4.3] 2. The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving downwards.
[bio28-s1.4.4] 3. The external intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs and sternum upwards and outwards.
[bio28-s1.4.5] 4. The volume of the thorax increases.
[bio28-s1.4.6] 5. The lungs expand with it, so the pressure inside them falls below atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.4.7] 6. Air rushes in through the nose and trachea until the pressures are equal. Muscle contraction uses ATP (the cell's energy currency), so inhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.5.b] Breathing out at rest needs no muscle contraction at all: the body simply springs back. During exercise, however, extra muscles make exhalation active.
[bio28-s1.5.0] Exhalation (expiration) at rest is a passive process, which means it uses no energy. 1. The impulses stop and the diaphragm and external intercostal muscles relax.
[bio28-s1.5.1] 2. The diaphragm returns to its dome shape, pushed up by the abdominal organs, and the ribs move down and in under their own weight.
[bio28-s1.5.2] 3. The stretched, elastic lung tissue recoils, like a stretched elastic band springing back.
[bio28-s1.5.3] 4. The volume of the thorax decreases and the pressure in the lungs rises above atmospheric pressure.
[bio28-s1.5.4] 5. Air is forced out. No muscle contracts, so no energy is used.
[bio28-s1.5.5] Forced exhalation, as in exercise, coughing, blowing or speaking loudly: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in, and the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the diaphragm up.
[bio28-s1.5.6] This expels air faster and more completely, and because muscles contract, forced exhalation is active.
[bio28-s1.6.b] Putting the two halves of the breathing cycle side by side shows that every change in one is reversed in the other.
[bio28-s1.6.0] Diaphragm: it contracts and flattens in inhalation, but relaxes and domes upwards in exhalation at rest.
[bio28-s1.6.1] External intercostal muscles: they contract in inhalation but relax in exhalation. The ribs move up and out in inhalation, and down and in in exhalation.
[bio28-s1.6.2] Thorax volume increases in inhalation and decreases in exhalation. Lung pressure falls below atmospheric pressure in inhalation and rises above it in exhalation, so air moves in, then out.
[bio28-s1.6.3] Inhalation is active, whereas exhalation at rest is passive.
[bio28-s1.6.4] The bell-jar model shows the volume and pressure principle: pulling the rubber sheet (the diaphragm) down inflates the balloons (the lungs). There is more on this in Models.
[bio28-s1.6.5] Diagram: two side-view sketches of the thorax, one at inhalation and one at exhalation, showing the diaphragm (flat or domed) and the ribs (raised or lowered), with arrows for rib movement and air flow. Label the diaphragm, ribs, intercostal muscles, lungs and trachea.
[bio28-s1.7.b] Breathing continues while you sleep because the brain controls it automatically. The signal that adjusts it is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood.
[bio28-s1.7.0] Breathing is controlled automatically by the breathing (respiratory) centre in the medulla oblongata, at the base of the brain. It sends rhythmic nerve impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles, so breathing continues without thought, even during sleep.
[bio28-s1.7.1] We can change breathing voluntarily for a short time, using the cerebrum (the thinking part of the brain), for example by holding our breath, speaking or singing. The automatic control takes over again afterwards.
[bio28-s1.7.2] The main factor that sets the rate and depth of breathing is the level of carbon dioxide in the blood. When CO₂ rises, as during exercise when muscles respire faster, the breathing centre increases the rate and depth of breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.3] This removes more CO₂ and takes in more oxygen. When CO₂ falls, breathing slows. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio28-s1.7.4] An adult at rest breathes about 12 to 20 times per minute, moving about 500 cm³ (half a litre) of air in each breath, the tidal volume.
[bio28-s1.7.5] During hard exercise both the rate and the depth (the volume of air per breath) increase greatly.
[bio28-s1.8.b] Gas exchange is a simple physical process: oxygen and carbon dioxide each move down their own concentration gradient between the air in the alveolus and the blood in the capillary beside it.
[bio28-s1.8.0] Gases cross between the air in an alveolus and the blood in its capillaries by diffusion, from where they are more concentrated to where they are less concentrated. No energy is used, so it is not active transport.
[bio28-s1.8.1] Oxygen: the air in the alveoli has a higher oxygen concentration than the deoxygenated blood arriving from the body in the pulmonary artery.
[bio28-s1.8.2] Oxygen dissolves in the moisture lining the alveolus, diffuses through the alveolus wall and the capillary wall into the plasma (the liquid part of the blood), and enters the red blood cells, where it combines with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio28-s1.8.3] Carbon dioxide: the blood arriving at the lungs has a higher CO₂ concentration than the air in the alveoli. CO₂, carried mostly in the plasma, diffuses out of the blood into the alveolus and is breathed out.
[bio28-s1.8.4] The oxygenated blood leaves the lungs in the pulmonary veins and returns to the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio28-s1.8.5] The concentration gradients are kept steep in two ways: breathing constantly brings in fresh air with a high oxygen and low CO₂ content and removes stale air.
[bio28-s1.8.6] Blood flow also keeps the gradients steep, because it constantly carries oxygenated blood away and brings new deoxygenated blood.
[bio28-s1.9.b] The alveoli are built so that diffusion is as fast as possible. Each feature either gives gases more room to cross, a shorter distance to travel, or a steeper gradient.
[bio28-s1.9.0] Very large total surface area: about 300 million alveoli in the two lungs give an exchange surface of roughly 70 m², so a large amount of gas can diffuse at once.
[bio28-s1.9.1] Very thin walls: the alveolus wall and the capillary wall are each one cell thick and pressed close together, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio28-s1.9.2] Moist lining: gases dissolve in the moisture before they diffuse across the wall.
[bio28-s1.9.3] Dense capillary network: every alveolus is surrounded by capillaries with a continuous blood flow, which keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio28-s1.9.4] Narrow capillaries: they are just wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, pressed against the wall. This shortens the distance further and slows the blood, giving time for exchange.
[bio28-s1.9.5] Elastic fibres around the alveoli let them stretch on inhalation and recoil on exhalation, which helps push air out.
[bio28-s1.9.6] Diagram: a single alveolus with a capillary wrapped around it. Label alveolus wall (one cell thick), capillary wall (one cell thick), moist lining, red blood cell and plasma. Add arrows: O₂ into the blood, CO₂ into the alveolus, and blood flow from pulmonary artery (deoxygenated) to pulmonary vein (oxygenated).
[bio28-s1.10.b] The air we breathe out differs from the air we breathe in, because the body takes oxygen from it and adds carbon dioxide and water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.0] Inhaled air contains about 21% oxygen, about 0.04% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen, with a variable amount of water vapour.
[bio28-s1.10.1] Exhaled air contains about 16% oxygen, about 4% carbon dioxide and 78% nitrogen. It is saturated with water vapour and is warmer than inhaled air.
[bio28-s1.10.2] Nitrogen is not used by the body, so its proportion is the same in both.
[bio28-s1.10.3] Exhaled air still contains much oxygen, which is why rescue breaths work in CPR (the emergency procedure that keeps a casualty's blood supplied with oxygen).
[bio28-s1.10.4] Lime water turns milky faster with exhaled air, showing that exhaled air contains more CO₂.
[bio28-s1.11.b] Damage to the breathing system shows why its structures matter: when cilia, alveoli or bronchioles stop working properly, gas exchange suffers.
[bio28-s1.11.0] Smoking: tar and other chemicals in cigarette smoke paralyse and destroy the cilia and increase mucus production, so mucus and trapped microorganisms build up in the airways, causing coughing (smoker's cough) and frequent chest infections.
[bio28-s1.11.1] Emphysema, mainly caused by smoking: if alveolus walls break down and merge into larger air spaces, the surface area for gas exchange falls and the lungs lose their elasticity, so the person becomes breathless. This shows why the large surface area of the alveoli matters.
[bio28-s1.11.2] Carbon monoxide in cigarette smoke combines with haemoglobin, reducing the amount of oxygen the blood can carry. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio28-s1.11.3] Asthma: the smooth muscle in the bronchiole walls contracts, and the lining swells and produces extra mucus, narrowing the airways so that less air reaches the alveoli. Inhalers deliver drugs that relax the smooth muscle.

CANDIDATES bio29-s4: Blood
[bio29-s4.0.b] Blood is a tissue, like muscle or bone, but a liquid one. It is made of cells and cell fragments carried in a fluid, and each part has its own job.
[bio29-s4.0.0] Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
[bio29-s4.0.1] If blood, with a chemical added to stop it clotting, is spun in a centrifuge (a machine that spins samples so the heaviest parts sink), it separates into about 55% plasma, a straw-coloured liquid on top, and about 45% cells.
[bio29-s4.0.2] The cells form a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
[bio29-s4.0.3] Numbers in a healthy adult: red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. Red cells therefore greatly outnumber white cells, by several hundred to one.
[bio29-s4.0.4] All blood cells and platelets are made in the red bone marrow, found in spongy bone such as the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones. They come from stem cells (unspecialised cells that can become other cell types) that divide by mitosis.
[bio29-s4.0.5] Functions of blood: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature and water balance).
[bio29-s4.0.6] Diagram: a stained blood smear under a light microscope. Draw many red blood cells (pale centres, no nucleus), one or two white blood cells (larger, with a stained nucleus: a phagocyte with a lobed nucleus and a lymphocyte with a large round nucleus) and small platelets. Label each.
[bio29-s4.0.7] Diagram: draw a centrifuged tube labelled plasma, white cells and platelets layer, and red cells layer, with percentages.
[bio29-s4.1.b] Plasma is the liquid part of blood, and everything else in blood is carried in it or by it. Because it is mostly water, it can dissolve and transport a huge range of substances around the body.
[bio29-s4.1.0] Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it. Water is a good solvent, so many substances dissolve in it, and it also carries heat.
[bio29-s4.1.1] Dissolved substances transported in plasma: nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals); wastes (urea, from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; and salts, such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio29-s4.1.2] Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻). They form in red blood cells, by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻).
[bio29-s4.1.3] A small amount of carbon dioxide is simply dissolved in plasma, and some is carried by haemoglobin. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s4.1.4] Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors, for clotting; antibodies, for defence against pathogens; and albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
[bio29-s4.1.5] Plasma distributes heat from active organs, such as working muscles, around the body. Its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers (chemicals that resist changes in acidity) keep the blood pH near 7.4.
[bio29-s4.2.b] Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body. Almost every feature of their structure helps them do that one job well.
[bio29-s4.2.0] Red blood cells, or erythrocytes, are biconcave discs (round and thinner in the centre than at the edge), about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
[bio29-s4.2.1] The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface.
[bio29-s4.2.2] There is no nucleus, and no mitochondria, in the mature cell, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. The cell is also packed with haemoglobin, so each cell can carry a lot of oxygen.
[bio29-s4.2.3] The cell is small and flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. This shortens the distance oxygen must diffuse.
[bio29-s4.2.4] Because they have no mitochondria, red blood cells respire anaerobically (without oxygen), so they do not use up the oxygen they carry.
[bio29-s4.2.5] Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron, so each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is bright red.
[bio29-s4.2.6] In respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. The reaction is reversible. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio29-s4.3.b] Red blood cells wear out and must be constantly replaced. Problems with their number or with haemoglobin affect how much oxygen reaches the tissues.
[bio29-s4.3.0] Red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days. With no nucleus they cannot repair themselves, so they wear out.
[bio29-s4.3.1] Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen. The iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments, which are excreted in bile.
[bio29-s4.3.2] The kidney hormone EPO stimulates the production of red blood cells. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio29-s4.3.3] Anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin. A common cause is iron deficiency, because iron is needed to make haem.
[bio29-s4.3.4] Carbon monoxide, in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances, combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried and it can kill.
[bio29-s4.3.5] Sickle-cell anaemia is a further disorder of red blood cells, named here only.
[bio29-s4.4.b] White blood cells are the body's defenders against infection. They differ from red cells in several ways, which makes a good comparison to learn.
[bio29-s4.4.0] White blood cells, or leucocytes, defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, are larger and colourless (they have no haemoglobin), and are far fewer in number.
[bio29-s4.4.1] They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues, where infections often are.
[bio29-s4.4.2] There are two broad groups: phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells.
[bio29-s4.4.3] The number of white cells rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections. Leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells.
[bio29-s4.5.b] Platelets are not whole cells but fragments, and they are the first responders when a blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.5.0] Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus. They break off from large cells in the red bone marrow.
[bio29-s4.5.1] Platelets live about 7 to 10 days and start the clotting of blood.
[bio29-s4.5.2] Clotting matters because it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so that pathogens cannot enter. It is part of the first line of defence against infection.
[bio29-s4.6.b] Clotting is a chain of events in which one change triggers the next, ending in a mesh of protein threads that plugs the wound. Learn the stages in order.
[bio29-s4.6.0] 1. A blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.6.1] 2. Platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug.
[bio29-s4.6.2] 3. The platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors.
[bio29-s4.6.3] 4. A series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver with the help of vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin.
[bio29-s4.6.4] 5. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin.
[bio29-s4.6.5] 6. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot.
[bio29-s4.6.6] 7. At the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs. Calcium and vitamin K are both needed for clotting. Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing.
[bio29-s4.6.7] An unwanted clot inside a vessel, a thrombosis, can block an artery and cause a heart attack or stroke. Diagram: a flow chart of damage, platelets, clotting factors (with Ca²⁺ and vitamin K), prothrombin, thrombin, fibrinogen, fibrin mesh and clot. Add fibrin threads trapping red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.7.b] Your ABO blood group depends on molecules on the surface of your red blood cells. The plasma then carries antibodies matched to whichever molecules your own cells lack.
[bio29-s4.7.0] A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person's red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response. In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
[bio29-s4.7.1] The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen or antigens that a person's own red cells lack. This stops the body attacking its own cells.
[bio29-s4.7.2] Group A has antigen A on the red cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.3] Group AB has both antigens and no antibodies. Group O has no antigens and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.4] Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody, for example A cells meeting anti-A, the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps.
[bio29-s4.7.5] In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed, or burst. This can cause kidney failure and death.
[bio29-s4.7.6] Diagram: four red blood cells, one for each group, showing the antigens on the membrane (A, B, both, none), with the antibodies in the plasma written beside each. Add a sketch of agglutination, with antibodies linking red cells into a clump.
[bio29-s4.8.b] Transfusion works only if the donor's red cells do not meet an antibody in the recipient's plasma that matches their antigens. The rule follows from the ABO antigens and antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.0] What matters is the donor's red-cell antigens and the recipient's antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen against which their own plasma has antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.1] Group O red cells carry no antigens, so they can be given to anyone, and group O is called the universal donor. This applies to red cells: group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B.
[bio29-s4.8.2] Group AB people have no antibodies, so they can receive red cells of any ABO group, and group AB is the universal recipient. In practice, hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
[bio29-s4.8.3] Compatibility: group O can receive O only; group A can receive A or O; group B can receive B or O; group AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
[bio29-s4.8.4] Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and with anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only means group A; clumping with anti-B only means group B; clumping with both means AB; clumping with neither means O.
[bio29-s4.8.5] A third test, with anti-D, tests the Rhesus group.
[bio29-s4.9.b] The Rhesus factor is a second red-cell antigen system, on top of ABO. Unlike the ABO antibodies, the Rhesus antibody has to be learned by the immune system first.
[bio29-s4.9.0] The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+), and those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−).
[bio29-s4.9.1] A person's full blood group combines both systems, for example O negative or A positive.
[bio29-s4.9.2] Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present. A Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood, for example through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy.
[bio29-s4.9.3] A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
[bio29-s4.10.b] A Rhesus negative mother carrying a Rhesus positive baby can become sensitised to her baby's blood, and that may endanger a later baby. The problem is called haemolytic disease of the newborn, and it can be prevented.
[bio29-s4.10.0] 1. A Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby, inherited from a Rh+ father.
[bio29-s4.10.1] 2. Normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth, or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury, some fetal red cells enter the mother's blood.
[bio29-s4.10.2] 3. Her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells, and she is now sensitised. The first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made.
[bio29-s4.10.3] 4. In a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.10.4] 5. The baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage or death.
[bio29-s4.10.5] Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies, anti-D immunoglobulin, during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby, or after a miscarriage or injury.
[bio29-s4.10.6] These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells. The injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks.
[bio29-s4.10.7] This is artificial passive immunity (antibodies given ready-made, not made by the body). Every pregnant woman's blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason.
[bio29-s4.11.b] Knowing a person's blood group saves lives in several ways, from transfusions to pregnancy care. Each use below comes from knowing the groups.
[bio29-s4.11.0] Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient's ABO and Rhesus groups are tested, and the donor blood is cross-matched, meaning it is mixed with the patient's plasma to check that no clumping occurs.
[bio29-s4.11.1] This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and allows transfusions in surgery, after accidents, in childbirth, for anaemia and in cancer treatment.
[bio29-s4.11.2] Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
[bio29-s4.11.3] Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
[bio29-s4.11.4] Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks. Donors of all groups are needed, because blood can be stored only for a limited time.
[bio29-s4.11.5] In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service, and donated blood is screened for infections.
[bio29-s4.11.6] Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group and tissue type to reduce the chance of rejection.
[bio29-s4.11.7] Nature of Science: before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, which made safe transfusion possible.
[bio29-s4.11.8] In 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after the rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).
