CANDIDATES bio1-s1: The nature of scientific knowledge
[bio1-s1.0.b] Science is both a body of knowledge and a process for building it. It explains the natural world using evidence gathered by observation and experiment, and that evidence requirement is what separates scientific claims from opinions.
[bio1-s1.0.0] A scientific claim must be testable against evidence from the natural world. Questions of value or morals, such as “should we clone animals?”, can be informed by science but cannot be answered by it, because no experiment can measure what is right or wrong.
[bio1-s1.0.1] Scientific knowledge is evidence-based (empirical): ideas are accepted because they agree with observations and experimental results, not because of who proposed them. A famous name does not make an idea correct.
[bio1-s1.0.2] An observation is what is directly detected with the senses or instruments, such as “the broth went cloudy”. An inference is an explanation drawn from an observation, such as “microorganisms grew in the broth”. Scientists keep them separate, as an inference can be wrong when the observation is right.
[bio1-s1.1.b] Scientific ideas are never declared final, yet the best of them last for generations. Two ideas explain why: ideas are open to revision, and they must be capable of being shown wrong.
[bio1-s1.1.0] Scientific knowledge is tentative but durable: any idea can be revised if new evidence contradicts it, yet well-tested ideas survive for a long time. The cell theory (the idea that all living things are made of cells) has survived more than 150 years of testing.
[bio1-s1.1.1] Tentative does not mean weak or a guess. It means the idea is held open to revision, while still being backed by a large amount of evidence.
[bio1-s1.1.2] An idea must be falsifiable: it must be possible, in principle, to imagine an observation that would show the idea is wrong. The philosopher Karl Popper made falsifiability his test of whether an idea is scientific.
[bio1-s1.1.3] Evidence can support an idea or refute it, but it can never prove an idea true for all time, because a later observation might contradict it. One reliable contrary result can disprove an idea.
[bio1-s1.2.b] Textbooks sometimes present “the scientific method” as a fixed recipe, but real science is messier. The first half of the usual cycle takes a scientist from noticing something to having data.
[bio1-s1.2.0] There is no single fixed scientific method. The process is often complex and iterative (it loops back on itself), following many different paths, but it usually involves the stages below.
[bio1-s1.2.1] 1. Observation: the scientist notices something in the natural world.
[bio1-s1.2.2] 2. Question: the scientist asks why or how it happens.
[bio1-s1.2.3] 3. Hypothesis: a proposed, testable explanation is put forward.
[bio1-s1.2.4] 4. Prediction: the scientist works out what should be found if the hypothesis is correct.
[bio1-s1.2.5] 5. Experiment or further observation: the prediction is tested.
[bio1-s1.2.6] 6. Collection and analysis of data: results are gathered and worked on to see what they show.
[bio1-s1.3.b] Once the data is in, the work is not finished. The second half of the cycle checks the result and shares it, so that other scientists can judge it.
[bio1-s1.3.0] 7. Conclusion: the hypothesis is judged to be supported or rejected by the data.
[bio1-s1.3.1] 8. Repetition and checking: the work is repeated, so that one odd result is not relied on.
[bio1-s1.3.2] 9. Comparison with existing knowledge: the result is set against what is already known.
[bio1-s1.3.3] 10. Publication in a scientific journal after peer review, which means other experts check the work before it is published.
[bio1-s1.3.4] 11. Testing of the findings by other scientists, who try to confirm or challenge them.
[bio1-s1.3.5] 12. Over time, well-supported findings are developed into a theory or law.
[bio1-s1.3.6] A rejected hypothesis leads to a new or modified hypothesis, so the cycle starts again. This is why the process is a loop and not a straight line.
[bio1-s1.3.7] Diagram: the cycle drawn as a loop with arrows. Labels: observation, question, hypothesis, prediction, experiment, data analysis, conclusion, repeat and check. Arrow from “hypothesis rejected” back to “new hypothesis”; “publish and peer review” leading out to “theory or law”.
[bio1-s1.4.b] The cycle suggests that every investigation is a laboratory experiment, but that is only one way of testing ideas. Science also needs imagination.
[bio1-s1.4.0] Not all science is experimental. Some fields test ideas by observation and comparison, for example fossil records and comparative anatomy as evidence for evolution, or field surveys of ecosystems.
[bio1-s1.4.1] Science is creative: imagining hypotheses and designing ways to test them takes imagination, and chance observations can start new lines of work, such as the discovery of penicillin, the standard example. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio1-s1.5.b] No scientist works alone for long. Science is a shared activity in which claims are expected to face challenge.
[bio1-s1.5.0] Scientists work in teams, build on others’ published work and expect their claims to be checked and challenged, because a claim that survives checking is more reliable.
[bio1-s1.5.1] Scepticism means doubting a claim until the evidence for it is good. It is a norm of science, not a personal attack on the scientist. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio1-s1.6.b] Scientists are human, so the way science is done reflects the world they live in.
[bio1-s1.6.0] Science is influenced by the society, culture, funding and technology of its time, which affect what questions get investigated and what can be measured.
[bio1-s1.6.1] Individual scientists can be biased. There is more on the types of bias in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio1-s1.7.b] The scientific method is powerful, but it has limits. Knowing them helps you judge how far a result can be trusted.
[bio1-s1.7.0] Limited by current knowledge and technology: some questions cannot yet be investigated. Cells could not be observed until microscopes were invented, and the internal structure of organelles could not be observed until electron microscopes were available.
[bio1-s1.7.1] The basis of the investigation may be wrong: if the theory or assumption a hypothesis rests on is false, the conclusions may be false too.
[bio1-s1.7.2] Human error and interpretation: data can be misread, and scientists can interpret the same results in different ways.
[bio1-s1.7.3] Experimental design: a poorly designed experiment can give results that seem to support a hypothesis when they do not. There is more on this in Designing and conducting investigations.
[bio1-s1.7.4] Applying results to nature: conditions in a laboratory are simpler and more controlled than in a living organism or an ecosystem, so results may not apply outside the laboratory. A drug that works on cells in a dish may not work in a person.
[bio1-s1.7.5] Science cannot answer questions of value, ethics or the supernatural, and it cannot prove a hypothesis absolutely, because evidence can only support or refute an idea.
[bio1-s1.8.b] A hypothesis is the first step from an observation towards an explanation. It is the lowest-status kind of scientific idea, since it has yet to be tested.
[bio1-s1.8.0] A hypothesis is a proposed, testable explanation for an observation. It makes predictions that can be checked by experiment or observation.
[bio1-s1.8.1] A hypothesis may be supported or rejected; it is never proved.
[bio1-s1.8.2] Example: in the early 1980s Robin Warren and Barry Marshall proposed that stomach ulcers are caused by infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, not by stress and excess acid as was then believed. There is more on writing a testable hypothesis in Questioning and predicting.
[bio1-s1.9.b] In everyday speech “theory” means a hunch. In science it means the opposite: the most thoroughly tested kind of explanation.
[bio1-s1.9.0] A theory is a broad, well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by many confirmed hypotheses and by evidence from many independent sources.
[bio1-s1.9.1] A theory explains why something happens and makes predictions. It is the highest status an explanation reaches in science.
[bio1-s1.9.2] A theory is not a guess, so the everyday phrase “just a theory” misuses the word.
[bio1-s1.9.3] The cell theory states that all living things are made of one or more cells, that the cell is the basic unit of structure and function of life, and that all cells come from pre-existing cells.
[bio1-s1.9.4] Matthias Schleiden proposed in 1838 that plants are made of cells. Theodor Schwann extended this to animals in 1839. Rudolf Virchow added in 1855 that all cells arise from existing cells (“omnis cellula e cellula”).
[bio1-s1.9.5] Other biological theories include the theory of evolution by natural selection, proposed by Darwin and Wallace, and the germ theory of disease, which holds that many diseases are caused by microorganisms. There is more on the first in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
[bio1-s1.10.b] A law is a different kind of knowledge from a theory. Where a theory explains, a law describes a pattern.
[bio1-s1.10.0] A law, or principle, is a statement, often mathematical, that describes a pattern that always occurs under stated conditions. It describes what happens but does not explain why.
[bio1-s1.10.1] Biology has few laws because living systems are variable, so patterns rarely hold without exception.
[bio1-s1.10.2] Mendel’s laws of segregation and independent assortment describe how alleles (versions of a gene) pass to offspring. They have known exceptions, for example linked genes. There is more on this in Mendel's Laws.
[bio1-s1.10.3] The laws of thermodynamics apply to living things. Energy is not created or destroyed, only converted, as when light energy is converted to chemical energy in photosynthesis.
[bio1-s1.11.b] Examiners now ask you to tell these three apart. They differ in scope, purpose and the evidence behind them, and none is a stage on the way to another.
[bio1-s1.11.0] Scope: a hypothesis concerns one observation, a theory covers a wide range of observations, and a law covers one pattern.
[bio1-s1.11.1] Purpose: a hypothesis proposes an explanation, a theory gives a tested explanation, and a law describes a pattern.
[bio1-s1.11.2] Evidence: a hypothesis has little or none yet, a theory rests on a large body of evidence from many independent sources, and a law is repeated observation without exception under stated conditions.
[bio1-s1.11.3] All three can be revised or rejected if the evidence demands it.
[bio1-s1.11.4] A theory does not grow up into a law: they are different kinds of knowledge. A law describes a pattern and a theory explains it. A theory can explain a law; for example, Mendel’s laws are explained by the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis.
[bio1-s1.11.5] A scientific fact is a repeatable observation, for example “DNA is found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells”. A model is a simplified representation used to explain or predict. There is more on this in Models.
[bio1-s1.12.b] Because scientific knowledge is tentative, it changes over time. The cases that follow show this in action.
[bio1-s1.12.0] Ideas change because of new evidence that the old idea cannot explain, new technology that allows new observations (for example microscopes and DNA sequencing), reinterpretation of existing evidence, and replication (repeating of the work) by other scientists.
[bio1-s1.12.1] Change is usually gradual refinement. Occasionally a whole framework is replaced; the historian Thomas Kuhn called this a paradigm shift, in his book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962).
[bio1-s1.13.b] For a long time people believed that small creatures could appear from non-living matter. Two careful experiments, a couple of centuries apart, showed otherwise.
[bio1-s1.13.0] Old idea (spontaneous generation): living things such as maggots and microorganisms arise from non-living matter.
[bio1-s1.13.1] In 1668 Francesco Redi put meat in jars that were open, sealed or covered with gauze. Maggots appeared only on the meat in the open jars, which flies could reach.
[bio1-s1.13.2] In the gauze-covered jars, flies laid their eggs on the gauze, not on the meat, so no maggots appeared.
[bio1-s1.13.3] In the early 1860s Louis Pasteur boiled broth in swan-neck flasks, which have an S-shaped neck. Air could enter, but dust and microorganisms were trapped in the bend, and the broth stayed clear.
[bio1-s1.13.4] When the neck was broken off, or the broth was tipped into the bend, microorganisms grew in it. This showed the microorganisms came from the dust and not from the broth itself.
[bio1-s1.13.5] New idea: living things come only from other living things (biogenesis), which supported the germ theory.
[bio1-s1.14.b] The cell theory was not announced in one go. It grew as better microscopes let scientists observe more.
[bio1-s1.14.0] The cell theory (all living things are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from pre-existing cells) was built up by several scientists over decades as microscopes improved.
[bio1-s1.14.1] It is still being extended. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have been found to contain their own DNA.
[bio1-s1.15.b] This case shows how a well-established belief was overturned by a bacterium that doctors thought could not exist in the stomach.
[bio1-s1.15.0] Old idea: ulcers are caused by stress, spicy food and excess acid, and no bacterium could survive stomach acid.
[bio1-s1.15.1] Warren observed curved bacteria in biopsies (small samples of tissue) of inflamed stomach linings. Marshall and Warren cultured (grew in the laboratory) Helicobacter pylori in 1982.
[bio1-s1.15.2] In 1984 Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria, developed gastritis (an inflamed stomach lining) and treated it with antibiotics.
[bio1-s1.15.3] Other scientists repeated the findings, and trials showed that antibiotics cured most ulcers.
[bio1-s1.15.4] New idea: most stomach ulcers are caused by H. pylori infection and are treated with antibiotics.
[bio1-s1.15.5] Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005.
[bio1-s1.15.6] The case shows that a new idea is often resisted at first and is accepted when the evidence is reproduced.
[bio1-s1.16.b] In this case the evidence was clear, yet the idea was ignored for years because nobody could say why it worked.
[bio1-s1.16.0] In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, at Vienna General Hospital, noticed that far more mothers died of childbed (puerperal) fever on the ward run by doctors than on the ward run by midwives.
[bio1-s1.16.1] The doctors who ran that ward came from dissecting corpses, while the midwives did not.
[bio1-s1.16.2] He made staff wash their hands in chlorinated lime solution, and deaths fell sharply.
[bio1-s1.16.3] Many doctors rejected his idea because it lacked an explanation. It was accepted only after the germ theory of disease explained it.
[bio1-s1.16.4] The case shows that evidence without an explanatory theory can be ignored.
[bio1-s1.17.b] The same pattern of old idea, new evidence and refined idea appears again in other topics in this course.
[bio1-s1.17.0] The five-kingdom system of classifying living things was replaced by three domains and six kingdoms.
[bio1-s1.17.1] The lock-and-key model of enzyme action was refined to the induced-fit model, and the membrane “sandwich” model was replaced by the fluid mosaic model. There is more on these in Domains of life, Classification and Models.

CANDIDATES bio13-s3: Investigation: rate of enzyme activity
[bio13-s3.0.b] An enzyme is a biological catalyst, so what we want to know in an investigation is how fast it works. That speed is called the rate, and it is always measured against time.
[bio13-s3.0.0] The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is the amount of substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on) converted into product per unit time, e.g. cm³ of oxygen produced per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.1] The rate can be measured either by how fast a product appears or by how fast the substrate disappears, whichever is easier to detect for that reaction.
[bio13-s3.0.2] Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂); the rate is measured by the oxygen released, as foam or as a volume of gas.
[bio13-s3.0.3] Amylase digests starch to maltose; the rate is measured by the time taken for the starch to disappear, tested with iodine.
[bio13-s3.0.4] Rate = amount of product ÷ time: e.g. 30 cm³ of foam in 2 minutes gives a rate of 15 cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.5] Where the time taken to reach an end-point (such as the starch disappearing) is measured, rate = 1 ÷ time: starch gone in 120 s gives a rate of 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹, often written as 1000/time to give whole numbers.
[bio13-s3.0.6] A shorter time means a faster rate, because the same change has been completed more quickly.
[bio13-s3.0.7] Measure the initial rate, at the start of the reaction (e.g. in the first one or two minutes), because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
[bio13-s3.1.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. Presenting them clearly in a table and a graph is how you show the pattern in your results.
[bio13-s3.1.0] A results table has the independent variable (the factor you change) in the first column, then the repeat readings, the mean and the rate, each column headed with its unit.
[bio13-s3.1.1] A graph has the independent variable on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis, with points plotted accurately and joined by a smooth curve or line of best fit.
[bio13-s3.2.b] All five investigations use the same small set of enzymes, substrates and equipment. Knowing what each is for makes the methods easy to follow.
[bio13-s3.2.0] Catalase is found in almost all cells that use oxygen, where it breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism. Good sources are celery, potato, liver and yeast.
[bio13-s3.2.1] Catalase extract: blend a fixed mass of chopped celery (e.g. 20 g) with distilled water (e.g. 100 cm³), then filter through muslin or filter paper. Make one batch and use it for every test, so the enzyme is the same each time.
[bio13-s3.2.2] Amylase: a commercial (e.g. fungal) amylase solution made up at a fixed concentration; starch solution (e.g. 1%) is its substrate. Saliva contains amylase but is avoided for hygiene reasons.
[bio13-s3.2.3] Equipment for measuring: 100 cm³ graduated cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes for measuring volumes, thermometer to check temperatures, stopwatch to time each run, electronic balance.
[bio13-s3.2.4] Other equipment: test tubes and boiling tubes, blender or mortar and pestle, knife and chopping board, filter funnel, spotting tile and droppers, conical flask with bung and delivery tube, trough, labels.
[bio13-s3.2.5] Water baths can be thermostatically controlled electric baths, or beakers of water warmed with kettle water or cooled with ice, checked with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.2.6] Reagents: hydrogen peroxide solution (one stock of fixed concentration), buffer solutions or buffer tablets of known pH, washing-up liquid, distilled water, starch solution, amylase solution and iodine solution.
[bio13-s3.3.b] Four ideas recur across the investigations: a buffer to hold pH steady, washing-up liquid to make the oxygen measurable in the foam methods, a waiting time so the mixture reaches the right temperature, and a control to prove the enzyme is responsible.
[bio13-s3.3.0] A buffer is a solution that keeps the pH constant even when acids or alkalis are produced or added.
[bio13-s3.3.1] A buffer at pH 7 (or at the enzyme's optimum) is added in every investigation except the pH investigation, so that pH is a controlled variable (kept the same each time).
[bio13-s3.3.2] In the foam methods, a drop of washing-up liquid traps the oxygen released by catalase as a foam, so its volume can be read in a graduated cylinder; the same amount is added every time.
[bio13-s3.3.3] Reaching temperature (equilibration): the enzyme and the substrate are placed in the same water bath, in separate containers, for about 5 minutes before they are mixed, so the reaction starts at the chosen temperature.
[bio13-s3.3.4] A control in each investigation uses enzyme that has been boiled (denatured), or distilled water in place of the enzyme, with everything else the same.
[bio13-s3.3.5] The control shows that the oxygen (or the loss of starch) is caused by the enzyme and not by the substrate breaking down on its own.
[bio13-s3.4.b] The chemicals and equipment here are mostly mild, but several can harm skin, eyes or clothes if handled carelessly.
[bio13-s3.4.0] Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent that irritates or burns the eyes and skin; wear safety glasses (and gloves), measure it with a syringe, and wash any splash off with plenty of water.
[bio13-s3.4.1] Buffers of very low or very high pH are irritants, so wear safety glasses.
[bio13-s3.4.2] Use tongs or a test-tube holder and take care with boiling water and hot water baths; keep electrical water baths and blenders away from spilt water.
[bio13-s3.4.3] Take care with knives and blender blades. If liver is used, treat it as raw meat: wear gloves, keep it off benches used for other work and wash hands afterwards.
[bio13-s3.4.4] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes, so handle it with care. Never eat or drink in the laboratory, because chemicals could be swallowed.
[bio13-s3.5.b] Here we vary how much hydrogen peroxide (the substrate) the same amount of catalase has to work on, and see how the rate of oxygen production changes.
[bio13-s3.5.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of substrate (hydrogen peroxide) concentration on the rate of catalase activity, by changing only the substrate so any change in rate must be due to it.
[bio13-s3.5.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increases the rate of oxygen production, because more substrate molecules collide with active sites per second, until all the active sites are occupied and the rate levels off.”
[bio13-s3.5.2] Independent variable: hydrogen peroxide concentration, made by diluting the stock with distilled water to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the stock (e.g. 2 cm³ stock + 8 cm³ water = 20%), always to the same total volume (e.g. 10 cm³).
[bio13-s3.5.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): volume of oxygen collected in the first minute (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.5.4] Controlled variables: volume of the same celery extract (so enzyme concentration is constant), temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), total volume of solution, timing and apparatus.
[bio13-s3.5.5] Control: the 100% hydrogen peroxide with boiled extract (or with water in place of the extract), which gives little or no oxygen; the 0% tube also shows that no oxygen forms without substrate.
[bio13-s3.6.b] The oxygen given off is collected in an upturned cylinder of water, so its volume can be read directly.
[bio13-s3.6.0] 1. Fill a graduated cylinder with water and turn it upside down in a trough of water, with no air at the top.
[bio13-s3.6.1] 2. Put 10 cm³ of the first hydrogen peroxide dilution and 2 cm³ of pH 7 buffer in a conical flask (or boiling tube) in the water bath, with 2 cm³ of celery extract in a separate tube beside it, for about 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.6.2] 3. Place the end of the delivery tube under the mouth of the upturned cylinder.
[bio13-s3.6.3] 4. Add the extract to the flask, immediately put in the bung with the delivery tube, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.6.4] 5. Read the volume of oxygen collected in the cylinder after 1 minute (or every 30 seconds for 3 minutes).
[bio13-s3.6.5] 6. Rinse the flask and repeat for each concentration. Do each concentration three times and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.6.6] Alternatives: a gas syringe connected to the flask measures the oxygen volume more accurately than collection over water; or the foam method used in the pH investigation can be used.
[bio13-s3.6.7] Another alternative: filter-paper discs soaked in catalase extract are dropped into each concentration and the time for each disc to rise to the surface (buoyed by oxygen bubbles) is timed, with rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.6.8] Diagram: a conical flask in a water bath, with a bung and delivery tube leading under an upturned graduated cylinder full of water in a trough. Label hydrogen peroxide + buffer + catalase extract, water bath, thermometer, delivery tube, oxygen collecting, trough of water.
[bio13-s3.7.b] The results follow a typical curve: a steep rise, then a plateau. Each part has its own explanation, and the plateau is caused by saturation of the active sites, not by the enzyme being denatured or used up.
[bio13-s3.7.0] Expected results: no oxygen at 0%; at low concentrations the rate rises steeply, roughly in proportion to concentration; at high concentrations it rises less and levels off into a plateau. The boiled-enzyme control gives little or no oxygen.
[bio13-s3.7.1] The rise is explained by collisions: more substrate molecules in the same volume means more collisions with active sites (the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds) per second, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and more product is made per minute.
[bio13-s3.7.2] The plateau is explained by saturation: at high substrate concentration every active site is occupied at any moment, so the enzyme (saturated) works at its maximum rate, and adding more substrate cannot increase the rate.
[bio13-s3.7.3] At the plateau the amount of enzyme is the limiting factor (the thing holding the rate back); adding more enzyme would raise the rate again.
[bio13-s3.7.4] Within the range used in a school investigation the plateau may not be reached if the enzyme is in excess.
[bio13-s3.7.5] Conclusion: state the trend shown by the mean rates, whether it supports the hypothesis, and the concentration above which the rate stopped increasing, if one was found.
[bio13-s3.7.6] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against substrate concentration (x-axis); a curve rising from the origin, steep at first and then levelling off, with the plateau labelled all active sites occupied (saturated).
[bio13-s3.8.b] Now the substrate stays the same and the amount of enzyme changes. Amylase digests starch, and the end-point is found with iodine, which turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown when starch is absent.
[bio13-s3.8.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of enzyme (amylase) concentration on the rate at which starch is digested.
[bio13-s3.8.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of amylase increases the rate of starch digestion, because more active sites are available to bind starch molecules, as long as starch is in excess.”
[bio13-s3.8.2] Independent variable: amylase concentration, made by diluting the stock amylase solution with distilled water to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the stock, always to the same volume.
[bio13-s3.8.3] Dependent variable: time taken for the starch to disappear (the iodine test no longer turns blue-black), converted to rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.8.4] Controlled variables: volume and concentration of starch solution, volume of amylase solution, temperature (water bath at e.g. 37 °C), pH (buffer at pH 7), volume of iodine in each well, and the sampling interval.
[bio13-s3.8.5] Control: starch solution with distilled water (0% amylase) or with boiled amylase in place of the amylase; it stays blue-black with iodine for the whole investigation, showing that the starch does not disappear without active enzyme.
[bio13-s3.9.b] Drops of the reacting mixture are tested with iodine at regular intervals until the blue-black colour no longer appears.
[bio13-s3.9.0] 1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile (a white tile with small hollows), so each sample can be tested as soon as it is taken.
[bio13-s3.9.1] 2. Put 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of pH 7 buffer into a test tube, and 2 cm³ of the first amylase dilution into another, then stand both in the water bath for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.9.2] 3. Pour the amylase into the starch, mix, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.9.3] 4. Every 30 seconds, take one drop of the mixture with a clean dropper and add it to the next well of iodine.
[bio13-s3.9.4] 5. Note the time at which a drop first leaves the iodine yellow-brown (no blue-black): this is the end-point, when all the starch has been digested.
[bio13-s3.9.5] 6. Repeat for each amylase concentration, three times each, so that a mean can be calculated and a one-off error does not mislead you; then calculate the mean time and the rate.
[bio13-s3.9.6] Alternative: with catalase, vary the volume of celery extract (e.g. 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 cm³, each made up to the same total volume with distilled water) or the number of equal-sized celery discs, and measure the foam or oxygen volume in a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.9.7] Diagram: a spotting tile with a row of wells, the first wells blue-black and the later wells yellow-brown, labelled with sampling times; and the starch–amylase tube in a water bath with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.10.b] With more enzyme, the starch disappears sooner. Unlike the substrate investigation, the graph is a straight line, as long as there is plenty of substrate.
[bio13-s3.10.0] Expected results: the higher the amylase concentration, the shorter the time for the starch to disappear, so the faster the rate; the 0% and boiled-enzyme controls never reach the end-point.
[bio13-s3.10.1] A graph of rate against enzyme concentration is a straight line through the origin: doubling the enzyme roughly doubles the rate.
[bio13-s3.10.2] Explanation: more enzyme molecules means more active sites, so more substrate molecules can be bound and converted at the same time, and more product is made per unit time.
[bio13-s3.10.3] The straight line continues only while substrate is in excess (more than the enzyme can use). If the enzyme concentration becomes very high, substrate becomes the limiting factor and the line levels off.
[bio13-s3.10.4] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against enzyme concentration (x-axis); a straight line rising from the origin, labelled substrate in excess.
[bio13-s3.11.b] Each enzyme works best at a particular pH. Here, buffers set the pH, and the volume of foam produced by catalase shows how active it is at each one.
[bio13-s3.11.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.11.1] Hypothesis: “Catalase has an optimum pH at which its rate is highest, and the rate decreases at pH values above and below it, because the shape of the active site is changed.”
[bio13-s3.11.2] Independent variable: pH, set with buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10), which hold each pH steady so it does not drift as the reaction proceeds.
[bio13-s3.11.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam produced in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.11.4] Controlled variables: temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract (or mass of celery), volume of buffer, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.11.5] Control: at each pH (or at pH 7), boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.12.b] The foam is built up in a graduated cylinder, so its height can be read at the end of a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.12.0] 1. Into a 100 cm³ graduated cylinder put 10 cm³ of pH 4 buffer, 2 cm³ of celery extract (or a fixed mass of chopped celery) and one drop of washing-up liquid.
[bio13-s3.12.1] 2. Stand the cylinder in the 25 °C water bath, with a boiling tube of hydrogen peroxide beside it, for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.12.2] 3. Note the starting volume of liquid in the cylinder, so the foam volume can be found by subtraction.
[bio13-s3.12.3] 4. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide with a syringe and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.12.4] 5. After 2 minutes, read the volume at the top of the foam, at eye level; the foam volume is this reading minus the starting volume.
[bio13-s3.12.5] 6. Wash the cylinder and repeat with each buffer, three times each, and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.12.6] Diagram: a graduated cylinder standing in a water bath with a thermometer, containing buffer, celery extract and washing-up liquid at the bottom and a column of foam above, with a syringe adding hydrogen peroxide. Label each part and the foam height being read.
[bio13-s3.13.b] The rate rises to a peak at one pH and falls on both sides. The explanation lies in how pH affects the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s3.13.0] Expected results: the most foam forms at around pH 7 (the optimum for celery and liver catalase), with less in more acidic and more alkaline buffers and very little at the extremes. The control gives no foam.
[bio13-s3.13.1] The optimum pH is the pH at which the active site has the shape and charges that best fit the substrate.
[bio13-s3.13.2] A higher or lower hydrogen ion concentration, in more acidic or more alkaline conditions, changes the charges in the active site and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
[bio13-s3.13.3] Small changes from the optimum are reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme permanently.
[bio13-s3.13.4] Each enzyme's optimum suits the place where it works: pepsin in the acid stomach has an optimum of about pH 2; salivary amylase in the mouth about pH 7; trypsin in the alkaline small intestine about pH 8.
[bio13-s3.13.5] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against pH (x-axis); a peaked, bell-shaped curve with its peak labelled optimum pH at about 7 for catalase; optionally a second curve peaking at about pH 2, labelled pepsin.
[bio13-s3.14.b] Temperature has two opposite effects on an enzyme: it speeds up collisions, but too much heat destroys the enzyme. This investigation measures catalase activity across a range of water-bath temperatures.
[bio13-s3.14.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.14.1] Hypothesis: “As temperature increases, the rate of catalase activity increases up to an optimum temperature, because molecules move faster and collide more often; above the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme is denatured.”
[bio13-s3.14.2] Independent variable: temperature, set with water baths at e.g. 0 °C (ice water), 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, each checked with a thermometer because baths drift from their set temperature.
[bio13-s3.14.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.14.4] Controlled variables: pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.14.5] Control: boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract at each temperature, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.15.b] The temperature method reuses the foam method from the pH investigation, with the buffer fixed and the water bath changed.
[bio13-s3.15.0] Method: follow the pH investigation method, but with pH 7 buffer in every cylinder, and with each cylinder (buffer, extract and washing-up liquid) and its tube of hydrogen peroxide left in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes before the peroxide is added.
[bio13-s3.15.1] Check the bath temperature with the thermometer during the 2 minutes, because it can drift; repeat three times at each temperature and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.15.2] The same method works with amylase and starch, timing the starch's disappearance at each temperature, as in the enzyme concentration investigation.
[bio13-s3.15.3] Diagram: the pH set-up, with a row of water baths at different labelled temperatures.
[bio13-s3.16.b] The curve rises slowly to a peak and then drops steeply. The rise and the fall have different causes.
[bio13-s3.16.0] Expected results: little foam at 0 °C; the rate increases as temperature rises, to a maximum at the optimum temperature; above the optimum the rate falls steeply, with little or no activity at the highest temperatures tested.
[bio13-s3.16.1] The graph is not symmetrical: a gradual rise and a steep fall. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 °C, body temperature.
[bio13-s3.16.2] Explanation of the rise: as temperature increases, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), move faster and collide more often, and more collisions have enough energy to react, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second.
[bio13-s3.16.3] Over the lower part of the range, the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (an approximate pattern, not an exact law).
[bio13-s3.16.4] Explanation of the fall: above the optimum, the enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured.
[bio13-s3.16.5] As temperature rises further, more enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls even though collisions are more frequent.
[bio13-s3.16.6] At low temperature the enzyme is inactive, not denatured: the molecules move too slowly for many collisions, but the active site keeps its shape, so activity returns when the enzyme is warmed (shown in the heat denaturation investigation).
[bio13-s3.16.7] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against temperature in °C (x-axis); a curve rising gradually to a peak labelled optimum temperature, then falling steeply to zero, with the falling part labelled enzyme denatured.
[bio13-s3.17.b] Heat can do more than slow an enzyme: it can destroy its shape for good. This investigation shows that boiled catalase stays inactive even after it is cooled.
[bio13-s3.17.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of heat denaturation on the activity of catalase.
[bio13-s3.17.1] Denaturation is the permanent loss of an enzyme's shape, and so of its active site and its activity, caused by high temperature or extreme pH.
[bio13-s3.17.2] Hypothesis: “Catalase that has been boiled will not break down hydrogen peroxide, even when cooled, because its active site has been permanently changed.”
[bio13-s3.17.3] Independent variable: whether the extract has been boiled (heat-treated) or not. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³).
[bio13-s3.17.4] Controlled variables: both extracts tested at the same temperature (e.g. 25 °C) and pH (pH 7 buffer), with the same volumes of extract, buffer and hydrogen peroxide, the same drop of washing-up liquid and the same time.
[bio13-s3.17.5] Control: the unboiled extract, which shows how much foam active catalase produces under the same conditions, so the boiled result has something to be compared with.
[bio13-s3.18.b] Half the extract is boiled and half is not; both are then tested at the same temperature, so any difference is due to boiling alone.
[bio13-s3.18.0] 1. Divide the celery extract between two labelled boiling tubes, A and B.
[bio13-s3.18.1] 2. Place tube A in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes; leave tube B at room temperature.
[bio13-s3.18.2] 3. Cool tube A, then stand both tubes in the 25 °C water bath for 5 minutes, so the boiled extract is tested at the same temperature as the unboiled.
[bio13-s3.18.3] 4. Set up two graduated cylinders, each with 10 cm³ of pH 7 buffer, a drop of washing-up liquid and 2 cm³ of extract A or B.
[bio13-s3.18.4] 5. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide to each and read the foam after 2 minutes. Repeat three times.
[bio13-s3.18.5] Expected results: the boiled extract gives little or no foam; the unboiled extract gives a large column of foam. Any small amount of foam from the boiled extract comes from hydrogen peroxide breaking down slowly on its own, or from catalase that was not fully denatured.
[bio13-s3.18.6] Conclusion: boiling denatured the catalase; its activity did not return on cooling, so the change is permanent. This supports the hypothesis and explains why boiled enzyme is used as the control in the other investigations.
[bio13-s3.18.7] Extension: extract kept in ice water for 10 minutes and then warmed to 25 °C gives as much foam as unchilled extract, showing that cooling makes an enzyme inactive without denaturing it, whereas heating denatures it.
[bio13-s3.18.8] The same investigation works with amylase: boiled amylase added to starch leaves the iodine test blue-black at every sampling time, while unboiled amylase digests the starch so the iodine stays yellow-brown.
[bio13-s3.19.b] Secondary data are results that someone else collected. They let you check and extend your own conclusions, and the specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data.
[bio13-s3.19.0] Secondary data are data collected by someone else: results pooled from other groups in the class, published tables, textbooks or research papers, or online databases.
[bio13-s3.19.1] Uses: compare your optimum with published optima (the optimum pH of digestive enzymes and the optimum temperature of human enzymes given in the pH and temperature investigations).
[bio13-s3.19.2] Uses: check whether your trend agrees with other groups' (reproducibility), and extend a conclusion beyond the range you could test.
[bio13-s3.19.3] Secondary data on enzymes from extreme environments support the link between structure and conditions: Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, works best at about 72 °C and is not denatured at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s3.19.4] When using secondary data, record its source, check that it was measured under comparable conditions (same enzyme source, substrate, pH or temperature), and judge whether the source is reliable. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio13-s3.20.b] Every method has weaknesses. Knowing where the error comes from tells you how to reduce it.
[bio13-s3.20.0] Foam volume is imprecise: bubbles burst, the top of the foam is uneven, and foam may stick to the cylinder walls. Read at eye level at exactly the same time each run; collecting the oxygen in a gas syringe is more accurate.
[bio13-s3.20.1] Gas is lost in the time between adding the enzyme and inserting the bung, or through leaks; some oxygen dissolves in the water when it is collected over water.
[bio13-s3.20.2] Water-bath temperatures drift, especially in beakers of hot water or ice; check with a thermometer throughout or use a thermostatically controlled bath.
[bio13-s3.20.3] The reaction mixture may not reach the bath temperature if equilibration is too short, so allow the full time.
[bio13-s3.21.b] Some errors come from the enzyme and reagents themselves, or from judging a colour change. Repeating and averaging makes results more reliable.
[bio13-s3.21.0] Enzyme activity varies between celery samples and falls as an extract stands, so make one batch, keep it cool and use it quickly for all the tests; a fresh batch for a repeat can give different values.
[bio13-s3.21.1] Hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down on standing, especially in light, so its concentration may fall during the session: use one fresh stock kept in a dark bottle.
[bio13-s3.21.2] The iodine end-point is a judgement of colour, and sampling every 30 seconds means the time is only known to within 30 seconds; sample more often near the end-point, compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter.
[bio13-s3.21.3] Measure volumes with syringes or graduated pipettes rather than by eye, and use a clean syringe or dropper for each solution to avoid contamination.
[bio13-s3.21.4] Repeat each value three times, calculate the mean, and identify and investigate any anomalous result; pool class data to check reproducibility.
[bio13-s3.21.5] Use at least five values of the independent variable so the shape of the graph (peak or plateau) can be seen.

CANDIDATES bio13-s4: Industrial enzymes
[bio13-s4.0.b] Industry needs reactions that are fast, clean and cheap. Enzymes (biological catalysts, which speed up reactions without being changed) often do this better than the chemical catalysts used in factories.
[bio13-s4.0.0] Specificity: an enzyme catalyses one reaction, so it makes the wanted product with few unwanted by-products. This gives a purer product that needs less purification.
[bio13-s4.0.1] Mild conditions: enzymes work at moderate temperatures (often 30–60 °C), normal pressure and near-neutral pH (neither strongly acidic nor alkaline), whereas many chemical catalysts need high temperatures and pressures.
[bio13-s4.0.2] Mild conditions save energy and money, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and make the process safer, because there are no very hot, high-pressure vessels.
[bio13-s4.0.3] Enzymes are efficient catalysts that are not used up, so a small amount can be used repeatedly. They are also biodegradable (broken down naturally), so they leave less harmful waste than many chemical catalysts.
[bio13-s4.1.b] Industry has to get its enzymes from living things, and enzymes have weaknesses as well as strengths. Knowing both explains how enzymes are produced and why the conditions in a factory are tightly controlled.
[bio13-s4.1.0] Most industrial enzymes are made by microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) grown in large vessels called fermenters or bioreactors. They are used because they grow fast, can be grown on cheap nutrients, and many release (secrete) their enzymes into the medium, where they are easy to collect.
[bio13-s4.1.1] Many industrial enzymes are now made by genetically modified microorganisms, which carry the gene for the enzyme. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio13-s4.1.2] A few enzymes come from plants (e.g. papain from papaya) or from animals, such as rennet (from calves' stomachs).
[bio13-s4.1.3] Limitation: enzymes are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working) by high temperatures and extreme pH, so conditions must be carefully controlled.
[bio13-s4.1.4] Limitation: enzymes can be expensive to produce and purify.
[bio13-s4.1.5] Limitation: free enzymes (ones simply dissolved in the mixture) are lost in the product after one use. Immobilisation, covered below, solves this.
[bio13-s4.1.6] Limitation: enzyme dust can cause allergies. In the early years of biological detergents, some factory workers developed allergies, so enzymes are now sealed in granules.
[bio13-s4.1.7] Enzymes from microorganisms that live in hot springs or in alkaline conditions are chosen or engineered for processes that need tougher enzymes, because they already work where normal enzymes would be denatured.
[bio13-s4.2.b] Milk is a good example of how industry uses single, specific enzymes. One enzyme turns milk into cheese, and another removes the sugar that some people cannot digest.
[bio13-s4.2.0] Cheese-making: chymosin (rennin), the active enzyme in rennet, clots the milk protein casein, so the milk separates into solid curds (made into cheese) and liquid whey.
[bio13-s4.2.1] Rennet was traditionally extracted from the stomachs of young calves. Much of the chymosin used today is made by genetically modified microorganisms, which suits vegetarians and needs no animals.
[bio13-s4.2.2] Lactose-free milk: lactase breaks lactose (the sugar in milk) down into glucose and galactose, so people with lactose intolerance (who cannot digest lactose) can drink the milk.
[bio13-s4.2.3] Lactose-free milk tastes sweeter, because glucose and galactose are sweeter than lactose. Lactase is often used immobilised.
[bio13-s4.3.b] Plant materials such as fruit and grain are full of structural and storage carbohydrates. Carbohydrate-digesting enzymes release the useful sugars and make processing easier.
[bio13-s4.3.0] Fruit juice: pectinase breaks down pectin, which helps hold plant cell walls together, so more juice is released from crushed fruit and the juice is clearer (less cloudy).
[bio13-s4.3.1] Sugar syrups: amylases break starch (e.g. from maize) into glucose syrup. Immobilised glucose isomerase then converts glucose into fructose, which is much sweeter.
[bio13-s4.3.2] High-fructose syrup is used to sweeten soft drinks and processed foods, so less is needed for the same sweetness.
[bio13-s4.3.3] Brewing and baking: amylases break starch in grain into maltose and glucose, which yeast ferments to make ethanol and carbon dioxide. In baking, the carbon dioxide helps the dough rise. There is more on fermentation in Respiration.
[bio13-s4.3.4] Proteases (enzymes that digest protein) are used to clear haze from beer.
[bio13-s4.4.b] Proteases, lipases and amylases digest the three main food molecules: protein, fat and starch. This is why they are useful both in sweets and meat and in household cleaning.
[bio13-s4.4.0] Proteases such as papain tenderise meat by breaking down muscle proteins.
[bio13-s4.4.1] Invertase (sucrase) converts sucrose into glucose and fructose, to make the soft centres of chocolates and other sweets.
[bio13-s4.4.2] Household link: biological washing powders contain proteases, lipases and amylases that digest protein stains (blood, egg, grass), fat and grease, and starch.
[bio13-s4.4.3] Biological washing powders work at low washing temperatures (e.g. 30–40 °C), saving energy. They are not used at very high temperatures, where the enzymes would be denatured.
[bio13-s4.5.b] Biofuels are fuels made from living material. Enzymes are needed to turn the carbohydrates and oils in plants into forms that yeast or chemistry can convert into fuel.
[bio13-s4.5.0] Bioethanol from starch crops: amylases break the starch of crops such as maize and wheat into glucose, which yeast ferments to ethanol. The ethanol is blended with petrol as a fuel.
[bio13-s4.5.1] Bioethanol from cellulose (a second-generation biofuel): cellulases break down the cellulose in straw, wood chips and other crop waste into glucose for fermentation.
[bio13-s4.5.2] Cellulose is tightly packed and hard to digest, so these enzymes are slow and costly and are an active area of research. There is more on cellulose in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s4.5.3] Biodiesel: lipases can catalyse the conversion of vegetable oils and waste cooking oils into biodiesel. This is an alternative to chemical catalysts that works at lower temperatures and leaves less waste.
[bio13-s4.6.b] Biofuels are often described as green, but the picture is more complicated. Exams ask you to weigh the benefits against the costs.
[bio13-s4.6.0] For: biofuels are renewable, because the crops can be regrown.
[bio13-s4.6.1] For: the carbon dioxide released when they burn was taken in by the crop during photosynthesis, so they add less to atmospheric CO₂ than fossil fuels.
[bio13-s4.6.2] For: using waste materials (straw, waste oil) adds value to waste.
[bio13-s4.6.3] Against: growing fuel crops uses land that could grow food, which can raise food prices.
[bio13-s4.6.4] Against: clearing forests for fuel crops destroys habitats and releases carbon.
[bio13-s4.6.5] Against: fertilisers, machinery and processing use fossil-fuel energy, so the saving is smaller than it first appears.
[bio13-s4.6.6] Cellulose-based and waste-based biofuels avoid most of the competition with food, because they use parts of the plant or waste that people do not eat. Carbon cycle and climate detail is in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio13-s4.7.b] Because enzymes are so specific, they are used in medicine to detect substances, break down harmful ones, and replace enzymes that the body fails to supply.
[bio13-s4.7.0] Diagnosis: glucose oxidase is used in blood-glucose test strips and electronic biosensors used by people with diabetes, and in urine test strips.
[bio13-s4.7.1] Glucose oxidase is specific to glucose, so other sugars do not interfere. The reaction produces a colour change or an electrical signal in proportion to the amount of glucose.
[bio13-s4.7.2] Dissolving blood clots: clot-dissolving enzymes such as streptokinase (from bacteria) and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) are given soon after a heart attack or a stroke caused by a clot, to break down the clot and restore blood flow.
[bio13-s4.7.3] Enzyme replacement: people with cystic fibrosis often cannot release enough digestive enzymes from the pancreas, so they take capsules of pancreatic enzymes (lipase, protease and amylase) with meals.
[bio13-s4.7.4] Lactase tablets help people with lactose intolerance, because they supply the missing enzyme.
[bio13-s4.7.5] Cancer treatment: the enzyme asparaginase is used to treat a type of childhood leukaemia (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia).
[bio13-s4.7.6] The leukaemia cells cannot make enough of the amino acid asparagine and take it from the blood. The enzyme breaks down asparagine in the blood, starving the cancer cells.
[bio13-s4.7.7] Medical technologies built on enzymes (named only here): restriction enzymes and DNA ligase in genetic engineering (see “Genetic engineering”); Taq polymerase in PCR for DNA profiling and diagnostic testing (see “Generating a DNA profile”).
[bio13-s4.8.b] Making drugs chemically can be wasteful and use harsh conditions. Enzymes can make the same drugs more precisely and more cleanly.
[bio13-s4.8.0] Making antibiotics: immobilised penicillin acylase converts natural penicillin, made by the Penicillium mould, into a starting molecule (6-APA) from which semi-synthetic penicillins, such as amoxicillin, are made.
[bio13-s4.8.1] Semi-synthetic penicillins can treat infections that the natural form does not.
[bio13-s4.8.2] Making single-form drugs: many drug molecules exist in two mirror-image forms, of which only one works (the other may be useless or harmful).
[bio13-s4.8.3] Because enzymes are specific, they make only the wanted form, avoiding wasteful separation. For example, an engineered enzyme (a transaminase) is used to manufacture the diabetes drug sitagliptin.
[bio13-s4.8.4] Enzymes allow drug manufacture at mild temperatures with less toxic solvent waste than chemical methods (“green chemistry”). Human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria depends on enzymes used to insert the gene (see “Genetic engineering”).
[bio13-s4.9.b] Free enzymes mix into the product and are lost after one use. Fixing the enzyme in place solves this, and it is a central idea in industrial enzyme use.
[bio13-s4.9.0] An immobilised enzyme is an enzyme (or a whole cell containing it) fixed to, or trapped in, an inert, insoluble material. It is held in place while the substrate solution passes over or through it, so it does not mix freely with the product.
[bio13-s4.9.1] Entrapment: the enzyme is trapped in a gel bead, such as calcium alginate.
[bio13-s4.9.2] Adsorption: the enzyme is stuck to the surface of an insoluble support, such as glass beads, resin or charcoal.
[bio13-s4.9.3] Covalent bonding: the enzyme is chemically bonded to a support.
[bio13-s4.9.4] Enclosure: the enzyme is enclosed behind a partially permeable membrane.
[bio13-s4.9.5] Examples: glucose isomerase for high-fructose syrup; lactase for lactose-free milk; penicillin acylase for semi-synthetic penicillins.
[bio13-s4.10.b] Immobilisation brings clear benefits in cost and purity, but it also adds complications. Exams ask for both sides.
[bio13-s4.10.0] Advantage: the enzyme can be recovered and reused many times, cutting costs.
[bio13-s4.10.1] Advantage: the product is not contaminated with enzyme, so it needs no purification.
[bio13-s4.10.2] Advantage: the enzyme can be packed in a column for a continuous-flow process, with substrate fed in at the top and product collected at the bottom.
[bio13-s4.10.3] Advantage: immobilised enzymes are often more stable, being less easily denatured by changes in temperature or pH. The reaction can be stopped at once by removing the enzyme.
[bio13-s4.10.4] Disadvantage: immobilising costs money and equipment to set up, and some activity is lost, as part of the active sites (where the substrate binds) may be blocked or altered.
[bio13-s4.10.5] Disadvantage: the reaction can be slower, because the substrate must diffuse into the bead to reach the enzyme. Enzyme may also leak out of the support.
[bio13-s4.10.6] Disadvantage: immobilised enzymes are unsuitable where the substrate molecules are too large to enter the beads.
[bio13-s4.11.b] This is the school method for immobilising an enzyme. Yeast is used because its cells contain the enzyme sucrase (also called invertase), which acts on sucrose.
[bio13-s4.11.0] 1. Mix sodium alginate solution with a suspension of yeast (which contains the enzyme sucrase, also called invertase) or with an enzyme solution.
[bio13-s4.11.1] 2. Draw the mixture into a syringe and drop it, one drop at a time, into calcium chloride solution: each drop forms a bead of insoluble calcium alginate gel trapping the yeast.
[bio13-s4.11.2] 3. Leave the beads to harden for about 15–20 minutes.
[bio13-s4.11.3] 4. Sieve them out and rinse them with distilled water to remove the calcium chloride and any free yeast.
[bio13-s4.11.4] 5. Place the beads in a separating funnel (or a syringe barrel with a tap) as a column.
[bio13-s4.11.5] Safety: calcium chloride irritates the eyes, so wear safety glasses and wash spills.
[bio13-s4.11.6] Diagram: (a) a syringe dripping the alginate–yeast mixture into a beaker of calcium chloride solution, with beads forming, labelled; (b) a separating funnel or column packed with beads, with sucrose solution added at the top, a tap, and product (glucose and fructose) collected below, labelled.
[bio13-s4.12.b] Once the column is made, sucrose solution is passed through it and the liquid that comes out is tested. A control and a comparison with free yeast show that the beads do the work and that immobilisation helps.
[bio13-s4.12.0] Using the beads: pour sucrose solution through the column and collect the liquid that drips out. Test it with a glucose test strip.
[bio13-s4.12.1] A positive glucose result shows that the sucrose has been converted to glucose and fructose. The sucrose solution tested before it enters the column gives a negative result.
[bio13-s4.12.2] Control: beads made without yeast (alginate only). Their product gives no glucose, so the conversion is due to the yeast and not the beads.
[bio13-s4.12.3] The product from the beads is clear, while sucrose mixed with free yeast becomes cloudy with yeast cells that must be removed, showing the advantage of immobilisation. The beads can be rinsed and used again.
[bio13-s4.13.b] Enzymes and cells work best when conditions are right. A bioreactor is the vessel that holds those conditions steady in industry.
[bio13-s4.13.0] A bioreactor is a vessel in which cells or enzymes carry out a reaction under controlled conditions: temperature, pH, oxygen (or its absence), nutrient supply, mixing and sterility are monitored and kept at the values that suit the enzyme.
[bio13-s4.13.1] A column of immobilised enzyme is a simple bioreactor. There is more on growing microorganisms in bioreactors, and on batch and continuous culture, in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio13-s4.14.b] The uses above fall into three themes that link enzymes to health, sustainability and technology. Exams may ask you to draw these together.
[bio13-s4.14.0] Health: diagnostic strips and biosensors, clot-busting and replacement enzymes, and lactose-free foods.
[bio13-s4.14.1] Sustainability: lower energy use and less chemical waste; low-temperature washing; biofuels and the arguments about them; more juice from the same fruit.
[bio13-s4.14.2] Technology: genetic modification of microorganisms to make enzymes, engineered enzymes, immobilisation and continuous-flow bioreactors.

CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.

CANDIDATES bio7-s4: Investigation: plant and animal cells
[bio7-s4.0.b] This investigation compares plant cells with animal cells. You gather first-hand (primary) data with a light microscope, then add second-hand (secondary) data from scanning electron microscope images. A fair comparison needs a clear aim, a prediction and tight control of variables.
[bio7-s4.0.0] Aim: to observe and compare the structures of plant cells (onion epidermis, the thin outer skin layer) and animal cells (human cheek cells) using a light microscope, and to use scanning electron microscope (SEM) images to see structures the light microscope cannot show.
[bio7-s4.0.1] Hypothesis (a testable prediction): plant cells will show a cell wall and a large vacuole, which animal cells lack. Both will show a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus.
[bio7-s4.0.2] Independent variable (the one thing you change): the type of cell, plant or animal.
[bio7-s4.0.3] Dependent variable (what you observe or measure): the structures observed, whether present or absent, and their shape and arrangement.
[bio7-s4.0.4] Controlled variables (kept the same so the comparison is fair): the same microscope and total magnification (×100 and ×400), the same light setting, a fresh thin specimen one cell layer thick, and the same volume of stain and staining time.
[bio7-s4.0.5] Control: an unstained wet mount (specimen in a drop of water) of each cell type, viewed in the same way. It shows what the stain adds, meaning which structures become visible, and that the structures seen are in the cells and not produced by the stain.
[bio7-s4.1.b] You need a short list of apparatus, and several of the items carry real hazards: sharp blades, stains, thin glass and body cells. Good practice is part of the method, so know the precaution for each hazard.
[bio7-s4.1.0] Equipment: light microscope; clean glass slides and coverslips; onion; scalpel or knife and white tile; forceps; mounted needle; dropper (pipette); filter paper or paper towel; iodine solution; methylene blue stain; sterile cotton buds; beaker of disinfectant for used buds; distilled water; gloves and safety glasses; pencil and paper for drawings.
[bio7-s4.1.1] Use only your own cheek cells, with a new sterile cotton bud. Put used buds, and slides after use, into disinfectant to prevent spreading infection, and wash hands afterwards.
[bio7-s4.1.2] Iodine solution and methylene blue stain skin and clothes and can irritate eyes, so wear gloves and safety glasses and wipe up spills.
[bio7-s4.1.3] Cut away from your fingers on a white tile with the scalpel, and carry scalpels pointing down so the blade cannot injure anyone if you trip.
[bio7-s4.1.4] Hold slides and coverslips by their edges. Coverslips are very thin and break easily, so dispose of broken glass in the glass bin, not the ordinary bin, where it could cut someone.
[bio7-s4.1.5] Keep water away from the microscope's electric lamp and cable to avoid electric shock. Never point a microscope mirror at direct sunlight, as this can damage the eye.
[bio7-s4.2.b] Onion epidermis peels off in a layer only one cell thick, so light passes through it and the cells can be seen clearly. The steps below prepare a stained slide and an unstained control. There is more on lowering the coverslip and focusing in Microscopy.
[bio7-s4.2.0] 1. Place a few drops of water in the centre of a clean slide.
[bio7-s4.2.1] 2. Cut an onion into quarters and remove one fleshy scale leaf.
[bio7-s4.2.2] 3. Snap the scale leaf and use forceps to peel the thin, transparent epidermis from its inner (concave) surface.
[bio7-s4.2.3] 4. Cut a piece about 1 cm square and lay it flat in the water, unfolding it with the mounted needle so there are no folds or overlaps.
[bio7-s4.2.4] 5. Add one or two drops of iodine solution and leave it for a few minutes to stain.
[bio7-s4.2.5] 6. Lower a coverslip at 45° with the mounted needle, which avoids trapping air bubbles, then blot excess liquid from the edges with filter paper.
[bio7-s4.2.6] 7. Prepare an unstained slide the same way, using water only, as the control.
[bio7-s4.2.7] 8. Examine under low power (×100), then high power (×400). Make a labelled pencil drawing of a few cells and record the total magnification.
[bio7-s4.3.b] Cheek cells are collected by scraping the lining of your mouth, then stained so they show up. Because they come from a person, hygiene steps are built into the method. There is also an alternative for schools that do not use cheek cells.
[bio7-s4.3.0] 1. Gently rub the inside of your own cheek with a clean, sterile cotton bud.
[bio7-s4.3.1] 2. Smear the bud across the centre of a clean slide in a thin layer.
[bio7-s4.3.2] 3. Immediately put the used bud into the beaker of disinfectant to prevent spreading infection.
[bio7-s4.3.3] 4. Add one or two drops of methylene blue and leave for one to two minutes.
[bio7-s4.3.4] 5. Lower a coverslip at 45° and blot the excess with filter paper.
[bio7-s4.3.5] 6. Prepare an unstained slide with a drop of water, as the control.
[bio7-s4.3.6] 7. Examine under low power, then high power. Look for flat, separate cells, avoiding clumps, which are hard to interpret. Make a labelled pencil drawing and record the magnification.
[bio7-s4.3.7] Alternative where a school does not use cheek cells: a prepared (commercial) slide of human cheek cells or another animal tissue, examined in the same way.
[bio7-s4.4.b] Onion bulb cells have no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), so this optional extension uses a leaf instead.
[bio7-s4.4.0] Mount a single leaf of a moss or of Elodea (Canadian pondweed) in a drop of water, without stain. These leaves are only one or two cells thick, so light passes through them.
[bio7-s4.4.1] The green chloroplasts are visible without any stain, which onion bulb cells do not have.
[bio7-s4.5.b] The stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm stand out, and the shape of the cells tells you whether they are plant or animal. You should be able to describe what each slide looks like and explain why.
[bio7-s4.5.0] Onion epidermis: cells are regular, brick-shaped or rectangular, and fitted closely together in rows. Each cell is outlined by a clear, rigid cell wall.
[bio7-s4.5.1] In onion cells the nucleus is stained yellow-brown by iodine and is often seen near the edge of the cell. The cytoplasm stains pale yellow and forms a thin layer pushed to the edge by a large central vacuole (a fluid-filled sac), which looks clear.
[bio7-s4.5.2] Onion cells have no chloroplasts, because the bulb grows underground and does not photosynthesise.
[bio7-s4.5.3] Cheek cells: cells are flat and irregular in shape, rounded or many-sided, lying singly or in small overlapping groups. Each has a cell membrane but no cell wall.
[bio7-s4.5.4] In cheek cells the nucleus is small, central and stained dark blue, and the cytoplasm stains pale blue. There is no large vacuole and no chloroplasts.
[bio7-s4.5.5] Unstained controls: the cells are almost transparent. The onion cell walls are faintly visible but the nucleus is hard to see, and the cheek cells are barely visible. So stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm visible by adding contrast.
[bio7-s4.5.6] Structures the light microscope cannot show: ribosomes, the internal detail of mitochondria, and nuclear pores. Mitochondria are too small to see clearly at ×400.
[bio7-s4.5.7] Diagram: pencil drawings of (i) a few onion epidermis cells labelled cell wall, cell membrane (pressed against the wall), cytoplasm, vacuole and nucleus, and (ii) two or three cheek cells labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus, each with the total magnification (e.g. ×400) written beside it.
[bio7-s4.6.b] Secondary data is information collected by someone else, here scanning electron microscope (SEM) images. They reveal detail the light microscope cannot, but they have limits of their own. You also need to be able to work out real cell size from an image.
[bio7-s4.6.0] Sources: published SEM images (micrographs) of onion epidermis, of cheek (epithelial) cells, and of other cells, from textbooks or reputable scientific image libraries. Each has a scale bar or magnification. Record the source of each image so others can check it.
[bio7-s4.6.1] What SEM images add: a three-dimensional view of the cell surface, at much higher magnification and resolution (the ability to separate fine detail) than the light microscope.
[bio7-s4.6.2] For example, SEM images show the regular, box-like shape of the onion cells and the outlines of their walls, and the flat, thin, overlapping shape of cheek cells and the texture of their surfaces.
[bio7-s4.6.3] Limits of SEM images: they show surfaces only, not internal organelles, which need TEM (transmission electron microscope) images. They show dead, metal-coated cells, so preparation can create artefacts (features that are not really in the living cell). Any colour is false colour added afterwards.
[bio7-s4.6.4] Measuring size from an image: actual size = image size ÷ magnification. Measure the image with a ruler in mm and convert to µm (1 mm = 1000 µm).
[bio7-s4.6.5] Worked example: an onion cell measures 40 mm long on an image magnified ×200. 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2 mm = 200 µm.
[bio7-s4.6.6] With a scale bar, measure the bar, then calculate: actual size = (image size of the cell ÷ image length of the bar) × the length the bar represents.
[bio7-s4.6.7] Comparing the data: the light microscope shows living-cell features in colour (with stain) and at low cost. The SEM confirms the shapes and surfaces in 3D at much greater detail. Using both gives a fuller and more reliable picture than either alone.
[bio7-s4.7.b] Your results can now be compared with the hypothesis. The differences between the two cell types fit what each cell does, so you should be able to link structure to function.
[bio7-s4.7.0] Both cell types have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus, so both are eukaryotic (their DNA is enclosed in a nucleus).
[bio7-s4.7.1] The onion cells have a cell wall, which gives their fixed, regular shape and support, and a large vacuole, which keeps the cell turgid (firm and full of water). The cheek cells have neither, so they have a flexible, irregular shape. This supports the hypothesis.
[bio7-s4.7.2] The cheek cells are lining (epithelial) cells: flat and thin, forming a protective layer that is continually rubbed off and replaced, which is why they come away easily.
[bio7-s4.7.3] The onion epidermis is a protective outer layer of tightly fitting cells with strong walls.
[bio7-s4.7.4] The onion has no chloroplasts because the bulb is a food store, not a photosynthetic organ.
[bio7-s4.8.b] Slide preparation is fiddly, and small mistakes give misleading images. For each problem, know what you would see and how to fix it.
[bio7-s4.8.0] Air bubbles (black-edged circles) can be mistaken for cells or can hide them. Lower the coverslip at 45°.
[bio7-s4.8.1] Folded or overlapping tissue, or a thick smear, makes cells hard to see because light cannot pass through. Use a flat piece of epidermis one cell layer thick, and a thin smear.
[bio7-s4.8.2] Too much stain darkens everything and hides detail; too little gives poor contrast. Use one or two drops and blot the excess.
[bio7-s4.8.3] The specimen drying out under the lamp distorts the cells. Add a drop of water at the edge of the coverslip.
[bio7-s4.8.4] Poor focus or wrong light settings: focus with the fine focus at high power and adjust the diaphragm (which controls how much light reaches the specimen).
[bio7-s4.8.5] Drawings depend on the observer, so compare them with other students' slides and with the SEM images. Examine several cells and several fields of view, not just one, so your conclusion does not rest on a single unusual cell.

CANDIDATES bio8-s7: Investigation: food tests
[bio8-s7.0.b] Food tests are a fair, planned investigation, not just a set of recipes. Before testing anything you decide what you are trying to find out, what you predict, and what you will change, measure and keep the same.
[bio8-s7.0.0] Aim: to find out which nutrients (starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid) are present in a range of food samples. The investigation is qualitative, which means you record whether a nutrient is present or absent, from a colour change or other observation, not how much of it there is.
[bio8-s7.0.1] Hypothesis, e.g.: “Potato contains starch but not lipid; milk contains reducing sugar (lactose), protein and lipid.” Students predict the result for each food from what the food is, so the results can later support or contradict the prediction.
[bio8-s7.0.2] Independent variable (the one you change): the food sample tested. Dependent variable (the one you observe): the result of each test, the colour or observation, recorded as present or absent.
[bio8-s7.0.3] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the volume of food solution and of reagent, the concentration of the reagents, the water-bath temperature and heating time for Benedict's, and clean apparatus for every test to avoid carry-over from one sample to the next.
[bio8-s7.0.4] Every test needs two controls. A negative control uses distilled water in place of the food and shows the colour when the nutrient is absent.
[bio8-s7.0.5] A positive control uses a known pure sample, such as starch solution, glucose solution, egg-white (albumen) solution or vegetable oil, and shows the colour when the nutrient is present.
[bio8-s7.0.6] Results for the foods are judged against these controls, so you can tell a real colour change from the reagent's starting colour, and know the test itself is working.
[bio8-s7.1.b] Choose foods that you expect to be rich in each nutrient, and gather the equipment and reagents (the chemicals that react with a nutrient to give a visible result) before you start.
[bio8-s7.1.0] Example food samples: potato, bread, rice or flour (starch); glucose, honey, grapes, fruit juice and milk (reducing sugar); egg white, milk, meat or cheese (protein); butter, vegetable oil, nuts, crisps and cheese (lipid); and table sugar, which gives a negative Benedict's result.
[bio8-s7.1.1] Equipment: test tubes and rack, test-tube holder, droppers, graduated pipettes or syringes (to measure equal volumes), spotting tile, mortar and pestle or knife and chopping board, beaker of distilled water, filter funnel and filter paper.
[bio8-s7.1.2] Also needed: a hot water bath (a beaker of near-boiling water from a kettle, or an electric water bath) with a thermometer, brown paper, labels, safety glasses and gloves.
[bio8-s7.1.3] Reagents: iodine solution (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide), Benedict's solution, biuret reagent (or sodium hydroxide solution and dilute copper sulfate solution) and Sudan III stain.
[bio8-s7.2.b] Most tests work on a liquid, so solid foods must first be turned into a food solution. This preparation is common to all the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.0] 1. Crush or grind a solid food with a little distilled water using a mortar and pestle. Crushing breaks open the cells so the nutrients can mix into the water.
[bio8-s7.2.1] 2. Add more water, stir, and filter or let the solid settle, then use the liquid food solution for the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.2] 3. Test liquid foods such as milk and juice directly, or dilute them.
[bio8-s7.2.3] 4. Label each tube with the food and the test, so the results cannot be mixed up.
[bio8-s7.3.b] Starch is the carbohydrate plants store, and iodine solution is the quickest way to detect it. It needs no heating and gives a result at once.
[bio8-s7.3.0] Method, step 1: place a few drops of food solution, or a small piece of solid food, on a white spotting tile, or place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. The white tile makes the colour easy to see.
[bio8-s7.3.1] Step 2: add a few drops of iodine solution. Step 3: observe the colour at once. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.3.2] Step 4: repeat with water as the negative control and starch solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.3.3] Result: iodine solution is yellow-brown (orange-brown) at the start. It turns blue-black if starch is present and stays yellow-brown if starch is absent.
[bio8-s7.4.b] Some sugars can react chemically with Benedict's solution and others cannot. This test detects the ones that can, and it is the one food test that needs heat.
[bio8-s7.4.0] A reducing sugar is one that can reduce (give electrons to) the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution. Glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose and lactose are reducing sugars; sucrose is not.
[bio8-s7.4.1] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add an equal volume, 2 cm³, of Benedict's solution and mix; the mixture is blue.
[bio8-s7.4.2] Step 3: place the tube in a hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) for about 5 minutes. Never heat the tube directly in a Bunsen flame, because the liquid can spurt out.
[bio8-s7.4.3] Step 4: observe the colour. Step 5: run water as the negative control and glucose solution as the positive control, in the same water bath at the same time so the conditions are identical.
[bio8-s7.4.4] Result: if a reducing sugar is present, the blue solution turns green, then yellow, then orange, and finally a brick-red precipitate (solid particles) forms. It stays blue if no reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.5] The reducing sugar reduces blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, the brick-red precipitate. The colour reached gives a rough idea of how much sugar is present: green for a little, brick red for a lot. There is more on this in Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively.
[bio8-s7.4.6] Fehling's test is an alternative: equal volumes of Fehling's solutions A and B are mixed with the sample and heated in a water bath. A blue mixture turns to a brick-red precipitate if a reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.7] Extension: sucrose stays blue. To show it is made of reducing sugars, boil a sucrose solution with a few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid to hydrolyse it to glucose and fructose. Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate until fizzing stops, then do the Benedict's test: it now turns brick red.
[bio8-s7.5.b] Biuret detects the bonds that join amino acids together in a protein. Like iodine, it works at room temperature with no heating.
[bio8-s7.5.0] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add 2 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution and mix (or add biuret reagent, which already contains it).
[bio8-s7.5.1] Step 3: add a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution, one drop at a time, shaking gently. Step 4: leave for a few minutes and observe. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.5.2] Step 5: run water as the negative control and egg-white solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.5.3] Result: the blue colour turns purple (violet or lilac) if protein is present, and stays blue if protein is absent. The copper ions react with the peptide bonds (the links between amino acids) in the protein, so the test detects peptide bonds, not single amino acids.
[bio8-s7.6.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do not dissolve in water, and two simple tests use this. They are alternatives, so you can use either one.
[bio8-s7.6.0] Grease-spot (brown paper) test, method: rub a small amount of food on a piece of brown paper, or place a drop of liquid food on it. Place a drop of water on another part of the paper as a control.
[bio8-s7.6.1] Leave the paper to dry, or warm it gently away from flames, then hold it up to the light.
[bio8-s7.6.2] Grease-spot result: a translucent (see-through), greasy spot that does not disappear when the paper dries shows that lipid is present. The water spot also looks translucent at first but disappears as it dries, which is why the paper must dry before the result is judged.
[bio8-s7.6.3] Sudan III test, method: place 2 cm³ of food solution, or a little crushed food in 2 cm³ of water, in a test tube. Add a few drops of Sudan III, a red dye that dissolves in lipid but not in water.
[bio8-s7.6.4] Shake gently and leave to stand for a minute. Run water as the negative control and vegetable oil in water as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.6.5] Sudan III result: if lipid is present, a red-stained oily layer forms on top of the water, because lipid is less dense than water and does not mix with it. With no lipid, the dye spreads through the water or settles as red specks, with no red oily layer.
[bio8-s7.7.b] Several reagents here are corrosive, harmful or flammable, and the Benedict's test uses near-boiling water. Safe practice is part of any investigation.
[bio8-s7.7.0] Wear safety glasses throughout. Sodium hydroxide (biuret test) is corrosive and especially harmful to the eyes. Benedict's and copper sulfate solutions are harmful if swallowed or splashed. Wash any splash off the skin with plenty of water.
[bio8-s7.7.1] Heat Benedict's tests only in a water bath, never directly over a flame, because the liquid can boil suddenly and spurt out of the tube. Use a test-tube holder, point the tube away from people, and take care with near-boiling water.
[bio8-s7.7.2] Sudan III stain is usually dissolved in ethanol, an alcohol that is highly flammable, so keep it away from flames. Do not warm brown paper near a flame.
[bio8-s7.7.3] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes; wear gloves if possible.
[bio8-s7.7.4] Never taste or eat food in the laboratory. Be aware of food allergies (e.g. to nuts) among the class. Clean up spills and wash hands after the practical.
[bio8-s7.8.b] Your observations are the primary data (results you collected yourself). A clear table lets you compare every food with the controls and draw a conclusion from the evidence.
[bio8-s7.8.0] Record primary data in a results table: one row per food and one column per test, recording the colour observed and whether the nutrient is present or absent (or + or −). Include the control rows.
[bio8-s7.8.1] Expected results: potato shows starch; glucose, honey and grapes show reducing sugar; table sugar is Benedict's negative, as sucrose is non-reducing; egg white shows protein; vegetable oil and butter show lipid; milk shows reducing sugar, protein and lipid; distilled water is negative for all tests.
[bio8-s7.8.2] Conclusion: state which nutrients each food contains, supported by the results compared with the controls, and say whether this supports the hypothesis. The tests show presence only, not the amount.
[bio8-s7.9.b] Food tests are simple, so small mistakes easily give a wrong result. Knowing the common errors, and how to reduce them, makes the conclusion more reliable.
[bio8-s7.9.0] Colour judgements are subjective, so different people may see different shades. Compare each tube with the controls against a white background, and have two people check the result.
[bio8-s7.9.1] Coloured foods, such as dark fruit juice, can mask a colour change. Dilute or filter them, or use a control of the food without the reagent.
[bio8-s7.9.2] Contamination: reusing a dropper or test tube can carry a nutrient from one sample to another, giving a false positive.
[bio8-s7.9.3] A very small amount of nutrient may not give a visible change, which is a false negative. Some nutrients are inside cells, so the food must be well crushed.
[bio8-s7.9.4] Benedict's detects reducing sugars only, so a food rich in sucrose can give a negative result even though it contains sugar.
[bio8-s7.9.5] Repeat each test, e.g. three times, to check that the results are repeatable.
