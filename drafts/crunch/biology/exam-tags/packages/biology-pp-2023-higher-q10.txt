CANDIDATES bio29-s1: Investigation: the heart
[bio29-s1.0.b] The heart is a muscular pump that never stops, and its structure is built around one job: moving blood. It is divided into four chambers, two upper and two lower, split into a left and a right side by a central wall.
[bio29-s1.0.0] Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax (the chest) between the two lungs and behind the sternum (breastbone), with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left.
[bio29-s1.0.1] The heart is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers. The fluid reduces friction as the heart beats, and the membrane prevents the heart from over-stretching.
[bio29-s1.0.2] Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
[bio29-s1.0.3] Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries (the vessels that carry blood away from the heart), so their walls are thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.0.4] The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance. The right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels).
[bio29-s1.0.5] Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.
[bio29-s1.0.6] In other words, the heart is two separate pumps side by side: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.b] Blood has to arrive at the heart and leave it by separate routes. Veins bring blood in to the atria, and arteries carry it out from the ventricles.
[bio29-s1.1.0] Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it towards it; this is not about oxygen, which is why one artery and one set of veins break the usual pattern below.
[bio29-s1.1.1] The venae cavae bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium: the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms, and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body.
[bio29-s1.1.2] The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
[bio29-s1.1.3] Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs.
[bio29-s1.1.4] The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.5] The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself with blood. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.2.b] Blood pressure inside the heart changes sharply as the muscle squeezes, and blood would simply flow back where it came from without help. Valves are flaps of tissue that keep blood flowing in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.2.0] Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. A reminder is tRIcuspid on the RIght.
[bio29-s1.2.1] AV valves let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
[bio29-s1.2.2] The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (heart strings), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
[bio29-s1.2.3] Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets.
[bio29-s1.2.4] Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
[bio29-s1.3.b] The heart wall is made of a special muscle found nowhere else in the body. Its features suit it to beating without rest for a whole lifetime.
[bio29-s1.3.0] Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped) and branched, and are joined end to end by intercalated discs.
[bio29-s1.3.1] Intercalated discs let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell, so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit.
[bio29-s1.3.2] Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue).
[bio29-s1.3.3] Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. There is more on the pacemaker that starts each beat in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.3.4] Diagram: a vertical section through the heart viewed from the front, so that the heart's left side is on the right of the page. Label the right and left atria, the right and left ventricles (left wall thicker), the septum, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves, the chordae tendineae, the papillary muscles, the semilunar valves, the superior and inferior venae cavae, the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary veins and the aorta. Add arrows for the direction of blood flow, and shade or label the oxygenated (left) and deoxygenated (right) sides.
[bio29-s1.3.5] Diagram: cardiac muscle as seen under the microscope, from a prepared slide or micrograph, showing branched, striated cells with a central nucleus and intercalated discs. Label the striations, nucleus, intercalated disc and branch.
[bio29-s1.4.b] A dissection is an investigation, so it needs an aim, hypotheses to test, controlled variables and both kinds of data. The heart you cut open is a model for the human heart.
[bio29-s1.4.0] Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection, and to relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs and images of the human heart).
[bio29-s1.4.1] Hypothesis to test: the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body.
[bio29-s1.4.2] Hypothesis to test: arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it.
[bio29-s1.4.3] Hypothesis to test: the valves allow blood to flow in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.4.4] Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): the independent variable (the one you change) is the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle or atrium); the dependent variable (the one you measure) is the wall thickness in mm.
[bio29-s1.4.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): measure at the same level, about halfway between the base and the apex, with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall.
[bio29-s1.4.6] Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean. Pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
[bio29-s1.4.7] Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle; published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart, because mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
[bio29-s1.4.8] Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep's, pig's or ox's heart from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long; dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; a camera or phone to record observations.
[bio29-s1.5.b] Start with protection and with the outside of the heart. Identifying the front surface and the vessels first tells you which side is which before you cut anything.
[bio29-s1.5.0] 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
[bio29-s1.5.1] 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it, and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart's left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
[bio29-s1.5.2] 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top), the ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top.
[bio29-s1.5.3] Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae and pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see, and photograph it.
[bio29-s1.5.4] 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters.
[bio29-s1.5.5] The vena cava leads to the right atrium; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the pulmonary veins lead to the left atrium; the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
[bio29-s1.5.6] Butchers often trim the vessels short, so use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
[bio29-s1.6.b] Now cut the heart open to look at the chambers, valves and wall thickness. Work on the right side first, then the left, then compare the walls in cross-section.
[bio29-s1.6.0] 5. Open the right side. Using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers.
[bio29-s1.6.1] Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles. Pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap. 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
[bio29-s1.6.2] 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve. Look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
[bio29-s1.6.3] 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing that the valves allow one-way flow.
[bio29-s1.6.4] Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
[bio29-s1.6.5] 9. Cut a transverse section (straight across) across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them.
[bio29-s1.6.6] Measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler, following the variables and repeats above. Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
[bio29-s1.6.7] 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope, or a micrograph, and draw it. 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings. Dispose of the heart and clean up as described under Safety and ethics.
[bio29-s1.7.b] A dissection uses sharp blades and raw animal tissue, so each hazard has a specific precaution. There is also an ethical side to using animal material.
[bio29-s1.7.0] Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person. Hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade, carry sharp instruments pointing down, and report any cut at once.
[bio29-s1.7.1] Raw meat can carry bacteria, such as Salmonella, that cause food poisoning. Wear gloves, do not touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
[bio29-s1.7.2] Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection. Wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher; it is not to be left in an open bin.
[bio29-s1.7.3] Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or when pouring water through the vessels.
[bio29-s1.7.4] Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food, a by-product of the food industry, so that no animal is killed for the dissection, and treat the tissue with respect.
[bio29-s1.7.5] A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos as secondary data, in line with the 3Rs of animal use. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio29-s1.8.b] Your results come from two sources: primary data, which you collect yourself from the dissected heart, and secondary data, which comes from diagrams, models and micrographs. Together they support the conclusions.
[bio29-s1.8.0] Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right, typically several times thicker in measurements; the septum thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.8.1] Valves: the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s1.8.2] Vessels: the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface, with their openings just above the aortic valve.
[bio29-s1.8.3] Results table to record: the structure; where it is found; its description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); the measured wall thickness in mm, with three readings and the mean; and its function.
[bio29-s1.8.4] Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels. Differences seen are due to the butcher's trimming, which may remove the atria or vessels, or to species size.
[bio29-s1.8.5] Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.
[bio29-s1.9.b] The conclusion links each structure you found to its function, and states whether each hypothesis was supported by the evidence.
[bio29-s1.9.0] The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
[bio29-s1.9.1] Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
[bio29-s1.9.2] The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
[bio29-s1.9.3] The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
[bio29-s1.9.4] Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls. There is more on this in Blood vessels.
[bio29-s1.9.5] For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.
[bio29-s1.10.b] A dissected animal heart is not a living human heart, and the measurements are rough. Knowing the limits shows how far the conclusions can be trusted and how to improve them.
[bio29-s1.10.0] Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
[bio29-s1.10.1] Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
[bio29-s1.10.2] Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
[bio29-s1.10.3] A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
[bio29-s1.10.4] One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio33-s2: Investigation: growth of microorganisms
[bio33-s2.0.b] Before testing what helps or harms microorganisms, you need to know what counts as growth and how to put a number on it. Most investigations in this subsection measure growth in one of the ways below; the antibacterial test measures the width of a clear zone instead.
[bio33-s2.0.0] Growth of a microbial population means an increase in the number of cells (bacteria and yeasts dividing) or in the size of the mycelium (the mass of thread-like filaments that makes up a mould), not the growth of one cell.
[bio33-s2.0.1] A colony is a visible mass of cells on a solid medium (a jelly-like surface the cells grow on), grown by repeated division from a single cell or spore. Counting colonies therefore estimates how many living cells were on the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.2] Bacterial colonies are usually small, round, shiny or matt spots; mould colonies are fuzzy and spread outwards. This difference in appearance helps you tell which organism has grown.
[bio33-s2.0.3] On plates, count the colonies on each plate if they are separate, or measure the diameter of a mould colony with a ruler under the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.4] Alternatively, estimate the percentage of the plate's surface covered, using a grid of squares drawn on the base. This works when colonies have merged and cannot be counted.
[bio33-s2.0.5] In liquid broth cultures, measure the cloudiness (turbidity) with a colorimeter, an instrument that measures how much light passes through. Cloudier broth has more cells and absorbs more light.
[bio33-s2.0.6] For yeast, count cells with a microscope and a counting chamber (a slide marked with a grid of known volume), or measure the CO₂ produced.
[bio33-s2.0.7] Microorganisms need a source of nutrients (for energy and raw materials), water, a suitable temperature and pH and, for most, oxygen. Each of these is one factor tested later in this subsection.
[bio33-s2.0.8] Harmful chemicals and high external solute concentrations (a lot of dissolved salt or sugar outside the cells) inhibit growth, so these are tested too.
[bio33-s2.1.b] Microorganisms are everywhere: in the air, on skin and on benches. Aseptic technique is how you grow only the organism you chose, without letting it or any contaminant spread.
[bio33-s2.1.0] Aseptic technique is the set of procedures that keep unwanted microorganisms out of cultures, so that only the intended organism grows and results are valid.
[bio33-s2.1.1] Aseptic technique also keeps the cultured microorganisms from escaping onto people and surfaces, for safety.
[bio33-s2.1.2] Any culture may contain or become contaminated with harmful microorganisms, so every culture is treated as a possible pathogen (a microorganism that causes disease).
[bio33-s2.1.3] Before starting: tie back long hair, cover cuts with a waterproof plaster, wash hands with soap and water, and wipe the bench with disinfectant. Each step reduces the microorganisms that could get into the culture or onto you.
[bio33-s2.1.4] Do not eat, drink or touch the face during the practical, because this could carry cultured microorganisms into your mouth or eyes.
[bio33-s2.1.5] Sterilise all equipment and media before use: nutrient agar, broths and glassware are heated in an autoclave (or pressure cooker) at 121 °C for 15 minutes, which kills all microorganisms, including bacterial endospores (tough resting cells that survive ordinary heat).
[bio33-s2.1.6] Plastic Petri dishes (the shallow lidded dishes that hold the agar) are bought pre-sterilised and sealed, because plastic would melt in an autoclave.
[bio33-s2.2.b] Once equipment is sterile, the risk is airborne microorganisms and anything that touches the culture. These bench habits deal with both.
[bio33-s2.2.0] Inoculating loop (a wire loop used to transfer microorganisms): heat it in the hottest part of a Bunsen flame until it glows red-hot, before and after every use, to kill any microorganisms on it.
[bio33-s2.2.1] Let the loop cool for a few seconds, without putting it down, before touching the culture, so that the heat does not kill the microorganisms being transferred.
[bio33-s2.2.2] Work close to a lit Bunsen burner: the rising hot air carries airborne microorganisms upwards and away from the open plates and bottles.
[bio33-s2.2.3] When opening a culture bottle or tube, hold the cap in the little finger and never put it down on the bench, because the bench could contaminate the inside of the cap.
[bio33-s2.2.4] Pass the neck of the bottle briefly through the flame after opening and before closing it, to kill microorganisms at the mouth and create an upward air current.
[bio33-s2.2.5] Open a Petri dish lid only slightly, tilted up like a clam shell, and for as short a time as possible, so that airborne microorganisms do not land on the agar.
[bio33-s2.3.b] After inoculating (putting the microorganism onto the plate), the plate must be closed, labelled, incubated (kept warm to let growth happen) and finally disposed of safely. Each rule here has a reason.
[bio33-s2.3.0] After inoculating, secure the lid to the base with two or four short strips of sticky tape, not sealed all the way round, so that oxygen can still enter. Sealing the lid fully would make conditions anaerobic (without oxygen), which would favour the growth of harmful anaerobic bacteria.
[bio33-s2.3.1] Label the base, not the lid (which could be swapped), around the edge with name, date and treatment.
[bio33-s2.3.2] Incubate plates upside down, lid at the bottom, so that condensation forming on the lid does not drip onto the agar and spread the colonies, and the agar does not dry out.
[bio33-s2.3.3] Incubate school cultures at about 25 °C, never at 37 °C, because body temperature favours the growth of microorganisms that are pathogenic to humans.
[bio33-s2.3.4] Never open plates after incubation: examine and count colonies through the closed lid. Never culture samples from the human body, toilets or animal waste, because these are likely to contain pathogens.
[bio33-s2.3.5] Disposal: put used plates and cultures into an autoclave bag and autoclave them (121 °C, 15 minutes) before putting them in the bin, or soak them in strong disinfectant. Disinfect the bench again and wash hands thoroughly at the end.
[bio33-s2.3.6] Spills: cover with paper towels soaked in disinfectant, leave for the time stated on the disinfectant, then clear up wearing gloves, and tell the teacher.
[bio33-s2.3.7] Ethanol, used to sterilise a glass spreader, is highly flammable: keep the beaker of ethanol away from the Bunsen flame. Pass the spreader through the flame only briefly, to burn off the ethanol, holding it pointing down.
[bio33-s2.4.b] A culture medium is the food and water the microorganisms grow on or in. Choosing the right one, and a safe organism, is the first step of every investigation.
[bio33-s2.4.0] A culture medium supplies microorganisms with nutrients and water. Nutrient agar contains agar, a jelly extracted from seaweed, which sets solid and which most microorganisms cannot digest, so the surface stays firm while they grow.
[bio33-s2.4.1] Nutrient agar also contains nutrients such as meat or yeast extract and peptone (partly digested protein), giving amino acids, sugars, vitamins and minerals.
[bio33-s2.4.2] Nutrient broth is the same as nutrient agar without the agar, so it is liquid.
[bio33-s2.4.3] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds grow better on malt agar or potato dextrose agar, which are sugar-rich and slightly acidic.
[bio33-s2.4.4] Suitable microorganisms are baker's yeast suspended in sterile water or sugar solution, moulds such as Rhizopus from bread, or non-pathogenic bacterial cultures supplied for schools.
[bio33-s2.4.5] The source of the microorganism must be known, so the risk is low.
[bio33-s2.5.b] A poured plate is a layer of sterile agar in a Petri dish. How you then put the microorganisms on it depends on what you want to see: separate colonies or an even covering.
[bio33-s2.5.0] Pouring a plate, step 1: melt the sterile agar in its bottle in a boiling water bath.
[bio33-s2.5.1] Step 2: cool the agar to about 50 °C, which is still liquid and cool enough not to kill an organism added to it (agar sets as it cools below about 40 °C).
[bio33-s2.5.2] Step 3: flame the neck of the bottle, to kill microorganisms at the mouth.
[bio33-s2.5.3] Step 4: lift the Petri dish lid slightly and pour in enough agar to cover the base (about 15–20 cm³).
[bio33-s2.5.4] Step 5: replace the lid, swirl gently and leave on a level surface to set.
[bio33-s2.5.5] Streaking: draw the flamed, cooled loop, dipped in the culture, across the agar in a zigzag. This thins the cells out along the line, so they form separate colonies.
[bio33-s2.5.6] Spreading: spread a set volume (e.g. 0.1 cm³) of a culture or diluted suspension evenly with a sterile glass spreader to make a lawn (growth covering the whole plate). A lawn is needed for the antibacterial test. There is more on this in the antibacterial and antifungal investigation below.
[bio33-s2.5.7] Diagram: a Petri dish after inoculation, shown upside down in the incubator. Labels: base labelled with name, date and treatment, lid held with two short strips of tape. Beside it, a flamed inoculating loop held in a Bunsen flame.
[bio33-s2.6.b] All six factor investigations below follow one plan: change one thing, keep everything else the same, and compare against controls. Learn this plan once and you can design any of them.
[bio33-s2.6.0] Aim: to investigate how a named factor affects the growth of a named microorganism. Each investigation changes one factor, the independent variable, and measures growth after a fixed time; the dependent variable is colony count, colony diameter, percentage cover or turbidity.
[bio33-s2.6.1] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the same microorganism, culture and volume of inoculum (the amount of culture added) on every plate; the same medium and volume of agar; and the same incubation time.
[bio33-s2.6.2] Also controlled, except when it is the factor tested: the same temperature, pH, nutrient level and water availability, and the same method of measuring.
[bio33-s2.6.3] Uninoculated control: an uninoculated plate (sterile medium only) is incubated with the others. No growth on it shows that the medium and technique were sterile, so any growth on the test plates came from the inoculum.
[bio33-s2.6.4] A reference plate with normal conditions (e.g. normal pH, no added salt, or a water disc in the antibacterial test) is compared with the tested conditions, so you can tell what the factor changed.
[bio33-s2.6.5] Replicates: set up at least three plates for each value of the factor and calculate the mean, so that one contaminated or unusual plate does not mislead. Worked example: colony counts of 42, 38 and 46 at 25 °C give a mean of 126 ÷ 3 = 42 colonies.
[bio33-s2.6.6] Record results in a table: value of the factor, growth on each replicate, and the mean. Plot a bar chart for categories or a line graph for a continuous factor, with mean growth on the y-axis.
[bio33-s2.6.7] Conclusion: describe the trend in the mean results and say whether they support the hypothesis. Explain the results using the biology given for each factor, and compare them with secondary data.
[bio33-s2.7.b] Temperature changes how fast the chemical reactions inside cells run. This investigation compares growth at several temperatures, and tests high temperatures by heating the culture before plating.
[bio33-s2.7.0] Hypothesis: “Growth increases with temperature up to an optimum, then decreases.” The optimum is the temperature at which growth is fastest.
[bio33-s2.7.1] Independent variable: temperature, i.e. about 4 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C and 25 °C, plus 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C for the heated cultures.
[bio33-s2.7.2] Step 1: inoculate identical plates with the same volume of the same culture, e.g. yeast.
[bio33-s2.7.3] Step 2: incubate sets of three at about 4 °C (refrigerator), 15 °C, 20 °C (room) and 25 °C (incubator) for the same time, e.g. 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.7.4] Step 3: count the colonies or measure colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.7.5] Step 4: to test high temperatures safely, heat tubes of the culture in water baths at 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C (or boil one) for 10 minutes before plating them. Heating the culture first avoids incubating dangerously warm plates.
[bio33-s2.7.6] Step 5: incubate all the plates at 25 °C, so that the only difference between them is the heat treatment.
[bio33-s2.8.b] The results fit a pattern: slow growth when cold, faster when warmer, and death when too hot. The explanation comes from enzymes, the proteins that control the reactions in cells.
[bio33-s2.8.0] Expected result: little or no growth at 4 °C. Growth increases with temperature over the range tested. Cultures heated to about 60 °C or more grow poorly or not at all.
[bio33-s2.8.1] Explanation: growth depends on enzyme-controlled reactions, and at low temperatures molecules move slowly, so reactions and growth are slow.
[bio33-s2.8.2] Cold does not kill the cells: growth resumes when they are warmed, which is why refrigerated food still goes off slowly and frozen food starts to go off when thawed.
[bio33-s2.8.3] Above the optimum, enzymes and other proteins are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working), so cells die. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio33-s2.8.4] Bacterial endospores survive higher temperatures than active cells, so heating that kills the active cells may leave endospores alive.
[bio33-s2.9.b] pH measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is (7 is neutral). This investigation grows bacteria on agar made up at different pH values to find the range they prefer.
[bio33-s2.9.0] Hypothesis: “Bacteria grow best at about neutral pH and poorly in strongly acidic or alkaline conditions.”
[bio33-s2.9.1] Independent variable: the pH of the agar or broth, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11.
[bio33-s2.9.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar (or broth) at different pH values, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11, by adding buffer solutions (which hold the pH steady) or dilute acid or alkali, checked with a pH meter, before pouring.
[bio33-s2.9.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of culture.
[bio33-s2.9.4] Step 3: incubate all at the same temperature for the same time.
[bio33-s2.9.5] Step 4: measure growth.
[bio33-s2.10.b] Bacteria and fungi prefer different pH ranges, and the reason lies in the proteins that run the cell. The same effect is used to preserve food.
[bio33-s2.10.0] Expected result: most bacteria grow best near neutral pH (about pH 6–8) and little in strongly acidic conditions.
[bio33-s2.10.1] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds tolerate, and often grow best in, slightly acidic conditions (about pH 5–6). Repeating the investigation with a fungus and a bacterium shows this difference.
[bio33-s2.10.2] Explanation: pH outside the optimum changes the charges on enzymes and other proteins, so their shape changes and they work slowly or are denatured.
[bio33-s2.10.3] Acid conditions are used to preserve food, e.g. pickling in vinegar, because few microorganisms grow well at low pH.
[bio33-s2.10.4] Yogurt keeps longer because the lactic acid made by its bacteria lowers the pH.
[bio33-s2.11.b] Nutrients are the food a microorganism uses for energy and to build new cells. This investigation compares media with and without nutrients, or with increasing amounts of sugar.
[bio33-s2.11.0] Hypothesis: “Microorganisms grow more when more nutrients are available, until another factor limits growth.”
[bio33-s2.11.1] Independent variable: the nutrient content of the medium, e.g. plain agar compared with nutrient agar, or glucose at 0%, 1%, 2% and 5% for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.2] Step 1: compare plain agar (agar and water only, no nutrients) with nutrient agar; or use broths (or agar) with increasing concentrations of glucose, e.g. 0%, 1%, 2%, 5%, for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of yeast culture.
[bio33-s2.11.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together at the same temperature.
[bio33-s2.11.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or percentage cover.
[bio33-s2.12.b] More food means more growth, but only up to a point. The results also show a link forward to the solute investigation, because very high sugar is harmful.
[bio33-s2.12.0] Expected result: little or no growth on plain agar, because there is nothing for the cells to use.
[bio33-s2.12.1] Growth increases with nutrient concentration and then levels off, as another factor, such as space or the build-up of wastes, becomes limiting (the one that stops growth rising further).
[bio33-s2.12.2] At very high sugar concentrations, growth falls again. There is more on this in Investigation: effect of external solute concentration.
[bio33-s2.12.3] Explanation: nutrients supply the energy, released by respiration of sugars, and the raw materials for new cells, i.e. sugars, amino acids, minerals such as nitrate and phosphate, and vitamins.
[bio33-s2.12.4] Variation: starch agar shows that only microorganisms that secrete amylase (the enzyme that digests starch) can use starch.
[bio33-s2.12.5] Flooding the plate with iodine after incubation leaves a clear zone around such colonies, where the starch has been digested. The rest of the plate turns dark because iodine stains starch.
[bio33-s2.13.b] This investigation uses bread and mould instead of agar plates. It tests whether mould needs moisture by comparing dried bread with moistened bread.
[bio33-s2.13.0] Hypothesis: “Moulds grow only on food that is moist.”
[bio33-s2.13.1] Independent variable: the water content of the bread, dried compared with moistened.
[bio33-s2.13.2] Step 1: take slices of the same bread (without preservatives, which would inhibit growth); dry some fully in an oven at a low temperature and moisten others with the same volume of sterile water.
[bio33-s2.13.3] Step 2: expose each slice to the air of the same room for the same time, e.g. 30 minutes, so that mould spores land on it.
[bio33-s2.13.4] Step 3: seal each in a clear plastic bag or sealed dish and label it.
[bio33-s2.13.5] Step 4: keep all in the same warm, dark place, about 25 °C, for 5–7 days.
[bio33-s2.13.6] Step 5: estimate the percentage cover of mould on each, using a transparent grid of squares held over the bag, e.g. 37 of 100 squares covered = 37%.
[bio33-s2.13.7] Do not open the bags, because the spores released could be breathed in.
[bio33-s2.14.b] Water is essential for life, and microorganisms are no exception. The result explains why drying is an old way of keeping food.
[bio33-s2.14.0] Expected result: mould grows on the moist bread and not, or very little, on the dried bread.
[bio33-s2.14.1] Explanation: microorganisms need water because their enzymes work only in solution, nutrients are absorbed in solution after extracellular digestion (digestion outside the cell), and water is needed for cells to grow and divide.
[bio33-s2.14.2] Dried foods, e.g. dried fruit, pasta, powdered milk and cereals, keep for a long time for this reason.
[bio33-s2.14.3] Safety: mould spores can trigger allergies and asthma, so keep the bags sealed throughout and dispose of them unopened.
[bio33-s2.15.b] A solute is something dissolved in water, such as salt or sugar. This investigation adds increasing amounts of solute to the agar to see what happens to growth.
[bio33-s2.15.0] Hypothesis: “Growth decreases as the concentration of salt (or sugar) outside the cells increases.”
[bio33-s2.15.1] Independent variable: the concentration of sodium chloride (salt), e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10% or 20%, or of sucrose (sugar).
[bio33-s2.15.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar containing increasing concentrations of sodium chloride (e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%) or of sucrose.
[bio33-s2.15.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of bacterial culture.
[bio33-s2.15.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together for the same time.
[bio33-s2.15.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.16.b] Salt and sugar slow growth because of what they do to water movement across the cell surface. This is the basis of salting and sugaring food.
[bio33-s2.16.0] Expected result: growth decreases as the solute concentration rises, with little or no growth at the highest concentrations.
[bio33-s2.16.1] Explanation: when the solution outside a cell has a higher solute concentration, and so lower water concentration, than the cytoplasm, water leaves the cell by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio33-s2.16.2] The cell then loses water, its enzymes cannot work, and it stops growing or dies.
[bio33-s2.16.3] In a very dilute solution water enters the cell, but the bacterial or fungal cell wall stops the cell bursting.
[bio33-s2.16.4] This is the basis of preserving food with salt (salted fish and bacon) and sugar (jam). There is more on this in Osmosis in food preservation and plant health.
[bio33-s2.17.b] Some chemicals stop microorganisms growing. Knowing which type acts on which group, and what the investigation sets out to show, comes before the method.
[bio33-s2.17.0] Antibacterial chemicals kill bacteria or stop them growing: antibiotics (e.g. penicillin, streptomycin), antiseptics (used on living tissue, e.g. on skin) and disinfectants (used on surfaces and objects).
[bio33-s2.17.1] Antifungal chemicals, e.g. the antifungal creams used for athlete's foot, or fungicides, act on fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.2] Antibiotics do not affect fungi, and antifungals generally do not affect bacteria, because the two groups have different structures. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.3] Hypothesis: “Different antiseptics (or antibiotics) inhibit the growth of a bacterium to different extents.”
[bio33-s2.17.4] Independent variable: the antibacterial or antifungal chemical on each disc; dependent variable: the diameter of the clear zone around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.b] In the disc diffusion method, paper discs soaked in chemicals are placed on a lawn of bacteria. Wherever the chemical stops growth, a clear zone appears around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.0] Step 1: spread a set volume of the culture evenly over a nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader, to make a lawn.
[bio33-s2.18.1] Step 2: using sterile forceps (flamed and cooled), dip paper discs of the same size (e.g. 6 mm, cut with a hole punch and sterilised) in each chemical for the same time, drain them, and place them on the lawn, well spaced.
[bio33-s2.18.2] Step 3: include a control disc dipped in sterile distilled water. Ready-made antibiotic discs, or a multi-disc with several antibiotics on one ring, can be used instead.
[bio33-s2.18.3] Step 4: tape and label the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.4] Step 5: incubate upside down at about 25 °C for 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.18.5] Step 6: measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc with a ruler on the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.6] For an antifungal chemical, make a lawn of yeast on malt agar and use discs of the antifungal, with a water control and, to show the difference in antibacterial and antifungal action, an antibiotic disc.
[bio33-s2.18.7] Diagram: a lawn plate with four discs. Labels: each disc's chemical, clear zones of inhibition of different sizes, and no zone around the water control.
[bio33-s2.19.b] The clear zones are the result. Their size tells you how well each chemical works, and the way they form explains why.
[bio33-s2.19.0] Expected result: a clear zone, the zone of inhibition, forms around discs whose chemical stops the bacterium growing.
[bio33-s2.19.1] There is no clear zone around the water control, which shows that the disc and the water alone do not stop growth.
[bio33-s2.19.2] The larger the zone, the more effective the chemical is against that organism at that concentration. In other words, the bacterium is more sensitive to it.
[bio33-s2.19.3] On the yeast lawn, the antifungal gives a clear zone and the antibiotic gives none.
[bio33-s2.19.4] The chemical diffuses outwards from the disc into the agar, so its concentration falls with distance; bacteria cannot grow where the concentration is high enough to kill or inhibit them.
[bio33-s2.19.5] A zone's size also depends on how fast the chemical diffuses, so it is a guide to strength, not an exact measure.
[bio33-s2.19.6] A bacterium that grows right up to an antibiotic disc is resistant to that antibiotic.
[bio33-s2.19.7] In hospitals the same test is used to find which antibiotic will work against the bacterium from a patient. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s2.20.b] Comparing zone areas, not just diameters, is fairer. Here is the calculation for a clear zone of diameter 18 mm around a 6 mm disc.
[bio33-s2.20.0] Radius = 9 mm; area of the zone = π r² = 3.14 × 9 × 9 ≈ 254 mm².
[bio33-s2.20.1] Subtracting the disc (radius 3 mm, area ≈ 28 mm²) gives a clear area of about 226 mm², which is the area where bacteria were actually stopped from growing.
[bio33-s2.20.2] Areas let zones be compared fairly, because doubling the diameter quadruples the area.
[bio33-s2.21.b] Your own results are primary data. A good conclusion also checks them against data collected by other people.
[bio33-s2.21.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, e.g. other class groups, textbooks, published research or food-safety guidance.
[bio33-s2.21.1] Conclusions must be supported by both primary and secondary data: compare your own results with the class's pooled results and with published findings.
[bio33-s2.21.2] Published findings include that most human pathogens grow best near body temperature and neutral pH, and that most bacteria grow little below refrigerator temperature.
[bio33-s2.22.b] Each factor you investigated is used to keep food safe. Preserving food means stopping microorganisms growing, either by killing them or by making conditions unsuitable.
[bio33-s2.22.0] Temperature: refrigeration and freezing slow or stop growth but do not kill microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.1] Heat: pasteurisation, heating milk briefly to a temperature below boiling and then cooling it quickly, kills most microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.2] Sterilisation: canning and ultra-heat treatment (UHT) heat food enough to sterilise it, and sealing keeps microorganisms out.
[bio33-s2.22.3] Water: drying removes water, which microorganisms need for their enzymes to work.
[bio33-s2.22.4] Solute: salting and sugaring use high solute concentration, which draws water out of microbial cells.
[bio33-s2.22.5] pH: pickling uses low pH.
[bio33-s2.23.b] The remaining factors give further preservation methods and wider uses in hygiene and in industry.
[bio33-s2.23.0] Chemicals: chemical preservatives inhibit growth.
[bio33-s2.23.1] Oxygen: vacuum packing removes oxygen, slowing aerobic microorganisms (those that need oxygen to grow).
[bio33-s2.23.2] Health and hygiene: antiseptics on wounds and skin, disinfectants on surfaces, and aseptic technique in surgery. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s2.23.3] Industry: bioreactors (vessels in which microorganisms are grown on a large scale) are kept at the optimum conditions found by such investigations to maximise growth.
[bio33-s2.24.b] Every fault below can make results unreliable. Each has a simple fix, so learn each error together with its fix.
[bio33-s2.24.0] Contamination by airborne or skin microorganisms gives colonies of a different appearance; check with the uninoculated control, and repeat with better aseptic technique.
[bio33-s2.24.1] Unequal inoculum, i.e. different volumes or numbers of cells, on different plates; use a measured volume from a well-mixed culture.
[bio33-s2.24.2] Colonies that grow together cannot be counted; dilute the culture so that colonies are separate, or measure percentage cover instead.
[bio33-s2.24.3] Agar or broth that is not at the intended pH or concentration; check with a pH meter and measure accurately.
[bio33-s2.24.4] Temperature not constant, e.g. the “room temperature” plates are near a radiator.
[bio33-s2.24.5] Discs not equally soaked, or chemicals not of known concentration; soak all discs for the same time.
[bio33-s2.24.6] Too few replicates; use at least three and calculate means.

CANDIDATES bio7-s2: Microscopy
[bio7-s2.0.b] Microscopes do two different jobs: they make things look bigger, and they let you see fine detail. Students often mix these up, but only the second one limits what a light microscope can show.
[bio7-s2.0.0] Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the real object, so a ×100 image is 100 times the real size.
[bio7-s2.0.1] Resolution (also called resolving power) is the ability to show two points that are close together as separate points. It decides how much detail can be seen.
[bio7-s2.0.2] Magnifying an image beyond the resolution limit only makes it bigger and blurrier, not more detailed, because the two close points have already merged into one blur.
[bio7-s2.0.3] Total magnification of a light microscope = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification. For example, a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.
[bio7-s2.1.b] The light (optical) microscope passes light through a thin specimen and uses glass lenses to enlarge the image. These parts form the image and bring it into focus.
[bio7-s2.1.0] Eyepiece (ocular) lens: the lens you look through. It magnifies the image from the objective, usually ×10.
[bio7-s2.1.1] Objective lenses: mounted on a revolving nosepiece so they can be swapped. They are usually ×4 (scanning), ×10 (low power) and ×40 (high power), sometimes ×100 (oil immersion). They produce the first magnified image.
[bio7-s2.1.2] Stage with clips: the platform that holds the slide in place over the hole through which light passes.
[bio7-s2.1.3] Coarse focus knob: moves the stage (or tube) a long way quickly, so it is used for focusing at low power only.
[bio7-s2.1.4] Fine focus knob: moves the stage or tube a small distance to give a sharp image. It is the only focus used at high power.
[bio7-s2.2.b] Light has to reach the specimen in the right amount and direction, and the instrument needs a stable frame. These parts do that.
[bio7-s2.2.0] Light source: a built-in lamp, or a mirror that reflects light up through the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.1] Condenser: a lens under the stage that focuses light onto the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.2] Diaphragm (iris): adjusts the amount of light reaching the specimen. Less light often improves contrast for pale, unstained cells, which are nearly transparent and look washed out in bright light.
[bio7-s2.2.3] Arm and base: support the microscope. The arm is used for carrying it.
[bio7-s2.2.4] Diagram: a light microscope labelled eyepiece, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source (or mirror), coarse focus knob, fine focus knob, arm and base.
[bio7-s2.3.b] The method is built to protect the slide and the lenses. You always start on the lowest power, which has the widest view and the most room between lens and slide.
[bio7-s2.3.0] 1. Carry the microscope with one hand on the arm and the other under the base, so its weight is supported.
[bio7-s2.3.1] 2. Switch on the light and turn the lowest-power objective into place.
[bio7-s2.3.2] 3. Clip the slide on the stage with the specimen over the hole, so light can pass through it.
[bio7-s2.3.3] 4. Looking from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide without touching it. Watching from the side stops you grinding the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.3.4] 5. Looking through the eyepiece, focus by moving the objective and slide apart until the image is clear.
[bio7-s2.3.5] 6. Use the fine focus to sharpen the image, and the diaphragm to adjust the light.
[bio7-s2.3.6] 7. Move the part to be studied to the centre of the field of view (the circle of specimen you can see), so it stays in view when you increase magnification.
[bio7-s2.3.7] 8. Turn the next objective into place and use only the fine focus.
[bio7-s2.3.8] 9. Record the total magnification with every drawing.
[bio7-s2.4.b] The image behaves in predictable ways as you change objective, and one safety rule matters for your eyes.
[bio7-s2.4.0] The image is inverted, meaning upside down and reversed left to right. This means moving the slide to the left moves the image to the right.
[bio7-s2.4.1] At higher magnification the field of view is smaller and the image is dimmer, so more light is needed.
[bio7-s2.4.2] At high power the objective is very close to the slide, which is why the coarse focus must not be used: it could crash the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.4.3] Never use a mirror to reflect direct sunlight into the microscope, as it can damage the eye.
[bio7-s2.5.b] To view a specimen with light passing through it, you mount it on a glass slide. The technique below is the general method that any slide uses.
[bio7-s2.5.0] A wet mount: the specimen is placed in a drop of water or stain on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip (a thin square of glass).
[bio7-s2.5.1] The specimen must be very thin, ideally one layer of cells thick, so that light can pass through it and the cells do not overlap.
[bio7-s2.5.2] Stains add contrast to transparent structures by colouring some parts more than others. Iodine solution is used for plant cells: it stains the nucleus yellow-brown and turns starch blue-black. Methylene blue is used for animal cells: it stains the nucleus dark blue.
[bio7-s2.5.3] Lowering the coverslip: touch one edge of the coverslip to the liquid at an angle of about 45° and lower it slowly with a mounted needle. This pushes air out ahead of it and avoids air bubbles, which appear as black-edged circles and can hide cells.
[bio7-s2.5.4] The coverslip keeps the specimen flat, stops it drying out, and protects the objective lens from the liquid. Excess liquid is removed by touching filter paper or a paper towel to the edge.
[bio7-s2.5.5] Stain can also be added after the coverslip is on. Put a drop at one edge and draw it under the coverslip with filter paper held at the opposite edge (irrigation). There is more on the full procedures in Investigation: plant and animal cells.
[bio7-s2.6.b] A light microscope cannot resolve anything much smaller than 0.2 µm. Electron microscopes get round this by using electrons instead of light, so they show far finer detail.
[bio7-s2.6.0] An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by electromagnets instead of glass lenses. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, so resolution is far better.
[bio7-s2.6.1] The specimen is in a vacuum, because air would scatter the electrons, so it must be dead. The image is viewed on a screen and recorded digitally. It is black and white, and colour is added afterwards (false colour).
[bio7-s2.6.2] Transmission electron microscope (TEM): electrons pass through an extremely thin, stained section of a specimen. It gives a flat (2D) image of internal structure at the highest magnifications, e.g. mitochondria, ribosomes, nuclear pores and viruses.
[bio7-s2.6.3] Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a beam of electrons scans across the surface of a specimen coated with a very thin layer of metal, e.g. gold. Electrons bouncing off it form an image. It gives a 3D image of surfaces, e.g. the surface of cells, pollen grains and insects.
[bio7-s2.6.4] The SEM has lower resolution than the TEM and shows no internal structure unless the specimen is broken open.
[bio7-s2.6.5] The first electron microscope was built in Germany in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska.
[bio7-s2.7.b] The two types differ in how they work, what they can resolve and what they demand of the specimen. Compare them point by point.
[bio7-s2.7.0] Radiation: light for the light microscope, electrons for the electron microscope. Lenses: glass for the light microscope, electromagnets for the electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.1] Maximum useful magnification: about ×1000–1500 for the light microscope, compared with hundreds of thousands of times or more for electron microscopes.
[bio7-s2.7.2] Resolution: about 0.2 µm (200 nm) for the light microscope, compared with less than 1 nm for the TEM, roughly a thousand times better.
[bio7-s2.7.3] So the light microscope shows the nucleus, cell wall, vacuole and chloroplasts. Ribosomes, nuclear pores, the inner structure of mitochondria and viruses are too small and need an electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.4] Specimens: the light microscope can view living or dead specimens, with colour visible. The electron microscope needs dead specimens in a vacuum, and its images are black and white.
[bio7-s2.7.5] Preparation and cost: light microscopes are simple, quick, cheap and portable. Electron microscopes need complex preparation (thin sectioning, metal coating) that can create artefacts, structures that are not really in the living cell. They are very expensive, large, and need trained operators.
[bio7-s2.8.b] Microscopy has a long history and is still developing. Newer techniques tag, scan or freeze specimens to reveal what older methods could not.
[bio7-s2.8.0] Robert Hooke described and named “cells” from thin slices of cork in his book Micrographia (1665).
[bio7-s2.8.1] Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using his own single-lens microscopes, was the first to see and describe bacteria and other single-celled organisms, in the 1670s.
[bio7-s2.8.2] Fluorescence microscopy: specific molecules are tagged with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins that glow when lit with a particular colour of light. This shows exactly where a protein is in a living cell.
[bio7-s2.8.3] Green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a jellyfish, earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.
[bio7-s2.8.4] Confocal laser scanning microscopy: a laser scans one thin plane of the specimen at a time, giving sharp images that a computer combines into a 3D model.
[bio7-s2.8.5] Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy overcomes the light microscope’s normal resolution limit. It earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William Moerner.
[bio7-s2.8.6] Cryo-electron microscopy: samples are flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice instead of being dried and stained, so molecules keep their natural shape. It shows the structure of proteins and viruses in near-atomic detail.
[bio7-s2.8.7] Cryo-electron microscopy earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson.
[bio7-s2.8.8] Cryo-electron microscopy was used to show the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which helped vaccine design.
[bio7-s2.9.b] Microscopes are everyday tools in medicine, research and forensics, and modern imaging adds automation.
[bio7-s2.9.0] Uses in health and technology: examining blood smears and biopsies to diagnose disease, including spotting cancer cells; identifying pathogens; research into how cells and viruses work; and forensic science, e.g. comparing hairs and fibres.
[bio7-s2.9.1] Digital cameras and computer image analysis now allow images to be measured, shared and analysed automatically.
