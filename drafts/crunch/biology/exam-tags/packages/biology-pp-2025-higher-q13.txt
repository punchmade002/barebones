CANDIDATES bio13-s1: Enzymes as catalysts
[bio13-s1.0.b] Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions, and left alone most of them would be far too slow to keep a cell alive. Catalysts speed reactions up, and enzymes are the catalysts that living things make for themselves.
[bio13-s1.0.0] A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by it, so it can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.0.1] A catalyst does not change which products form or how much product is made in the end; it only makes the reaction reach that point faster.
[bio13-s1.0.2] An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a catalyst made by a living cell.
[bio13-s1.0.3] Almost all enzymes are globular proteins (proteins folded into a compact, rounded ball), folded into a precise three-dimensional shape. That shape decides what the enzyme can do. There is more on protein structure in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s1.0.4] Because enzymes are proteins, they are made at ribosomes (the tiny structures in a cell where proteins are built), following the instructions in a gene (the length of DNA that codes for the protein).
[bio13-s1.1.b] Because an enzyme is a protein that is reused rather than consumed, it has a set of properties that follow from that.
[bio13-s1.1.0] Enzymes are needed only in small amounts, because each molecule is reused many times; catalase, among the fastest, breaks down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second.
[bio13-s1.1.1] Enzymes are unchanged at the end of the reaction, which is why the same molecule can start work on another substrate straight away.
[bio13-s1.1.2] Enzymes are specific: each acts on one reaction or one type of reaction, because only a substrate of the right shape fits the enzyme's active site (the region where the substrate binds).
[bio13-s1.1.3] Enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH, and enzymes are denatured (their shape is changed so that they stop working) by heat and by extremes of pH. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio13-s1.2.b] Enzyme names usually give away the job the enzyme does. Enzymes also work in different places, depending on whether their job is inside or outside the cell.
[bio13-s1.2.0] Most enzyme names end in -ase, and many are named after their substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on), so the name tells you the job: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins, sucrase on sucrose.
[bio13-s1.2.1] Some older names, such as pepsin and trypsin, don't follow this rule, so don't assume that every enzyme name ends in -ase.
[bio13-s1.2.2] Most enzymes work inside the cell that made them (intracellular), for example the enzymes of respiration (releasing energy from food) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and of photosynthesis (making food using light) in chloroplasts.
[bio13-s1.2.3] Some enzymes are secreted to work outside the cell (extracellular), for example digestive enzymes released into the gut and the enzymes that fungi and bacteria release onto their food. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio13-s1.3.b] Even a reaction that releases energy overall needs a push to get started. An enzyme works by making that push smaller, not by adding energy.
[bio13-s1.3.0] Activation energy is the minimum energy that reacting molecules need before a reaction can start: the energy needed to break or strain existing bonds.
[bio13-s1.3.1] Think of activation energy as an energy hill that must be climbed even when the reaction releases energy overall.
[bio13-s1.3.2] Without a catalyst, most reactions of metabolism (the chemical reactions of life) have such a high activation energy that, at body temperature (37 °C), they would happen far too slowly to keep a cell alive.
[bio13-s1.3.3] Heating would supply the energy, but high temperatures would damage cells and their proteins, so heating is not an option for living things.
[bio13-s1.3.4] An enzyme provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at the temperature of the cell, and the reaction is many times faster.
[bio13-s1.3.5] The overall energy change of the reaction (energy released or taken in) stays the same; only the hill to be climbed gets lower.
[bio13-s1.3.6] Diagram: energy-profile graph, energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis. Reactants high on the left, products lower on the right.
[bio13-s1.3.7] Draw two humps, a tall one labelled without enzyme and a lower one labelled with enzyme. Mark each activation energy with a vertical arrow from the reactants to the top of its hump.
[bio13-s1.4.b] Every enzyme reaction has two partners: the enzyme and the molecule it works on. One small region of the enzyme does the work, and its shape is what makes the enzyme selective.
[bio13-s1.4.0] The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on; the products are the molecules made.
[bio13-s1.4.1] Example: catalase acts on its substrate hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
[bio13-s1.4.2] The active site is a small region of the enzyme, a pocket or groove on its surface, where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place.
[bio13-s1.4.3] The active site's shape, and the chemical charges in it, are complementary to the substrate, meaning the two fit together like matching pieces.
[bio13-s1.4.4] The active site is a part of the enzyme, not of the substrate. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site, not the same as it.
[bio13-s1.5.b] Once the substrate meets the active site, the reaction follows three steps. Together they make the active-site (lock-and-key) model of enzyme action.
[bio13-s1.5.0] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex (the enzyme and substrate joined together).
[bio13-s1.5.1] Enzyme and substrate molecules meet by random movement, so anything that increases collisions (more substrate, more enzyme, higher temperature) increases the rate, up to a point.
[bio13-s1.5.2] 2. While the substrate is held in the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, for example by straining the bonds that must break, or by holding two substrate molecules close together in the right position to react.
[bio13-s1.5.3] The substrate is then converted to products, forming an enzyme–product complex.
[bio13-s1.5.4] 3. The products have a different shape, no longer fit the active site, and are released.
[bio13-s1.5.5] The active site is then free and unchanged, ready to bind another substrate molecule, which is why one enzyme molecule can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.5.6] In summary: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products.
[bio13-s1.5.7] Diagram: three stages in a row: enzyme with active site and complementary substrate approaching; enzyme–substrate complex; unchanged enzyme with two products leaving. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex and products. A matching diagram for a joining reaction is optional.
[bio13-s1.6.b] The same active-site mechanism works whichever way a reaction runs. Metabolism has reactions that break molecules down and reactions that build them up, and enzymes catalyse both.
[bio13-s1.6.0] In catabolic reactions one substrate is split into smaller products, for example amylase splits starch into maltose.
[bio13-s1.6.1] In anabolic reactions two or more substrates are joined into a larger product. For example, DNA polymerase joins nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA) into a new DNA strand when DNA is copied.
[bio13-s1.6.2] Another anabolic example is ATP synthase, which joins ADP and phosphate into ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy.
[bio13-s1.7.b] A cell contains thousands of different molecules but does not react them all at random. Enzymes are selective: each one picks out its own substrate, and that selectivity comes from the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s1.7.0] Specificity means that each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with the complementary shape fits its active site.
[bio13-s1.7.1] This is what selective catalysts means: amylase digests starch but not protein or fat; pepsin digests protein but not starch.
[bio13-s1.7.2] The lock-and-key model is the simplest explanation: the substrate (the key) fits only the active site (the lock) of the right shape.
[bio13-s1.7.3] The lock-and-key model was later refined to the induced-fit model, in which the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio13-s1.7.4] Because enzymes are specific, a cell needs thousands of different enzymes, one for each of its reactions, and it can control a reaction by making, or not making, the one enzyme that catalyses it.
[bio13-s1.7.5] Anything that changes the shape of the active site stops the substrate fitting, so the enzyme stops working: for example high temperature or extreme pH (denaturation).
[bio13-s1.7.6] A change in the enzyme's amino-acid sequence (the order of the amino acids that make up the protein), caused by a mutation in its gene, also changes the active site and stops the substrate fitting.
[bio13-s1.8.b] Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in a living thing. Enzymes make these reactions possible, and they usually work in sequences called pathways.
[bio13-s1.8.0] Every reaction of metabolism is catalysed by its own specific enzyme.
[bio13-s1.8.1] Without enzymes, the reactions that keep cells alive (respiration, photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA copying, digestion) would be far too slow at the temperatures and pH inside living things.
[bio13-s1.8.2] Most processes are metabolic pathways: a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme, in which the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next.
[bio13-s1.8.3] Respiration and photosynthesis are such pathways. There is more on this in Respiration and Photosynthesis.
[bio13-s1.8.4] Diagram: a pathway written as A → B → C → D, with enzyme 1, enzyme 2 and enzyme 3 written over the arrows.
[bio13-s1.9.b] Because every step in a pathway needs its own enzyme, a cell controls its chemistry by controlling its enzymes.
[bio13-s1.9.0] A cell controls which reactions happen, and how fast, by switching on or off the genes for particular enzymes and by activating or inhibiting enzymes already made.
[bio13-s1.9.1] If one enzyme in a pathway is missing, the pathway stops at that step: its substrate builds up and the later products are not made.
[bio13-s1.9.2] Many enzymes need a non-protein helper to work: a mineral ion (for example magnesium for many enzymes that use ATP) or a coenzyme made from a vitamin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio13-s1.10.b] Because each reaction depends on one enzyme, blocking or losing that enzyme has serious effects. Some poisons act this way, and some inherited or common conditions come from a missing enzyme.
[bio13-s1.10.0] Some drugs and poisons work by blocking an enzyme: for example some insecticides and nerve agents inhibit the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter (a chemical messenger between nerve cells). There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.
[bio13-s1.10.1] Lactose intolerance: many adults make little lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose (milk sugar), so undigested lactose passes to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing bloating, wind, cramps and diarrhoea.
[bio13-s1.10.2] Lactose-free milk is made using lactase, which digests the lactose in the milk before it is drunk.
[bio13-s1.10.3] Phenylketonuria (PKU): an inherited lack of the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine into another amino acid (tyrosine), so phenylalanine builds up in the blood and damages the developing brain.
[bio13-s1.10.4] PKU is detected by the heel-prick test given to newborn babies in Ireland, and is controlled by a diet low in phenylalanine.

CANDIDATES bio13-s2: Enzyme structure and specificity
[bio13-s2.0.b] An enzyme is a protein, and the way its chain is folded decides what it can do. Folding builds a small pocket called the active site (the region where the substrate, the molecule the enzyme acts on, binds and is converted).
[bio13-s2.0.0] An enzyme's three-dimensional shape is set by the sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain (its primary structure), and that sequence is set by its gene, so the gene ultimately decides the shape.
[bio13-s2.0.1] The chain coils and folds (secondary structure), then folds further into a precise globular (rounded) shape (tertiary structure); some enzymes are made of more than one polypeptide (quaternary structure).
[bio13-s2.0.2] The active site is formed by folding: it is made of a small number of amino acids, often far apart in the chain, that folding brings together into a pocket or cleft.
[bio13-s2.0.3] Most of the rest of the enzyme holds these few active-site amino acids in exactly the right positions, so the whole molecule matters even though only a small part touches the substrate.
[bio13-s2.0.4] The R groups (side groups, the part of each amino acid that differs from the others) of the active-site amino acids give the site its shape and its pattern of charges and of water-attracting and water-repelling regions.
[bio13-s2.0.5] The R groups bind the substrate by weak attractions (hydrogen bonds and ionic attractions between opposite charges), and some take part directly in the reaction.
[bio13-s2.1.b] An enzyme acts only on particular substrates, a property called specificity. It comes from the active site's shape and chemistry, and that shape is only as stable as the bonds that hold the protein folded.
[bio13-s2.1.0] Specificity depends on two things: the shape of the active site (only a substrate of complementary shape fits) and its chemistry (only a substrate with matching charges and groups is held in the right position).
[bio13-s2.1.1] A molecule of similar shape but with the wrong charges is not catalysed, because its groups are not held in the correct position.
[bio13-s2.1.2] The shape is held by hydrogen bonds, by ionic bonds between charged R groups, and by attractions between water-repelling R groups; these are all weak bonds.
[bio13-s2.1.3] Disulfide (sulfur) bridges between cysteine units (cysteine is an amino acid that contains sulfur) are strong covalent bonds that also hold the shape.
[bio13-s2.1.4] Because most of these bonds are weak, the shape is easily changed, which is why enzymes are sensitive to conditions.
[bio13-s2.1.5] Diagram: a folded polypeptide chain drawn as a looping ribbon, with three or four amino acids (circles) from distant parts of the chain meeting to form a labelled active site; label polypeptide chain, R groups of active-site amino acids, active site, a hydrogen bond and a disulfide bridge.
[bio13-s2.2.b] An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its exact shape. Anything that alters the shape of the protein, or the amino acids that make it, changes how well it works.
[bio13-s2.2.0] Activity depends on the active site keeping its shape.
[bio13-s2.2.1] Heat makes the enzyme molecule vibrate until weak bonds break; a change in pH changes the charges on R groups (by adding or removing hydrogen ions), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds.
[bio13-s2.2.2] Either way the active site loses its shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured (permanently changed in shape and unable to work). The effects on rate, and the investigations, are in “Investigation: rate of enzyme activity”.
[bio13-s2.2.3] A change in a single amino acid can change the shape or charge of the active site, so a mutation in the gene can make an enzyme work less well or not at all.
[bio13-s2.2.4] Example: in phenylketonuria the enzyme that converts phenylalanine is faulty, so the conversion does not happen properly.
[bio13-s2.2.5] Some enzymes need a cofactor, such as a metal ion, held in or near the active site to complete its shape or chemistry; without it the enzyme is inactive.
[bio13-s2.3.b] Enzymes differ in how narrowly they choose a substrate. Some enzymes also have structures that keep their shape in conditions that would ruin most others.
[bio13-s2.3.0] Some enzymes act on only one substrate (absolute specificity): for example, urease acts only on urea, breaking it into ammonia and carbon dioxide.
[bio13-s2.3.1] Others act on a group of substrates that share the same type of bond (group specificity): for example, lipase acts on many different triglycerides, and proteases such as pepsin break peptide bonds in many different proteins.
[bio13-s2.3.2] A rigid lock-and-key active site does not explain group specificity well, because one fixed shape cannot fit many different substrates; the induced-fit model does.
[bio13-s2.3.3] Enzymes that work in extreme conditions have structures that hold their shape there: for example, Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, stays active at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s2.3.4] Pepsin keeps its shape in the strong acid of the stomach, where most enzymes are denatured.
[bio13-s2.4.b] The induced-fit model describes an active site that is flexible, not a fixed mould. It explains how an enzyme grips its substrate, strains it and releases the products, then is ready to go again.
[bio13-s2.4.0] The induced-fit model says the active site is flexible, not rigid. Before binding, its shape is close to, but not exactly, complementary to the substrate; binding of the substrate induces (causes) a change in the enzyme's shape that produces a close fit.
[bio13-s2.4.1] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and starts to bind to the active site through weak attractions to its R groups.
[bio13-s2.4.2] 2. Binding causes the enzyme to change shape: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove taking the shape of a hand, forming a tightly fitting enzyme–substrate complex.
[bio13-s2.4.3] 3. The change in shape puts strain on particular bonds in the substrate, or brings reacting groups of two substrates close together in the correct orientation.
[bio13-s2.4.4] 3 (continued). The R groups of the active site may lend or take hydrogen ions or electrons, which lowers the activation energy (the energy needed to start the reaction), and the substrate is converted to products.
[bio13-s2.4.5] 4. The products no longer fit the active site closely, so they are released.
[bio13-s2.4.6] 5. The enzyme returns to its original shape, unchanged and ready to bind another substrate molecule.
[bio13-s2.5.b] The earlier lock-and-key model treated the active site as rigid. Induced fit replaced it because it explains observations that a rigid site cannot.
[bio13-s2.5.0] Example: the enzyme hexokinase (used in the first stage of respiration) closes around its substrate glucose like a clamp when glucose binds; structural studies of enzymes showed this change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.1] Induced fit explains why some enzymes act on several similar substrates: a flexible site can mould around each of them.
[bio13-s2.5.2] Induced fit explains how binding itself helps catalysis: the shape change strains the substrate's bonds, lowering the activation energy.
[bio13-s2.5.3] Induced fit explains why molecules of similar shape may bind but not react: they do not induce the correct change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.4] Lock and key remains a useful simple model of specificity.
[bio13-s2.5.5] Diagram: induced fit in four labelled stages: (1) an enzyme whose active site does not quite match the approaching substrate; (2) substrate binding and the active site changing shape around it, forming the enzyme–substrate complex, with arrows showing the change.
[bio13-s2.5.6] Diagram (continued): (3) products formed in the active site; (4) products released and the enzyme back in its original shape. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, change in shape, enzyme–substrate complex and products.
[bio13-s2.5.7] Diagram (contrast): include a one-line sketch of lock and key, with a rigid active site that already fits the substrate exactly.
[bio13-s2.6.b] The two models differ in whether the active site can move. Setting them side by side shows why induced fit explains more.
[bio13-s2.6.0] Active site: rigid, with a fixed shape that exactly matches the substrate (lock and key); flexible, changing shape when the substrate binds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.1] Fit: an exact fit before binding (lock and key); a close fit only after binding (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.2] Role of binding: it holds the substrate only (lock and key); the change of shape itself helps the reaction by straining bonds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.3] Specificity explained: absolute specificity only (lock and key); both absolute and group specificity (induced fit), because a flexible site can mould around several similar substrates.

CANDIDATES bio13-s3: Investigation: rate of enzyme activity
[bio13-s3.0.b] An enzyme is a biological catalyst, so what we want to know in an investigation is how fast it works. That speed is called the rate, and it is always measured against time.
[bio13-s3.0.0] The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is the amount of substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on) converted into product per unit time, e.g. cm³ of oxygen produced per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.1] The rate can be measured either by how fast a product appears or by how fast the substrate disappears, whichever is easier to detect for that reaction.
[bio13-s3.0.2] Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂); the rate is measured by the oxygen released, as foam or as a volume of gas.
[bio13-s3.0.3] Amylase digests starch to maltose; the rate is measured by the time taken for the starch to disappear, tested with iodine.
[bio13-s3.0.4] Rate = amount of product ÷ time: e.g. 30 cm³ of foam in 2 minutes gives a rate of 15 cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.5] Where the time taken to reach an end-point (such as the starch disappearing) is measured, rate = 1 ÷ time: starch gone in 120 s gives a rate of 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹, often written as 1000/time to give whole numbers.
[bio13-s3.0.6] A shorter time means a faster rate, because the same change has been completed more quickly.
[bio13-s3.0.7] Measure the initial rate, at the start of the reaction (e.g. in the first one or two minutes), because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
[bio13-s3.1.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. Presenting them clearly in a table and a graph is how you show the pattern in your results.
[bio13-s3.1.0] A results table has the independent variable (the factor you change) in the first column, then the repeat readings, the mean and the rate, each column headed with its unit.
[bio13-s3.1.1] A graph has the independent variable on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis, with points plotted accurately and joined by a smooth curve or line of best fit.
[bio13-s3.2.b] All five investigations use the same small set of enzymes, substrates and equipment. Knowing what each is for makes the methods easy to follow.
[bio13-s3.2.0] Catalase is found in almost all cells that use oxygen, where it breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism. Good sources are celery, potato, liver and yeast.
[bio13-s3.2.1] Catalase extract: blend a fixed mass of chopped celery (e.g. 20 g) with distilled water (e.g. 100 cm³), then filter through muslin or filter paper. Make one batch and use it for every test, so the enzyme is the same each time.
[bio13-s3.2.2] Amylase: a commercial (e.g. fungal) amylase solution made up at a fixed concentration; starch solution (e.g. 1%) is its substrate. Saliva contains amylase but is avoided for hygiene reasons.
[bio13-s3.2.3] Equipment for measuring: 100 cm³ graduated cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes for measuring volumes, thermometer to check temperatures, stopwatch to time each run, electronic balance.
[bio13-s3.2.4] Other equipment: test tubes and boiling tubes, blender or mortar and pestle, knife and chopping board, filter funnel, spotting tile and droppers, conical flask with bung and delivery tube, trough, labels.
[bio13-s3.2.5] Water baths can be thermostatically controlled electric baths, or beakers of water warmed with kettle water or cooled with ice, checked with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.2.6] Reagents: hydrogen peroxide solution (one stock of fixed concentration), buffer solutions or buffer tablets of known pH, washing-up liquid, distilled water, starch solution, amylase solution and iodine solution.
[bio13-s3.3.b] Four ideas recur across the investigations: a buffer to hold pH steady, washing-up liquid to make the oxygen measurable in the foam methods, a waiting time so the mixture reaches the right temperature, and a control to prove the enzyme is responsible.
[bio13-s3.3.0] A buffer is a solution that keeps the pH constant even when acids or alkalis are produced or added.
[bio13-s3.3.1] A buffer at pH 7 (or at the enzyme's optimum) is added in every investigation except the pH investigation, so that pH is a controlled variable (kept the same each time).
[bio13-s3.3.2] In the foam methods, a drop of washing-up liquid traps the oxygen released by catalase as a foam, so its volume can be read in a graduated cylinder; the same amount is added every time.
[bio13-s3.3.3] Reaching temperature (equilibration): the enzyme and the substrate are placed in the same water bath, in separate containers, for about 5 minutes before they are mixed, so the reaction starts at the chosen temperature.
[bio13-s3.3.4] A control in each investigation uses enzyme that has been boiled (denatured), or distilled water in place of the enzyme, with everything else the same.
[bio13-s3.3.5] The control shows that the oxygen (or the loss of starch) is caused by the enzyme and not by the substrate breaking down on its own.
[bio13-s3.4.b] The chemicals and equipment here are mostly mild, but several can harm skin, eyes or clothes if handled carelessly.
[bio13-s3.4.0] Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent that irritates or burns the eyes and skin; wear safety glasses (and gloves), measure it with a syringe, and wash any splash off with plenty of water.
[bio13-s3.4.1] Buffers of very low or very high pH are irritants, so wear safety glasses.
[bio13-s3.4.2] Use tongs or a test-tube holder and take care with boiling water and hot water baths; keep electrical water baths and blenders away from spilt water.
[bio13-s3.4.3] Take care with knives and blender blades. If liver is used, treat it as raw meat: wear gloves, keep it off benches used for other work and wash hands afterwards.
[bio13-s3.4.4] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes, so handle it with care. Never eat or drink in the laboratory, because chemicals could be swallowed.
[bio13-s3.5.b] Here we vary how much hydrogen peroxide (the substrate) the same amount of catalase has to work on, and see how the rate of oxygen production changes.
[bio13-s3.5.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of substrate (hydrogen peroxide) concentration on the rate of catalase activity, by changing only the substrate so any change in rate must be due to it.
[bio13-s3.5.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increases the rate of oxygen production, because more substrate molecules collide with active sites per second, until all the active sites are occupied and the rate levels off.”
[bio13-s3.5.2] Independent variable: hydrogen peroxide concentration, made by diluting the stock with distilled water to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the stock (e.g. 2 cm³ stock + 8 cm³ water = 20%), always to the same total volume (e.g. 10 cm³).
[bio13-s3.5.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): volume of oxygen collected in the first minute (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.5.4] Controlled variables: volume of the same celery extract (so enzyme concentration is constant), temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), total volume of solution, timing and apparatus.
[bio13-s3.5.5] Control: the 100% hydrogen peroxide with boiled extract (or with water in place of the extract), which gives little or no oxygen; the 0% tube also shows that no oxygen forms without substrate.
[bio13-s3.6.b] The oxygen given off is collected in an upturned cylinder of water, so its volume can be read directly.
[bio13-s3.6.0] 1. Fill a graduated cylinder with water and turn it upside down in a trough of water, with no air at the top.
[bio13-s3.6.1] 2. Put 10 cm³ of the first hydrogen peroxide dilution and 2 cm³ of pH 7 buffer in a conical flask (or boiling tube) in the water bath, with 2 cm³ of celery extract in a separate tube beside it, for about 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.6.2] 3. Place the end of the delivery tube under the mouth of the upturned cylinder.
[bio13-s3.6.3] 4. Add the extract to the flask, immediately put in the bung with the delivery tube, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.6.4] 5. Read the volume of oxygen collected in the cylinder after 1 minute (or every 30 seconds for 3 minutes).
[bio13-s3.6.5] 6. Rinse the flask and repeat for each concentration. Do each concentration three times and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.6.6] Alternatives: a gas syringe connected to the flask measures the oxygen volume more accurately than collection over water; or the foam method used in the pH investigation can be used.
[bio13-s3.6.7] Another alternative: filter-paper discs soaked in catalase extract are dropped into each concentration and the time for each disc to rise to the surface (buoyed by oxygen bubbles) is timed, with rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.6.8] Diagram: a conical flask in a water bath, with a bung and delivery tube leading under an upturned graduated cylinder full of water in a trough. Label hydrogen peroxide + buffer + catalase extract, water bath, thermometer, delivery tube, oxygen collecting, trough of water.
[bio13-s3.7.b] The results follow a typical curve: a steep rise, then a plateau. Each part has its own explanation, and the plateau is caused by saturation of the active sites, not by the enzyme being denatured or used up.
[bio13-s3.7.0] Expected results: no oxygen at 0%; at low concentrations the rate rises steeply, roughly in proportion to concentration; at high concentrations it rises less and levels off into a plateau. The boiled-enzyme control gives little or no oxygen.
[bio13-s3.7.1] The rise is explained by collisions: more substrate molecules in the same volume means more collisions with active sites (the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds) per second, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and more product is made per minute.
[bio13-s3.7.2] The plateau is explained by saturation: at high substrate concentration every active site is occupied at any moment, so the enzyme (saturated) works at its maximum rate, and adding more substrate cannot increase the rate.
[bio13-s3.7.3] At the plateau the amount of enzyme is the limiting factor (the thing holding the rate back); adding more enzyme would raise the rate again.
[bio13-s3.7.4] Within the range used in a school investigation the plateau may not be reached if the enzyme is in excess.
[bio13-s3.7.5] Conclusion: state the trend shown by the mean rates, whether it supports the hypothesis, and the concentration above which the rate stopped increasing, if one was found.
[bio13-s3.7.6] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against substrate concentration (x-axis); a curve rising from the origin, steep at first and then levelling off, with the plateau labelled all active sites occupied (saturated).
[bio13-s3.8.b] Now the substrate stays the same and the amount of enzyme changes. Amylase digests starch, and the end-point is found with iodine, which turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown when starch is absent.
[bio13-s3.8.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of enzyme (amylase) concentration on the rate at which starch is digested.
[bio13-s3.8.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of amylase increases the rate of starch digestion, because more active sites are available to bind starch molecules, as long as starch is in excess.”
[bio13-s3.8.2] Independent variable: amylase concentration, made by diluting the stock amylase solution with distilled water to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the stock, always to the same volume.
[bio13-s3.8.3] Dependent variable: time taken for the starch to disappear (the iodine test no longer turns blue-black), converted to rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.8.4] Controlled variables: volume and concentration of starch solution, volume of amylase solution, temperature (water bath at e.g. 37 °C), pH (buffer at pH 7), volume of iodine in each well, and the sampling interval.
[bio13-s3.8.5] Control: starch solution with distilled water (0% amylase) or with boiled amylase in place of the amylase; it stays blue-black with iodine for the whole investigation, showing that the starch does not disappear without active enzyme.
[bio13-s3.9.b] Drops of the reacting mixture are tested with iodine at regular intervals until the blue-black colour no longer appears.
[bio13-s3.9.0] 1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile (a white tile with small hollows), so each sample can be tested as soon as it is taken.
[bio13-s3.9.1] 2. Put 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of pH 7 buffer into a test tube, and 2 cm³ of the first amylase dilution into another, then stand both in the water bath for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.9.2] 3. Pour the amylase into the starch, mix, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.9.3] 4. Every 30 seconds, take one drop of the mixture with a clean dropper and add it to the next well of iodine.
[bio13-s3.9.4] 5. Note the time at which a drop first leaves the iodine yellow-brown (no blue-black): this is the end-point, when all the starch has been digested.
[bio13-s3.9.5] 6. Repeat for each amylase concentration, three times each, so that a mean can be calculated and a one-off error does not mislead you; then calculate the mean time and the rate.
[bio13-s3.9.6] Alternative: with catalase, vary the volume of celery extract (e.g. 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 cm³, each made up to the same total volume with distilled water) or the number of equal-sized celery discs, and measure the foam or oxygen volume in a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.9.7] Diagram: a spotting tile with a row of wells, the first wells blue-black and the later wells yellow-brown, labelled with sampling times; and the starch–amylase tube in a water bath with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.10.b] With more enzyme, the starch disappears sooner. Unlike the substrate investigation, the graph is a straight line, as long as there is plenty of substrate.
[bio13-s3.10.0] Expected results: the higher the amylase concentration, the shorter the time for the starch to disappear, so the faster the rate; the 0% and boiled-enzyme controls never reach the end-point.
[bio13-s3.10.1] A graph of rate against enzyme concentration is a straight line through the origin: doubling the enzyme roughly doubles the rate.
[bio13-s3.10.2] Explanation: more enzyme molecules means more active sites, so more substrate molecules can be bound and converted at the same time, and more product is made per unit time.
[bio13-s3.10.3] The straight line continues only while substrate is in excess (more than the enzyme can use). If the enzyme concentration becomes very high, substrate becomes the limiting factor and the line levels off.
[bio13-s3.10.4] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against enzyme concentration (x-axis); a straight line rising from the origin, labelled substrate in excess.
[bio13-s3.11.b] Each enzyme works best at a particular pH. Here, buffers set the pH, and the volume of foam produced by catalase shows how active it is at each one.
[bio13-s3.11.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.11.1] Hypothesis: “Catalase has an optimum pH at which its rate is highest, and the rate decreases at pH values above and below it, because the shape of the active site is changed.”
[bio13-s3.11.2] Independent variable: pH, set with buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10), which hold each pH steady so it does not drift as the reaction proceeds.
[bio13-s3.11.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam produced in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.11.4] Controlled variables: temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract (or mass of celery), volume of buffer, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.11.5] Control: at each pH (or at pH 7), boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.12.b] The foam is built up in a graduated cylinder, so its height can be read at the end of a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.12.0] 1. Into a 100 cm³ graduated cylinder put 10 cm³ of pH 4 buffer, 2 cm³ of celery extract (or a fixed mass of chopped celery) and one drop of washing-up liquid.
[bio13-s3.12.1] 2. Stand the cylinder in the 25 °C water bath, with a boiling tube of hydrogen peroxide beside it, for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.12.2] 3. Note the starting volume of liquid in the cylinder, so the foam volume can be found by subtraction.
[bio13-s3.12.3] 4. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide with a syringe and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.12.4] 5. After 2 minutes, read the volume at the top of the foam, at eye level; the foam volume is this reading minus the starting volume.
[bio13-s3.12.5] 6. Wash the cylinder and repeat with each buffer, three times each, and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.12.6] Diagram: a graduated cylinder standing in a water bath with a thermometer, containing buffer, celery extract and washing-up liquid at the bottom and a column of foam above, with a syringe adding hydrogen peroxide. Label each part and the foam height being read.
[bio13-s3.13.b] The rate rises to a peak at one pH and falls on both sides. The explanation lies in how pH affects the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s3.13.0] Expected results: the most foam forms at around pH 7 (the optimum for celery and liver catalase), with less in more acidic and more alkaline buffers and very little at the extremes. The control gives no foam.
[bio13-s3.13.1] The optimum pH is the pH at which the active site has the shape and charges that best fit the substrate.
[bio13-s3.13.2] A higher or lower hydrogen ion concentration, in more acidic or more alkaline conditions, changes the charges in the active site and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
[bio13-s3.13.3] Small changes from the optimum are reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme permanently.
[bio13-s3.13.4] Each enzyme's optimum suits the place where it works: pepsin in the acid stomach has an optimum of about pH 2; salivary amylase in the mouth about pH 7; trypsin in the alkaline small intestine about pH 8.
[bio13-s3.13.5] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against pH (x-axis); a peaked, bell-shaped curve with its peak labelled optimum pH at about 7 for catalase; optionally a second curve peaking at about pH 2, labelled pepsin.
[bio13-s3.14.b] Temperature has two opposite effects on an enzyme: it speeds up collisions, but too much heat destroys the enzyme. This investigation measures catalase activity across a range of water-bath temperatures.
[bio13-s3.14.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.14.1] Hypothesis: “As temperature increases, the rate of catalase activity increases up to an optimum temperature, because molecules move faster and collide more often; above the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme is denatured.”
[bio13-s3.14.2] Independent variable: temperature, set with water baths at e.g. 0 °C (ice water), 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, each checked with a thermometer because baths drift from their set temperature.
[bio13-s3.14.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.14.4] Controlled variables: pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.14.5] Control: boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract at each temperature, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.15.b] The temperature method reuses the foam method from the pH investigation, with the buffer fixed and the water bath changed.
[bio13-s3.15.0] Method: follow the pH investigation method, but with pH 7 buffer in every cylinder, and with each cylinder (buffer, extract and washing-up liquid) and its tube of hydrogen peroxide left in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes before the peroxide is added.
[bio13-s3.15.1] Check the bath temperature with the thermometer during the 2 minutes, because it can drift; repeat three times at each temperature and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.15.2] The same method works with amylase and starch, timing the starch's disappearance at each temperature, as in the enzyme concentration investigation.
[bio13-s3.15.3] Diagram: the pH set-up, with a row of water baths at different labelled temperatures.
[bio13-s3.16.b] The curve rises slowly to a peak and then drops steeply. The rise and the fall have different causes.
[bio13-s3.16.0] Expected results: little foam at 0 °C; the rate increases as temperature rises, to a maximum at the optimum temperature; above the optimum the rate falls steeply, with little or no activity at the highest temperatures tested.
[bio13-s3.16.1] The graph is not symmetrical: a gradual rise and a steep fall. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 °C, body temperature.
[bio13-s3.16.2] Explanation of the rise: as temperature increases, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), move faster and collide more often, and more collisions have enough energy to react, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second.
[bio13-s3.16.3] Over the lower part of the range, the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (an approximate pattern, not an exact law).
[bio13-s3.16.4] Explanation of the fall: above the optimum, the enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured.
[bio13-s3.16.5] As temperature rises further, more enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls even though collisions are more frequent.
[bio13-s3.16.6] At low temperature the enzyme is inactive, not denatured: the molecules move too slowly for many collisions, but the active site keeps its shape, so activity returns when the enzyme is warmed (shown in the heat denaturation investigation).
[bio13-s3.16.7] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against temperature in °C (x-axis); a curve rising gradually to a peak labelled optimum temperature, then falling steeply to zero, with the falling part labelled enzyme denatured.
[bio13-s3.17.b] Heat can do more than slow an enzyme: it can destroy its shape for good. This investigation shows that boiled catalase stays inactive even after it is cooled.
[bio13-s3.17.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of heat denaturation on the activity of catalase.
[bio13-s3.17.1] Denaturation is the permanent loss of an enzyme's shape, and so of its active site and its activity, caused by high temperature or extreme pH.
[bio13-s3.17.2] Hypothesis: “Catalase that has been boiled will not break down hydrogen peroxide, even when cooled, because its active site has been permanently changed.”
[bio13-s3.17.3] Independent variable: whether the extract has been boiled (heat-treated) or not. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³).
[bio13-s3.17.4] Controlled variables: both extracts tested at the same temperature (e.g. 25 °C) and pH (pH 7 buffer), with the same volumes of extract, buffer and hydrogen peroxide, the same drop of washing-up liquid and the same time.
[bio13-s3.17.5] Control: the unboiled extract, which shows how much foam active catalase produces under the same conditions, so the boiled result has something to be compared with.
[bio13-s3.18.b] Half the extract is boiled and half is not; both are then tested at the same temperature, so any difference is due to boiling alone.
[bio13-s3.18.0] 1. Divide the celery extract between two labelled boiling tubes, A and B.
[bio13-s3.18.1] 2. Place tube A in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes; leave tube B at room temperature.
[bio13-s3.18.2] 3. Cool tube A, then stand both tubes in the 25 °C water bath for 5 minutes, so the boiled extract is tested at the same temperature as the unboiled.
[bio13-s3.18.3] 4. Set up two graduated cylinders, each with 10 cm³ of pH 7 buffer, a drop of washing-up liquid and 2 cm³ of extract A or B.
[bio13-s3.18.4] 5. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide to each and read the foam after 2 minutes. Repeat three times.
[bio13-s3.18.5] Expected results: the boiled extract gives little or no foam; the unboiled extract gives a large column of foam. Any small amount of foam from the boiled extract comes from hydrogen peroxide breaking down slowly on its own, or from catalase that was not fully denatured.
[bio13-s3.18.6] Conclusion: boiling denatured the catalase; its activity did not return on cooling, so the change is permanent. This supports the hypothesis and explains why boiled enzyme is used as the control in the other investigations.
[bio13-s3.18.7] Extension: extract kept in ice water for 10 minutes and then warmed to 25 °C gives as much foam as unchilled extract, showing that cooling makes an enzyme inactive without denaturing it, whereas heating denatures it.
[bio13-s3.18.8] The same investigation works with amylase: boiled amylase added to starch leaves the iodine test blue-black at every sampling time, while unboiled amylase digests the starch so the iodine stays yellow-brown.
[bio13-s3.19.b] Secondary data are results that someone else collected. They let you check and extend your own conclusions, and the specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data.
[bio13-s3.19.0] Secondary data are data collected by someone else: results pooled from other groups in the class, published tables, textbooks or research papers, or online databases.
[bio13-s3.19.1] Uses: compare your optimum with published optima (the optimum pH of digestive enzymes and the optimum temperature of human enzymes given in the pH and temperature investigations).
[bio13-s3.19.2] Uses: check whether your trend agrees with other groups' (reproducibility), and extend a conclusion beyond the range you could test.
[bio13-s3.19.3] Secondary data on enzymes from extreme environments support the link between structure and conditions: Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, works best at about 72 °C and is not denatured at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s3.19.4] When using secondary data, record its source, check that it was measured under comparable conditions (same enzyme source, substrate, pH or temperature), and judge whether the source is reliable. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio13-s3.20.b] Every method has weaknesses. Knowing where the error comes from tells you how to reduce it.
[bio13-s3.20.0] Foam volume is imprecise: bubbles burst, the top of the foam is uneven, and foam may stick to the cylinder walls. Read at eye level at exactly the same time each run; collecting the oxygen in a gas syringe is more accurate.
[bio13-s3.20.1] Gas is lost in the time between adding the enzyme and inserting the bung, or through leaks; some oxygen dissolves in the water when it is collected over water.
[bio13-s3.20.2] Water-bath temperatures drift, especially in beakers of hot water or ice; check with a thermometer throughout or use a thermostatically controlled bath.
[bio13-s3.20.3] The reaction mixture may not reach the bath temperature if equilibration is too short, so allow the full time.
[bio13-s3.21.b] Some errors come from the enzyme and reagents themselves, or from judging a colour change. Repeating and averaging makes results more reliable.
[bio13-s3.21.0] Enzyme activity varies between celery samples and falls as an extract stands, so make one batch, keep it cool and use it quickly for all the tests; a fresh batch for a repeat can give different values.
[bio13-s3.21.1] Hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down on standing, especially in light, so its concentration may fall during the session: use one fresh stock kept in a dark bottle.
[bio13-s3.21.2] The iodine end-point is a judgement of colour, and sampling every 30 seconds means the time is only known to within 30 seconds; sample more often near the end-point, compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter.
[bio13-s3.21.3] Measure volumes with syringes or graduated pipettes rather than by eye, and use a clean syringe or dropper for each solution to avoid contamination.
[bio13-s3.21.4] Repeat each value three times, calculate the mean, and identify and investigate any anomalous result; pool class data to check reproducibility.
[bio13-s3.21.5] Use at least five values of the independent variable so the shape of the graph (peak or plateau) can be seen.

CANDIDATES bio13-s4: Industrial enzymes
[bio13-s4.0.b] Industry needs reactions that are fast, clean and cheap. Enzymes (biological catalysts, which speed up reactions without being changed) often do this better than the chemical catalysts used in factories.
[bio13-s4.0.0] Specificity: an enzyme catalyses one reaction, so it makes the wanted product with few unwanted by-products. This gives a purer product that needs less purification.
[bio13-s4.0.1] Mild conditions: enzymes work at moderate temperatures (often 30–60 °C), normal pressure and near-neutral pH (neither strongly acidic nor alkaline), whereas many chemical catalysts need high temperatures and pressures.
[bio13-s4.0.2] Mild conditions save energy and money, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and make the process safer, because there are no very hot, high-pressure vessels.
[bio13-s4.0.3] Enzymes are efficient catalysts that are not used up, so a small amount can be used repeatedly. They are also biodegradable (broken down naturally), so they leave less harmful waste than many chemical catalysts.
[bio13-s4.1.b] Industry has to get its enzymes from living things, and enzymes have weaknesses as well as strengths. Knowing both explains how enzymes are produced and why the conditions in a factory are tightly controlled.
[bio13-s4.1.0] Most industrial enzymes are made by microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) grown in large vessels called fermenters or bioreactors. They are used because they grow fast, can be grown on cheap nutrients, and many release (secrete) their enzymes into the medium, where they are easy to collect.
[bio13-s4.1.1] Many industrial enzymes are now made by genetically modified microorganisms, which carry the gene for the enzyme. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio13-s4.1.2] A few enzymes come from plants (e.g. papain from papaya) or from animals, such as rennet (from calves' stomachs).
[bio13-s4.1.3] Limitation: enzymes are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working) by high temperatures and extreme pH, so conditions must be carefully controlled.
[bio13-s4.1.4] Limitation: enzymes can be expensive to produce and purify.
[bio13-s4.1.5] Limitation: free enzymes (ones simply dissolved in the mixture) are lost in the product after one use. Immobilisation, covered below, solves this.
[bio13-s4.1.6] Limitation: enzyme dust can cause allergies. In the early years of biological detergents, some factory workers developed allergies, so enzymes are now sealed in granules.
[bio13-s4.1.7] Enzymes from microorganisms that live in hot springs or in alkaline conditions are chosen or engineered for processes that need tougher enzymes, because they already work where normal enzymes would be denatured.
[bio13-s4.2.b] Milk is a good example of how industry uses single, specific enzymes. One enzyme turns milk into cheese, and another removes the sugar that some people cannot digest.
[bio13-s4.2.0] Cheese-making: chymosin (rennin), the active enzyme in rennet, clots the milk protein casein, so the milk separates into solid curds (made into cheese) and liquid whey.
[bio13-s4.2.1] Rennet was traditionally extracted from the stomachs of young calves. Much of the chymosin used today is made by genetically modified microorganisms, which suits vegetarians and needs no animals.
[bio13-s4.2.2] Lactose-free milk: lactase breaks lactose (the sugar in milk) down into glucose and galactose, so people with lactose intolerance (who cannot digest lactose) can drink the milk.
[bio13-s4.2.3] Lactose-free milk tastes sweeter, because glucose and galactose are sweeter than lactose. Lactase is often used immobilised.
[bio13-s4.3.b] Plant materials such as fruit and grain are full of structural and storage carbohydrates. Carbohydrate-digesting enzymes release the useful sugars and make processing easier.
[bio13-s4.3.0] Fruit juice: pectinase breaks down pectin, which helps hold plant cell walls together, so more juice is released from crushed fruit and the juice is clearer (less cloudy).
[bio13-s4.3.1] Sugar syrups: amylases break starch (e.g. from maize) into glucose syrup. Immobilised glucose isomerase then converts glucose into fructose, which is much sweeter.
[bio13-s4.3.2] High-fructose syrup is used to sweeten soft drinks and processed foods, so less is needed for the same sweetness.
[bio13-s4.3.3] Brewing and baking: amylases break starch in grain into maltose and glucose, which yeast ferments to make ethanol and carbon dioxide. In baking, the carbon dioxide helps the dough rise. There is more on fermentation in Respiration.
[bio13-s4.3.4] Proteases (enzymes that digest protein) are used to clear haze from beer.
[bio13-s4.4.b] Proteases, lipases and amylases digest the three main food molecules: protein, fat and starch. This is why they are useful both in sweets and meat and in household cleaning.
[bio13-s4.4.0] Proteases such as papain tenderise meat by breaking down muscle proteins.
[bio13-s4.4.1] Invertase (sucrase) converts sucrose into glucose and fructose, to make the soft centres of chocolates and other sweets.
[bio13-s4.4.2] Household link: biological washing powders contain proteases, lipases and amylases that digest protein stains (blood, egg, grass), fat and grease, and starch.
[bio13-s4.4.3] Biological washing powders work at low washing temperatures (e.g. 30–40 °C), saving energy. They are not used at very high temperatures, where the enzymes would be denatured.
[bio13-s4.5.b] Biofuels are fuels made from living material. Enzymes are needed to turn the carbohydrates and oils in plants into forms that yeast or chemistry can convert into fuel.
[bio13-s4.5.0] Bioethanol from starch crops: amylases break the starch of crops such as maize and wheat into glucose, which yeast ferments to ethanol. The ethanol is blended with petrol as a fuel.
[bio13-s4.5.1] Bioethanol from cellulose (a second-generation biofuel): cellulases break down the cellulose in straw, wood chips and other crop waste into glucose for fermentation.
[bio13-s4.5.2] Cellulose is tightly packed and hard to digest, so these enzymes are slow and costly and are an active area of research. There is more on cellulose in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s4.5.3] Biodiesel: lipases can catalyse the conversion of vegetable oils and waste cooking oils into biodiesel. This is an alternative to chemical catalysts that works at lower temperatures and leaves less waste.
[bio13-s4.6.b] Biofuels are often described as green, but the picture is more complicated. Exams ask you to weigh the benefits against the costs.
[bio13-s4.6.0] For: biofuels are renewable, because the crops can be regrown.
[bio13-s4.6.1] For: the carbon dioxide released when they burn was taken in by the crop during photosynthesis, so they add less to atmospheric CO₂ than fossil fuels.
[bio13-s4.6.2] For: using waste materials (straw, waste oil) adds value to waste.
[bio13-s4.6.3] Against: growing fuel crops uses land that could grow food, which can raise food prices.
[bio13-s4.6.4] Against: clearing forests for fuel crops destroys habitats and releases carbon.
[bio13-s4.6.5] Against: fertilisers, machinery and processing use fossil-fuel energy, so the saving is smaller than it first appears.
[bio13-s4.6.6] Cellulose-based and waste-based biofuels avoid most of the competition with food, because they use parts of the plant or waste that people do not eat. Carbon cycle and climate detail is in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio13-s4.7.b] Because enzymes are so specific, they are used in medicine to detect substances, break down harmful ones, and replace enzymes that the body fails to supply.
[bio13-s4.7.0] Diagnosis: glucose oxidase is used in blood-glucose test strips and electronic biosensors used by people with diabetes, and in urine test strips.
[bio13-s4.7.1] Glucose oxidase is specific to glucose, so other sugars do not interfere. The reaction produces a colour change or an electrical signal in proportion to the amount of glucose.
[bio13-s4.7.2] Dissolving blood clots: clot-dissolving enzymes such as streptokinase (from bacteria) and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) are given soon after a heart attack or a stroke caused by a clot, to break down the clot and restore blood flow.
[bio13-s4.7.3] Enzyme replacement: people with cystic fibrosis often cannot release enough digestive enzymes from the pancreas, so they take capsules of pancreatic enzymes (lipase, protease and amylase) with meals.
[bio13-s4.7.4] Lactase tablets help people with lactose intolerance, because they supply the missing enzyme.
[bio13-s4.7.5] Cancer treatment: the enzyme asparaginase is used to treat a type of childhood leukaemia (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia).
[bio13-s4.7.6] The leukaemia cells cannot make enough of the amino acid asparagine and take it from the blood. The enzyme breaks down asparagine in the blood, starving the cancer cells.
[bio13-s4.7.7] Medical technologies built on enzymes (named only here): restriction enzymes and DNA ligase in genetic engineering (see “Genetic engineering”); Taq polymerase in PCR for DNA profiling and diagnostic testing (see “Generating a DNA profile”).
[bio13-s4.8.b] Making drugs chemically can be wasteful and use harsh conditions. Enzymes can make the same drugs more precisely and more cleanly.
[bio13-s4.8.0] Making antibiotics: immobilised penicillin acylase converts natural penicillin, made by the Penicillium mould, into a starting molecule (6-APA) from which semi-synthetic penicillins, such as amoxicillin, are made.
[bio13-s4.8.1] Semi-synthetic penicillins can treat infections that the natural form does not.
[bio13-s4.8.2] Making single-form drugs: many drug molecules exist in two mirror-image forms, of which only one works (the other may be useless or harmful).
[bio13-s4.8.3] Because enzymes are specific, they make only the wanted form, avoiding wasteful separation. For example, an engineered enzyme (a transaminase) is used to manufacture the diabetes drug sitagliptin.
[bio13-s4.8.4] Enzymes allow drug manufacture at mild temperatures with less toxic solvent waste than chemical methods (“green chemistry”). Human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria depends on enzymes used to insert the gene (see “Genetic engineering”).
[bio13-s4.9.b] Free enzymes mix into the product and are lost after one use. Fixing the enzyme in place solves this, and it is a central idea in industrial enzyme use.
[bio13-s4.9.0] An immobilised enzyme is an enzyme (or a whole cell containing it) fixed to, or trapped in, an inert, insoluble material. It is held in place while the substrate solution passes over or through it, so it does not mix freely with the product.
[bio13-s4.9.1] Entrapment: the enzyme is trapped in a gel bead, such as calcium alginate.
[bio13-s4.9.2] Adsorption: the enzyme is stuck to the surface of an insoluble support, such as glass beads, resin or charcoal.
[bio13-s4.9.3] Covalent bonding: the enzyme is chemically bonded to a support.
[bio13-s4.9.4] Enclosure: the enzyme is enclosed behind a partially permeable membrane.
[bio13-s4.9.5] Examples: glucose isomerase for high-fructose syrup; lactase for lactose-free milk; penicillin acylase for semi-synthetic penicillins.
[bio13-s4.10.b] Immobilisation brings clear benefits in cost and purity, but it also adds complications. Exams ask for both sides.
[bio13-s4.10.0] Advantage: the enzyme can be recovered and reused many times, cutting costs.
[bio13-s4.10.1] Advantage: the product is not contaminated with enzyme, so it needs no purification.
[bio13-s4.10.2] Advantage: the enzyme can be packed in a column for a continuous-flow process, with substrate fed in at the top and product collected at the bottom.
[bio13-s4.10.3] Advantage: immobilised enzymes are often more stable, being less easily denatured by changes in temperature or pH. The reaction can be stopped at once by removing the enzyme.
[bio13-s4.10.4] Disadvantage: immobilising costs money and equipment to set up, and some activity is lost, as part of the active sites (where the substrate binds) may be blocked or altered.
[bio13-s4.10.5] Disadvantage: the reaction can be slower, because the substrate must diffuse into the bead to reach the enzyme. Enzyme may also leak out of the support.
[bio13-s4.10.6] Disadvantage: immobilised enzymes are unsuitable where the substrate molecules are too large to enter the beads.
[bio13-s4.11.b] This is the school method for immobilising an enzyme. Yeast is used because its cells contain the enzyme sucrase (also called invertase), which acts on sucrose.
[bio13-s4.11.0] 1. Mix sodium alginate solution with a suspension of yeast (which contains the enzyme sucrase, also called invertase) or with an enzyme solution.
[bio13-s4.11.1] 2. Draw the mixture into a syringe and drop it, one drop at a time, into calcium chloride solution: each drop forms a bead of insoluble calcium alginate gel trapping the yeast.
[bio13-s4.11.2] 3. Leave the beads to harden for about 15–20 minutes.
[bio13-s4.11.3] 4. Sieve them out and rinse them with distilled water to remove the calcium chloride and any free yeast.
[bio13-s4.11.4] 5. Place the beads in a separating funnel (or a syringe barrel with a tap) as a column.
[bio13-s4.11.5] Safety: calcium chloride irritates the eyes, so wear safety glasses and wash spills.
[bio13-s4.11.6] Diagram: (a) a syringe dripping the alginate–yeast mixture into a beaker of calcium chloride solution, with beads forming, labelled; (b) a separating funnel or column packed with beads, with sucrose solution added at the top, a tap, and product (glucose and fructose) collected below, labelled.
[bio13-s4.12.b] Once the column is made, sucrose solution is passed through it and the liquid that comes out is tested. A control and a comparison with free yeast show that the beads do the work and that immobilisation helps.
[bio13-s4.12.0] Using the beads: pour sucrose solution through the column and collect the liquid that drips out. Test it with a glucose test strip.
[bio13-s4.12.1] A positive glucose result shows that the sucrose has been converted to glucose and fructose. The sucrose solution tested before it enters the column gives a negative result.
[bio13-s4.12.2] Control: beads made without yeast (alginate only). Their product gives no glucose, so the conversion is due to the yeast and not the beads.
[bio13-s4.12.3] The product from the beads is clear, while sucrose mixed with free yeast becomes cloudy with yeast cells that must be removed, showing the advantage of immobilisation. The beads can be rinsed and used again.
[bio13-s4.13.b] Enzymes and cells work best when conditions are right. A bioreactor is the vessel that holds those conditions steady in industry.
[bio13-s4.13.0] A bioreactor is a vessel in which cells or enzymes carry out a reaction under controlled conditions: temperature, pH, oxygen (or its absence), nutrient supply, mixing and sterility are monitored and kept at the values that suit the enzyme.
[bio13-s4.13.1] A column of immobilised enzyme is a simple bioreactor. There is more on growing microorganisms in bioreactors, and on batch and continuous culture, in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio13-s4.14.b] The uses above fall into three themes that link enzymes to health, sustainability and technology. Exams may ask you to draw these together.
[bio13-s4.14.0] Health: diagnostic strips and biosensors, clot-busting and replacement enzymes, and lactose-free foods.
[bio13-s4.14.1] Sustainability: lower energy use and less chemical waste; low-temperature washing; biofuels and the arguments about them; more juice from the same fruit.
[bio13-s4.14.2] Technology: genetic modification of microorganisms to make enzymes, engineered enzymes, immobilisation and continuous-flow bioreactors.

CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio14-s3: The light-dependent reactions
[bio14-s3.0.b] The light-dependent reactions start when light hits a leaf. Before any chemistry can happen, the plant has to catch that light energy and channel it to one special chlorophyll molecule, which uses it to fire off an electron.
[bio14-s3.0.0] Light travels as tiny packets of energy called photons. A pigment is a molecule that absorbs light of particular colours (wavelengths).
[bio14-s3.0.1] The thylakoid membranes hold several pigments: chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and yellow and orange pigments called carotenoids. Each absorbs slightly different colours, so having several widens the range of light the plant can use.
[bio14-s3.0.2] The pigment molecules sit in clusters of hundreds, called light-harvesting or antenna complexes, around a reaction centre. The cluster works like a satellite dish, collecting light over a wide area and focusing it on one point.
[bio14-s3.0.3] When any pigment in the cluster absorbs a photon, the energy is passed from one pigment molecule to the next until it reaches the reaction centre, a special chlorophyll a molecule.
[bio14-s3.0.4] At the reaction centre, the energy raises (excites) one of the chlorophyll's electrons to a high energy level. This energised electron leaves the chlorophyll and is taken up by an electron acceptor, the first carrier of an electron transport chain.
[bio14-s3.0.5] Because an electron carries a negative charge, losing one leaves the reaction-centre chlorophyll positively charged. It must get an electron back before it can fire off another one, and in the non-cyclic pathway it takes that electron from water.
[bio14-s3.1.b] The energised electron now has to go somewhere useful. In the non-cyclic pathway, it travels one way, from water to NADP⁺, and along the way its energy is used to make the two things the second stage of photosynthesis needs: ATP and NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.0] 1. Light energy reaches the reaction centre, and energised electrons leave the chlorophyll.
[bio14-s3.1.1] 2. The electrons are passed along an electron transport chain, a series of electron carriers in the thylakoid membrane. At each hand-over they lose a little energy, like a ball bouncing down a staircase, and this released energy is used to make ATP from ADP and phosphate (Pi).
[bio14-s3.1.2] 3. At the end of the chain, the electrons, now at lower energy, are passed to NADP⁺, a hydrogen carrier. Each NADP⁺ takes two electrons and one proton (H⁺) to form NADPH: NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.3] 4. The chlorophyll is still positively charged, so it strongly attracts electrons and pulls them from water molecules. This splits the water, a step called photolysis (splitting by light, because the energy for it ultimately came from light): 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂.
[bio14-s3.1.4] 5. The electrons from water replace the ones the chlorophyll lost, so it is neutral again and ready to absorb more energy.
[bio14-s3.1.5] The protons (H⁺) from photolysis are used to form NADPH, and they also build up inside the thylakoid, where this crowd of protons is used to power ATP-making. There is more on this in The chloroplast's internal structure.
[bio14-s3.1.6] The oxygen from photolysis is a by-product. It is used in the cell's respiration or released through the stomata.
[bio14-s3.1.7] It is called non-cyclic because the electrons go one way only, from water through the chlorophyll and the chain to NADP⁺, and never return to where they started. The electrons aren't used up; they end up on NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.8] Products of the non-cyclic pathway: ATP, NADPH and oxygen.
[bio14-s3.2.b] Sometimes the energised electrons take a shortcut. In the cyclic pathway they loop back to the chlorophyll they came from, making ATP on the way but nothing else.
[bio14-s3.2.0] In the cyclic pathway, some energised electrons leaving the reaction-centre chlorophyll pass along carriers of the electron transport chain and then return to the same chlorophyll molecule, instead of going on to NADP⁺.
[bio14-s3.2.1] As they pass along the carriers, they give up their extra energy, which is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio14-s3.2.2] Because the electrons come back, the chlorophyll is neutral again without needing electrons from water. So water is not split, no oxygen is released and no NADPH is made.
[bio14-s3.2.3] Products of the cyclic pathway: ATP only.
[bio14-s3.2.4] Why the plant needs it: the light-independent reactions use more ATP than NADPH, 3 ATP for every 2 NADPH for each CO₂ molecule fixed (built into sugar) in the light-independent reactions, the second stage that makes glucose. The cyclic pathway tops up the extra ATP.
[bio14-s3.2.5] It also runs when NADP⁺ is in short supply because most of it is already loaded as NADPH. The energised electrons then still have somewhere to go.
[bio14-s3.3.b] Exams ask you to tell the two pathways apart. The differences all come back to one thing: where the electrons end up.
[bio14-s3.3.0] Route of the electrons: non-cyclic goes one way, from water to NADP⁺; cyclic goes from the chlorophyll round the carriers and back to the same chlorophyll.
[bio14-s3.3.1] Final electron acceptor: NADP⁺ in the non-cyclic pathway; the chlorophyll molecule itself in the cyclic pathway.
[bio14-s3.3.2] Water: split by photolysis in the non-cyclic pathway, to replace the lost electrons; not needed in the cyclic pathway, because the electrons come back.
[bio14-s3.3.3] Products: non-cyclic makes ATP, NADPH and oxygen; cyclic makes ATP only.
[bio14-s3.3.4] What they share: both start with light energy reaching the reaction centre and an energised electron leaving the chlorophyll, both pass electrons along carriers to make ATP, and both happen on the thylakoid membranes.
[bio14-s3.3.5] Older Irish textbooks and past papers call the cyclic pathway pathway 1 and the non-cyclic pathway pathway 2, and describe NADP⁺ first picking up two electrons (becoming NADP⁻) and then a proton. Recognise these names, but learn the single step to NADPH given above.
[bio14-s3.3.6] Diagram: one energy diagram with chlorophyll at the bottom receiving light from pigment molecules; the electron rising to an acceptor; a downhill chain (ATP made) ending at NADP⁺ → NADPH; water entering below the chlorophyll; a loop back to the chlorophyll for the cyclic pathway (ATP made).
[bio14-s3.4.b] Put together, the light-dependent reactions turn light into a form of energy the rest of the chloroplast can use, and hand it on to the second stage.
[bio14-s3.4.0] Location: the thylakoid membranes (grana) of the chloroplast, because that is where the pigments and electron carriers are held.
[bio14-s3.4.1] Inputs: light energy, water, ADP + Pi and NADP⁺.
[bio14-s3.4.2] Outputs: ATP and NADPH, which pass into the stroma for the light-independent reactions, and oxygen, released as a by-product.
[bio14-s3.4.3] Energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in ATP and in the high-energy electrons carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s3.4.4] The two products have different jobs: ATP carries energy, and NADPH carries electrons and hydrogen to reduce carbon dioxide to glucose. After use, they return as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ to be reloaded. There is more on this in The light-independent reactions.

CANDIDATES bio14-s4: The light-independent reactions
[bio14-s4.0.b] The second stage of photosynthesis is where glucose is actually built. It uses the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage as its energy and hydrogen supply, and in return sends the empty carriers back.
[bio14-s4.0.0] Location: the stroma (the fluid that fills the chloroplast), which contains the enzymes for these reactions. The reactions need no light directly, but they depend on the ATP and NADPH made in the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoids.
[bio14-s4.0.1] 1. Carbon dioxide diffuses into the stroma, from the air spaces of the leaf through the cell and the chloroplast's membranes. It is taken up, or fixed, by combining with a carbon dioxide-accepting compound already present in the stroma.
[bio14-s4.0.2] 2. NADPH and ATP from the light-dependent reactions enter the reactions, because they supply the hydrogen and the energy that CO₂ needs to become glucose.
[bio14-s4.0.3] 3. Protons and electrons, that is hydrogen, are transferred from NADPH to the carbon compounds made from CO₂. This adds hydrogen to the carbon, which is reduction, turning CO₂ into carbohydrate. NADPH becomes NADP⁺.
[bio14-s4.0.4] 4. The energy to achieve this conversion comes from ATP, which is broken down to ADP + Pi (phosphate).
[bio14-s4.0.5] 5. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the product. Six molecules of CO₂ are fixed for each molecule of glucose, because each supplies one of its six carbon atoms. The glucose is then used, or converted to sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s4.0.6] 6. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return to the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes, where they are reloaded to ATP and NADPH and can be re-used.
[bio14-s4.0.7] The reactions form a cycle, the Calvin cycle. Most of the carbon compounds made regenerate the CO₂-accepting compound, so the cycle can keep fixing CO₂, and the rest is built into glucose.
[bio14-s4.1.b] Once you know the inputs and outputs, you can explain why this stage responds to temperature and CO₂ and why it stops soon after dark, even though it uses no light directly.
[bio14-s4.1.0] Summary in words: carbon dioxide + hydrogen (from NADPH) + energy (from ATP) → glucose, with NADP⁺ and ADP + Pi released, so the carriers are free to be reloaded.
[bio14-s4.1.1] Every step is catalysed by enzymes in the stroma, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, and falls when the enzymes are denatured. This is why temperature affects the rate of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s4.1.2] CO₂ is the raw material of this stage, so its supply directly limits the rate of glucose production.
[bio14-s4.1.3] In darkness the light-dependent reactions stop, so ATP and NADPH run out within a short time. The light-independent reactions then stop too, even though they do not use light directly.
[bio14-s4.2.b] Photosynthesis is best understood as two linked stages joined by recycled carrier molecules. Modelling it means showing what goes in and out of each stage and what the carriers move between them.
[bio14-s4.2.0] Stage 1, the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes (the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast): light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll, splits water and drives electron transfer. This produces O₂, which is released, and loads the transfer molecules: ADP + Pi → ATP and NADP⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.1] Stage 2, the light-independent reactions in the stroma: the transfer molecules are unloaded, which is why this stage needs stage 1. NADPH gives its protons and electrons to CO₂, and ATP gives the energy, producing glucose. NADPH → NADP⁺ and ATP → ADP + Pi.
[bio14-s4.2.2] The transfer molecules link the two stages. They carry energy (ATP) and electrons and hydrogen (NADPH) from stage 1 to stage 2, and return empty, as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺, from stage 2 to stage 1.
[bio14-s4.2.3] Because the transfer molecules are recycled, the chloroplast needs only a small supply of each.
[bio14-s4.2.4] Tracing the atoms: the oxygen gas comes from water (stage 1). The carbon and the oxygen in glucose come from CO₂ (stage 2). The hydrogen in glucose comes from water, carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.5] Diagram: thylakoid (stage 1) beside stroma (stage 2). Stage 1: in light, H₂O; out O₂. Stage 2: in CO₂; out glucose. Two loops between them: ATP across and ADP + Pi back, NADPH across and NADP⁺ back, each arrow labelled energy, or protons and electrons.

CANDIDATES bio14-s5: The chloroplast's internal structure
[bio14-s5.0.b] Photosynthesis happens inside the chloroplast, and its two stages happen in different parts of it. Each internal part has a structure that suits its job.
[bio14-s5.0.0] Envelope: two membranes, an outer and an inner, surround the chloroplast. The inner membrane controls which substances enter and leave, such as CO₂ going in and the products of photosynthesis going out, so the stroma (the fluid inside the envelope) keeps its enzymes and conditions separate from the cytoplasm.
[bio14-s5.0.1] Thylakoids are flattened membrane sacs inside the chloroplast, separate from the envelope. Each one encloses a fluid-filled space called the thylakoid space, or lumen.
[bio14-s5.0.2] Thylakoids are stacked like piles of coins into grana (singular: granum). Grana are joined by single, unstacked thylakoid membranes called intergranal lamellae, so the whole system is one connected network of membranes.
[bio14-s5.0.3] The thylakoid membranes hold the pigments (molecules that absorb light, such as chlorophyll) in their clusters and reaction centres, the electron transport chains (chains of electron carriers) and the enzyme ATP synthase. This is why they are the site of the light-dependent reactions, the first stage of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s5.0.4] The stroma contains the enzymes of the light-independent reactions, the second stage of photosynthesis, so it is the site of those reactions. It also holds starch grains, which are a temporary store of glucose, lipid droplets, ribosomes (the small structures that make proteins) and a small loop of DNA.
[bio14-s5.0.5] The chloroplast makes some of its own proteins, including some of the proteins of the thylakoid membrane and of the light-independent reactions, using its own DNA and ribosomes. There is more on this in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio14-s5.0.6] Diagram: an electron-microscope-level drawing of a chloroplast in section, labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, stroma, granum, thylakoid, thylakoid space, intergranal lamella, starch grain, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio14-s5.1.b] The light-dependent reactions need a chain that moves electrons along and releases their energy bit by bit. In the chloroplast, this chain is built into the thylakoid membrane.
[bio14-s5.1.0] An electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers that pass electrons from one to the next, each at a slightly lower energy level. It releases energy in small steps that can be used, such as water running downhill in stages, losing height and so energy at each step.
[bio14-s5.1.1] In the thylakoid membrane, the chain is made of a number of large protein complexes (large groups of proteins that work together) embedded in, or spanning, the membrane. Smaller mobile electron carriers that move within or along the membrane link the complexes, carrying electrons from one to the next.
[bio14-s5.1.2] Some protein complexes contain the clusters of pigment molecules and the reaction-centre chlorophyll, the special chlorophyll molecule that loses electrons when light hits it. Others pass electrons on and use their energy.
[bio14-s5.1.3] NADP⁺, a carrier that takes up electrons and hydrogen, is reduced (gains electrons) to NADPH, as NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH, on the stroma side of the membrane. This means NADPH is made right where the light-independent reactions use it.
[bio14-s5.1.4] Embedding the complexes in a membrane holds them in a fixed order and close together, so electrons pass quickly and in the right sequence. The energy released can move protons across the membrane, setting up a proton gradient (a difference in proton concentration across it) that drives ATP making.
[bio14-s5.2.b] The energy released by the electron transport chain is used to move protons (H⁺) into a small enclosed space. The protons then flow back out through an enzyme, and this flow powers the making of ATP.
[bio14-s5.2.0] As electrons pass along the chain, protons (H⁺, hydrogen ions) are moved from the stroma into the thylakoid space. Protons released by the photolysis of water (splitting water using light energy) are also released there, so the small, enclosed thylakoid space builds up a high concentration of protons.
[bio14-s5.2.1] Photolysis happens on the thylakoid-space side of the membrane, and its equation is 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂, so the protons it makes go straight into the thylakoid space.
[bio14-s5.2.2] The protons flow back out of the thylakoid space into the stroma through ATP synthase in the thylakoid membrane. The enzyme uses this flow to make ATP from ADP and phosphate (Pi), as ADP + Pi → ATP, on the stroma side, where the light-independent reactions need it.
[bio14-s5.2.3] The same mechanism makes ATP in mitochondria. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio14-s5.3.b] Exams ask how the structure of the chloroplast suits it to photosynthesis. The key idea is that each feature gives the reactions more room to work, or keeps them in the right place.
[bio14-s5.3.0] Large surface area: the many stacked thylakoids give a huge area of membrane for pigment clusters, electron transport chains and ATP synthase, so more light can be absorbed and more ATP and NADPH can be made.
[bio14-s5.3.1] Organisation of pigments: grouping many pigment molecules around each reaction centre funnels the energy of photons absorbed over a wide area, and over a range of colours, to the reaction centres. This means more of the light energy reaches the reaction centres.
[bio14-s5.3.2] Separate compartments: the thylakoid space is a small, enclosed compartment, so protons can be concentrated in it to drive ATP synthase. The thylakoid membrane is not freely permeable to protons, so the gradient is kept.
[bio14-s5.3.3] Two stages side by side: the light-dependent reactions on the membranes and the light-independent reactions in the stroma are next to each other, so ATP and NADPH are released into the stroma directly where they are used. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return only a short distance.
[bio14-s5.3.4] The stroma holds all the enzymes of the light-independent reactions in one compartment, with their substrates (the substances they act on), so they meet easily. It stores its glucose temporarily as starch.
[bio14-s5.3.5] Own DNA and ribosomes let the chloroplast make some of its own proteins on the spot, such as the membrane and enzyme proteins its two stages need.
[bio14-s5.3.6] Related: the palisade mesophyll, the leaf cells that do most photosynthesis, are packed with chloroplasts, which can move within the cell towards or away from the light.
[bio14-s5.3.7] Diagram: a thylakoid membrane, stroma above and thylakoid space below. Draw pigment clusters, protein complexes and electron carriers with electron arrows, water splitting and H⁺ building up inside, and ATP synthase making ATP in the stroma. Label thylakoid membrane, thylakoid space, stroma, protein complex, mobile electron carrier, ATP synthase.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.
