MODEL ANSWER: In negative feedback the response reverses the original change, so the factor returns towards its set point and the response then stops. Example: when thyroxine in the blood is low, the pituitary releases TSH, and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine. CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio15-s1: Respiration as a catabolic process
[bio15-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a constant supply of usable energy. Respiration is the process that gets it, by breaking down food molecules and transferring the energy they hold to ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio15-s1.0.0] Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food, mainly glucose, in every living cell. The energy released is transferred to ATP, so respiration releases energy rather than making it.
[bio15-s1.0.1] Respiration is catabolic, meaning a large molecule such as glucose is broken down into smaller molecules and energy is released. The products are CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂, depending on the conditions.
[bio15-s1.0.2] Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process, because it builds small molecules up into large ones and takes in energy, whereas respiration breaks large ones down.
[bio15-s1.0.3] The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once, as in burning. This is why much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. Energy not trapped as ATP is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
[bio15-s1.0.4] The ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport (moving substances across a membrane using energy) and building large molecules.
[bio15-s1.0.5] Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed (built into sugar).
[bio15-s1.0.6] Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake of carbon by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle. There is more on this in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio15-s1.0.7] Glucose is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down). Glycogen (in animals) and starch (in plants) are first broken down to glucose, and fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired.
[bio15-s1.1.b] Aerobic (with oxygen) respiration breaks glucose down completely, releasing energy. Knowing where the glucose and oxygen come from, and where the carbon dioxide and water go, shows how organisms and their surroundings are linked.
[bio15-s1.1.0] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
[bio15-s1.1.1] Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Its chemicals are those of the photosynthesis equation reversed, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
[bio15-s1.1.2] Source of glucose in plants: photosynthesis, or stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant). In animals: digested food absorbed into the blood, or glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
[bio15-s1.1.3] Source of oxygen in humans: the air, taken in through the lungs and carried to the cells by red blood cells, which is how it reaches every cell.
[bio15-s1.1.4] Source of oxygen in plants: the air, entering through stomata (leaf pores) and lenticels (pores in woody stems); soil air, taken in by the roots; and the plant's own photosynthesis. In aquatic organisms it is oxygen dissolved in water.
[bio15-s1.1.5] Fate of carbon dioxide: it diffuses out of cells. In humans it is carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
[bio15-s1.1.6] Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted. Some is breathed out as water vapour.
[bio15-s1.2.b] When oxygen is scarce or absent, cells can still release some energy from glucose, but not nearly as much. The products depend on the organism.
[bio15-s1.2.0] Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. Because the glucose is only partly broken down, much of the energy stays in the products, so much less energy is released per glucose and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.2.1] In animals, such as human muscle during intense exercise, and in some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
[bio15-s1.2.2] In yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen such as roots in waterlogged soil, the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
[bio15-s1.2.3] Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. In baking, yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise and the ethanol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and wine-making, yeast's ethanol is the useful product. In making yogurt and cheese, bacteria's lactic acid is the useful product.
[bio15-s1.2.4] In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire.
[bio15-s1.2.5] Oxygen debt: after exercise, extra oxygen is needed to remove the lactic acid. It is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose, and the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
[bio15-s1.2.6] Obligate anaerobes respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen, for example the bacteria that cause tetanus. Yeast can respire either way, so it is a facultative anaerobe.
[bio15-s1.3.b] Respiration happens in two stages in different parts of the cell. Stage 1 is the same whether or not oxygen is present, and what happens next depends on oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid inside the cell) of every cell and does not need oxygen. 1. Glucose, with 6 carbon atoms, is split into two molecules of pyruvate, each with 3 carbon atoms. 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
[bio15-s1.3.1] With oxygen, stage 2 takes place in the mitochondria (the organelles where aerobic respiration is completed). 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water.
[bio15-s1.3.2] 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.3] Without oxygen, pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (in animals and some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast and plants). No further ATP is made, so anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s1.3.4] Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria, so their aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane.
[bio15-s1.3.5] Diagram: flow diagram. Glucose (cytoplasm), then glycolysis (small ATP), then 2 pyruvate. Oxygen present: mitochondrion, then CO₂, water, large ATP. No oxygen: cytoplasm, then lactic acid (animals) or ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1, stage 2.
[bio15-s1.4.b] Aerobic and anaerobic respiration start the same way but differ in how far glucose is broken down and how much ATP results. Respiration also contrasts with photosynthesis: respiration is catabolic and happens in all living cells, while photosynthesis is anabolic and happens only in cells with chloroplasts.
[bio15-s1.4.0] Oxygen: needed in aerobic respiration; not needed in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.1] Location: aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria; anaerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm only.
[bio15-s1.4.2] Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O, in aerobic respiration; incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂, in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.3] Energy: a large amount of ATP in aerobic respiration; a small amount, only the ATP made in glycolysis, in anaerobic respiration, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio15-s1.4.4] Stages: stage 1 followed by stage 2 in aerobic respiration; stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.5] Respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂. It happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
[bio15-s1.4.6] Photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂. It happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only in the light.

CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio20-s1.10.5] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; oestrogen; female secondary sexual characteristics and thickening of the endometrium.
[bio20-s1.10.6] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; progesterone; maintains the endometrium and pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio20-s2: Hormonal manipulation
[bio20-s2.0.b] Hormones are chemical messengers that control growth and body functions. Because they act in tiny amounts, people can change how an organism works by adding hormones or blocking them. This is called hormonal manipulation, and it is used in sport, health and agriculture.
[bio20-s2.0.0] Hormonal manipulation is the deliberate use of natural or synthetic (man-made) hormones, or of drugs that block hormones, to change how an organism grows or functions.
[bio20-s2.0.1] It can work by adding a hormone the body lacks or by supplying extra, or by blocking a hormone's release or action, so the body's own control system is deliberately altered.
[bio20-s2.0.2] The syllabus asks you to recognise the impact of these manipulations on organisms, so for each use you need the benefit and the cost.
[bio20-s2.1.b] Some athletes take hormones to gain a physical edge. Two are misused: anabolic steroids, which build muscle, and EPO, which boosts the blood's oxygen supply. Both are dangerous and banned.
[bio20-s2.1.0] Anabolic steroids are synthetic derivatives of testosterone (the male sex hormone). They increase muscle mass and strength and speed up recovery from training, which is why athletes misuse them.
[bio20-s2.1.1] Anabolic steroids cause liver damage, increase the risk of heart disease, and cause acne and mood changes including aggression.
[bio20-s2.1.2] In males, anabolic steroids reduce sperm production, cause infertility and cause shrinking of the testes.
[bio20-s2.1.3] In females, anabolic steroids cause male characteristics to develop, because the drug is a form of a male hormone.
[bio20-s2.1.4] EPO (erythropoietin) is a hormone made by the kidneys that stimulates red blood cell production.
[bio20-s2.1.5] Athletes misuse EPO to increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, because red blood cells carry oxygen, and so their endurance.
[bio20-s2.1.6] Extra red cells thicken the blood, which increases the risk of clots, heart attack and stroke.
[bio20-s2.1.7] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) bans these substances in competitive sport and tests athletes through urine and blood samples. Their use is unfair to other competitors.
[bio20-s2.2.b] Doctors use hormones to replace ones the body no longer makes enough of, to control fertility and to relieve symptoms of the menopause such as hot flushes. Each treatment fixes a specific hormone problem.
[bio20-s2.2.0] Insulin injections treat type 1 diabetes, in which the pancreas makes little or no insulin, so blood glucose would otherwise rise too high.
[bio20-s2.2.1] Most insulin used today is human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria (bacteria given the human insulin gene). There is more on this in Genetic Engineering.
[bio20-s2.2.2] The contraceptive pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone, or progesterone alone. These are female sex hormones.
[bio20-s2.2.3] The pill inhibits (reduces) the release of FSH and LH, the pituitary hormones that make an egg mature and be released, so no egg matures and ovulation (release of an egg) is prevented.
[bio20-s2.2.4] Fertility treatment uses hormones to stimulate the ovaries to release eggs, which helps women who do not ovulate normally to become pregnant.
[bio20-s2.2.5] Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) supplies oestrogen, often with progesterone, after the menopause (when the ovaries stop producing these hormones) to relieve symptoms such as hot flushes and to reduce bone loss.
[bio20-s2.2.6] For some women, HRT slightly increases the risk of some cancers and of blood clots, so it carries a risk as well as a benefit.
[bio20-s2.2.7] Thyroxine tablets are given to people whose thyroid gland produces too little thyroxine, replacing the missing hormone.
[bio20-s2.3.b] Farmers use hormones to control breeding, boost yields and manage plants. Animal uses raise welfare and food-safety concerns, while plant hormones are used as weedkillers, rooting aids and ripening agents.
[bio20-s2.3.0] Progesterone, given as a vaginal sponge or implant, synchronises the breeding cycles of cattle and sheep, so a herd can be bred and give birth at the same time.
[bio20-s2.3.1] Growth-promoting hormones increase meat production in cattle.
[bio20-s2.3.2] Bovine somatotropin (BST), a growth hormone from cattle, increases milk yield in cattle.
[bio20-s2.3.3] Growth-promoting hormones and BST are banned in the EU, including Ireland, because of concerns about animal welfare and hormone residues (traces left behind) in food. BST is permitted in the USA.
[bio20-s2.3.4] Synthetic auxins such as 2,4-D are selective weedkillers (they kill some plants but not others). They kill broad-leaved weeds in cereal crops by making them grow uncontrollably.
[bio20-s2.3.5] Auxin rooting powder makes cuttings (pieces cut from a plant) form roots, so new plants can be grown from them.
[bio20-s2.3.6] Ethene gas is used to ripen fruit such as bananas in storage after transport, so fruit can be shipped unripe and ripened when needed.
[bio20-s2.4.b] Hormonal manipulation brings real gains but also real harms. To evaluate it, weigh the benefits against the costs for each use.
[bio20-s2.4.0] Benefits: better health, treatment of disease (such as insulin for type 1 diabetes), increased food production and convenience (such as synchronised breeding and ripening on demand).
[bio20-s2.4.1] Costs: side effects (such as those of anabolic steroids, EPO and HRT), an unfair advantage in sport, animal welfare concerns, hormone residues in food and environmental effects of weedkillers.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio24-s4: Fruit and seed dispersal
[bio24-s4.0.b] Dispersal is the carrying of fruits and seeds away from the parent plant. Plants cannot move, so dispersal is how they spread their offspring, and it gives the young plants a much better chance of surviving.
[bio24-s4.0.0] Dispersal reduces competition between the young plants and the parent, and among the young plants themselves, for light, water, minerals and space, because seeds spread out are not all drawing on the same patch of soil.
[bio24-s4.0.1] Dispersal lets the species colonise new areas and new habitats, so it can grow beyond the place where the parent happens to live.
[bio24-s4.0.2] Dispersal spreads the population out, so a local disaster, such as disease, fire or flooding, is less likely to destroy all of it.
[bio24-s4.0.3] Seeds that fall directly under the parent are usually shaded and short of water and minerals, so few survive. Plants produce many seeds because most are lost during dispersal or eaten.
[bio24-s4.0.4] Sustainability link: the ability of some plants to spread their seeds widely makes them invasive (they take over) when introduced to new countries, e.g. Himalayan balsam, an invasive plant along Irish rivers with exploding seed pods.
[bio24-s4.1.b] Dispersal works through fruits, so it helps to know what a fruit is. The type of fruit a plant makes decides which dispersal method suits it.
[bio24-s4.1.0] A fruit is a mature ovary (the part of the flower that holds the ovules) containing seeds; its wall is the pericarp. It protects the seeds and is adapted to disperse them. Botanically, many foods called vegetables are fruits, e.g. tomatoes, cucumbers and pea pods.
[bio24-s4.1.1] Fleshy (succulent) fruits have a soft, juicy pericarp, often brightly coloured and sweet when ripe, e.g. blackberry, tomato and cherry; they are mostly dispersed by animals that eat them.
[bio24-s4.1.2] Dry fruits have a hard or papery pericarp, e.g. pea pod, hazelnut and sycamore; they are dispersed by wind, water, animals carrying them, or by splitting open.
[bio24-s4.1.3] A false fruit is one in which other parts of the flower, not only the ovary, form the fruit, e.g. strawberry, where the fleshy part is the receptacle (the swollen top of the flower stalk).
[bio24-s4.2.b] Wind carries a fruit or seed further the more slowly it falls. Wind-dispersed plants therefore make fruits or seeds that are light and have a large surface area compared with their mass.
[bio24-s4.2.0] Adaptations: fruits or seeds are small and light, or have wings or parachutes that increase their surface area, so they fall slowly and are carried further by the wind.
[bio24-s4.2.1] Parachutes: dandelion and thistle fruits each carry a parachute of fine hairs that lets them float long distances.
[bio24-s4.2.2] Wings: sycamore has paired winged fruits and ash has single winged fruits. The wing makes the fruit spin like a helicopter blade as it falls, slowing it so the wind carries it away from the parent.
[bio24-s4.2.3] Censer (pepper-pot) mechanism: the poppy's dry fruit, a capsule, has a ring of pores near the top. When the wind sways the stalk, the tiny seeds are shaken out a few at a time, like pepper from a shaker.
[bio24-s4.2.4] Dust-like seeds: orchid seeds are so tiny and light that they are blown like dust.
[bio24-s4.3.b] Plants growing beside or on water use it to carry their offspring. The key adaptation is the ability to float, so the fruit is carried rather than sinking.
[bio24-s4.3.0] Adaptations: fruits or seeds that float, because they contain air spaces or have a light, waterproof covering, so they can be carried by streams, rivers or the sea.
[bio24-s4.3.1] Coconut: the fruit has a thick, fibrous, air-filled husk and a waterproof outer layer, so it floats for long periods and is carried by ocean currents to other shores and islands.
[bio24-s4.3.2] Alder: a tree of riverbanks whose small fruits contain air and float, and are carried downstream to settle on new banks.
[bio24-s4.4.b] Some plants use animals as couriers by offering them food. The animal eats the fruit, and the seeds travel inside it before being left somewhere new.
[bio24-s4.4.0] Fleshy fruits are eaten by birds and mammals. The animal digests the fleshy pericarp, but the seeds have a tough testa (the hard seed coat) or a hard stone that resists digestion. 
[bio24-s4.4.1] The seeds pass out in the faeces, often far from the parent, and the droppings act as a supply of minerals.
[bio24-s4.4.2] Adaptations: bright colours (often red, black or purple) when ripe, attracting birds; sweet, juicy, nutritious flesh as a reward for the animal.
[bio24-s4.4.3] Unripe fruit is green and sour while the seeds are unripe, so the fruit isn't eaten too early, before the seeds can survive and grow.
[bio24-s4.4.4] Examples: blackberry, holly berries and rowan berries (eaten by birds), and tomato.
[bio24-s4.4.5] Animal caching: squirrels and jays bury acorns and hazelnuts as winter stores and fail to recover some, which then grow into new trees.
[bio24-s4.5.b] Other plants do not offer food. Instead their fruits hitch a ride on the outside of an animal.
[bio24-s4.5.0] Fruits have hooks or sticky surfaces that catch on the fur or feathers of passing animals (or on human clothing). They fall or are brushed off later, away from the parent.
[bio24-s4.5.1] Examples: burdock has hooked burs; goosegrass (cleavers) has small fruits covered in hooked hairs.
[bio24-s4.5.2] Technology link: the hooked burs of burdock inspired Velcro, invented by the Swiss engineer George de Mestral after he found burs stuck to his dog's coat.
[bio24-s4.6.b] A few plants need no outside carrier. The fruit itself throws the seeds out.
[bio24-s4.6.0] Some dry fruits split open forcefully as they dry, flinging the seeds out: gorse and pea pods twist and burst in dry weather, and Himalayan balsam pods explode when touched.
[bio24-s4.7.b] Each method of dispersal goes with a matching set of adaptations and named examples. Learn them as method, adaptation and example together.
[bio24-s4.7.0] Wind: wings, parachutes and light seeds (sycamore, ash, dandelion, poppy).
[bio24-s4.7.1] Water: floating, air-filled fruits (coconut, alder).
[bio24-s4.7.2] Animal, internal: fleshy, coloured, edible fruits with indigestible seeds (blackberry, holly).
[bio24-s4.7.3] Animal, external: hooks (burdock, goosegrass).
[bio24-s4.7.4] Self-dispersal: exploding pods (gorse, pea).
[bio24-s4.7.5] Diagram: labelled drawings of one fruit for each method: sycamore (wing and seed), dandelion (parachute of hairs and the fruit containing a seed), burdock bur (hooks) and blackberry (fleshy fruit and seeds). A coconut in section (fibrous, air-filled husk and seed) is optional.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.

CANDIDATES bio30-s4: CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata
[bio30-s4.0.b] Stomata are the tiny pores in a leaf's surface through which CO₂ enters and water vapour escapes. Their width is not fixed: the plant adjusts it in response to several signals, and the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is one of them.
[bio30-s4.0.0] The concentration of CO₂ in the air spaces inside the leaf (the gaps between the cells) is one of the factors that controls the width of the stomatal pores, along with light and water supply.
[bio30-s4.0.1] A low concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf causes the stomata to open, or to open wider, so that more CO₂ can diffuse in.
[bio30-s4.0.2] A high concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf causes the stomata to close, or partly close, so less gas passes through the pore.
[bio30-s4.0.3] Each stoma is surrounded by two guard cells, which change shape to open or close the pore. Guard cells respond to the CO₂ concentration around them, so the response is local to each stoma.
[bio30-s4.0.4] Low CO₂ leads to potassium ions (K⁺) being taken into the guard cells, water entering by osmosis and the guard cells becoming turgid (swollen and firm), so the pore opens.
[bio30-s4.0.5] High CO₂ leads to K⁺ leaving the guard cells, water following by osmosis and the guard cells becoming flaccid (limp), so the pore closes.
[bio30-s4.0.6] The response is to the concentration inside the leaf, which depends on the balance between photosynthesis, which uses CO₂, and respiration, which releases it. It is not simply the concentration in the outside air.
[bio30-s4.1.b] The CO₂ response is useful because the CO₂ level inside a leaf changes with the balance between photosynthesis and respiration, so the stomata open when CO₂ is needed and close when it is not.
[bio30-s4.1.0] In the light, photosynthesis uses CO₂ faster than respiration produces it, so the CO₂ concentration in the air spaces falls. The low CO₂ opens the stomata, letting in more CO₂ for photosynthesis exactly when it is needed.
[bio30-s4.1.1] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, but respiration continues, so CO₂ builds up in the air spaces. The high CO₂ closes the stomata.
[bio30-s4.1.2] The plant does not need CO₂ in the dark, so closing the stomata saves water that would otherwise be lost by transpiration (evaporation of water vapour through the open stomata).
[bio30-s4.1.3] This is a negative feedback loop (a change sets off a response that reverses the change). When internal CO₂ is low, the stomata open and CO₂ enters, so its concentration rises; as it rises, the stomata begin to close.
[bio30-s4.1.4] CO₂ concentration therefore helps match the opening of the stomata to the leaf's demand for CO₂, keeping the balance between taking in CO₂ and losing water. There is more on negative feedback in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio30-s4.1.5] The CO₂ response works together with the other controls. Light opens the stomata directly as well as by lowering CO₂ through photosynthesis.
[bio30-s4.1.6] Shortage of water closes the stomata even when CO₂ is low, because preventing wilting takes priority over photosynthesis. So on a hot, dry afternoon the stomata may close although the leaf needs CO₂, and photosynthesis slows.
[bio30-s4.1.7] Experimental evidence: if a leaf in the light is given air with extra CO₂, its stomata close partly. If a leaf in the dark is given CO₂-free air, its stomata open partly. This shows that CO₂ acts on the stomata on its own, separately from light.
[bio30-s4.2.b] Stomata are the main route by which a leaf loses water, so anything that changes their width also changes the rate of transpiration. This links CO₂ to water loss, and to wider questions about farming and climate.
[bio30-s4.2.0] Because CO₂ controls the width of the stomata, it affects transpiration. Raising the CO₂ concentration around a leaf closes the stomata partly and reduces the rate of transpiration; lowering it opens them and increases the rate.
[bio30-s4.2.1] This is why CO₂ concentration is one of the factors in Investigation: rate of transpiration.
[bio30-s4.2.2] Sustainability link: the CO₂ concentration of the atmosphere is rising, mainly from burning fossil fuels. Plants grown in raised CO₂ tend to open their stomata less, so they lose less water per unit of sugar made.
[bio30-s4.2.3] Leaves of the same species collected over the last two centuries and kept in herbarium collections (preserved plant specimens) show fewer stomata per unit area as CO₂ has risen.
[bio30-s4.2.4] Technology link: glasshouse growers add CO₂ to raise photosynthesis. The extra CO₂ also partly closes the stomata, so the plants lose less water.
[bio30-s4.3.b] Plants and humans both use CO₂ concentration to control gas exchange, but they respond in opposite ways. Comparing them shows that the difference comes from what CO₂ means to each organism.
[bio30-s4.3.0] What is detected, in plants: the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf, detected by the guard cells themselves. Plants have no nerves, so the response is local, in each stoma.
[bio30-s4.3.1] What is detected, in humans: the CO₂ concentration of the blood, through its effect on blood pH. Chemoreceptors (cells that detect chemical change) in the medulla oblongata and in the carotid and aortic bodies detect it and signal the breathing centre in the medulla.
[bio30-s4.3.2] Response to high CO₂: in plants the stomata close, reducing gas exchange. In humans the rate and depth of breathing increase, increasing gas exchange.
[bio30-s4.3.3] Response to low CO₂: in plants the stomata open. In humans breathing slows.
[bio30-s4.3.4] Why the responses are opposite, in plants: for a plant in the light, CO₂ is a raw material for photosynthesis, so low internal CO₂ signals that more is needed, and high internal CO₂ signals that none is being used, so water should be saved.
[bio30-s4.3.5] Why the responses are opposite, in humans: CO₂ is a waste product of respiration that makes the blood more acidic, so high CO₂ signals that more must be removed.
[bio30-s4.3.6] What is similar: in both, CO₂ concentration is the main controlling signal for gas exchange, and the control works by negative feedback, returning the CO₂ level towards normal.
[bio30-s4.3.7] In both, the controlled structures adjust how much gas passes across the exchange surface: the stomata in plants, and the diaphragm and intercostal muscles that ventilate the alveoli in humans. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
