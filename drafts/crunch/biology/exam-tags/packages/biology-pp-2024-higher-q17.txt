CANDIDATES bio13-s1: Enzymes as catalysts
[bio13-s1.0.b] Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions, and left alone most of them would be far too slow to keep a cell alive. Catalysts speed reactions up, and enzymes are the catalysts that living things make for themselves.
[bio13-s1.0.0] A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by it, so it can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.0.1] A catalyst does not change which products form or how much product is made in the end; it only makes the reaction reach that point faster.
[bio13-s1.0.2] An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a catalyst made by a living cell.
[bio13-s1.0.3] Almost all enzymes are globular proteins (proteins folded into a compact, rounded ball), folded into a precise three-dimensional shape. That shape decides what the enzyme can do. There is more on protein structure in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s1.0.4] Because enzymes are proteins, they are made at ribosomes (the tiny structures in a cell where proteins are built), following the instructions in a gene (the length of DNA that codes for the protein).
[bio13-s1.1.b] Because an enzyme is a protein that is reused rather than consumed, it has a set of properties that follow from that.
[bio13-s1.1.0] Enzymes are needed only in small amounts, because each molecule is reused many times; catalase, among the fastest, breaks down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second.
[bio13-s1.1.1] Enzymes are unchanged at the end of the reaction, which is why the same molecule can start work on another substrate straight away.
[bio13-s1.1.2] Enzymes are specific: each acts on one reaction or one type of reaction, because only a substrate of the right shape fits the enzyme's active site (the region where the substrate binds).
[bio13-s1.1.3] Enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH, and enzymes are denatured (their shape is changed so that they stop working) by heat and by extremes of pH. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio13-s1.2.b] Enzyme names usually give away the job the enzyme does. Enzymes also work in different places, depending on whether their job is inside or outside the cell.
[bio13-s1.2.0] Most enzyme names end in -ase, and many are named after their substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on), so the name tells you the job: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins, sucrase on sucrose.
[bio13-s1.2.1] Some older names, such as pepsin and trypsin, don't follow this rule, so don't assume that every enzyme name ends in -ase.
[bio13-s1.2.2] Most enzymes work inside the cell that made them (intracellular), for example the enzymes of respiration (releasing energy from food) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and of photosynthesis (making food using light) in chloroplasts.
[bio13-s1.2.3] Some enzymes are secreted to work outside the cell (extracellular), for example digestive enzymes released into the gut and the enzymes that fungi and bacteria release onto their food. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio13-s1.3.b] Even a reaction that releases energy overall needs a push to get started. An enzyme works by making that push smaller, not by adding energy.
[bio13-s1.3.0] Activation energy is the minimum energy that reacting molecules need before a reaction can start: the energy needed to break or strain existing bonds.
[bio13-s1.3.1] Think of activation energy as an energy hill that must be climbed even when the reaction releases energy overall.
[bio13-s1.3.2] Without a catalyst, most reactions of metabolism (the chemical reactions of life) have such a high activation energy that, at body temperature (37 °C), they would happen far too slowly to keep a cell alive.
[bio13-s1.3.3] Heating would supply the energy, but high temperatures would damage cells and their proteins, so heating is not an option for living things.
[bio13-s1.3.4] An enzyme provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at the temperature of the cell, and the reaction is many times faster.
[bio13-s1.3.5] The overall energy change of the reaction (energy released or taken in) stays the same; only the hill to be climbed gets lower.
[bio13-s1.3.6] Diagram: energy-profile graph, energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis. Reactants high on the left, products lower on the right.
[bio13-s1.3.7] Draw two humps, a tall one labelled without enzyme and a lower one labelled with enzyme. Mark each activation energy with a vertical arrow from the reactants to the top of its hump.
[bio13-s1.4.b] Every enzyme reaction has two partners: the enzyme and the molecule it works on. One small region of the enzyme does the work, and its shape is what makes the enzyme selective.
[bio13-s1.4.0] The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on; the products are the molecules made.
[bio13-s1.4.1] Example: catalase acts on its substrate hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
[bio13-s1.4.2] The active site is a small region of the enzyme, a pocket or groove on its surface, where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place.
[bio13-s1.4.3] The active site's shape, and the chemical charges in it, are complementary to the substrate, meaning the two fit together like matching pieces.
[bio13-s1.4.4] The active site is a part of the enzyme, not of the substrate. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site, not the same as it.
[bio13-s1.5.b] Once the substrate meets the active site, the reaction follows three steps. Together they make the active-site (lock-and-key) model of enzyme action.
[bio13-s1.5.0] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex (the enzyme and substrate joined together).
[bio13-s1.5.1] Enzyme and substrate molecules meet by random movement, so anything that increases collisions (more substrate, more enzyme, higher temperature) increases the rate, up to a point.
[bio13-s1.5.2] 2. While the substrate is held in the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, for example by straining the bonds that must break, or by holding two substrate molecules close together in the right position to react.
[bio13-s1.5.3] The substrate is then converted to products, forming an enzyme–product complex.
[bio13-s1.5.4] 3. The products have a different shape, no longer fit the active site, and are released.
[bio13-s1.5.5] The active site is then free and unchanged, ready to bind another substrate molecule, which is why one enzyme molecule can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.5.6] In summary: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products.
[bio13-s1.5.7] Diagram: three stages in a row: enzyme with active site and complementary substrate approaching; enzyme–substrate complex; unchanged enzyme with two products leaving. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex and products. A matching diagram for a joining reaction is optional.
[bio13-s1.6.b] The same active-site mechanism works whichever way a reaction runs. Metabolism has reactions that break molecules down and reactions that build them up, and enzymes catalyse both.
[bio13-s1.6.0] In catabolic reactions one substrate is split into smaller products, for example amylase splits starch into maltose.
[bio13-s1.6.1] In anabolic reactions two or more substrates are joined into a larger product. For example, DNA polymerase joins nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA) into a new DNA strand when DNA is copied.
[bio13-s1.6.2] Another anabolic example is ATP synthase, which joins ADP and phosphate into ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy.
[bio13-s1.7.b] A cell contains thousands of different molecules but does not react them all at random. Enzymes are selective: each one picks out its own substrate, and that selectivity comes from the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s1.7.0] Specificity means that each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with the complementary shape fits its active site.
[bio13-s1.7.1] This is what selective catalysts means: amylase digests starch but not protein or fat; pepsin digests protein but not starch.
[bio13-s1.7.2] The lock-and-key model is the simplest explanation: the substrate (the key) fits only the active site (the lock) of the right shape.
[bio13-s1.7.3] The lock-and-key model was later refined to the induced-fit model, in which the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio13-s1.7.4] Because enzymes are specific, a cell needs thousands of different enzymes, one for each of its reactions, and it can control a reaction by making, or not making, the one enzyme that catalyses it.
[bio13-s1.7.5] Anything that changes the shape of the active site stops the substrate fitting, so the enzyme stops working: for example high temperature or extreme pH (denaturation).
[bio13-s1.7.6] A change in the enzyme's amino-acid sequence (the order of the amino acids that make up the protein), caused by a mutation in its gene, also changes the active site and stops the substrate fitting.
[bio13-s1.8.b] Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in a living thing. Enzymes make these reactions possible, and they usually work in sequences called pathways.
[bio13-s1.8.0] Every reaction of metabolism is catalysed by its own specific enzyme.
[bio13-s1.8.1] Without enzymes, the reactions that keep cells alive (respiration, photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA copying, digestion) would be far too slow at the temperatures and pH inside living things.
[bio13-s1.8.2] Most processes are metabolic pathways: a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme, in which the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next.
[bio13-s1.8.3] Respiration and photosynthesis are such pathways. There is more on this in Respiration and Photosynthesis.
[bio13-s1.8.4] Diagram: a pathway written as A → B → C → D, with enzyme 1, enzyme 2 and enzyme 3 written over the arrows.
[bio13-s1.9.b] Because every step in a pathway needs its own enzyme, a cell controls its chemistry by controlling its enzymes.
[bio13-s1.9.0] A cell controls which reactions happen, and how fast, by switching on or off the genes for particular enzymes and by activating or inhibiting enzymes already made.
[bio13-s1.9.1] If one enzyme in a pathway is missing, the pathway stops at that step: its substrate builds up and the later products are not made.
[bio13-s1.9.2] Many enzymes need a non-protein helper to work: a mineral ion (for example magnesium for many enzymes that use ATP) or a coenzyme made from a vitamin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio13-s1.10.b] Because each reaction depends on one enzyme, blocking or losing that enzyme has serious effects. Some poisons act this way, and some inherited or common conditions come from a missing enzyme.
[bio13-s1.10.0] Some drugs and poisons work by blocking an enzyme: for example some insecticides and nerve agents inhibit the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter (a chemical messenger between nerve cells). There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.
[bio13-s1.10.1] Lactose intolerance: many adults make little lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose (milk sugar), so undigested lactose passes to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing bloating, wind, cramps and diarrhoea.
[bio13-s1.10.2] Lactose-free milk is made using lactase, which digests the lactose in the milk before it is drunk.
[bio13-s1.10.3] Phenylketonuria (PKU): an inherited lack of the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine into another amino acid (tyrosine), so phenylalanine builds up in the blood and damages the developing brain.
[bio13-s1.10.4] PKU is detected by the heel-prick test given to newborn babies in Ireland, and is controlled by a diet low in phenylalanine.

CANDIDATES bio13-s2: Enzyme structure and specificity
[bio13-s2.0.b] An enzyme is a protein, and the way its chain is folded decides what it can do. Folding builds a small pocket called the active site (the region where the substrate, the molecule the enzyme acts on, binds and is converted).
[bio13-s2.0.0] An enzyme's three-dimensional shape is set by the sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain (its primary structure), and that sequence is set by its gene, so the gene ultimately decides the shape.
[bio13-s2.0.1] The chain coils and folds (secondary structure), then folds further into a precise globular (rounded) shape (tertiary structure); some enzymes are made of more than one polypeptide (quaternary structure).
[bio13-s2.0.2] The active site is formed by folding: it is made of a small number of amino acids, often far apart in the chain, that folding brings together into a pocket or cleft.
[bio13-s2.0.3] Most of the rest of the enzyme holds these few active-site amino acids in exactly the right positions, so the whole molecule matters even though only a small part touches the substrate.
[bio13-s2.0.4] The R groups (side groups, the part of each amino acid that differs from the others) of the active-site amino acids give the site its shape and its pattern of charges and of water-attracting and water-repelling regions.
[bio13-s2.0.5] The R groups bind the substrate by weak attractions (hydrogen bonds and ionic attractions between opposite charges), and some take part directly in the reaction.
[bio13-s2.1.b] An enzyme acts only on particular substrates, a property called specificity. It comes from the active site's shape and chemistry, and that shape is only as stable as the bonds that hold the protein folded.
[bio13-s2.1.0] Specificity depends on two things: the shape of the active site (only a substrate of complementary shape fits) and its chemistry (only a substrate with matching charges and groups is held in the right position).
[bio13-s2.1.1] A molecule of similar shape but with the wrong charges is not catalysed, because its groups are not held in the correct position.
[bio13-s2.1.2] The shape is held by hydrogen bonds, by ionic bonds between charged R groups, and by attractions between water-repelling R groups; these are all weak bonds.
[bio13-s2.1.3] Disulfide (sulfur) bridges between cysteine units (cysteine is an amino acid that contains sulfur) are strong covalent bonds that also hold the shape.
[bio13-s2.1.4] Because most of these bonds are weak, the shape is easily changed, which is why enzymes are sensitive to conditions.
[bio13-s2.1.5] Diagram: a folded polypeptide chain drawn as a looping ribbon, with three or four amino acids (circles) from distant parts of the chain meeting to form a labelled active site; label polypeptide chain, R groups of active-site amino acids, active site, a hydrogen bond and a disulfide bridge.
[bio13-s2.2.b] An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its exact shape. Anything that alters the shape of the protein, or the amino acids that make it, changes how well it works.
[bio13-s2.2.0] Activity depends on the active site keeping its shape.
[bio13-s2.2.1] Heat makes the enzyme molecule vibrate until weak bonds break; a change in pH changes the charges on R groups (by adding or removing hydrogen ions), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds.
[bio13-s2.2.2] Either way the active site loses its shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured (permanently changed in shape and unable to work). The effects on rate, and the investigations, are in “Investigation: rate of enzyme activity”.
[bio13-s2.2.3] A change in a single amino acid can change the shape or charge of the active site, so a mutation in the gene can make an enzyme work less well or not at all.
[bio13-s2.2.4] Example: in phenylketonuria the enzyme that converts phenylalanine is faulty, so the conversion does not happen properly.
[bio13-s2.2.5] Some enzymes need a cofactor, such as a metal ion, held in or near the active site to complete its shape or chemistry; without it the enzyme is inactive.
[bio13-s2.3.b] Enzymes differ in how narrowly they choose a substrate. Some enzymes also have structures that keep their shape in conditions that would ruin most others.
[bio13-s2.3.0] Some enzymes act on only one substrate (absolute specificity): for example, urease acts only on urea, breaking it into ammonia and carbon dioxide.
[bio13-s2.3.1] Others act on a group of substrates that share the same type of bond (group specificity): for example, lipase acts on many different triglycerides, and proteases such as pepsin break peptide bonds in many different proteins.
[bio13-s2.3.2] A rigid lock-and-key active site does not explain group specificity well, because one fixed shape cannot fit many different substrates; the induced-fit model does.
[bio13-s2.3.3] Enzymes that work in extreme conditions have structures that hold their shape there: for example, Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, stays active at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s2.3.4] Pepsin keeps its shape in the strong acid of the stomach, where most enzymes are denatured.
[bio13-s2.4.b] The induced-fit model describes an active site that is flexible, not a fixed mould. It explains how an enzyme grips its substrate, strains it and releases the products, then is ready to go again.
[bio13-s2.4.0] The induced-fit model says the active site is flexible, not rigid. Before binding, its shape is close to, but not exactly, complementary to the substrate; binding of the substrate induces (causes) a change in the enzyme's shape that produces a close fit.
[bio13-s2.4.1] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and starts to bind to the active site through weak attractions to its R groups.
[bio13-s2.4.2] 2. Binding causes the enzyme to change shape: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove taking the shape of a hand, forming a tightly fitting enzyme–substrate complex.
[bio13-s2.4.3] 3. The change in shape puts strain on particular bonds in the substrate, or brings reacting groups of two substrates close together in the correct orientation.
[bio13-s2.4.4] 3 (continued). The R groups of the active site may lend or take hydrogen ions or electrons, which lowers the activation energy (the energy needed to start the reaction), and the substrate is converted to products.
[bio13-s2.4.5] 4. The products no longer fit the active site closely, so they are released.
[bio13-s2.4.6] 5. The enzyme returns to its original shape, unchanged and ready to bind another substrate molecule.
[bio13-s2.5.b] The earlier lock-and-key model treated the active site as rigid. Induced fit replaced it because it explains observations that a rigid site cannot.
[bio13-s2.5.0] Example: the enzyme hexokinase (used in the first stage of respiration) closes around its substrate glucose like a clamp when glucose binds; structural studies of enzymes showed this change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.1] Induced fit explains why some enzymes act on several similar substrates: a flexible site can mould around each of them.
[bio13-s2.5.2] Induced fit explains how binding itself helps catalysis: the shape change strains the substrate's bonds, lowering the activation energy.
[bio13-s2.5.3] Induced fit explains why molecules of similar shape may bind but not react: they do not induce the correct change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.4] Lock and key remains a useful simple model of specificity.
[bio13-s2.5.5] Diagram: induced fit in four labelled stages: (1) an enzyme whose active site does not quite match the approaching substrate; (2) substrate binding and the active site changing shape around it, forming the enzyme–substrate complex, with arrows showing the change.
[bio13-s2.5.6] Diagram (continued): (3) products formed in the active site; (4) products released and the enzyme back in its original shape. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, change in shape, enzyme–substrate complex and products.
[bio13-s2.5.7] Diagram (contrast): include a one-line sketch of lock and key, with a rigid active site that already fits the substrate exactly.
[bio13-s2.6.b] The two models differ in whether the active site can move. Setting them side by side shows why induced fit explains more.
[bio13-s2.6.0] Active site: rigid, with a fixed shape that exactly matches the substrate (lock and key); flexible, changing shape when the substrate binds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.1] Fit: an exact fit before binding (lock and key); a close fit only after binding (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.2] Role of binding: it holds the substrate only (lock and key); the change of shape itself helps the reaction by straining bonds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.3] Specificity explained: absolute specificity only (lock and key); both absolute and group specificity (induced fit), because a flexible site can mould around several similar substrates.

CANDIDATES bio13-s3: Investigation: rate of enzyme activity
[bio13-s3.0.b] An enzyme is a biological catalyst, so what we want to know in an investigation is how fast it works. That speed is called the rate, and it is always measured against time.
[bio13-s3.0.0] The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is the amount of substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on) converted into product per unit time, e.g. cm³ of oxygen produced per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.1] The rate can be measured either by how fast a product appears or by how fast the substrate disappears, whichever is easier to detect for that reaction.
[bio13-s3.0.2] Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂); the rate is measured by the oxygen released, as foam or as a volume of gas.
[bio13-s3.0.3] Amylase digests starch to maltose; the rate is measured by the time taken for the starch to disappear, tested with iodine.
[bio13-s3.0.4] Rate = amount of product ÷ time: e.g. 30 cm³ of foam in 2 minutes gives a rate of 15 cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.5] Where the time taken to reach an end-point (such as the starch disappearing) is measured, rate = 1 ÷ time: starch gone in 120 s gives a rate of 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹, often written as 1000/time to give whole numbers.
[bio13-s3.0.6] A shorter time means a faster rate, because the same change has been completed more quickly.
[bio13-s3.0.7] Measure the initial rate, at the start of the reaction (e.g. in the first one or two minutes), because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
[bio13-s3.1.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. Presenting them clearly in a table and a graph is how you show the pattern in your results.
[bio13-s3.1.0] A results table has the independent variable (the factor you change) in the first column, then the repeat readings, the mean and the rate, each column headed with its unit.
[bio13-s3.1.1] A graph has the independent variable on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis, with points plotted accurately and joined by a smooth curve or line of best fit.
[bio13-s3.2.b] All five investigations use the same small set of enzymes, substrates and equipment. Knowing what each is for makes the methods easy to follow.
[bio13-s3.2.0] Catalase is found in almost all cells that use oxygen, where it breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism. Good sources are celery, potato, liver and yeast.
[bio13-s3.2.1] Catalase extract: blend a fixed mass of chopped celery (e.g. 20 g) with distilled water (e.g. 100 cm³), then filter through muslin or filter paper. Make one batch and use it for every test, so the enzyme is the same each time.
[bio13-s3.2.2] Amylase: a commercial (e.g. fungal) amylase solution made up at a fixed concentration; starch solution (e.g. 1%) is its substrate. Saliva contains amylase but is avoided for hygiene reasons.
[bio13-s3.2.3] Equipment for measuring: 100 cm³ graduated cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes for measuring volumes, thermometer to check temperatures, stopwatch to time each run, electronic balance.
[bio13-s3.2.4] Other equipment: test tubes and boiling tubes, blender or mortar and pestle, knife and chopping board, filter funnel, spotting tile and droppers, conical flask with bung and delivery tube, trough, labels.
[bio13-s3.2.5] Water baths can be thermostatically controlled electric baths, or beakers of water warmed with kettle water or cooled with ice, checked with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.2.6] Reagents: hydrogen peroxide solution (one stock of fixed concentration), buffer solutions or buffer tablets of known pH, washing-up liquid, distilled water, starch solution, amylase solution and iodine solution.
[bio13-s3.3.b] Four ideas recur across the investigations: a buffer to hold pH steady, washing-up liquid to make the oxygen measurable in the foam methods, a waiting time so the mixture reaches the right temperature, and a control to prove the enzyme is responsible.
[bio13-s3.3.0] A buffer is a solution that keeps the pH constant even when acids or alkalis are produced or added.
[bio13-s3.3.1] A buffer at pH 7 (or at the enzyme's optimum) is added in every investigation except the pH investigation, so that pH is a controlled variable (kept the same each time).
[bio13-s3.3.2] In the foam methods, a drop of washing-up liquid traps the oxygen released by catalase as a foam, so its volume can be read in a graduated cylinder; the same amount is added every time.
[bio13-s3.3.3] Reaching temperature (equilibration): the enzyme and the substrate are placed in the same water bath, in separate containers, for about 5 minutes before they are mixed, so the reaction starts at the chosen temperature.
[bio13-s3.3.4] A control in each investigation uses enzyme that has been boiled (denatured), or distilled water in place of the enzyme, with everything else the same.
[bio13-s3.3.5] The control shows that the oxygen (or the loss of starch) is caused by the enzyme and not by the substrate breaking down on its own.
[bio13-s3.4.b] The chemicals and equipment here are mostly mild, but several can harm skin, eyes or clothes if handled carelessly.
[bio13-s3.4.0] Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent that irritates or burns the eyes and skin; wear safety glasses (and gloves), measure it with a syringe, and wash any splash off with plenty of water.
[bio13-s3.4.1] Buffers of very low or very high pH are irritants, so wear safety glasses.
[bio13-s3.4.2] Use tongs or a test-tube holder and take care with boiling water and hot water baths; keep electrical water baths and blenders away from spilt water.
[bio13-s3.4.3] Take care with knives and blender blades. If liver is used, treat it as raw meat: wear gloves, keep it off benches used for other work and wash hands afterwards.
[bio13-s3.4.4] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes, so handle it with care. Never eat or drink in the laboratory, because chemicals could be swallowed.
[bio13-s3.5.b] Here we vary how much hydrogen peroxide (the substrate) the same amount of catalase has to work on, and see how the rate of oxygen production changes.
[bio13-s3.5.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of substrate (hydrogen peroxide) concentration on the rate of catalase activity, by changing only the substrate so any change in rate must be due to it.
[bio13-s3.5.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increases the rate of oxygen production, because more substrate molecules collide with active sites per second, until all the active sites are occupied and the rate levels off.”
[bio13-s3.5.2] Independent variable: hydrogen peroxide concentration, made by diluting the stock with distilled water to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the stock (e.g. 2 cm³ stock + 8 cm³ water = 20%), always to the same total volume (e.g. 10 cm³).
[bio13-s3.5.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): volume of oxygen collected in the first minute (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.5.4] Controlled variables: volume of the same celery extract (so enzyme concentration is constant), temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), total volume of solution, timing and apparatus.
[bio13-s3.5.5] Control: the 100% hydrogen peroxide with boiled extract (or with water in place of the extract), which gives little or no oxygen; the 0% tube also shows that no oxygen forms without substrate.
[bio13-s3.6.b] The oxygen given off is collected in an upturned cylinder of water, so its volume can be read directly.
[bio13-s3.6.0] 1. Fill a graduated cylinder with water and turn it upside down in a trough of water, with no air at the top.
[bio13-s3.6.1] 2. Put 10 cm³ of the first hydrogen peroxide dilution and 2 cm³ of pH 7 buffer in a conical flask (or boiling tube) in the water bath, with 2 cm³ of celery extract in a separate tube beside it, for about 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.6.2] 3. Place the end of the delivery tube under the mouth of the upturned cylinder.
[bio13-s3.6.3] 4. Add the extract to the flask, immediately put in the bung with the delivery tube, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.6.4] 5. Read the volume of oxygen collected in the cylinder after 1 minute (or every 30 seconds for 3 minutes).
[bio13-s3.6.5] 6. Rinse the flask and repeat for each concentration. Do each concentration three times and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.6.6] Alternatives: a gas syringe connected to the flask measures the oxygen volume more accurately than collection over water; or the foam method used in the pH investigation can be used.
[bio13-s3.6.7] Another alternative: filter-paper discs soaked in catalase extract are dropped into each concentration and the time for each disc to rise to the surface (buoyed by oxygen bubbles) is timed, with rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.6.8] Diagram: a conical flask in a water bath, with a bung and delivery tube leading under an upturned graduated cylinder full of water in a trough. Label hydrogen peroxide + buffer + catalase extract, water bath, thermometer, delivery tube, oxygen collecting, trough of water.
[bio13-s3.7.b] The results follow a typical curve: a steep rise, then a plateau. Each part has its own explanation, and the plateau is caused by saturation of the active sites, not by the enzyme being denatured or used up.
[bio13-s3.7.0] Expected results: no oxygen at 0%; at low concentrations the rate rises steeply, roughly in proportion to concentration; at high concentrations it rises less and levels off into a plateau. The boiled-enzyme control gives little or no oxygen.
[bio13-s3.7.1] The rise is explained by collisions: more substrate molecules in the same volume means more collisions with active sites (the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds) per second, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and more product is made per minute.
[bio13-s3.7.2] The plateau is explained by saturation: at high substrate concentration every active site is occupied at any moment, so the enzyme (saturated) works at its maximum rate, and adding more substrate cannot increase the rate.
[bio13-s3.7.3] At the plateau the amount of enzyme is the limiting factor (the thing holding the rate back); adding more enzyme would raise the rate again.
[bio13-s3.7.4] Within the range used in a school investigation the plateau may not be reached if the enzyme is in excess.
[bio13-s3.7.5] Conclusion: state the trend shown by the mean rates, whether it supports the hypothesis, and the concentration above which the rate stopped increasing, if one was found.
[bio13-s3.7.6] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against substrate concentration (x-axis); a curve rising from the origin, steep at first and then levelling off, with the plateau labelled all active sites occupied (saturated).
[bio13-s3.8.b] Now the substrate stays the same and the amount of enzyme changes. Amylase digests starch, and the end-point is found with iodine, which turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown when starch is absent.
[bio13-s3.8.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of enzyme (amylase) concentration on the rate at which starch is digested.
[bio13-s3.8.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of amylase increases the rate of starch digestion, because more active sites are available to bind starch molecules, as long as starch is in excess.”
[bio13-s3.8.2] Independent variable: amylase concentration, made by diluting the stock amylase solution with distilled water to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the stock, always to the same volume.
[bio13-s3.8.3] Dependent variable: time taken for the starch to disappear (the iodine test no longer turns blue-black), converted to rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.8.4] Controlled variables: volume and concentration of starch solution, volume of amylase solution, temperature (water bath at e.g. 37 °C), pH (buffer at pH 7), volume of iodine in each well, and the sampling interval.
[bio13-s3.8.5] Control: starch solution with distilled water (0% amylase) or with boiled amylase in place of the amylase; it stays blue-black with iodine for the whole investigation, showing that the starch does not disappear without active enzyme.
[bio13-s3.9.b] Drops of the reacting mixture are tested with iodine at regular intervals until the blue-black colour no longer appears.
[bio13-s3.9.0] 1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile (a white tile with small hollows), so each sample can be tested as soon as it is taken.
[bio13-s3.9.1] 2. Put 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of pH 7 buffer into a test tube, and 2 cm³ of the first amylase dilution into another, then stand both in the water bath for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.9.2] 3. Pour the amylase into the starch, mix, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.9.3] 4. Every 30 seconds, take one drop of the mixture with a clean dropper and add it to the next well of iodine.
[bio13-s3.9.4] 5. Note the time at which a drop first leaves the iodine yellow-brown (no blue-black): this is the end-point, when all the starch has been digested.
[bio13-s3.9.5] 6. Repeat for each amylase concentration, three times each, so that a mean can be calculated and a one-off error does not mislead you; then calculate the mean time and the rate.
[bio13-s3.9.6] Alternative: with catalase, vary the volume of celery extract (e.g. 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 cm³, each made up to the same total volume with distilled water) or the number of equal-sized celery discs, and measure the foam or oxygen volume in a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.9.7] Diagram: a spotting tile with a row of wells, the first wells blue-black and the later wells yellow-brown, labelled with sampling times; and the starch–amylase tube in a water bath with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.10.b] With more enzyme, the starch disappears sooner. Unlike the substrate investigation, the graph is a straight line, as long as there is plenty of substrate.
[bio13-s3.10.0] Expected results: the higher the amylase concentration, the shorter the time for the starch to disappear, so the faster the rate; the 0% and boiled-enzyme controls never reach the end-point.
[bio13-s3.10.1] A graph of rate against enzyme concentration is a straight line through the origin: doubling the enzyme roughly doubles the rate.
[bio13-s3.10.2] Explanation: more enzyme molecules means more active sites, so more substrate molecules can be bound and converted at the same time, and more product is made per unit time.
[bio13-s3.10.3] The straight line continues only while substrate is in excess (more than the enzyme can use). If the enzyme concentration becomes very high, substrate becomes the limiting factor and the line levels off.
[bio13-s3.10.4] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against enzyme concentration (x-axis); a straight line rising from the origin, labelled substrate in excess.
[bio13-s3.11.b] Each enzyme works best at a particular pH. Here, buffers set the pH, and the volume of foam produced by catalase shows how active it is at each one.
[bio13-s3.11.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.11.1] Hypothesis: “Catalase has an optimum pH at which its rate is highest, and the rate decreases at pH values above and below it, because the shape of the active site is changed.”
[bio13-s3.11.2] Independent variable: pH, set with buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10), which hold each pH steady so it does not drift as the reaction proceeds.
[bio13-s3.11.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam produced in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.11.4] Controlled variables: temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract (or mass of celery), volume of buffer, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.11.5] Control: at each pH (or at pH 7), boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.12.b] The foam is built up in a graduated cylinder, so its height can be read at the end of a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.12.0] 1. Into a 100 cm³ graduated cylinder put 10 cm³ of pH 4 buffer, 2 cm³ of celery extract (or a fixed mass of chopped celery) and one drop of washing-up liquid.
[bio13-s3.12.1] 2. Stand the cylinder in the 25 °C water bath, with a boiling tube of hydrogen peroxide beside it, for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.12.2] 3. Note the starting volume of liquid in the cylinder, so the foam volume can be found by subtraction.
[bio13-s3.12.3] 4. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide with a syringe and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.12.4] 5. After 2 minutes, read the volume at the top of the foam, at eye level; the foam volume is this reading minus the starting volume.
[bio13-s3.12.5] 6. Wash the cylinder and repeat with each buffer, three times each, and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.12.6] Diagram: a graduated cylinder standing in a water bath with a thermometer, containing buffer, celery extract and washing-up liquid at the bottom and a column of foam above, with a syringe adding hydrogen peroxide. Label each part and the foam height being read.
[bio13-s3.13.b] The rate rises to a peak at one pH and falls on both sides. The explanation lies in how pH affects the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s3.13.0] Expected results: the most foam forms at around pH 7 (the optimum for celery and liver catalase), with less in more acidic and more alkaline buffers and very little at the extremes. The control gives no foam.
[bio13-s3.13.1] The optimum pH is the pH at which the active site has the shape and charges that best fit the substrate.
[bio13-s3.13.2] A higher or lower hydrogen ion concentration, in more acidic or more alkaline conditions, changes the charges in the active site and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
[bio13-s3.13.3] Small changes from the optimum are reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme permanently.
[bio13-s3.13.4] Each enzyme's optimum suits the place where it works: pepsin in the acid stomach has an optimum of about pH 2; salivary amylase in the mouth about pH 7; trypsin in the alkaline small intestine about pH 8.
[bio13-s3.13.5] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against pH (x-axis); a peaked, bell-shaped curve with its peak labelled optimum pH at about 7 for catalase; optionally a second curve peaking at about pH 2, labelled pepsin.
[bio13-s3.14.b] Temperature has two opposite effects on an enzyme: it speeds up collisions, but too much heat destroys the enzyme. This investigation measures catalase activity across a range of water-bath temperatures.
[bio13-s3.14.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.14.1] Hypothesis: “As temperature increases, the rate of catalase activity increases up to an optimum temperature, because molecules move faster and collide more often; above the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme is denatured.”
[bio13-s3.14.2] Independent variable: temperature, set with water baths at e.g. 0 °C (ice water), 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, each checked with a thermometer because baths drift from their set temperature.
[bio13-s3.14.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.14.4] Controlled variables: pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.14.5] Control: boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract at each temperature, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.15.b] The temperature method reuses the foam method from the pH investigation, with the buffer fixed and the water bath changed.
[bio13-s3.15.0] Method: follow the pH investigation method, but with pH 7 buffer in every cylinder, and with each cylinder (buffer, extract and washing-up liquid) and its tube of hydrogen peroxide left in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes before the peroxide is added.
[bio13-s3.15.1] Check the bath temperature with the thermometer during the 2 minutes, because it can drift; repeat three times at each temperature and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.15.2] The same method works with amylase and starch, timing the starch's disappearance at each temperature, as in the enzyme concentration investigation.
[bio13-s3.15.3] Diagram: the pH set-up, with a row of water baths at different labelled temperatures.
[bio13-s3.16.b] The curve rises slowly to a peak and then drops steeply. The rise and the fall have different causes.
[bio13-s3.16.0] Expected results: little foam at 0 °C; the rate increases as temperature rises, to a maximum at the optimum temperature; above the optimum the rate falls steeply, with little or no activity at the highest temperatures tested.
[bio13-s3.16.1] The graph is not symmetrical: a gradual rise and a steep fall. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 °C, body temperature.
[bio13-s3.16.2] Explanation of the rise: as temperature increases, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), move faster and collide more often, and more collisions have enough energy to react, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second.
[bio13-s3.16.3] Over the lower part of the range, the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (an approximate pattern, not an exact law).
[bio13-s3.16.4] Explanation of the fall: above the optimum, the enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured.
[bio13-s3.16.5] As temperature rises further, more enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls even though collisions are more frequent.
[bio13-s3.16.6] At low temperature the enzyme is inactive, not denatured: the molecules move too slowly for many collisions, but the active site keeps its shape, so activity returns when the enzyme is warmed (shown in the heat denaturation investigation).
[bio13-s3.16.7] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against temperature in °C (x-axis); a curve rising gradually to a peak labelled optimum temperature, then falling steeply to zero, with the falling part labelled enzyme denatured.
[bio13-s3.17.b] Heat can do more than slow an enzyme: it can destroy its shape for good. This investigation shows that boiled catalase stays inactive even after it is cooled.
[bio13-s3.17.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of heat denaturation on the activity of catalase.
[bio13-s3.17.1] Denaturation is the permanent loss of an enzyme's shape, and so of its active site and its activity, caused by high temperature or extreme pH.
[bio13-s3.17.2] Hypothesis: “Catalase that has been boiled will not break down hydrogen peroxide, even when cooled, because its active site has been permanently changed.”
[bio13-s3.17.3] Independent variable: whether the extract has been boiled (heat-treated) or not. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³).
[bio13-s3.17.4] Controlled variables: both extracts tested at the same temperature (e.g. 25 °C) and pH (pH 7 buffer), with the same volumes of extract, buffer and hydrogen peroxide, the same drop of washing-up liquid and the same time.
[bio13-s3.17.5] Control: the unboiled extract, which shows how much foam active catalase produces under the same conditions, so the boiled result has something to be compared with.
[bio13-s3.18.b] Half the extract is boiled and half is not; both are then tested at the same temperature, so any difference is due to boiling alone.
[bio13-s3.18.0] 1. Divide the celery extract between two labelled boiling tubes, A and B.
[bio13-s3.18.1] 2. Place tube A in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes; leave tube B at room temperature.
[bio13-s3.18.2] 3. Cool tube A, then stand both tubes in the 25 °C water bath for 5 minutes, so the boiled extract is tested at the same temperature as the unboiled.
[bio13-s3.18.3] 4. Set up two graduated cylinders, each with 10 cm³ of pH 7 buffer, a drop of washing-up liquid and 2 cm³ of extract A or B.
[bio13-s3.18.4] 5. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide to each and read the foam after 2 minutes. Repeat three times.
[bio13-s3.18.5] Expected results: the boiled extract gives little or no foam; the unboiled extract gives a large column of foam. Any small amount of foam from the boiled extract comes from hydrogen peroxide breaking down slowly on its own, or from catalase that was not fully denatured.
[bio13-s3.18.6] Conclusion: boiling denatured the catalase; its activity did not return on cooling, so the change is permanent. This supports the hypothesis and explains why boiled enzyme is used as the control in the other investigations.
[bio13-s3.18.7] Extension: extract kept in ice water for 10 minutes and then warmed to 25 °C gives as much foam as unchilled extract, showing that cooling makes an enzyme inactive without denaturing it, whereas heating denatures it.
[bio13-s3.18.8] The same investigation works with amylase: boiled amylase added to starch leaves the iodine test blue-black at every sampling time, while unboiled amylase digests the starch so the iodine stays yellow-brown.
[bio13-s3.19.b] Secondary data are results that someone else collected. They let you check and extend your own conclusions, and the specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data.
[bio13-s3.19.0] Secondary data are data collected by someone else: results pooled from other groups in the class, published tables, textbooks or research papers, or online databases.
[bio13-s3.19.1] Uses: compare your optimum with published optima (the optimum pH of digestive enzymes and the optimum temperature of human enzymes given in the pH and temperature investigations).
[bio13-s3.19.2] Uses: check whether your trend agrees with other groups' (reproducibility), and extend a conclusion beyond the range you could test.
[bio13-s3.19.3] Secondary data on enzymes from extreme environments support the link between structure and conditions: Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, works best at about 72 °C and is not denatured at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s3.19.4] When using secondary data, record its source, check that it was measured under comparable conditions (same enzyme source, substrate, pH or temperature), and judge whether the source is reliable. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio13-s3.20.b] Every method has weaknesses. Knowing where the error comes from tells you how to reduce it.
[bio13-s3.20.0] Foam volume is imprecise: bubbles burst, the top of the foam is uneven, and foam may stick to the cylinder walls. Read at eye level at exactly the same time each run; collecting the oxygen in a gas syringe is more accurate.
[bio13-s3.20.1] Gas is lost in the time between adding the enzyme and inserting the bung, or through leaks; some oxygen dissolves in the water when it is collected over water.
[bio13-s3.20.2] Water-bath temperatures drift, especially in beakers of hot water or ice; check with a thermometer throughout or use a thermostatically controlled bath.
[bio13-s3.20.3] The reaction mixture may not reach the bath temperature if equilibration is too short, so allow the full time.
[bio13-s3.21.b] Some errors come from the enzyme and reagents themselves, or from judging a colour change. Repeating and averaging makes results more reliable.
[bio13-s3.21.0] Enzyme activity varies between celery samples and falls as an extract stands, so make one batch, keep it cool and use it quickly for all the tests; a fresh batch for a repeat can give different values.
[bio13-s3.21.1] Hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down on standing, especially in light, so its concentration may fall during the session: use one fresh stock kept in a dark bottle.
[bio13-s3.21.2] The iodine end-point is a judgement of colour, and sampling every 30 seconds means the time is only known to within 30 seconds; sample more often near the end-point, compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter.
[bio13-s3.21.3] Measure volumes with syringes or graduated pipettes rather than by eye, and use a clean syringe or dropper for each solution to avoid contamination.
[bio13-s3.21.4] Repeat each value three times, calculate the mean, and identify and investigate any anomalous result; pool class data to check reproducibility.
[bio13-s3.21.5] Use at least five values of the independent variable so the shape of the graph (peak or plateau) can be seen.

CANDIDATES bio16-s1: The cell cycle
[bio16-s1.0.b] Before you can follow the cell cycle you need to know what is being copied and shared out: the chromosomes. How many a cell has, and how they are arranged, decides whether it is diploid or haploid.
[bio16-s1.0.0] A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (the units of inheritance). There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio16-s1.0.1] Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin (DNA with its proteins). Before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio16-s1.0.2] Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent; the alleles (versions of a gene) on them may differ. Human body cells contain 23 such pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.3] Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, so that its chromosomes lie in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46. The 46 counts chromosomes, not pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.4] Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23. Haploid means one complete set, not half a chromosome.
[bio16-s1.0.5] When two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.
[bio16-s1.0.6] Haploid cells are made by meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number; mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s1.0.7] Each species has its own chromosome number: human 2n = 46, the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8, the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.
[bio16-s1.1.b] Cells don't just divide and stop: each one lives through a repeating sequence of events called the cell cycle. It shows how a cell prepares for division and then carries it out.
[bio16-s1.1.0] The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
[bio16-s1.1.1] It has three main activities, in this order. First, growth: the cell makes new proteins and organelles (the working parts of a cell) and gets bigger.
[bio16-s1.1.2] Second, DNA synthesis: every chromosome is copied, so the cell has two copies of its genetic information to hand out.
[bio16-s1.1.3] Third, division: the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells.
[bio16-s1.1.4] The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, about 90% of the cycle, so division is short.
[bio16-s1.1.5] In many human cells grown in the laboratory, one cycle takes about 24 hours.
[bio16-s1.1.6] Interphase is not a resting stage. The cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
[bio16-s1.1.7] One cycle, including division, produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s1.2.b] Not every cell divides at the same pace, and no cell divides without being told it is safe to. Whether a cell cycles depends on its job and on control checks built into the cycle.
[bio16-s1.2.0] Some cells leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, for example most mature nerve cells. Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide.
[bio16-s1.2.1] Other cells divide often throughout life, for example cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems, regions where growth happens).
[bio16-s1.2.2] The cycle is controlled. Proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on.
[bio16-s1.2.3] Signals from other cells (growth factors, chemical messages) tell a cell whether to divide. There is more on how loss of this control leads to cancer in Cancer.
[bio16-s1.2.4] Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle. Large sector: interphase (cell growth and DNA replication). Small sector: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis). Arrow for direction. Higher Level: also label G1, S, G2 and the four stages of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.3.b] Interphase is long and busy, and it has three parts: G1, S and G2. Together they prepare the cell so that division can be quick and accurate.
[bio16-s1.3.0] G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including the enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job.
[bio16-s1.3.1] S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere (the narrow point where they are held together).
[bio16-s1.3.2] G2 (second growth phase): further growth and more organelles, for example mitochondria. The proteins of the spindle (the fibres that later pull chromosomes apart) are made, and ATP is stored for division.
[bio16-s1.3.3] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), for example mature nerve cells.
[bio16-s1.3.4] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.
[bio16-s1.4.b] Mitosis is the division of the nucleus. It runs as one smooth process, but it is split into four stages to make it easier to describe, and each stage has a characteristic look and job.
[bio16-s1.4.0] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process, divided for convenience into four stages in this order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. It is followed by cytokinesis, which is division of the cytoplasm and not a stage of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.4.1] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear membrane breaks down, and spindle fibres (protein tubes called microtubules) begin to form.
[bio16-s1.4.2] In animal cells, two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them. Plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
[bio16-s1.4.3] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
[bio16-s1.4.4] Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first, which gives a V shape.
[bio16-s1.4.5] Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome, so each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio16-s1.4.6] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
[bio16-s1.4.7] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell, 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, shaded to show the two parents). Labels: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow (end of telophase).
[bio16-s1.5.b] You can tell which stage a cell is in just by looking at how its chromosomes are arranged under the microscope. Counting dividing cells then tells you how fast a tissue is dividing.
[bio16-s1.5.0] In stained cells from a growing root tip (for example onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase, seen as a nucleus with no visible chromosomes, because interphase is the longest part of the cycle.
[bio16-s1.5.1] Dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes: condensed and scattered for prophase; in a line across the middle for metaphase.
[bio16-s1.5.2] Two groups moving apart is anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, with new nuclei forming, is telophase.
[bio16-s1.5.3] The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing. For example, 24 dividing cells out of 200 gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%.
[bio16-s1.5.4] A high mitotic index shows a tissue that is dividing rapidly, such as a root tip or a tumour.
[bio16-s1.6.b] After the nucleus has divided, the cell still has to split in two. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and plant and animal cells do it differently because plant cells have a rigid wall.
[bio16-s1.6.0] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.1] In animal cells the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
[bio16-s1.6.2] In plant cells the rigid cell wall can't pinch in. Instead, vesicles (small membrane sacs) from the Golgi apparatus collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate.
[bio16-s1.6.3] The cell plate grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.4] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio16-s4: Cancer
[bio16-s4.0.b] Your body replaces and repairs tissue by mitosis (cell division that makes two identical cells), and it keeps this tightly controlled. Cancer is what happens when that control fails.
[bio16-s4.0.0] Cancer is a group of diseases in which some body cells lose control of mitosis. They divide too often (the rate of division is abnormal) and too many times (the number of divisions is not limited).
[bio16-s4.0.1] Cancer cells can also invade nearby tissues and spread to other parts of the body, which is what makes cancer more than just extra cells.
[bio16-s4.0.2] There are over 200 kinds of cancer, named after the organ or tissue where they start, e.g. lung, breast, skin, bowel and blood.
[bio16-s4.0.3] Carcinomas are the group that start in epithelial tissues (the tissues that line or cover organs), e.g. most lung, breast and bowel cancers.
[bio16-s4.0.4] Sarcomas start in bone, muscle or connective tissue (the supporting tissue that holds other tissues together).
[bio16-s4.0.5] Leukaemias start in the blood-forming cells of the bone marrow, and lymphomas start in the lymphatic system (the network of vessels and nodes that drains fluid and carries immune cells).
[bio16-s4.0.6] Cancer starts from a single cell whose DNA has changed (mutated). All the cancer cells in a tumour are descended from that one cell by mitosis, so they all carry its mutation.
[bio16-s4.1.b] To see how cancer starts, you first need to know how healthy cells stay in check. Normal cells divide only when told to, and have built-in ways to stop damaged cells from dividing.
[bio16-s4.1.0] Normal cells divide only when they receive signals to divide (growth factors, chemical messengers from other cells), and stop when signals tell them to stop.
[bio16-s4.1.1] Checkpoints in the cell cycle (the ordered series of stages a cell goes through to divide) halt the cycle if the DNA is damaged or not copied correctly, so errors are not passed on.
[bio16-s4.1.2] Two groups of genes control this. Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that stimulate the cell cycle, so they are the accelerator, e.g. growth factor receptors (proteins that detect the growth signal).
[bio16-s4.1.3] Tumour suppressor genes code for proteins that stop the cycle at checkpoints, so they are the brakes. They also repair damaged DNA or make a badly damaged cell destroy itself.
[bio16-s4.1.4] Apoptosis is programmed cell death: a damaged or unwanted cell breaks itself down in a controlled way, without harming neighbouring cells. It removes cells with damaged DNA before they can divide.
[bio16-s4.1.5] TP53 is an example of a tumour suppressor gene. It codes for the protein p53, which stops the cell cycle when DNA is damaged so that the DNA can be repaired, or triggers apoptosis if the damage is too great.
[bio16-s4.1.6] p53 is called the guardian of the genome because it protects the DNA. TP53 is mutated in about half of all human cancers, which shows how important its protection is.
[bio16-s4.1.7] Contact inhibition: normal cells in a tissue, and in a culture dish, stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells, so they form a single layer.
[bio16-s4.1.8] Most normal human cells can divide only a limited number of times before they stop, because the protective caps at the ends of their chromosomes (telomeres) shorten with each division.
[bio16-s4.2.b] Cancer develops when the genes that control division are damaged by mutation (a change in the DNA). The damage affects the accelerator, the brakes, or both, and it builds up step by step.
[bio16-s4.2.0] 1. A mutation in a proto-oncogene can turn it into an oncogene, which is permanently switched on and keeps telling the cell to divide even with no signal, like an accelerator stuck down.
[bio16-s4.2.1] 2. A mutation that inactivates a tumour suppressor gene removes the brakes: damaged cells keep dividing instead of stopping, being repaired or dying by apoptosis.
[bio16-s4.2.2] Usually both copies (alleles) of a tumour suppressor gene must be inactivated before control is lost, because one working copy can still make enough of the protein.
[bio16-s4.2.3] 3. One mutation is usually not enough. Several mutations in different control genes must build up in the same cell, often over many years.
[bio16-s4.2.4] This is why the risk of most cancers rises with age, and why anything that increases the rate of mutation raises the risk.
[bio16-s4.2.5] 4. Once one cell has lost control, every daughter cell inherits the same mutations. As they divide faster and faster, further mutations arise, and the cells that divide fastest or survive best take over, so the cancer cells become more abnormal over time.
[bio16-s4.2.6] 5. The immune system (the body's defence against disease) destroys many abnormal cells before they form a tumour. People whose immune system is weakened have a higher risk of some cancers.
[bio16-s4.3.b] Cancer cells differ from normal cells in many ways. Most of the differences come straight from the lost controls on division, and they are best learned as pairs.
[bio16-s4.3.0] Division: normal cells divide only when signalled, at a controlled rate. Cancer cells divide rapidly and continuously without signals, and ignore signals to stop.
[bio16-s4.3.1] Lifespan: normal cells divide a limited number of times and die by apoptosis when damaged. Cancer cells avoid apoptosis, and many keep their telomeres long by using the enzyme telomerase, so they can divide indefinitely (immortal cells).
[bio16-s4.3.2] Contact inhibition is lost: cancer cells ignore contact with neighbours and pile up into a mass, whereas normal cells stop at contact.
[bio16-s4.3.3] Specialisation: normal cells are specialised (differentiated) for a job. Cancer cells are unspecialised (poorly differentiated), so they don't carry out the tissue's function.
[bio16-s4.3.4] Appearance under the microscope: cancer cells vary in size and shape and have large, irregular, darkly staining nuclei, often with abnormal numbers of chromosomes, and many are seen dividing. This is how cancer is diagnosed from a biopsy (tissue sample) or smear.
[bio16-s4.3.5] Blood supply: cancer cells can release signals that make new blood vessels grow into the tumour (angiogenesis), supplying the oxygen and nutrients needed for further growth.
[bio16-s4.3.6] Attachment: normal cells stick to their neighbours and stay in place. Cancer cells can lose this adhesion (stickiness), break away and move.
[bio16-s4.3.7] HeLa cells are a famous example of immortal cancer cells. They were taken in 1951 from a cervical cancer of Henrietta Lacks at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, USA, without her knowledge or consent.
[bio16-s4.3.8] They were the first human cells to keep dividing indefinitely in the laboratory, and they are still used in research worldwide, including in developing the polio vaccine. Her case is a well-known example of the ethics of consent in research.
[bio16-s4.4.b] Not every lump of abnormal cells is cancer. What decides it is whether the cells stay put or invade and spread.
[bio16-s4.4.0] A tumour is an abnormal mass of cells produced by uncontrolled division.
[bio16-s4.4.1] A benign tumour grows slowly, is usually enclosed in a capsule (a layer of tissue around it), does not invade surrounding tissue or spread, and its cells look fairly normal.
[bio16-s4.4.2] A benign tumour is usually removable by surgery and not life-threatening, though it can be dangerous if it presses on a vital organ such as the brain. Examples are most moles and warts. A benign tumour is not cancer.
[bio16-s4.4.3] A malignant tumour, which is a cancer, grows quickly, has no capsule, invades and destroys surrounding tissue, can spread around the body, and has abnormal cells.
[bio16-s4.4.4] Benign and malignant tumours differ in rate of growth (slow or quick), capsule (present or absent), invasion (none or destructive), spread (none or around the body) and appearance of cells (fairly normal or abnormal).
[bio16-s4.4.5] They also differ in danger: a benign tumour is usually not life-threatening, while a malignant tumour is a cancer and can be life-threatening because it invades and spreads.
[bio16-s4.5.b] A cancer becomes dangerous when it spreads beyond where it began. These are the steps from one mutated cell to tumours in other organs.
[bio16-s4.5.0] 1. A cell mutates and divides without control, producing a clone (a group of identical cells) of abnormal cells.
[bio16-s4.5.1] 2. Further mutations and division form a small tumour in the tissue of origin, the primary tumour.
[bio16-s4.5.2] 3. Angiogenesis (the growth of new blood vessels) supplies the tumour with blood, so it grows larger.
[bio16-s4.5.3] 4. Cancer cells invade the surrounding tissue.
[bio16-s4.5.4] 5. Some cells break away, enter blood vessels or lymph vessels, and are carried around the body.
[bio16-s4.5.5] 6. They leave the vessels in another organ and divide there to form secondary tumours, called metastases.
[bio16-s4.5.6] Diagram: draw normal tissue (a neat single layer), then one mutated cell, a primary tumour with a vessel growing in, cells entering a vessel, a secondary tumour elsewhere. Labels: normal cells, cancer cell with large nucleus, primary tumour, blood vessel, invasion, metastasis, secondary tumour.
[bio16-s4.6.b] Spread is what makes cancer so serious, and the harm tumours do comes from their size, position and appetite for nutrients.
[bio16-s4.6.0] Metastasis is the spread of cancer from the primary tumour to form secondary tumours elsewhere.
[bio16-s4.6.1] Secondary tumours contain the same type of cells as the primary tumour: breast cancer that spreads to the bones is still breast cancer cells, not bone cancer.
[bio16-s4.6.2] Metastasis makes cancer much harder to treat, because the cells are in many places, which is why early detection matters. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio16-s4.6.3] Tumours harm the body by pressing on and damaging organs, and by blocking tubes such as the airways, gut or blood vessels.
[bio16-s4.6.4] They also destroy normal tissue and stop it working, e.g. leukaemia cells crowd out normal blood-forming cells in the bone marrow, causing anaemia (too few red blood cells) and infections.
[bio16-s4.6.5] Tumours use up the body's nutrients, causing weight loss.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio26-s2: The nephron
[bio26-s2.0.b] Urine is made by tiny tubes called nephrons, so you need to know the parts of one in the order the fluid flows through them. Each part has its own job, and its position in the kidney is part of the answer.
[bio26-s2.0.0] A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney. It is the kidney's functional unit (the smallest part that does the kidney's job), where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one cell thick throughout.
[bio26-s2.0.1] Bowman's capsule is the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex (the outer layer of the kidney), wrapped around the glomerulus (a capillary knot). Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the capsule's inner space.
[bio26-s2.0.2] The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the first, long, coiled (convoluted) section after the capsule, in the cortex. Proximal means nearer the capsule.
[bio26-s2.0.3] The loop of Henle is a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla (the inner part of the kidney), with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
[bio26-s2.0.4] The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) is the second coiled section, back in the cortex. Distal means further from the capsule. It empties into a collecting duct.
[bio26-s2.0.5] The collecting duct is a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla, through a pyramid, and opens into the renal pelvis (the funnel that collects urine).
[bio26-s2.0.6] The syllabus does not list the collecting duct, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH. Some textbooks do not count it as part of the nephron.
[bio26-s2.1.b] A nephron works because blood is brought right up against it. The blood vessels are arranged so that pressure builds up for filtering, and so that the tubule is wrapped in a second set of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) to take back what it reabsorbs.
[bio26-s2.1.0] A branch of the renal artery (the artery that brings blood to the kidney) divides into arterioles (small arteries). The afferent arteriole (afferent means carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
[bio26-s2.1.1] The capillaries of the glomerulus rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent means carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. The afferent is the wider one, so do not say the efferent is wider.
[bio26-s2.1.2] Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus, like water backing up behind a narrowed nozzle. This high pressure drives filtration.
[bio26-s2.1.3] The glomerulus is unusual because it is a capillary network lying between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule (a small vein) as capillaries normally do.
[bio26-s2.1.4] The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries, so they must be close to the tubule.
[bio26-s2.1.5] These capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration, because the cells need ATP for active transport, and they carry away the cells' carbon dioxide.
[bio26-s2.1.6] The capillaries join into venules, then into a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
[bio26-s2.1.7] Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below; mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).
[bio26-s2.2.b] The first step in making urine is filtration. It happens in the glomerulus and capsule, where pressure forces fluid out of the blood without checking whether the body needs it.
[bio26-s2.2.0] Filtration, also called ultrafiltration or filtration under pressure, is the forcing of small molecules out of the blood by the high pressure in the glomerulus. They pass through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
[bio26-s2.2.1] Substances that pass into the filtrate (the fluid that enters the capsule) are water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea (the waste made from breaking down amino acids), vitamins and some hormones.
[bio26-s2.2.2] Substances that are not filtered and stay in the blood are red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
[bio26-s2.2.3] Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances such as glucose and amino acids are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
[bio26-s2.2.4] Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood); this is why reabsorption is essential.
[bio26-s2.2.5] Structure suits filtration: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arteriole raise the pressure, and the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous, so fluid passes through easily.
[bio26-s2.2.6] The many capillaries in each glomerulus, with about a million glomeruli in the kidney, give a large filtering surface, so a large volume of fluid can be filtered every day.
[bio26-s2.3.b] Filtration lets everything small through, so the body has to take back what it wants. Most of this reabsorption happens in the PCT, whose structure is built for the job.
[bio26-s2.3.0] Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
[bio26-s2.3.1] The PCT is the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here.
[bio26-s2.3.2] Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, which uses ATP (the cell's energy currency) to move them into the cells. Water follows by osmosis, and some urea diffuses back into the blood.
[bio26-s2.3.3] Water is always reabsorbed passively by osmosis; it is never actively pumped. Only salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.3.4] The PCT is long and coiled, which gives time and surface area for reabsorption. Its cells have microvilli (tiny finger-like projections) on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area.
[bio26-s2.3.5] The PCT cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. The tubule is closely surrounded by capillaries, and its wall is one cell thick, so the distance to the blood is short.
[bio26-s2.3.6] The carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.
[bio26-s2.4.b] After the PCT, the rest of the nephron fine-tunes the filtrate. The loop of Henle makes the medulla salty, and this lets the collecting duct take back more water.
[bio26-s2.4.0] The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty, with the highest solute concentration deep in the medulla.
[bio26-s2.4.1] The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
[bio26-s2.4.2] In the DCT more salts and water are reabsorbed, with the amounts adjusted to the body's needs.
[bio26-s2.4.3] The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption.
[bio26-s2.4.4] In the collecting duct, as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood, so the urine becomes more concentrated. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH, a hormone. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation.
[bio26-s2.4.5] What remains in the collecting duct is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s2.5.b] Comparing the composition of blood plasma, glomerular filtrate and urine shows what each part of the nephron has done. Each difference between them has one explanation.
[bio26-s2.5.0] Protein is present in plasma but absent from the filtrate and urine, because it is too large to be filtered.
[bio26-s2.5.1] Glucose and amino acids are present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration, because filtration lets them through freely. They are absent from urine, because all of them are reabsorbed in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.5.2] Urea is at the same concentration in plasma and filtrate, because it is freely filtered. It is much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
[bio26-s2.5.3] Salts are similar in plasma and filtrate. In urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
[bio26-s2.5.4] Skill: given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference. Absent from the filtrate means not filtered (too large); absent from the urine means reabsorbed; more concentrated in the urine means water was reabsorbed and the substance was not.
[bio26-s2.6.b] Following the fluid from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, each part of the nephron does one main job.
[bio26-s2.6.0] Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule: filtration under pressure.
[bio26-s2.6.1] Proximal convoluted tubule: reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and most water.
[bio26-s2.6.2] Loop of Henle: makes the medulla salty; water is reabsorbed from the descending limb.
[bio26-s2.6.3] Distal convoluted tubule: adjusts salts and water, and secretes hydrogen ions, potassium ions and some drugs.
[bio26-s2.6.4] Collecting duct: water is reabsorbed under ADH control.
[bio26-s2.6.5] Renal pelvis: receives the urine that is left.

CANDIDATES bio27-s3: Chemical breakdown
[bio27-s3.0.b] Food is made of large molecules that are too big to pass into the blood. Chemical digestion cuts them into small units using enzymes (biological catalysts made by the body).
[bio27-s3.0.0] Chemical digestion is the hydrolysis of large food molecules into their small, soluble units by digestive enzymes: each bond is broken by adding a molecule of water, which is what hydrolysis means.
[bio27-s3.0.1] Carbohydrates become simple sugars, proteins become amino acids, and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. These small units are soluble and small enough to be absorbed.
[bio27-s3.0.2] Each digestive enzyme is specific to one type of substrate (the molecule it acts on) and works best at its optimum pH, the pH of the part of the gut where it acts.
[bio27-s3.0.3] The gut changes pH along its length: neutral in the mouth, acid in the stomach and alkaline in the small intestine. This suits the different enzymes working in each place.
[bio27-s3.0.4] Most digestive enzymes are released from glands into the gut cavity, so digestion there is extracellular, meaning it happens outside cells.
[bio27-s3.1.b] Digestion begins in the mouth, where food is mixed with saliva. Saliva moistens the food and also starts the digestion of starch.
[bio27-s3.1.0] Saliva is produced by the three pairs of salivary glands and released into the mouth.
[bio27-s3.1.1] The pH in the mouth is about 7 (neutral to slightly alkaline), which is the optimum for salivary amylase, the enzyme in saliva.
[bio27-s3.1.2] Salivary amylase digests starch to maltose (a sugar made of two glucose units). The product of an amylase is maltose.
[bio27-s3.1.3] Food is in the mouth only briefly, so only a little starch is digested there. Stomach acid denatures (destroys the shape of) the amylase soon after it reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s3.1.4] Saliva also contains water, which dissolves food so that it can be tasted and moistens it, and mucus, which lubricates swallowing.
[bio27-s3.1.5] Saliva also contains lysozyme, an enzyme that kills some bacteria.
[bio27-s3.2.b] In the stomach, food meets a strongly acidic fluid called gastric juice. It begins protein digestion and also protects the body from bacteria that arrive in food.
[bio27-s3.2.0] Gastric juice is produced by gastric glands in the stomach lining and released into the stomach. It contains hydrochloric acid, pepsin (released as inactive pepsinogen) and mucus.
[bio27-s3.2.1] The pH in the stomach is about 2 (strongly acidic), which is the optimum for pepsin.
[bio27-s3.2.2] Hydrochloric acid kills most bacteria in food, which is a defence against infection. It also provides the acid pH that pepsin needs.
[bio27-s3.2.3] Hydrochloric acid converts inactive pepsinogen to active pepsin, and it stops salivary amylase, which cannot work at this pH.
[bio27-s3.2.4] Pepsin is a protease (a protein-digesting enzyme): it hydrolyses peptide bonds inside protein chains, breaking proteins into shorter chains called polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.2.5] The product of a protease is polypeptides. Complete protein digestion needs further enzymes in the small intestine to give amino acids.
[bio27-s3.2.6] Pepsin is made in the inactive form pepsinogen so that it does not digest the gastric gland cells that make it. It becomes active only in the stomach cavity.
[bio27-s3.2.7] A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus barrier fails, the lining is damaged, causing a stomach ulcer.
[bio27-s3.3.b] Fat digestion needs help before enzymes can act, and the acid from the stomach has to be dealt with. Bile does both jobs when food enters the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s3.3.0] Bile is produced by the liver, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, and released through the bile duct into the duodenum when chyme (the partly digested, acidic food mixture leaving the stomach), especially fatty chyme, arrives.
[bio27-s3.3.1] Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments and sodium hydrogencarbonate. It contains no enzymes.
[bio27-s3.3.2] Bile salts emulsify fats, which means they break large fat droplets into tiny droplets. This is a physical change, not a chemical one. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s3.3.3] Sodium hydrogencarbonate is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach. This raises the pH to suit the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes and stops the action of pepsin.
[bio27-s3.3.4] Bile pigments are excretory wastes from the breakdown of haemoglobin from old red blood cells in the liver. They give faeces their brown colour.
[bio27-s3.4.b] The pancreas adds a second fluid to the duodenum, one that carries most of the enzymes needed to digest starch, protein and fat. Because the duodenum is alkaline, these enzymes work best there.
[bio27-s3.4.0] Pancreatic juice is produced by the pancreas and released through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. It contains sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralises acid, and three enzymes: pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.4.1] Pancreatic amylase digests the remaining starch (the starch not digested in the mouth) to maltose.
[bio27-s3.4.2] Trypsin is a protease, released in an inactive form and activated in the duodenum. It digests proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptides.
[bio27-s3.4.3] Lipase digests fats and oils (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. The product of a lipase is fatty acids and glycerol, not fatty acids alone.
[bio27-s3.4.4] Lipase acts on the tiny droplets made by emulsification, because they give it a much larger surface to work on.
[bio27-s3.4.5] The pH in the duodenum and small intestine is about 8 (slightly alkaline), because of the sodium hydrogencarbonate in bile and pancreatic juice. This suits pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.5.b] The last stage of digestion is done by enzymes made by the cells lining the small intestine. They finish breaking down the sugars and peptides that earlier enzymes produced.
[bio27-s3.5.0] The cells lining the villi (the tiny finger-like projections of the small intestine) make enzymes that complete digestion.
[bio27-s3.5.1] Many of these enzymes are held in the membranes of the lining cells, so the last step of digestion happens right at the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s3.5.2] Maltase digests maltose to two glucose molecules. Sucrase digests sucrose to glucose and fructose. Lactase digests lactose to glucose and galactose.
[bio27-s3.5.3] Peptidases digest peptides to amino acids.
[bio27-s3.5.4] These enzymes work at a pH of about 8, the same alkaline conditions as the duodenum.
[bio27-s3.5.5] Lactose intolerance: people who stop making lactase after childhood cannot digest lactose, so bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, causing bloating, wind and diarrhoea. There is more on this in Variation and Diet.
[bio27-s3.6.b] This table pulls together where each enzyme is made, where it acts, its pH and its products. Each line has the same pattern so you can compare them quickly.
[bio27-s3.6.0] Salivary amylase – made in salivary glands – acts in mouth – pH about 7 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.1] Pepsin (protease) – made in gastric glands of stomach wall – acts in stomach – pH about 2 – protein → polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.6.2] Pancreatic amylase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.3] Trypsin (protease) – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – proteins and polypeptides → peptides.
[bio27-s3.6.4] Lipase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.6.5] Maltase – made in the wall of the small intestine – acts there – pH about 8 – maltose → glucose.
[bio27-s3.6.6] Peptidases – made in the wall of the small intestine – act there – pH about 8 – peptides → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.6.7] Bile salts (not an enzyme) – made in liver, stored in gallbladder – act in duodenum – emulsify fats.
[bio27-s3.7.b] Putting the enzymes in order shows how each nutrient is broken down step by step along the gut. You can then use these flow models to predict what goes wrong when part of the system fails.
[bio27-s3.7.0] Carbohydrate model: starch is digested by salivary amylase in the mouth (pH 7) to maltose. The remaining starch is digested by pancreatic amylase in the duodenum (pH 8) to maltose. Maltose is digested by maltase in the small intestine wall to glucose.
[bio27-s3.7.1] Sucrose and lactose are digested by sucrase and lactase in the small intestine wall. Cellulose is not digested, because humans make no enzyme for it, so it passes on as fibre.
[bio27-s3.7.2] Protein model: protein → (pepsin, stomach, pH 2) → polypeptides → (trypsin, duodenum, pH 8) → shorter peptides → (peptidases, small intestine wall) → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.7.3] Fat model: large fat droplets → (bile salts, duodenum: emulsification, a physical change) → tiny droplets → (lipase from the pancreas, duodenum, pH 8) → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.7.4] Fat is digested only in the small intestine, after emulsification.
[bio27-s3.7.5] Using the models to predict: if the bile duct is blocked, for example by gallstones, fats are poorly emulsified and digested, and undigested fat appears in the faeces.
[bio27-s3.7.6] If the pancreas releases too little juice, as in cystic fibrosis, digestion of all three nutrients is reduced, which is why patients take enzyme capsules. There is more on this in Industrial enzymes.
[bio27-s3.7.7] Diagram: a flow chart with three rows (carbohydrate, protein, fat), each running left to right through mouth, stomach, duodenum and small intestine wall, showing enzyme or bile salts, pH and product at each step; or an alimentary canal (whole gut) outline with enzymes and pH at each site.
[bio27-s3.8.b] Visking tubing is a simple physical model of the small intestine. It shows why food must be digested into small molecules before it can pass into the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.0] Visking (dialysis) tubing is a selectively permeable membrane: it lets small molecules (water, glucose and maltose) through but not large ones (starch). It models the wall of the small intestine, and the water around it models the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.1] Method, step 1: tie one end of two lengths of Visking tubing, after soaking them in water.
[bio27-s3.8.2] Method, step 2: fill tube A with starch solution plus amylase solution. Fill tube B, the control (the comparison with no enzyme), with the same volume of starch solution plus water (or boiled amylase).
[bio27-s3.8.3] Method, step 3: tie the open ends, rinse the outsides, and place each tube in a separate boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C.
[bio27-s3.8.4] Method, step 4: at the start, and again after about 30 minutes, test samples of the water outside each tube with iodine solution (for starch) and with Benedict's solution in a hot water bath (for reducing sugar), using the food tests.
[bio27-s3.8.5] Expected results: the water outside both tubes stays yellow-brown with iodine, so no starch passes out.
[bio27-s3.8.6] After 30 minutes the water outside tube A turns brick red with Benedict's solution, showing reducing sugar, while the water outside tube B stays blue. Inside tube A, a test would show less starch and much reducing sugar.
[bio27-s3.8.7] Conclusion: starch molecules are too large to pass through the membrane. Amylase digests starch to maltose, which is small enough to pass through. This models why food must be digested before it can be absorbed into the blood. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio27-s3.8.8] Safety: wear safety glasses; Benedict's solution is an irritant; take care with hot water; heat the tubes in a water bath, never directly.
[bio27-s3.9.b] A model is useful only if you know where it differs from the real gut. The tubing copies the barrier but little else.
[bio27-s3.9.0] The tubing has no villi or microvilli, so its surface area is far smaller than the gut's.
[bio27-s3.9.1] No blood flows past to carry products away and keep the concentration gradient (the difference in concentration that drives diffusion).
[bio27-s3.9.2] The real gut absorbs some products by active transport (using energy to move molecules), which tubing cannot do.
[bio27-s3.9.3] Only one enzyme and one nutrient are modelled, at a constant pH, whereas the gut uses a sequence of enzymes and pH values.
[bio27-s3.9.4] The tubing does not move food by peristalsis (the waves of muscle contraction that push food along the gut).
[bio27-s3.9.5] Diagram: a boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C, with a knotted length of Visking tubing containing starch and amylase hanging in it; label Visking tubing, starch and amylase, distilled water, water bath at 37 °C; show the control tube beside it.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).
