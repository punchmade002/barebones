CANDIDATES bio13-s1: Enzymes as catalysts
[bio13-s1.0.b] Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions, and left alone most of them would be far too slow to keep a cell alive. Catalysts speed reactions up, and enzymes are the catalysts that living things make for themselves.
[bio13-s1.0.0] A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by it, so it can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.0.1] A catalyst does not change which products form or how much product is made in the end; it only makes the reaction reach that point faster.
[bio13-s1.0.2] An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a catalyst made by a living cell.
[bio13-s1.0.3] Almost all enzymes are globular proteins (proteins folded into a compact, rounded ball), folded into a precise three-dimensional shape. That shape decides what the enzyme can do. There is more on protein structure in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s1.0.4] Because enzymes are proteins, they are made at ribosomes (the tiny structures in a cell where proteins are built), following the instructions in a gene (the length of DNA that codes for the protein).
[bio13-s1.1.b] Because an enzyme is a protein that is reused rather than consumed, it has a set of properties that follow from that.
[bio13-s1.1.0] Enzymes are needed only in small amounts, because each molecule is reused many times; catalase, among the fastest, breaks down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second.
[bio13-s1.1.1] Enzymes are unchanged at the end of the reaction, which is why the same molecule can start work on another substrate straight away.
[bio13-s1.1.2] Enzymes are specific: each acts on one reaction or one type of reaction, because only a substrate of the right shape fits the enzyme's active site (the region where the substrate binds).
[bio13-s1.1.3] Enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH, and enzymes are denatured (their shape is changed so that they stop working) by heat and by extremes of pH. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio13-s1.2.b] Enzyme names usually give away the job the enzyme does. Enzymes also work in different places, depending on whether their job is inside or outside the cell.
[bio13-s1.2.0] Most enzyme names end in -ase, and many are named after their substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on), so the name tells you the job: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins, sucrase on sucrose.
[bio13-s1.2.1] Some older names, such as pepsin and trypsin, don't follow this rule, so don't assume that every enzyme name ends in -ase.
[bio13-s1.2.2] Most enzymes work inside the cell that made them (intracellular), for example the enzymes of respiration (releasing energy from food) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and of photosynthesis (making food using light) in chloroplasts.
[bio13-s1.2.3] Some enzymes are secreted to work outside the cell (extracellular), for example digestive enzymes released into the gut and the enzymes that fungi and bacteria release onto their food. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio13-s1.3.b] Even a reaction that releases energy overall needs a push to get started. An enzyme works by making that push smaller, not by adding energy.
[bio13-s1.3.0] Activation energy is the minimum energy that reacting molecules need before a reaction can start: the energy needed to break or strain existing bonds.
[bio13-s1.3.1] Think of activation energy as an energy hill that must be climbed even when the reaction releases energy overall.
[bio13-s1.3.2] Without a catalyst, most reactions of metabolism (the chemical reactions of life) have such a high activation energy that, at body temperature (37 °C), they would happen far too slowly to keep a cell alive.
[bio13-s1.3.3] Heating would supply the energy, but high temperatures would damage cells and their proteins, so heating is not an option for living things.
[bio13-s1.3.4] An enzyme provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at the temperature of the cell, and the reaction is many times faster.
[bio13-s1.3.5] The overall energy change of the reaction (energy released or taken in) stays the same; only the hill to be climbed gets lower.
[bio13-s1.3.6] Diagram: energy-profile graph, energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis. Reactants high on the left, products lower on the right.
[bio13-s1.3.7] Draw two humps, a tall one labelled without enzyme and a lower one labelled with enzyme. Mark each activation energy with a vertical arrow from the reactants to the top of its hump.
[bio13-s1.4.b] Every enzyme reaction has two partners: the enzyme and the molecule it works on. One small region of the enzyme does the work, and its shape is what makes the enzyme selective.
[bio13-s1.4.0] The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on; the products are the molecules made.
[bio13-s1.4.1] Example: catalase acts on its substrate hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
[bio13-s1.4.2] The active site is a small region of the enzyme, a pocket or groove on its surface, where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place.
[bio13-s1.4.3] The active site's shape, and the chemical charges in it, are complementary to the substrate, meaning the two fit together like matching pieces.
[bio13-s1.4.4] The active site is a part of the enzyme, not of the substrate. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site, not the same as it.
[bio13-s1.5.b] Once the substrate meets the active site, the reaction follows three steps. Together they make the active-site (lock-and-key) model of enzyme action.
[bio13-s1.5.0] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex (the enzyme and substrate joined together).
[bio13-s1.5.1] Enzyme and substrate molecules meet by random movement, so anything that increases collisions (more substrate, more enzyme, higher temperature) increases the rate, up to a point.
[bio13-s1.5.2] 2. While the substrate is held in the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, for example by straining the bonds that must break, or by holding two substrate molecules close together in the right position to react.
[bio13-s1.5.3] The substrate is then converted to products, forming an enzyme–product complex.
[bio13-s1.5.4] 3. The products have a different shape, no longer fit the active site, and are released.
[bio13-s1.5.5] The active site is then free and unchanged, ready to bind another substrate molecule, which is why one enzyme molecule can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.5.6] In summary: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products.
[bio13-s1.5.7] Diagram: three stages in a row: enzyme with active site and complementary substrate approaching; enzyme–substrate complex; unchanged enzyme with two products leaving. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex and products. A matching diagram for a joining reaction is optional.
[bio13-s1.6.b] The same active-site mechanism works whichever way a reaction runs. Metabolism has reactions that break molecules down and reactions that build them up, and enzymes catalyse both.
[bio13-s1.6.0] In catabolic reactions one substrate is split into smaller products, for example amylase splits starch into maltose.
[bio13-s1.6.1] In anabolic reactions two or more substrates are joined into a larger product. For example, DNA polymerase joins nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA) into a new DNA strand when DNA is copied.
[bio13-s1.6.2] Another anabolic example is ATP synthase, which joins ADP and phosphate into ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy.
[bio13-s1.7.b] A cell contains thousands of different molecules but does not react them all at random. Enzymes are selective: each one picks out its own substrate, and that selectivity comes from the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s1.7.0] Specificity means that each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with the complementary shape fits its active site.
[bio13-s1.7.1] This is what selective catalysts means: amylase digests starch but not protein or fat; pepsin digests protein but not starch.
[bio13-s1.7.2] The lock-and-key model is the simplest explanation: the substrate (the key) fits only the active site (the lock) of the right shape.
[bio13-s1.7.3] The lock-and-key model was later refined to the induced-fit model, in which the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio13-s1.7.4] Because enzymes are specific, a cell needs thousands of different enzymes, one for each of its reactions, and it can control a reaction by making, or not making, the one enzyme that catalyses it.
[bio13-s1.7.5] Anything that changes the shape of the active site stops the substrate fitting, so the enzyme stops working: for example high temperature or extreme pH (denaturation).
[bio13-s1.7.6] A change in the enzyme's amino-acid sequence (the order of the amino acids that make up the protein), caused by a mutation in its gene, also changes the active site and stops the substrate fitting.
[bio13-s1.8.b] Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in a living thing. Enzymes make these reactions possible, and they usually work in sequences called pathways.
[bio13-s1.8.0] Every reaction of metabolism is catalysed by its own specific enzyme.
[bio13-s1.8.1] Without enzymes, the reactions that keep cells alive (respiration, photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA copying, digestion) would be far too slow at the temperatures and pH inside living things.
[bio13-s1.8.2] Most processes are metabolic pathways: a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme, in which the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next.
[bio13-s1.8.3] Respiration and photosynthesis are such pathways. There is more on this in Respiration and Photosynthesis.
[bio13-s1.8.4] Diagram: a pathway written as A → B → C → D, with enzyme 1, enzyme 2 and enzyme 3 written over the arrows.
[bio13-s1.9.b] Because every step in a pathway needs its own enzyme, a cell controls its chemistry by controlling its enzymes.
[bio13-s1.9.0] A cell controls which reactions happen, and how fast, by switching on or off the genes for particular enzymes and by activating or inhibiting enzymes already made.
[bio13-s1.9.1] If one enzyme in a pathway is missing, the pathway stops at that step: its substrate builds up and the later products are not made.
[bio13-s1.9.2] Many enzymes need a non-protein helper to work: a mineral ion (for example magnesium for many enzymes that use ATP) or a coenzyme made from a vitamin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio13-s1.10.b] Because each reaction depends on one enzyme, blocking or losing that enzyme has serious effects. Some poisons act this way, and some inherited or common conditions come from a missing enzyme.
[bio13-s1.10.0] Some drugs and poisons work by blocking an enzyme: for example some insecticides and nerve agents inhibit the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter (a chemical messenger between nerve cells). There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.
[bio13-s1.10.1] Lactose intolerance: many adults make little lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose (milk sugar), so undigested lactose passes to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing bloating, wind, cramps and diarrhoea.
[bio13-s1.10.2] Lactose-free milk is made using lactase, which digests the lactose in the milk before it is drunk.
[bio13-s1.10.3] Phenylketonuria (PKU): an inherited lack of the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine into another amino acid (tyrosine), so phenylalanine builds up in the blood and damages the developing brain.
[bio13-s1.10.4] PKU is detected by the heel-prick test given to newborn babies in Ireland, and is controlled by a diet low in phenylalanine.

CANDIDATES bio13-s2: Enzyme structure and specificity
[bio13-s2.0.b] An enzyme is a protein, and the way its chain is folded decides what it can do. Folding builds a small pocket called the active site (the region where the substrate, the molecule the enzyme acts on, binds and is converted).
[bio13-s2.0.0] An enzyme's three-dimensional shape is set by the sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain (its primary structure), and that sequence is set by its gene, so the gene ultimately decides the shape.
[bio13-s2.0.1] The chain coils and folds (secondary structure), then folds further into a precise globular (rounded) shape (tertiary structure); some enzymes are made of more than one polypeptide (quaternary structure).
[bio13-s2.0.2] The active site is formed by folding: it is made of a small number of amino acids, often far apart in the chain, that folding brings together into a pocket or cleft.
[bio13-s2.0.3] Most of the rest of the enzyme holds these few active-site amino acids in exactly the right positions, so the whole molecule matters even though only a small part touches the substrate.
[bio13-s2.0.4] The R groups (side groups, the part of each amino acid that differs from the others) of the active-site amino acids give the site its shape and its pattern of charges and of water-attracting and water-repelling regions.
[bio13-s2.0.5] The R groups bind the substrate by weak attractions (hydrogen bonds and ionic attractions between opposite charges), and some take part directly in the reaction.
[bio13-s2.1.b] An enzyme acts only on particular substrates, a property called specificity. It comes from the active site's shape and chemistry, and that shape is only as stable as the bonds that hold the protein folded.
[bio13-s2.1.0] Specificity depends on two things: the shape of the active site (only a substrate of complementary shape fits) and its chemistry (only a substrate with matching charges and groups is held in the right position).
[bio13-s2.1.1] A molecule of similar shape but with the wrong charges is not catalysed, because its groups are not held in the correct position.
[bio13-s2.1.2] The shape is held by hydrogen bonds, by ionic bonds between charged R groups, and by attractions between water-repelling R groups; these are all weak bonds.
[bio13-s2.1.3] Disulfide (sulfur) bridges between cysteine units (cysteine is an amino acid that contains sulfur) are strong covalent bonds that also hold the shape.
[bio13-s2.1.4] Because most of these bonds are weak, the shape is easily changed, which is why enzymes are sensitive to conditions.
[bio13-s2.1.5] Diagram: a folded polypeptide chain drawn as a looping ribbon, with three or four amino acids (circles) from distant parts of the chain meeting to form a labelled active site; label polypeptide chain, R groups of active-site amino acids, active site, a hydrogen bond and a disulfide bridge.
[bio13-s2.2.b] An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its exact shape. Anything that alters the shape of the protein, or the amino acids that make it, changes how well it works.
[bio13-s2.2.0] Activity depends on the active site keeping its shape.
[bio13-s2.2.1] Heat makes the enzyme molecule vibrate until weak bonds break; a change in pH changes the charges on R groups (by adding or removing hydrogen ions), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds.
[bio13-s2.2.2] Either way the active site loses its shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured (permanently changed in shape and unable to work). The effects on rate, and the investigations, are in “Investigation: rate of enzyme activity”.
[bio13-s2.2.3] A change in a single amino acid can change the shape or charge of the active site, so a mutation in the gene can make an enzyme work less well or not at all.
[bio13-s2.2.4] Example: in phenylketonuria the enzyme that converts phenylalanine is faulty, so the conversion does not happen properly.
[bio13-s2.2.5] Some enzymes need a cofactor, such as a metal ion, held in or near the active site to complete its shape or chemistry; without it the enzyme is inactive.
[bio13-s2.3.b] Enzymes differ in how narrowly they choose a substrate. Some enzymes also have structures that keep their shape in conditions that would ruin most others.
[bio13-s2.3.0] Some enzymes act on only one substrate (absolute specificity): for example, urease acts only on urea, breaking it into ammonia and carbon dioxide.
[bio13-s2.3.1] Others act on a group of substrates that share the same type of bond (group specificity): for example, lipase acts on many different triglycerides, and proteases such as pepsin break peptide bonds in many different proteins.
[bio13-s2.3.2] A rigid lock-and-key active site does not explain group specificity well, because one fixed shape cannot fit many different substrates; the induced-fit model does.
[bio13-s2.3.3] Enzymes that work in extreme conditions have structures that hold their shape there: for example, Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, stays active at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s2.3.4] Pepsin keeps its shape in the strong acid of the stomach, where most enzymes are denatured.
[bio13-s2.4.b] The induced-fit model describes an active site that is flexible, not a fixed mould. It explains how an enzyme grips its substrate, strains it and releases the products, then is ready to go again.
[bio13-s2.4.0] The induced-fit model says the active site is flexible, not rigid. Before binding, its shape is close to, but not exactly, complementary to the substrate; binding of the substrate induces (causes) a change in the enzyme's shape that produces a close fit.
[bio13-s2.4.1] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and starts to bind to the active site through weak attractions to its R groups.
[bio13-s2.4.2] 2. Binding causes the enzyme to change shape: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove taking the shape of a hand, forming a tightly fitting enzyme–substrate complex.
[bio13-s2.4.3] 3. The change in shape puts strain on particular bonds in the substrate, or brings reacting groups of two substrates close together in the correct orientation.
[bio13-s2.4.4] 3 (continued). The R groups of the active site may lend or take hydrogen ions or electrons, which lowers the activation energy (the energy needed to start the reaction), and the substrate is converted to products.
[bio13-s2.4.5] 4. The products no longer fit the active site closely, so they are released.
[bio13-s2.4.6] 5. The enzyme returns to its original shape, unchanged and ready to bind another substrate molecule.
[bio13-s2.5.b] The earlier lock-and-key model treated the active site as rigid. Induced fit replaced it because it explains observations that a rigid site cannot.
[bio13-s2.5.0] Example: the enzyme hexokinase (used in the first stage of respiration) closes around its substrate glucose like a clamp when glucose binds; structural studies of enzymes showed this change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.1] Induced fit explains why some enzymes act on several similar substrates: a flexible site can mould around each of them.
[bio13-s2.5.2] Induced fit explains how binding itself helps catalysis: the shape change strains the substrate's bonds, lowering the activation energy.
[bio13-s2.5.3] Induced fit explains why molecules of similar shape may bind but not react: they do not induce the correct change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.4] Lock and key remains a useful simple model of specificity.
[bio13-s2.5.5] Diagram: induced fit in four labelled stages: (1) an enzyme whose active site does not quite match the approaching substrate; (2) substrate binding and the active site changing shape around it, forming the enzyme–substrate complex, with arrows showing the change.
[bio13-s2.5.6] Diagram (continued): (3) products formed in the active site; (4) products released and the enzyme back in its original shape. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, change in shape, enzyme–substrate complex and products.
[bio13-s2.5.7] Diagram (contrast): include a one-line sketch of lock and key, with a rigid active site that already fits the substrate exactly.
[bio13-s2.6.b] The two models differ in whether the active site can move. Setting them side by side shows why induced fit explains more.
[bio13-s2.6.0] Active site: rigid, with a fixed shape that exactly matches the substrate (lock and key); flexible, changing shape when the substrate binds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.1] Fit: an exact fit before binding (lock and key); a close fit only after binding (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.2] Role of binding: it holds the substrate only (lock and key); the change of shape itself helps the reaction by straining bonds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.3] Specificity explained: absolute specificity only (lock and key); both absolute and group specificity (induced fit), because a flexible site can mould around several similar substrates.

CANDIDATES bio13-s4: Industrial enzymes
[bio13-s4.0.b] Industry needs reactions that are fast, clean and cheap. Enzymes (biological catalysts, which speed up reactions without being changed) often do this better than the chemical catalysts used in factories.
[bio13-s4.0.0] Specificity: an enzyme catalyses one reaction, so it makes the wanted product with few unwanted by-products. This gives a purer product that needs less purification.
[bio13-s4.0.1] Mild conditions: enzymes work at moderate temperatures (often 30–60 °C), normal pressure and near-neutral pH (neither strongly acidic nor alkaline), whereas many chemical catalysts need high temperatures and pressures.
[bio13-s4.0.2] Mild conditions save energy and money, reduce greenhouse gas emissions, and make the process safer, because there are no very hot, high-pressure vessels.
[bio13-s4.0.3] Enzymes are efficient catalysts that are not used up, so a small amount can be used repeatedly. They are also biodegradable (broken down naturally), so they leave less harmful waste than many chemical catalysts.
[bio13-s4.1.b] Industry has to get its enzymes from living things, and enzymes have weaknesses as well as strengths. Knowing both explains how enzymes are produced and why the conditions in a factory are tightly controlled.
[bio13-s4.1.0] Most industrial enzymes are made by microorganisms (bacteria and fungi) grown in large vessels called fermenters or bioreactors. They are used because they grow fast, can be grown on cheap nutrients, and many release (secrete) their enzymes into the medium, where they are easy to collect.
[bio13-s4.1.1] Many industrial enzymes are now made by genetically modified microorganisms, which carry the gene for the enzyme. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio13-s4.1.2] A few enzymes come from plants (e.g. papain from papaya) or from animals, such as rennet (from calves' stomachs).
[bio13-s4.1.3] Limitation: enzymes are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working) by high temperatures and extreme pH, so conditions must be carefully controlled.
[bio13-s4.1.4] Limitation: enzymes can be expensive to produce and purify.
[bio13-s4.1.5] Limitation: free enzymes (ones simply dissolved in the mixture) are lost in the product after one use. Immobilisation, covered below, solves this.
[bio13-s4.1.6] Limitation: enzyme dust can cause allergies. In the early years of biological detergents, some factory workers developed allergies, so enzymes are now sealed in granules.
[bio13-s4.1.7] Enzymes from microorganisms that live in hot springs or in alkaline conditions are chosen or engineered for processes that need tougher enzymes, because they already work where normal enzymes would be denatured.
[bio13-s4.2.b] Milk is a good example of how industry uses single, specific enzymes. One enzyme turns milk into cheese, and another removes the sugar that some people cannot digest.
[bio13-s4.2.0] Cheese-making: chymosin (rennin), the active enzyme in rennet, clots the milk protein casein, so the milk separates into solid curds (made into cheese) and liquid whey.
[bio13-s4.2.1] Rennet was traditionally extracted from the stomachs of young calves. Much of the chymosin used today is made by genetically modified microorganisms, which suits vegetarians and needs no animals.
[bio13-s4.2.2] Lactose-free milk: lactase breaks lactose (the sugar in milk) down into glucose and galactose, so people with lactose intolerance (who cannot digest lactose) can drink the milk.
[bio13-s4.2.3] Lactose-free milk tastes sweeter, because glucose and galactose are sweeter than lactose. Lactase is often used immobilised.
[bio13-s4.3.b] Plant materials such as fruit and grain are full of structural and storage carbohydrates. Carbohydrate-digesting enzymes release the useful sugars and make processing easier.
[bio13-s4.3.0] Fruit juice: pectinase breaks down pectin, which helps hold plant cell walls together, so more juice is released from crushed fruit and the juice is clearer (less cloudy).
[bio13-s4.3.1] Sugar syrups: amylases break starch (e.g. from maize) into glucose syrup. Immobilised glucose isomerase then converts glucose into fructose, which is much sweeter.
[bio13-s4.3.2] High-fructose syrup is used to sweeten soft drinks and processed foods, so less is needed for the same sweetness.
[bio13-s4.3.3] Brewing and baking: amylases break starch in grain into maltose and glucose, which yeast ferments to make ethanol and carbon dioxide. In baking, the carbon dioxide helps the dough rise. There is more on fermentation in Respiration.
[bio13-s4.3.4] Proteases (enzymes that digest protein) are used to clear haze from beer.
[bio13-s4.4.b] Proteases, lipases and amylases digest the three main food molecules: protein, fat and starch. This is why they are useful both in sweets and meat and in household cleaning.
[bio13-s4.4.0] Proteases such as papain tenderise meat by breaking down muscle proteins.
[bio13-s4.4.1] Invertase (sucrase) converts sucrose into glucose and fructose, to make the soft centres of chocolates and other sweets.
[bio13-s4.4.2] Household link: biological washing powders contain proteases, lipases and amylases that digest protein stains (blood, egg, grass), fat and grease, and starch.
[bio13-s4.4.3] Biological washing powders work at low washing temperatures (e.g. 30–40 °C), saving energy. They are not used at very high temperatures, where the enzymes would be denatured.
[bio13-s4.5.b] Biofuels are fuels made from living material. Enzymes are needed to turn the carbohydrates and oils in plants into forms that yeast or chemistry can convert into fuel.
[bio13-s4.5.0] Bioethanol from starch crops: amylases break the starch of crops such as maize and wheat into glucose, which yeast ferments to ethanol. The ethanol is blended with petrol as a fuel.
[bio13-s4.5.1] Bioethanol from cellulose (a second-generation biofuel): cellulases break down the cellulose in straw, wood chips and other crop waste into glucose for fermentation.
[bio13-s4.5.2] Cellulose is tightly packed and hard to digest, so these enzymes are slow and costly and are an active area of research. There is more on cellulose in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s4.5.3] Biodiesel: lipases can catalyse the conversion of vegetable oils and waste cooking oils into biodiesel. This is an alternative to chemical catalysts that works at lower temperatures and leaves less waste.
[bio13-s4.6.b] Biofuels are often described as green, but the picture is more complicated. Exams ask you to weigh the benefits against the costs.
[bio13-s4.6.0] For: biofuels are renewable, because the crops can be regrown.
[bio13-s4.6.1] For: the carbon dioxide released when they burn was taken in by the crop during photosynthesis, so they add less to atmospheric CO₂ than fossil fuels.
[bio13-s4.6.2] For: using waste materials (straw, waste oil) adds value to waste.
[bio13-s4.6.3] Against: growing fuel crops uses land that could grow food, which can raise food prices.
[bio13-s4.6.4] Against: clearing forests for fuel crops destroys habitats and releases carbon.
[bio13-s4.6.5] Against: fertilisers, machinery and processing use fossil-fuel energy, so the saving is smaller than it first appears.
[bio13-s4.6.6] Cellulose-based and waste-based biofuels avoid most of the competition with food, because they use parts of the plant or waste that people do not eat. Carbon cycle and climate detail is in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio13-s4.7.b] Because enzymes are so specific, they are used in medicine to detect substances, break down harmful ones, and replace enzymes that the body fails to supply.
[bio13-s4.7.0] Diagnosis: glucose oxidase is used in blood-glucose test strips and electronic biosensors used by people with diabetes, and in urine test strips.
[bio13-s4.7.1] Glucose oxidase is specific to glucose, so other sugars do not interfere. The reaction produces a colour change or an electrical signal in proportion to the amount of glucose.
[bio13-s4.7.2] Dissolving blood clots: clot-dissolving enzymes such as streptokinase (from bacteria) and tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) are given soon after a heart attack or a stroke caused by a clot, to break down the clot and restore blood flow.
[bio13-s4.7.3] Enzyme replacement: people with cystic fibrosis often cannot release enough digestive enzymes from the pancreas, so they take capsules of pancreatic enzymes (lipase, protease and amylase) with meals.
[bio13-s4.7.4] Lactase tablets help people with lactose intolerance, because they supply the missing enzyme.
[bio13-s4.7.5] Cancer treatment: the enzyme asparaginase is used to treat a type of childhood leukaemia (acute lymphoblastic leukaemia).
[bio13-s4.7.6] The leukaemia cells cannot make enough of the amino acid asparagine and take it from the blood. The enzyme breaks down asparagine in the blood, starving the cancer cells.
[bio13-s4.7.7] Medical technologies built on enzymes (named only here): restriction enzymes and DNA ligase in genetic engineering (see “Genetic engineering”); Taq polymerase in PCR for DNA profiling and diagnostic testing (see “Generating a DNA profile”).
[bio13-s4.8.b] Making drugs chemically can be wasteful and use harsh conditions. Enzymes can make the same drugs more precisely and more cleanly.
[bio13-s4.8.0] Making antibiotics: immobilised penicillin acylase converts natural penicillin, made by the Penicillium mould, into a starting molecule (6-APA) from which semi-synthetic penicillins, such as amoxicillin, are made.
[bio13-s4.8.1] Semi-synthetic penicillins can treat infections that the natural form does not.
[bio13-s4.8.2] Making single-form drugs: many drug molecules exist in two mirror-image forms, of which only one works (the other may be useless or harmful).
[bio13-s4.8.3] Because enzymes are specific, they make only the wanted form, avoiding wasteful separation. For example, an engineered enzyme (a transaminase) is used to manufacture the diabetes drug sitagliptin.
[bio13-s4.8.4] Enzymes allow drug manufacture at mild temperatures with less toxic solvent waste than chemical methods (“green chemistry”). Human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria depends on enzymes used to insert the gene (see “Genetic engineering”).
[bio13-s4.9.b] Free enzymes mix into the product and are lost after one use. Fixing the enzyme in place solves this, and it is a central idea in industrial enzyme use.
[bio13-s4.9.0] An immobilised enzyme is an enzyme (or a whole cell containing it) fixed to, or trapped in, an inert, insoluble material. It is held in place while the substrate solution passes over or through it, so it does not mix freely with the product.
[bio13-s4.9.1] Entrapment: the enzyme is trapped in a gel bead, such as calcium alginate.
[bio13-s4.9.2] Adsorption: the enzyme is stuck to the surface of an insoluble support, such as glass beads, resin or charcoal.
[bio13-s4.9.3] Covalent bonding: the enzyme is chemically bonded to a support.
[bio13-s4.9.4] Enclosure: the enzyme is enclosed behind a partially permeable membrane.
[bio13-s4.9.5] Examples: glucose isomerase for high-fructose syrup; lactase for lactose-free milk; penicillin acylase for semi-synthetic penicillins.
[bio13-s4.10.b] Immobilisation brings clear benefits in cost and purity, but it also adds complications. Exams ask for both sides.
[bio13-s4.10.0] Advantage: the enzyme can be recovered and reused many times, cutting costs.
[bio13-s4.10.1] Advantage: the product is not contaminated with enzyme, so it needs no purification.
[bio13-s4.10.2] Advantage: the enzyme can be packed in a column for a continuous-flow process, with substrate fed in at the top and product collected at the bottom.
[bio13-s4.10.3] Advantage: immobilised enzymes are often more stable, being less easily denatured by changes in temperature or pH. The reaction can be stopped at once by removing the enzyme.
[bio13-s4.10.4] Disadvantage: immobilising costs money and equipment to set up, and some activity is lost, as part of the active sites (where the substrate binds) may be blocked or altered.
[bio13-s4.10.5] Disadvantage: the reaction can be slower, because the substrate must diffuse into the bead to reach the enzyme. Enzyme may also leak out of the support.
[bio13-s4.10.6] Disadvantage: immobilised enzymes are unsuitable where the substrate molecules are too large to enter the beads.
[bio13-s4.11.b] This is the school method for immobilising an enzyme. Yeast is used because its cells contain the enzyme sucrase (also called invertase), which acts on sucrose.
[bio13-s4.11.0] 1. Mix sodium alginate solution with a suspension of yeast (which contains the enzyme sucrase, also called invertase) or with an enzyme solution.
[bio13-s4.11.1] 2. Draw the mixture into a syringe and drop it, one drop at a time, into calcium chloride solution: each drop forms a bead of insoluble calcium alginate gel trapping the yeast.
[bio13-s4.11.2] 3. Leave the beads to harden for about 15–20 minutes.
[bio13-s4.11.3] 4. Sieve them out and rinse them with distilled water to remove the calcium chloride and any free yeast.
[bio13-s4.11.4] 5. Place the beads in a separating funnel (or a syringe barrel with a tap) as a column.
[bio13-s4.11.5] Safety: calcium chloride irritates the eyes, so wear safety glasses and wash spills.
[bio13-s4.11.6] Diagram: (a) a syringe dripping the alginate–yeast mixture into a beaker of calcium chloride solution, with beads forming, labelled; (b) a separating funnel or column packed with beads, with sucrose solution added at the top, a tap, and product (glucose and fructose) collected below, labelled.
[bio13-s4.12.b] Once the column is made, sucrose solution is passed through it and the liquid that comes out is tested. A control and a comparison with free yeast show that the beads do the work and that immobilisation helps.
[bio13-s4.12.0] Using the beads: pour sucrose solution through the column and collect the liquid that drips out. Test it with a glucose test strip.
[bio13-s4.12.1] A positive glucose result shows that the sucrose has been converted to glucose and fructose. The sucrose solution tested before it enters the column gives a negative result.
[bio13-s4.12.2] Control: beads made without yeast (alginate only). Their product gives no glucose, so the conversion is due to the yeast and not the beads.
[bio13-s4.12.3] The product from the beads is clear, while sucrose mixed with free yeast becomes cloudy with yeast cells that must be removed, showing the advantage of immobilisation. The beads can be rinsed and used again.
[bio13-s4.13.b] Enzymes and cells work best when conditions are right. A bioreactor is the vessel that holds those conditions steady in industry.
[bio13-s4.13.0] A bioreactor is a vessel in which cells or enzymes carry out a reaction under controlled conditions: temperature, pH, oxygen (or its absence), nutrient supply, mixing and sterility are monitored and kept at the values that suit the enzyme.
[bio13-s4.13.1] A column of immobilised enzyme is a simple bioreactor. There is more on growing microorganisms in bioreactors, and on batch and continuous culture, in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio13-s4.14.b] The uses above fall into three themes that link enzymes to health, sustainability and technology. Exams may ask you to draw these together.
[bio13-s4.14.0] Health: diagnostic strips and biosensors, clot-busting and replacement enzymes, and lactose-free foods.
[bio13-s4.14.1] Sustainability: lower energy use and less chemical waste; low-temperature washing; biofuels and the arguments about them; more juice from the same fruit.
[bio13-s4.14.2] Technology: genetic modification of microorganisms to make enzymes, engineered enzymes, immobilisation and continuous-flow bioreactors.

CANDIDATES bio15-s1: Respiration as a catabolic process
[bio15-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a constant supply of usable energy. Respiration is the process that gets it, by breaking down food molecules and transferring the energy they hold to ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio15-s1.0.0] Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food, mainly glucose, in every living cell. The energy released is transferred to ATP, so respiration releases energy rather than making it.
[bio15-s1.0.1] Respiration is catabolic, meaning a large molecule such as glucose is broken down into smaller molecules and energy is released. The products are CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂, depending on the conditions.
[bio15-s1.0.2] Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process, because it builds small molecules up into large ones and takes in energy, whereas respiration breaks large ones down.
[bio15-s1.0.3] The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once, as in burning. This is why much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. Energy not trapped as ATP is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
[bio15-s1.0.4] The ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport (moving substances across a membrane using energy) and building large molecules.
[bio15-s1.0.5] Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed (built into sugar).
[bio15-s1.0.6] Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake of carbon by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle. There is more on this in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio15-s1.0.7] Glucose is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down). Glycogen (in animals) and starch (in plants) are first broken down to glucose, and fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired.
[bio15-s1.1.b] Aerobic (with oxygen) respiration breaks glucose down completely, releasing energy. Knowing where the glucose and oxygen come from, and where the carbon dioxide and water go, shows how organisms and their surroundings are linked.
[bio15-s1.1.0] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
[bio15-s1.1.1] Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Its chemicals are those of the photosynthesis equation reversed, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
[bio15-s1.1.2] Source of glucose in plants: photosynthesis, or stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant). In animals: digested food absorbed into the blood, or glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
[bio15-s1.1.3] Source of oxygen in humans: the air, taken in through the lungs and carried to the cells by red blood cells, which is how it reaches every cell.
[bio15-s1.1.4] Source of oxygen in plants: the air, entering through stomata (leaf pores) and lenticels (pores in woody stems); soil air, taken in by the roots; and the plant's own photosynthesis. In aquatic organisms it is oxygen dissolved in water.
[bio15-s1.1.5] Fate of carbon dioxide: it diffuses out of cells. In humans it is carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
[bio15-s1.1.6] Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted. Some is breathed out as water vapour.
[bio15-s1.2.b] When oxygen is scarce or absent, cells can still release some energy from glucose, but not nearly as much. The products depend on the organism.
[bio15-s1.2.0] Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. Because the glucose is only partly broken down, much of the energy stays in the products, so much less energy is released per glucose and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.2.1] In animals, such as human muscle during intense exercise, and in some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
[bio15-s1.2.2] In yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen such as roots in waterlogged soil, the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
[bio15-s1.2.3] Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. In baking, yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise and the ethanol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and wine-making, yeast's ethanol is the useful product. In making yogurt and cheese, bacteria's lactic acid is the useful product.
[bio15-s1.2.4] In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire.
[bio15-s1.2.5] Oxygen debt: after exercise, extra oxygen is needed to remove the lactic acid. It is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose, and the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
[bio15-s1.2.6] Obligate anaerobes respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen, for example the bacteria that cause tetanus. Yeast can respire either way, so it is a facultative anaerobe.
[bio15-s1.3.b] Respiration happens in two stages in different parts of the cell. Stage 1 is the same whether or not oxygen is present, and what happens next depends on oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid inside the cell) of every cell and does not need oxygen. 1. Glucose, with 6 carbon atoms, is split into two molecules of pyruvate, each with 3 carbon atoms. 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
[bio15-s1.3.1] With oxygen, stage 2 takes place in the mitochondria (the organelles where aerobic respiration is completed). 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water.
[bio15-s1.3.2] 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.3] Without oxygen, pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (in animals and some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast and plants). No further ATP is made, so anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s1.3.4] Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria, so their aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane.
[bio15-s1.3.5] Diagram: flow diagram. Glucose (cytoplasm), then glycolysis (small ATP), then 2 pyruvate. Oxygen present: mitochondrion, then CO₂, water, large ATP. No oxygen: cytoplasm, then lactic acid (animals) or ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1, stage 2.
[bio15-s1.4.b] Aerobic and anaerobic respiration start the same way but differ in how far glucose is broken down and how much ATP results. Respiration also contrasts with photosynthesis: respiration is catabolic and happens in all living cells, while photosynthesis is anabolic and happens only in cells with chloroplasts.
[bio15-s1.4.0] Oxygen: needed in aerobic respiration; not needed in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.1] Location: aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria; anaerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm only.
[bio15-s1.4.2] Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O, in aerobic respiration; incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂, in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.3] Energy: a large amount of ATP in aerobic respiration; a small amount, only the ATP made in glycolysis, in anaerobic respiration, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio15-s1.4.4] Stages: stage 1 followed by stage 2 in aerobic respiration; stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.5] Respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂. It happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
[bio15-s1.4.6] Photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂. It happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only in the light.

CANDIDATES bio15-s4: Second-stage process
[bio15-s4.0.b] Glycolysis (the first stage of respiration, in the cytoplasm) only splits glucose part of the way and ends with pyruvate. If oxygen is available, the cell passes pyruvate on to the second stage, and the first job there is a short link step that prepares it for the citric acid cycle.
[bio15-s4.0.0] When oxygen is present, each pyruvate (a 3-carbon molecule, C₃) made in glycolysis moves from the cytoplasm into the matrix of a mitochondrion. The matrix is the fluid-filled space inside the inner membrane.
[bio15-s4.0.1] In the matrix, pyruvate is broken down. One carbon atom is removed as one molecule of carbon dioxide, and hydrogen is removed and picked up by NAD⁺ (a hydrogen carrier), which turns it into NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.2] The remaining two-carbon (acetyl) group is joined to coenzyme A, a carrier molecule, forming acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA, C₂). Per pyruvate: pyruvate + coenzyme A + NAD⁺ → acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.3] Each glucose gives two pyruvate, so this link step happens twice per glucose, producing 2 CO₂ and 2 NADH.
[bio15-s4.1.b] Acetyl CoA is the fuel for the citric acid cycle, which strips the carbon from the acetyl group off as CO₂ and loads hydrogen onto carriers. Because the cycle rebuilds its starting compound every turn, it can keep running as long as acetyl CoA keeps arriving.
[bio15-s4.1.0] The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, is a cycle of enzyme-controlled reactions in the matrix of the mitochondrion, not on the cristae (the folds of the inner membrane).
[bio15-s4.1.1] 1. The acetyl CoA passes its two-carbon acetyl group into the cycle, where it joins a four-carbon compound to form citric acid (C₆), which gives the cycle its name. Coenzyme A is released to collect another acetyl group.
[bio15-s4.1.2] 2. In a series of steps, citric acid is broken down back to the four-carbon compound. This is regenerated to accept the next acetyl group, which is why it is a cycle.
[bio15-s4.1.3] 3. During each turn, two carbon atoms are removed as two molecules of CO₂, so the carbon of the acetyl group leaves as CO₂.
[bio15-s4.1.4] 4. Hydrogen, as protons and electrons, is removed at several steps and loaded onto NAD⁺, forming NADH. A small amount of ATP, one per turn, is also made directly.
[bio15-s4.1.5] The cycle turns twice for each glucose, once per acetyl CoA. With the link step, all six carbon atoms of glucose have now left as six molecules of CO₂, matching the 6CO₂ in the respiration equation. Glycolysis releases none.
[bio15-s4.1.6] The specification's products are CO₂ and H₂O. The CO₂ is released directly. The hydrogen removed is carried by NADH to the electron transport chain, where it ends up in water, so water is not made in the cycle itself.
[bio15-s4.1.7] The cycle does not need oxygen directly, but it stops without oxygen, because NADH cannot be unloaded and NAD⁺ runs out.
[bio15-s4.2.b] Most of the energy in glucose is still locked up in the hydrogen carried by NADH. The electron transport chain releases it in small steps and uses it to make ATP, which is why far more ATP per glucose is made in the second stage than the net 2 ATP of glycolysis. Aerobic respiration (respiration with oxygen) therefore releases many times more energy per glucose than fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.0] The electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers (protein complexes and mobile carriers) in the inner mitochondrial membrane, especially on the folds called cristae.
[bio15-s4.2.1] 1. NADH from glycolysis, the link step and the citric acid cycle delivers its high-energy electrons and protons to the start of the chain. NAD⁺ is released to collect more.
[bio15-s4.2.2] 2. The electrons are passed from carrier to carrier, each at a lower energy level. The energy released at each transfer is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi (phosphate).
[bio15-s4.2.3] 3. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor. At the end of the chain, low-energy electrons combine with oxygen and protons (H⁺) to form water: 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ + O₂ → 2H₂O. It is the only step of respiration using oxygen directly, and the source of the 6H₂O in the equation.
[bio15-s4.2.4] 4. If oxygen is absent, electrons cannot leave the end of the chain, so the whole chain stops. NADH cannot be unloaded, so the citric acid cycle and the link step stop too, and the cell can then only carry out glycolysis and fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.5] Cyanide is a poison because it blocks the last complex of the chain, which has the same effect as having no oxygen: the chain stops and aerobic ATP production collapses.
[bio15-s4.2.6] 5. The ATP made is exported from the mitochondrion to the cytoplasm and used for other biochemical reactions in the cell, such as synthesis of proteins, active transport and muscle contraction.
[bio15-s4.2.7] Most of the ATP from aerobic respiration is made here, via a proton gradient and ATP synthase. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.b] Respiration can be modelled as two stages joined by carrier molecules that shuttle electrons, hydrogen, acetyl groups and energy around the cell. Putting the stages together shows where each product comes from and where each reactant is used. There is more on the membrane comparison with photosynthesis in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm: glucose becomes 2 pyruvate, a net 2 ATP is made directly, and NAD⁺ is converted to NADH.
[bio15-s4.3.1] Stage 2 takes place in the mitochondrion. The link step and the citric acid cycle, in the matrix, turn pyruvate into acetyl CoA and then into CO₂, converting NAD⁺ to NADH and making a little ATP directly.
[bio15-s4.3.2] The electron transport chain, on the inner membrane (cristae), unloads NADH to NAD⁺, passes electrons to oxygen to form water, and makes most of the ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio15-s4.3.3] Transfer molecules carry energy and electrons between the stages. NAD⁺/NADH carries electrons and hydrogen from the breakdown of glucose (glycolysis, link step and cycle) to the electron transport chain, and returns empty. Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group into the cycle and returns.
[bio15-s4.3.4] ATP carries energy from the mitochondrion and cytoplasm to the cell's activities, returning as ADP + Pi to be reloaded. All the transfer molecules are recycled, so only small amounts are needed.
[bio15-s4.3.5] Tracing the atoms: the carbon of glucose leaves as CO₂ in the link step and the cycle. The hydrogen of glucose ends up in water, formed by combining with oxygen breathed in at the electron transport chain. The oxygen breathed in therefore ends up in water, not in CO₂.
[bio15-s4.3.6] Comparison with photosynthesis: in photosynthesis, NADPH and ATP are made in stage 1, using light energy, and used in stage 2 to build glucose. In respiration, NADH is made as glucose is broken down and is used at the end of stage 2 to make ATP.
[bio15-s4.3.7] Diagram: cytoplasm and a mitochondrion (outer membrane, inner membrane with cristae, matrix). Label: glucose, 2 pyruvate, NAD⁺ → NADH, acetyl CoA, CO₂, citric acid cycle (acetyl group + C₄ → citric acid (C₆) → back to C₄), electron transport chain, O₂ + H⁺ → H₂O, ATP, NAD⁺; what each carrier carries.

CANDIDATES bio15-s5: The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase
[bio15-s5.0.b] A mitochondrion is an organelle (a specialised compartment inside a cell). Its inside is divided into distinct regions by two membranes, and each region has a job.
[bio15-s5.0.0] Outer membrane: smooth, it surrounds the mitochondrion and is permeable to small molecules such as pyruvate, ADP and ATP, so these can move freely in and out.
[bio15-s5.0.1] Inner membrane: folded into many cristae (singular crista), which greatly increase its surface area. It holds the electron transport chains and ATP synthase (the enzyme that makes ATP). It is impermeable to protons except through ATP synthase.
[bio15-s5.0.2] Intermembrane space: the narrow space between the outer and inner membranes, into which protons are moved. This is the space "outside the inner membrane" in the specification's wording, not outside the mitochondrion.
[bio15-s5.0.3] Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane. It contains the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle (the earlier stages of respiration that deliver electrons to the chain).
[bio15-s5.0.4] The matrix also holds the mitochondrion's own small loop of DNA and its ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), so the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins.
[bio15-s5.0.5] Diagram: electron-microscope-level drawing of a mitochondrion in section. Label outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae, intermembrane space, matrix, DNA, ribosomes, and ATP synthase drawn as small knobs on the matrix side of the cristae.
[bio15-s5.1.b] The electron transport chain is the machinery that passes electrons along a series of steps, releasing energy at each one. It is built into the inner membrane, so the membrane's structure matters.
[bio15-s5.1.0] The chain is made of a number of large protein complexes embedded in, and spanning, the inner membrane (reaching from one side to the other).
[bio15-s5.1.1] Smaller mobile electron carriers link the complexes. They move within or along the membrane and carry electrons from one complex to the next, so the electrons are handed on step by step.
[bio15-s5.1.2] NADH (the carrier that brings electrons from earlier stages of respiration) delivers electrons to the first complex. The electrons then move from complex to complex, each at a lower energy level.
[bio15-s5.1.3] At the last complex the electrons are passed to oxygen, forming water. There is more on this in Second-stage process.
[bio15-s5.2.b] The electrons do not make ATP directly. Their energy is used to build up a store of protons on one side of the inner membrane, and the protons' flow back drives ATP synthase. The sequence runs in seven steps.
[bio15-s5.2.0] 1. Electrons arrive: high-energy electrons from NADH are transferred to a protein complex in the inner membrane. Electrons are negatively charged, so their arrival gives the complex a negative charge, a charge difference.
[bio15-s5.2.1] 2. Protons are attracted: the charge difference attracts positively charged protons (H⁺) to the complex. The protons are given by donor molecules: NADH releases a proton when it gives up its electrons, and more protons come from the solution in the matrix.
[bio15-s5.2.2] 3. Protons are moved out: the protons pass through the complex from the matrix side to the outside of the inner membrane, into the intermembrane space. Energy from the electrons drives this. It happens at several complexes along the chain as the electrons pass from one to the next. Textbooks call this proton pumping.
[bio15-s5.2.3] 4. A gradient builds: protons accumulate in the narrow intermembrane space, giving a high concentration of protons outside the inner membrane and a low concentration in the matrix. This proton (concentration) gradient is a store of energy, like water held behind a dam. It also makes the intermembrane space more positive and more acidic than the matrix.
[bio15-s5.2.4] 5. Protons flow back: the inner membrane lets protons through only at the enzyme ATP synthase. They flow back down their gradient, from the intermembrane space across the inner membrane into the matrix, through a channel in ATP synthase. They do not pass back through the electron transport complexes.
[bio15-s5.2.5] 6. ATP is made: ATP synthase harnesses the flow of protons. It turns part of the enzyme, like a water wheel or turbine, and uses the energy to join ADP and Pi (inorganic phosphate) into ATP on the matrix side.
[bio15-s5.2.6] 7. Oxygen keeps the system running: oxygen accepts the electrons and protons at the end of the chain, forming water, so electrons keep flowing and protons keep being moved out. Without oxygen, the gradient is not maintained and ATP synthase stops.
[bio15-s5.2.7] Diagram: inner membrane, matrix below, intermembrane space above. Show NADH → NAD⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻; electron arrow; many H⁺ up; ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O; H⁺ down ATP synthase, ADP + Pi → ATP. Label protein complex, mobile electron carrier, inner membrane, intermembrane space, matrix, ATP synthase, proton gradient.
[bio15-s5.3.b] The process in the last block has a name, and its history shows how a new scientific idea can win acceptance.
[bio15-s5.3.0] Making ATP using a proton gradient across a membrane is called chemiosmosis.
[bio15-s5.3.1] Nature of Science: the idea was proposed by the British biochemist Peter Mitchell in 1961 and was at first widely doubted. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978, once experiments had supported it.
[bio15-s5.4.b] Each feature of the mitochondrion's structure suits the way the proton gradient is built and used. Linking a feature to its effect is how you answer structure-and-function questions.
[bio15-s5.4.0] Cristae: folding gives a large area of inner membrane, which holds very many electron transport chains and ATP synthase molecules, so more ATP can be made. Mitochondria in very active cells, such as heart and flight muscle, have more densely packed cristae.
[bio15-s5.4.1] Narrow intermembrane space: its small volume means that protons moved into it quickly build up a high concentration and a steep gradient.
[bio15-s5.4.2] Impermeable inner membrane: the gradient is kept, and protons can return only through ATP synthase, so their energy is not wasted.
[bio15-s5.4.3] Complexes embedded in the membrane in a fixed order: electrons pass quickly and in sequence, and protons are moved in one direction only, across the membrane.
[bio15-s5.4.4] Matrix next to the inner membrane: the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle are right beside the chains, so NADH is delivered quickly. ATP is made into the matrix, from where it is exported to the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s5.4.5] Double membrane: separates the matrix's enzymes and conditions from the cytoplasm. The outer and inner membranes together create the intermembrane space.
[bio15-s5.4.6] Own DNA and ribosomes: the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins, including some of the chain's, and can divide to make more mitochondria when a cell needs more energy, for example in muscles during training.
[bio15-s5.5.b] Chloroplasts (the organelles where photosynthesis happens) make ATP using a very similar mechanism, built into their thylakoid membranes (flattened internal membrane sacs). The two organelles are alike in several ways, then differ in the details.
[bio15-s5.5.0] Both have electron transport chains of protein complexes and mobile electron carriers embedded in a membrane.
[bio15-s5.5.1] Both use the energy of electrons passing along the chain to move protons across the membrane, building a proton gradient, and both make ATP as protons flow back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis).
[bio15-s5.5.2] Both have a large membrane area, from cristae in the mitochondrion or stacked thylakoids in the chloroplast, so each can hold many chains and ATP synthase molecules.
[bio15-s5.5.3] Both organelles have their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio15-s5.6.b] The shared mechanism is fed by different energy and electron sources, and it sits in different membranes and compartments. Compare each feature as a pair.
[bio15-s5.6.0] Source of energy: the mitochondrion uses chemical energy from food (glucose), delivered as electrons by NADH; the chloroplast uses light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll.
[bio15-s5.6.1] Source of electrons: NADH, from glucose, in the mitochondrion; water, via the reaction-centre chlorophyll, in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.2] Final electron acceptor: oxygen, forming water, in the mitochondrion; NADP⁺, forming NADPH, in the chloroplast (non-cyclic pathway).
[bio15-s5.6.3] Oxygen: used at the end of the chain in mitochondria; released by photolysis (the splitting of water) in chloroplasts.
[bio15-s5.6.4] Which membrane: the inner membrane of the envelope (cristae) in the mitochondrion; the thylakoid membrane, a separate internal membrane system, in the chloroplast, not its envelope.
[bio15-s5.6.5] Where protons build up: in the intermembrane space, between the inner and outer membranes, in the mitochondrion; inside the thylakoid space in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.6] Where ATP is made and used: in the matrix, then exported for use all over the cell, in the mitochondrion; in the stroma (the fluid around the thylakoids), where it is used in the light-independent reactions, in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.7] Diagram: side-by-side sketches of a section of the inner mitochondrial membrane and a section of the thylakoid membrane. Each shows its chain, the direction of proton movement, the high-proton compartment (intermembrane space; thylakoid space) and ATP synthase. Label the electron source and final acceptor on each.

CANDIDATES bio8-s2: Structures of biomolecules
[bio8-s2.0.b] Carbohydrates are the first of the four main biomolecules, the large carbon-based molecules of living things. They share one simple pattern in their formula and fall into three groups by size.
[bio8-s2.0.0] Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. Their general formula is Cx(H₂O)y, so hydrogen and oxygen are in the ratio 2 : 1, as in water. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is an example.
[bio8-s2.0.1] The name means “hydrated carbon” because of that ratio, but the molecule does not contain water molecules. The H and O atoms are simply bonded into the sugar in the same 2 : 1 proportion.
[bio8-s2.0.2] There are three groups by size: monosaccharides (one sugar unit), disaccharides (two units) and polysaccharides (many units).
[bio8-s2.0.3] Mono- and disaccharides are sugars: sweet, soluble in water and crystalline.
[bio8-s2.0.4] Polysaccharides are not sweet and are insoluble or only slightly soluble, which suits them to storage and structure, because a molecule that does not dissolve stays put inside a cell or in a wall.
[bio8-s2.1.b] Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates: a single sugar unit. They are the monomers (single building-block units) from which every other carbohydrate is made.
[bio8-s2.1.0] Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule broken down in respiration to release energy) and the product of photosynthesis.
[bio8-s2.1.1] Fructose, the fruit sugar found in fruit and honey, has the same formula, C₆H₁₂O₆, but a different arrangement of atoms. Two molecules can share a formula and still be different sugars.
[bio8-s2.1.2] Galactose also has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆ and is a unit of lactose, the sugar in milk.
[bio8-s2.1.3] Glucose, fructose and galactose are all six-carbon sugars (hexoses).
[bio8-s2.1.4] Ribose and deoxyribose are five-carbon monosaccharides (pentoses). Ribose is found in RNA nucleotides and deoxyribose in DNA nucleotides. There is more on this in DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code.
[bio8-s2.1.5] Diagram: glucose drawn as a simple six-sided ring (hexagon) labelled glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆. Atom-by-atom structural formulas are not needed.
[bio8-s2.2.b] Two monosaccharides can be joined to make a disaccharide. The way they join is the same reaction that later builds starch and other large molecules.
[bio8-s2.2.0] A disaccharide forms when two monosaccharides join by condensation, a reaction that releases one molecule of water. The link between the sugar units is a glycosidic bond.
[bio8-s2.2.1] Disaccharides have the formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, which is 2 × C₆H₁₂O₆ minus H₂O, because one water molecule is lost when the two units join.
[bio8-s2.2.2] Hydrolysis (splitting a bond by adding water) splits a disaccharide back into monosaccharides. It is the reverse of condensation.
[bio8-s2.2.3] Maltose (malt sugar) is glucose + glucose. It forms when starch is digested by amylase (an enzyme that breaks down starch) and is found in germinating grain.
[bio8-s2.2.4] Sucrose (table sugar, cane or beet sugar) is glucose + fructose. It is the form in which plants transport sugar in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant).
[bio8-s2.2.5] Lactose (milk sugar) is glucose + galactose and is found in milk.
[bio8-s2.2.6] Diagram: two hexagons joined, with H₂O removed by condensation and returned by hydrolysis; label maltose = glucose + glucose.
[bio8-s2.3.b] A polysaccharide is a very long chain of sugar units. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all made of glucose, yet they do very different jobs because of how the units are linked and whether the chain branches.
[bio8-s2.3.0] A polysaccharide is a polymer (a long molecule made of many repeating units) of many monosaccharide units, often thousands, joined by condensation. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all polymers of glucose; they differ in how the glucose units are linked and whether the chains branch.
[bio8-s2.3.1] Starch is the storage carbohydrate of plants, held as starch grains in cells of potato tubers, seeds and cereal grains. It is a mixture of amylose, an unbranched chain that coils into a helix, and amylopectin, a branched chain.
[bio8-s2.3.2] Being insoluble, starch does not draw water into cells by osmosis (water moving in towards a more concentrated solution), and its compact shape stores much glucose in a small space.
[bio8-s2.3.3] Glycogen is the storage carbohydrate of animals and fungi, stored mainly in the liver and muscles. It is more highly branched than amylopectin, so it has many chain ends from which glucose can be released quickly when energy is needed.
[bio8-s2.3.4] Cellulose is the structural carbohydrate of plants, forming the cell wall. Its glucose units are linked in a different orientation from starch, giving long, straight, unbranched chains that lie side by side. Many hydrogen bonds (weak attractions between neighbouring chains) hold them together, forming strong fibres.
[bio8-s2.3.5] Humans have no enzyme to digest cellulose, so it passes through the gut as dietary fibre (roughage). There is more on cellulose in Biomolecules and metabolism.
[bio8-s2.3.6] Chitin is a polysaccharide that contains nitrogen. It forms the cell walls of fungi and the exoskeletons (hard outer coverings) of insects and other arthropods.
[bio8-s2.3.7] Diagram: a chain of hexagons for starch (unbranched amylose and branched amylopectin), a more branched chain for glycogen, and parallel straight chains linked by cross-links for cellulose fibres.
[bio8-s2.4.b] Lipids are the second biomolecule group. The triglyceride, one of the main kinds, is built from just two kinds of smaller molecule joined by condensation.
[bio8-s2.4.0] Lipids are fats, oils and related compounds such as phospholipids. They contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with much less oxygen in proportion to carbon and hydrogen than carbohydrates, so they are not of the form Cx(H₂O)y. Phospholipids also contain phosphorus.
[bio8-s2.4.1] Lipids are insoluble in water but dissolve in organic solvents such as ethanol.
[bio8-s2.4.2] A triglyceride is one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids. Glycerol is a small molecule with three carbons, each carrying an –OH group, so it can bond to three fatty acids.
[bio8-s2.4.3] A fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain of carbon and hydrogen atoms with an acid (carboxyl, –COOH) group at one end.
[bio8-s2.4.4] Each fatty acid joins to glycerol by condensation, so forming one triglyceride releases three water molecules. The bond formed is an ester bond.
[bio8-s2.4.5] Hydrolysis by lipase (an enzyme that digests fats) reverses this, giving glycerol and fatty acids. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio8-s2.4.6] Diagram: a triglyceride drawn as an E shape, with a vertical glycerol bar and three horizontal fatty acid tails, labelled glycerol, fatty acid and 3 H₂O removed.
[bio8-s2.5.b] Fats and oils have the same basic unit, the triglyceride. They differ in the fatty acids attached, and that decides whether they are solid or liquid at room temperature.
[bio8-s2.5.0] There are many different fatty acids, varying in chain length and in whether they contain carbon–carbon double bonds. Glycerol combines with different fatty acids to give many different fats and oils.
[bio8-s2.5.1] Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds, so the chain holds the maximum hydrogen. Their straight chains pack closely, so fats rich in them are solid at room temperature.
[bio8-s2.5.2] Saturated examples are butter, lard and the fat on meat. These are mostly animal fats.
[bio8-s2.5.3] Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds, which put kinks (bends) in the chain. Kinked chains pack loosely, so oils are liquid at room temperature.
[bio8-s2.5.4] Unsaturated examples are olive oil, sunflower oil and fish oils. These are mostly from plants and fish.
[bio8-s2.6.b] A phospholipid is a triglyceride with one change: a phosphate group takes the place of a fatty acid. That small change gives the molecule one end that likes water and one that avoids it.
[bio8-s2.6.0] A phospholipid is glycerol joined to two fatty acids and a phosphate-containing group in place of the third fatty acid.
[bio8-s2.6.1] The phosphate head is hydrophilic (attracted to water). The two fatty acid tails are hydrophobic (repelled by water).
[bio8-s2.6.2] In water, phospholipids arrange themselves in a double layer (bilayer), with the heads facing the watery fluid on each side and the tails pointing inwards, away from the water.
[bio8-s2.6.3] This bilayer is the basis of every cell membrane. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes and The Unit of Life — The Cell.
[bio8-s2.6.4] Diagram: a phospholipid drawn as a circle (head) with two wavy tails, labelled hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty acid tails; and a section of a bilayer.
[bio8-s2.7.b] Proteins are the third biomolecule group. Every protein is a chain built from the same 20 kinds of amino acid, joined by peptide bonds.
[bio8-s2.7.0] Proteins contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and sometimes sulfur, phosphorus or other elements. Sulfur is in some amino acids such as cysteine and methionine, phosphorus is in casein (the main protein of milk), and iron is in haemoglobin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio8-s2.7.1] Proteins are made of amino acids. There are 20 different amino acids in the proteins of living things.
[bio8-s2.7.2] Each amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to an amino group (–NH₂), an acid (carboxyl, –COOH) group, a hydrogen atom and a variable side group (R group). Only the R group differs between amino acids.
[bio8-s2.7.3] Humans cannot make some amino acids (essential amino acids), so these must be taken in from food.
[bio8-s2.7.4] Amino acids join by condensation between the –COOH group of one and the –NH₂ group of the next, releasing water. The bond formed is a peptide bond.
[bio8-s2.7.5] Two amino acids form a dipeptide; a short chain is a peptide; a long chain is a polypeptide. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a particular shape.
[bio8-s2.7.6] The sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide (its primary structure) is set by a gene. Because 20 amino acids can be arranged in any order and length, a vast number of different proteins is possible.
[bio8-s2.7.7] Diagram: the general structure of an amino acid, a central carbon with –NH₂, –COOH, –H and –R attached; and two amino acids joined by a peptide bond with H₂O released.
[bio8-s2.8.b] A polypeptide chain does not stay straight. It folds into a precise shape, and that shape is what lets a protein do its job. Damage the shape and the job is lost.
[bio8-s2.8.0] The chain coils into a helix or folds into a pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds (secondary structure). This folds further into a precise three-dimensional shape (tertiary structure), held by bonds between R groups, including sulfur bridges between cysteine units.
[bio8-s2.8.1] Some proteins consist of several polypeptides fitted together (quaternary structure). Haemoglobin has four polypeptide chains.
[bio8-s2.8.2] The shape determines the function, because other molecules must fit it. An enzyme’s active site or an antibody’s binding site fits only particular molecules.
[bio8-s2.8.3] Heat, extremes of pH and some chemicals break the bonds holding the shape, so the protein loses its shape and function. This is denaturation, usually permanent, e.g. egg white turns solid and white when cooked. There is more on this in Enzymes.
[bio8-s2.8.4] Fibrous proteins are long strands, insoluble and structural, such as keratin (hair, nails) and collagen (skin, tendons).
[bio8-s2.8.5] Globular proteins are folded into compact, roughly spherical shapes and are usually soluble, e.g. enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin and insulin.
[bio8-s2.9.b] Nucleic acids are the fourth biomolecule group. Like the other groups, they are built from repeating units, which are called nucleotides.
[bio8-s2.9.0] Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base, so nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and P. There is more on this in DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code.
[bio8-s2.10.b] Putting the four groups side by side makes the differences easy to compare: which elements each contains, what its units are, which bond joins them and an example.
[bio8-s2.10.0] Carbohydrate: C, H, O; monosaccharides; glycosidic bond; glucose, sucrose, starch.
[bio8-s2.10.1] Lipid: C, H, O (plus P in phospholipids); glycerol and fatty acids; ester bond; triglycerides, phospholipids.
[bio8-s2.10.2] Protein: C, H, O, N (sometimes S, P or other elements); amino acids; peptide bond; enzymes, keratin.
[bio8-s2.10.3] Nucleic acid: C, H, O, N, P; nucleotides; DNA, RNA.

CANDIDATES bio8-s3: Metabolic roles and nutritional sources
[bio8-s3.0.b] Carbohydrates do far more than supply energy. Glucose is fuel, a product of photosynthesis and a building block for storage molecules, and other carbohydrates give structure and carry sugar around organisms.
[bio8-s3.0.0] Respiratory and catabolic role (catabolic means breaking molecules down): glucose is the main respiratory substrate, the fuel respiration uses. It is broken down to release energy, which is trapped as ATP, the cell's energy carrier. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio8-s3.0.1] The brain relies mainly on glucose for its energy, so a steady supply of glucose in the blood matters.
[bio8-s3.0.2] Photosynthetic role: glucose is the product of photosynthesis, the anabolic process (anabolic means building molecules up) in which plants use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio8-s3.0.3] Almost all the carbohydrate in food chains therefore comes from photosynthesis, because plants are the producers that make it first and every consumer depends on them.
[bio8-s3.0.4] Anabolic and storage role: excess glucose is built up into storage polysaccharides (long chains of sugar units), which are starch in plants and glycogen in the liver and muscles of animals.
[bio8-s3.0.5] These stores are broken down again (catabolism) when glucose is needed, so the organism can release it back as fuel.
[bio8-s3.0.6] Structural role: cellulose forms plant cell walls, and chitin forms fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons (the hard outer body casing). The pentose (five-carbon) sugars ribose and deoxyribose are part of RNA and DNA nucleotides.
[bio8-s3.0.7] Transport role: plants move sugar from the leaves to other parts as sucrose, carried in the phloem (the tissue that transports food in plants).
[bio8-s3.1.b] Lipids (fats, oils and related molecules) are used for energy storage, but their uses go much further. They insulate, protect, build membranes, waterproof surfaces and act as regulators.
[bio8-s3.1.0] Respiratory and catabolic role: fats are respired to release energy, especially when glucose and glycogen run low.
[bio8-s3.1.1] A gram of fat releases about twice as much energy as a gram of carbohydrate or protein, so lipid is a compact long-term energy store.
[bio8-s3.1.2] Storage (anabolic): excess food is built into triglycerides (the main type of fat molecule) and stored as fat in adipose tissue, under the skin and around organs in animals.
[bio8-s3.1.3] Plants store oils in seeds, such as sunflower seeds, and in fruits, such as olives.
[bio8-s3.1.4] Insulation: fat under the skin reduces heat loss, as in the thick blubber of whales and seals. The myelin sheath around nerve fibres is rich in lipid and insulates them.
[bio8-s3.1.5] Protection: fat around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against physical damage.
[bio8-s3.1.6] Structural role: phospholipids form the bilayer (double layer) of all cell membranes, including the membranes inside chloroplasts and mitochondria.
[bio8-s3.1.7] Photosynthetic role: the light-absorbing pigments of photosynthesis are held in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast, which are built of phospholipids. Plants also convert some of the glucose made in photosynthesis into oils.
[bio8-s3.2.b] Two further roles of lipids come from their water-repelling nature and from their chemistry as signalling molecules.
[bio8-s3.2.0] Waterproofing: waxes, which are lipids, form the waxy cuticle on leaves, reducing water loss. They also waterproof feathers and fur.
[bio8-s3.2.1] Regulators: steroid hormones such as testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone are lipids, which means not all hormones are proteins.
[bio8-s3.2.2] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fat. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio8-s3.3.b] Proteins have many different jobs in the body, from speeding up reactions to defence, movement and transport. Each role below has a named protein you can give as an example.
[bio8-s3.3.0] Enzymes: almost all enzymes are proteins. They are biological catalysts that speed up the reactions of metabolism (all the chemical reactions in a cell) without being used up, for example amylase digests starch to maltose.
[bio8-s3.3.1] Hormones: some hormones are proteins, for example insulin, made by the pancreas, which lowers blood glucose. Other hormones are lipids.
[bio8-s3.3.2] Immunity: antibodies are proteins made by lymphocytes (B cells, a type of white blood cell). Each binds to a specific antigen (a marker molecule) on a pathogen (a disease-causing organism), marking it for destruction.
[bio8-s3.3.3] Transport of substances in the body: haemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen.
[bio8-s3.3.4] Movement: actin and myosin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they shorten to make the muscle contract.
[bio8-s3.3.5] Energy: protein is respired only when carbohydrate and fat are in short supply.
[bio8-s3.3.6] Excess amino acids (the units proteins are made of) cannot be stored. The liver removes their amino group (deamination), forming urea, which is excreted.
[bio8-s3.3.7] Growth and repair: proteins are the main raw material for new cells and tissues, so children, pregnant women and people recovering from injury need more.
[bio8-s3.4.b] Many proteins sit in the cell membrane (the phospholipid bilayer around every cell) and decide what crosses it and how cells respond to signals. The specification names four kinds.
[bio8-s3.4.0] Membrane proteins are embedded in the phospholipid bilayer. They control what enters and leaves cells and how cells communicate.
[bio8-s3.4.1] Channel proteins form water-filled pores through the membrane that let specific ions or molecules pass by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), for example the sodium and potassium channels of neurons, which allow nerve impulses.
[bio8-s3.4.2] Transporters (carrier proteins) bind a specific molecule and change shape to move it across the membrane.
[bio8-s3.4.3] Some transporters use energy from ATP to move substances against a concentration gradient (active transport), for example the sodium-potassium pump. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio8-s3.4.4] Receptors are proteins on the cell surface, or inside the cell, with a shape that fits one signalling molecule such as a hormone or neurotransmitter. When the signal binds, the cell responds, for example insulin receptors on liver and muscle cells.
[bio8-s3.4.5] This is why a hormone affects only its target cells: only cells with the matching receptor can respond.
[bio8-s3.4.6] Structural proteins give strength and support. Keratin forms hair, nails and the outer skin, and collagen forms skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone. Proteins also form the cytoskeleton, the internal scaffold of cells.
[bio8-s3.4.7] Diagram: a section of cell membrane (phospholipid bilayer) showing a channel protein, a carrier protein, and a receptor protein with its signalling molecule. Label each.
[bio8-s3.5.b] Hormones link the metabolic roles of proteins and lipids, because they are made from both. They let one part of the body control the activity of another.
[bio8-s3.5.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood to target cells, where it regulates their activity. Hormones regulate growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
[bio8-s3.5.1] Hormones are biomolecules from two groups: proteins, such as insulin, and steroid lipids, such as testosterone and oestrogen. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio8-s3.5.2] Hormones work only on cells with the matching receptor proteins, so only target cells respond.
[bio8-s3.5.3] Plants also make chemical regulators, called plant growth regulators, for example auxin.
[bio8-s3.6.b] Knowing the roles of each biomolecule is only half the story. You also need to name the foods that supply each one.
[bio8-s3.6.0] Carbohydrate sources, sugars: fruit, honey, jam, sweets and fizzy drinks, and lactose (milk sugar) in milk.
[bio8-s3.6.1] Carbohydrate sources, starch: bread, pasta, rice, potatoes and breakfast cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.2] Carbohydrate sources, fibre (cellulose): vegetables, fruit, wholegrain cereals and brown bread, and beans.
[bio8-s3.6.3] Lipid sources, fats (mostly saturated): butter, cream, cheese, lard and fatty meat.
[bio8-s3.6.4] Lipid sources, oils (mostly unsaturated): olive and sunflower oils, nuts and seeds, avocados, and oily fish such as salmon and mackerel.
[bio8-s3.6.5] Protein sources, animal: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Protein sources, plant: beans, peas, lentils, nuts, soya and tofu, and cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.6] Animal proteins contain all the essential amino acids (those the body cannot make). Many single plant foods lack one or more, so vegetarian and vegan diets combine a range of plant proteins, for example beans with cereal.
[bio8-s3.7.b] What we eat affects both our health and the environment, so food sources link back to the roles of each biomolecule.
[bio8-s3.7.0] Diets high in saturated fat are linked to raised blood cholesterol and heart disease.
[bio8-s3.7.1] Diets high in free sugars are linked to tooth decay, obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[bio8-s3.7.2] Producing plant protein generally needs less land and water, and causes lower greenhouse gas emissions, than producing meat. There is more on balanced diets in The Digestive System.

CANDIDATES bio9-s1: ATP
[bio9-s1.0.b] A transfer molecule carries energy, or electrons and hydrogen, from the reaction that releases them to the reaction that uses them.
[bio9-s1.0.0] A transfer molecule is not used up: after handing on its load it returns to be recharged, like a rechargeable battery, so a cell needs only a small amount of each one, recycled continuously.
[bio9-s1.0.1] ATP is the main energy-transfer molecule in all living cells, from bacteria to humans, which is why it is called the energy currency of the cell: it is the common 'money' every reaction accepts.
[bio9-s1.0.2] Metabolism is all the chemical reactions in a cell. These reactions fall into two types, catabolic and anabolic.
[bio9-s1.0.3] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, which breaks down food such as glucose.
[bio9-s1.0.4] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. protein synthesis and photosynthesis.
[bio9-s1.0.5] ATP links the two: catabolic reactions make ATP, and anabolic reactions and other cell work use it, so energy released in one place can be spent in another. There is more on this in Biomolecules and metabolism.
[bio9-s1.1.b] ATP is a small molecule with three parts, and its name tells you what they are. Knowing the parts matters because the exam asks you to name them and draw them.
[bio9-s1.1.0] ATP stands for adenosine triphosphate. It is made of three parts: the nitrogenous base adenine, the five-carbon sugar ribose, and a chain of three phosphate groups attached to the ribose.
[bio9-s1.1.1] Adenine joined to ribose is called adenosine; 'tri' means three phosphates. So adenosine triphosphate is adenosine with three phosphates attached.
[bio9-s1.1.2] ATP is a modified nucleotide, built to the same plan of base, sugar and phosphate as an RNA nucleotide (a building block of RNA). There is more on this in Nucleotides, DNA and RNA.
[bio9-s1.1.3] ADP (adenosine diphosphate) has only two phosphate groups, one fewer than ATP.
[bio9-s1.1.4] Pi (inorganic phosphate) is a single free phosphate group, not attached to any larger molecule.
[bio9-s1.1.5] Diagram: ATP drawn as a box labelled adenine, joined to a pentagon labelled ribose, joined to a chain of three circles labelled phosphate, with the bond to the third (end) phosphate marked as the one broken to release energy; a second sketch showing ADP + Pi.
[bio9-s1.2.b] A cell gets energy from ATP by removing its end phosphate. The reaction is simple, but its features explain why ATP is such a useful energy source.
[bio9-s1.2.0] Energy is released when the end phosphate is removed: ATP + H₂O → ADP + Pi + energy. So the products of ATP breakdown are ADP, Pi and energy.
[bio9-s1.2.1] The reaction uses water to split the molecule, so it is a hydrolysis reaction. It is catalysed (sped up) by an enzyme called ATPase.
[bio9-s1.2.2] The bond to the end phosphate is often called a high-energy bond: it is easily broken, and the reaction releases about 30 kJ per mole of ATP (30.5 kJ/mol) under standard conditions.
[bio9-s1.2.3] The energy is released in one quick step, in a small packet that suits a single reaction and is available right where the cell needs it.
[bio9-s1.2.4] Often the released phosphate is attached to another molecule, such as a carrier protein or a reactant (a molecule taking part in a reaction). This makes it more reactive or changes its shape so that it can do work.
[bio9-s1.3.b] ATP is not used up for good. Once its end phosphate has been removed, the cell puts a phosphate back on to ADP, which needs an input of energy.
[bio9-s1.3.0] ATP is remade by adding a phosphate group back on to ADP: ADP + Pi + energy → ATP + H₂O.
[bio9-s1.3.1] Adding phosphate is called phosphorylation. It is catalysed by enzymes, mainly ATP synthase, the enzyme that makes most of the cell's ATP.
[bio9-s1.3.2] In all living cells, the energy to make ATP comes from respiration, the breakdown of food such as glucose. Some ATP is made in the cytoplasm and most in the mitochondria. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio9-s1.3.3] In plants and algae, ATP is also made in the light-dependent stage of photosynthesis in the chloroplasts, using light energy. This ATP is used inside the chloroplast to make glucose.
[bio9-s1.4.b] Breaking down ATP and remaking it are two halves of one loop. Because the loop runs all the time, a cell can keep spending energy without running out of ATP.
[bio9-s1.4.0] ATP is continuously broken down to ADP + Pi to release energy for cell work, and ADP + Pi are continuously rebuilt into ATP using energy from respiration (or light). This loop is the ATP/ADP cycle.
[bio9-s1.4.1] Cells hold only a small store of ATP at any time. Each molecule is recycled many times, so ATP must be made constantly while a cell is alive.
[bio9-s1.4.2] When respiration stops, for example after a long time without oxygen or in the presence of a poison that blocks respiration, ATP runs out within minutes and cell processes fail.
[bio9-s1.4.3] Diagram: the ATP/ADP cycle as a circle, with ATP at the top and ADP + Pi at the bottom; one arrow down labelled energy released for cell work: muscle contraction, active transport, synthesis; one arrow up labelled energy in from respiration (and light in photosynthesis).
[bio9-s1.5.b] Almost every energy-demanding job in a cell runs on ATP. The main uses are movement, moving substances across membranes and building molecules.
[bio9-s1.5.0] Muscle contraction: ATP provides the energy for protein filaments in muscle fibres to slide past each other, shortening the muscle. Very active muscle cells therefore contain many mitochondria, to make plenty of ATP.
[bio9-s1.5.1] Active transport: carrier proteins in the cell membrane use energy from ATP to pump substances against their concentration gradient (from low to high concentration), e.g. root hair cells taking in mineral ions from dilute soil water.
[bio9-s1.5.2] The sodium–potassium pump in nerve cells uses ATP to restore the ion balance after an impulse. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio9-s1.5.3] Synthesis (anabolic reactions): ATP is needed to join amino acids into proteins at ribosomes, to make DNA when it is copied before cell division, and to make glycogen and starch from glucose.
[bio9-s1.5.4] The light-independent stage of photosynthesis uses ATP to build glucose from carbon dioxide.
[bio9-s1.5.5] Other uses: cell division (moving chromosomes), movement of cilia and flagella (hair-like and whip-like projections), and the light produced by fireflies (bioluminescence), where the enzyme luciferase uses ATP.
[bio9-s1.6.b] Glucose holds a lot of energy, yet cells do not run reactions straight from it. They first use its energy to make ATP, and this is the better arrangement for several reasons.
[bio9-s1.6.0] Breaking down one glucose molecule releases far more energy than any single reaction needs, and it does so over many steps.
[bio9-s1.6.1] ATP releases a small, manageable amount, matched to one reaction, in a single step, so less energy is wasted as heat.
[bio9-s1.6.2] ATP is available instantly where it is needed and is easily recycled. One type of molecule can supply energy to every kind of reaction in the cell.
[bio9-s1.6.3] ATP is a short-term energy carrier, not a store. Long-term energy is stored in starch (plants), glycogen (animals and fungi) and fats and oils, which respiration uses to make ATP when needed.
[bio9-s1.7.b] Fireflies make light using ATP, and this has been put to practical use. Because the light needs ATP, it can reveal whether living material is present.
[bio9-s1.7.0] Because every living cell contains ATP, hygiene tests in food factories and hospitals swab a surface and add luciferase, the firefly enzyme that gives off light when ATP is present.
[bio9-s1.7.1] Light given off shows that ATP is present, and therefore that living or recently living material, such as bacteria or food residue, is on the surface.
