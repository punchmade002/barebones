CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio23-s4: The menstrual cycle
[bio23-s4.0.b] Once a month, the female reproductive system gets ready for a possible pregnancy and, if none begins, starts over. This repeating pattern is the menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s4.0.0] The menstrual cycle is the series of changes in the ovary (where eggs are stored) and the uterus (the womb) that happens about once a month in a woman from puberty to menopause, except during pregnancy.
[bio23-s4.0.1] Its purpose is to release an egg and to prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) to receive an embryo, so a fertilised egg has somewhere to implant and grow.
[bio23-s4.0.2] The cycle lasts about 28 days on average, but a cycle of about 21 to 35 days is normal, and its length varies between women and from month to month.
[bio23-s4.0.3] Day 1 is the first day of bleeding, so every day number in the cycle is counted from the start of a period.
[bio23-s4.0.4] Two sets of changes run side by side: the ovarian cycle, in which a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac holding an egg) develops, releases its egg and becomes the corpus luteum, and the uterine cycle, in which the endometrium is shed, rebuilt and thickened.
[bio23-s4.0.5] Hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood) coordinate the two sets of changes, which is why the ovary and uterus stay in step with each other.
[bio23-s4.0.6] The first period (menarche) occurs during puberty, and the cycles end at the menopause. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.1.b] The cycle starts with the period, the phase where the lining built up in the last cycle is lost. Counting from day 1, it is the first of the four phases.
[bio23-s4.1.0] Menstruation lasts about days 1–5 of a typical 28-day cycle, although the typical days given for each phase are only approximate.
[bio23-s4.1.1] If no egg was fertilised in the previous cycle, the thickened outer layer of the endometrium is no longer needed, so it breaks down and is shed through the cervix (the neck of the uterus) and vagina.
[bio23-s4.1.2] The shed lining leaves with a small amount of blood. This is a period, and the shedding lasts about 3–7 days.
[bio23-s4.2.b] After the old lining is lost, the body prepares a new egg and rebuilds the lining. This phase is named after the follicles in the ovary that grow during it.
[bio23-s4.2.0] The follicular phase runs from day 1 to about day 13, so it overlaps with menstruation.
[bio23-s4.2.1] In the ovary, several follicles start to develop, and usually one grows into a large, mature, fluid-filled follicle containing the egg, called a Graafian follicle.
[bio23-s4.2.2] In the uterus, after menstruation ends, the endometrium is repaired and grows thicker from about day 6, rebuilding the lining that was lost.
[bio23-s4.2.3] Near the end of this phase the cervical mucus (the fluid made at the cervix) becomes thin, clear and slippery, which lets sperm through.
[bio23-s4.3.b] Ovulation is the high point of the ovarian cycle: the mature egg leaves the ovary.
[bio23-s4.3.0] Ovulation happens at about day 14: the Graafian follicle bursts at the surface of the ovary and releases its egg, which is swept into the fallopian tube (the tube that carries the egg towards the uterus).
[bio23-s4.3.1] This is the most fertile time, because the egg survives only about 12–24 hours, so sperm must already be present or arrive within that short window. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s4.4.b] After the egg is released, the ovary and the uterus prepare for a possible embryo. What happens next depends on whether an embryo implants.
[bio23-s4.4.0] The luteal phase lasts about days 15–28. The empty follicle turns into a yellow mass of cells, the corpus luteum (Latin for “yellow body”).
[bio23-s4.4.1] The endometrium thickens further, becoming spongy, with more blood vessels and glands, so that it is ready for an embryo to implant (embed in the lining).
[bio23-s4.4.2] If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down after about 10–14 days and the endometrium is shed, so the next menstruation begins on day 1 of a new cycle.
[bio23-s4.4.3] If an embryo implants, the corpus luteum stays active, so the lining is kept and there is no period. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.5.b] Not every woman ovulates on day 14. The timing depends on a fixed gap before the next period, not a fixed day number.
[bio23-s4.5.0] Ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period starts, because the luteal phase is fairly constant at about 14 days.
[bio23-s4.5.1] Differences in cycle length come mostly from the follicular phase, since that is the phase that varies in length.
[bio23-s4.5.2] So in a 35-day cycle ovulation is about day 21, not day 14, because 35 minus 14 gives day 21.
[bio23-s4.6.b] Here is each phase with its days, what the ovary is doing and what the endometrium is doing, for a typical 28-day cycle.
[bio23-s4.6.0] Menstruation: days 1–5; ovary: the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down; endometrium: shed.
[bio23-s4.6.1] Follicular phase: days 1–13; ovary: a follicle develops into the Graafian follicle; endometrium: repaired and growing thicker from about day 6.
[bio23-s4.6.2] Ovulation: about day 14; ovary: the Graafian follicle bursts and releases the egg; endometrium: at the thickness built up in the follicular phase.
[bio23-s4.6.3] Luteal phase: days 15–28; ovary: the corpus luteum forms and then breaks down if there is no pregnancy; endometrium: thickens further, becoming spongy, and is shed if no embryo implants.
[bio23-s4.7.b] A timeline puts the ovary and the uterus side by side, so you can see how the two cycles line up day by day.
[bio23-s4.7.0] Diagram: a timeline, day 0 to day 28. Above: the ovary (follicle growing, Graafian follicle, ovulation at day 14, corpus luteum forming then breaking down). Below: endometrium thickness (shed days 1–5, thickening to day 28). Label the four phases along the timeline.
[bio23-s4.8.b] Four hormones coordinate the cycle. Knowing which organ makes each one is the key to explaining the rest of this topic.
[bio23-s4.8.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone) are made by the pituitary gland (a small gland at the base of the brain). Oestrogen is made mainly by the developing follicle, and in smaller amounts by the corpus luteum. Progesterone is made by the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s4.9.b] The cycle restarts when progesterone falls, and the follicular phase is then driven by FSH and oestrogen. Each hormone's level explains what happens in the ovary and uterus.
[bio23-s4.9.0] Menstruation: oestrogen and progesterone are both low, because the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down.
[bio23-s4.9.1] The fall in progesterone is what causes the endometrium to break down. Low oestrogen alone is not the cause.
[bio23-s4.9.2] Low progesterone no longer holds back the pituitary, so FSH begins to rise.
[bio23-s4.9.3] Follicular phase: FSH stimulates follicles to develop, and one becomes the Graafian follicle. The growing follicle secretes oestrogen, so oestrogen rises steadily.
[bio23-s4.9.4] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium and makes the cervical mucus thin.
[bio23-s4.9.5] Rising oestrogen at first inhibits (holds back) FSH, so FSH falls back and usually only one follicle completes development.
[bio23-s4.10.b] Ovulation is triggered by a sudden surge of LH, which is itself set off by high oestrogen.
[bio23-s4.10.0] When oestrogen reaches a high peak (around day 12–13), it stimulates the pituitary to release a large surge of LH, with a smaller rise in FSH.
[bio23-s4.10.1] The LH surge causes the Graafian follicle to burst and release its egg about a day later (ovulation, about day 14).
[bio23-s4.10.2] Oestrogen falls just after ovulation.
[bio23-s4.11.b] After ovulation the corpus luteum takes over, and progesterone rises. Whether the cycle ends or a pregnancy continues depends on whether the corpus luteum survives.
[bio23-s4.11.0] Luteal phase: LH causes the empty Graafian follicle to develop into the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s4.11.1] The corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and some oestrogen), so progesterone rises to a peak around day 21.
[bio23-s4.11.2] Progesterone maintains and further thickens the endometrium, making it ready for implantation, and makes the cervical mucus thick again.
[bio23-s4.11.3] High progesterone, with oestrogen, inhibits FSH and LH, so no new follicle develops and no further egg is released while the corpus luteum is active. FSH and LH stay low.
[bio23-s4.11.4] End of the cycle: without pregnancy, low LH means the corpus luteum is no longer maintained. It breaks down after about 10–14 days, so progesterone and oestrogen fall.
[bio23-s4.11.5] The endometrium can no longer be maintained and is shed (menstruation). The fall removes the inhibition of FSH, which rises and starts a new cycle.
[bio23-s4.11.6] If an embryo implants, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so progesterone stays high and menstruation does not happen. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.12.b] This summary gives the level of each hormone in each phase, with the state of the ovary.
[bio23-s4.12.0] Menstruation: FSH rising, LH low, oestrogen low, progesterone low; a new follicle begins to grow.
[bio23-s4.12.1] Follicular phase: FSH high then falling, LH low, oestrogen rising, progesterone low; a follicle grows to the Graafian follicle.
[bio23-s4.12.2] Ovulation: LH surge, a small FSH peak, oestrogen at its peak then falling, progesterone low; the Graafian follicle bursts.
[bio23-s4.12.3] Luteal phase: FSH and LH low, progesterone high with a peak about day 21, oestrogen at a moderate second, smaller peak, both falling at the end; the corpus luteum active, then breaking down.
[bio23-s4.13.b] The ovary and the pituitary signal each other in a loop, and that feedback is what keeps the cycle going from one month to the next.
[bio23-s4.13.0] The cycle is controlled by feedback, meaning each hormone's level changes the release of the next.
[bio23-s4.13.1] Negative feedback: progesterone, and moderate oestrogen, inhibits the pituitary's release of FSH and LH. This keeps FSH and LH low in the luteal phase, and their fall lets the corpus luteum break down. There is more on negative feedback in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio23-s4.13.2] Positive feedback: high oestrogen just before ovulation causes more LH to be released, giving the LH surge.
[bio23-s4.13.3] Each hormone controls the next: FSH → follicle grows → oestrogen → LH surge → ovulation and corpus luteum → progesterone → FSH and LH inhibited → corpus luteum breaks down → progesterone falls → menstruation and FSH rises.
[bio23-s4.13.4] Technology link: home ovulation test kits detect the LH surge in urine, showing that ovulation is likely within about a day or two.
[bio23-s4.13.5] Technology link: the combined contraceptive pill gives steady oestrogen and progesterone, which by negative feedback keep FSH and LH low, so no follicle matures and no LH surge occurs.
[bio23-s4.13.6] Diagram: this sequence as a loop (a flow chart) linking the pituitary, the ovary and the endometrium, with “+” on the oestrogen-to-LH arrow and “−” on the progesterone-to-FSH/LH arrow.
[bio23-s4.14.b] Exams show graphs of the four hormones across a 28-day cycle. You need to recognise each curve and explain every change by its cause.
[bio23-s4.14.0] How to read the standard graphs of a 28-day cycle: FSH has a rise early in the cycle and a small peak at day 14.
[bio23-s4.14.1] LH is low except for a sharp, tall peak just before day 14.
[bio23-s4.14.2] Oestrogen rises through the follicular phase to a peak just before the LH surge, drops after ovulation, then has a lower second rise in the luteal phase.
[bio23-s4.14.3] Progesterone is low until ovulation, rises to a peak around day 21, then falls to day 28.
[bio23-s4.14.4] Exam answers explain each change by its cause, for example: progesterone rises after day 14 because the corpus luteum secretes it.
[bio23-s4.14.5] Diagram: two graphs on one 28-day time axis, one for FSH and LH (pituitary), one for oestrogen and progesterone (ovarian), lined up with the ovary events and endometrium thickness from the timeline. Label the LH surge, oestrogen peak, progesterone peak and ovulation.

CANDIDATES bio23-s6: Hormones in reproduction
[bio23-s6.0.b] Reproduction is run by chemical messengers (hormones) carried in the blood. Three places make nearly all of them, and in pregnancy a fourth organ joins in.
[bio23-s6.0.0] Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain, the testes (the male gonads, which make sperm) and the ovaries (the female gonads, which make eggs).
[bio23-s6.0.1] The pituitary gland makes oxytocin and prolactin, and also the hormones that stimulate the gonads, which is how the brain controls the testes and ovaries.
[bio23-s6.0.2] The testes make testosterone, and the ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone. In pregnancy the placenta (the organ that links mother and foetus) also makes oestrogen and progesterone.
[bio23-s6.0.3] Prolactin is made by the pituitary gland, and its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts. There is more on where each gland sits in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.b] Puberty is the changeover from child to adult body. A hormone signal from the brain switches the reproductive system on.
[bio23-s6.1.0] Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes (sperm or eggs), and the secondary sexual characteristics develop. These are the body changes, apart from the reproductive organs themselves, that mark a person as male or female.
[bio23-s6.1.1] Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls, and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
[bio23-s6.1.2] It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads (the testes and ovaries). There is more on these pituitary hormones in the Higher Level block on the gonad-stimulating hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.3] As a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone, and the ovaries much more oestrogen. It is these sex hormones that cause the changes of puberty.
[bio23-s6.1.4] Testosterone at puberty starts sperm production, causes growth of the penis and testes, and causes the male secondary sexual characteristics. It also increases sex drive.
[bio23-s6.1.5] Oestrogen at puberty causes growth of the uterus (womb) and vagina, preparing the body for pregnancy, and the female secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.1.6] Oestrogen, working with progesterone, starts the menstrual cycles, so a girl becomes able to ovulate and conceive; the first period is called menarche.
[bio23-s6.2.b] Once puberty has started the gametes, hormones keep them coming. The pattern differs between the sexes: continuous in men, monthly in women.
[bio23-s6.2.0] Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes where sperm are made), and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.2.1] Testosterone is made by the interstitial cells between the tubules, not by the tubules themselves, which make sperm.
[bio23-s6.2.2] Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously rather than in a cycle, which is why men stay fertile from day to day.
[bio23-s6.2.3] Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle (a fluid-filled sac holding a developing egg) to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus). There is more on the hormone levels and feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.3.b] After fertilisation the embryo has to bury itself in the uterus lining (implantation) and the lining must then be kept, not shed. Two hormones make this possible.
[bio23-s6.3.0] Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms.
[bio23-s6.3.1] Without high progesterone, implantation cannot succeed, because the embryo would have no suitable lining to attach to or feed from.
[bio23-s6.3.2] hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation.
[bio23-s6.3.3] hCG keeps the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after ovulation) alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine, using a monoclonal-antibody test.
[bio23-s6.4.b] The corpus luteum cannot support a whole pregnancy, so the placenta takes over hormone production. Progesterone from it then keeps the pregnancy going in several ways.
[bio23-s6.4.0] From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
[bio23-s6.4.1] Progesterone during pregnancy maintains the endometrium and so the placenta, which is attached to it.
[bio23-s6.4.2] Progesterone keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it does not contract and expel the foetus early.
[bio23-s6.4.3] Progesterone keeps the cervical mucus plug (the mucus that seals the cervix, the neck of the uterus) thick, and prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts.
[bio23-s6.4.4] Progesterone, with oestrogen, prevents further ovulation and menstruation during pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.5.b] Oestrogen supports the growth of the foetus by building up the mother's tissues, and late in pregnancy it prepares her body for birth.
[bio23-s6.5.0] Oestrogen during pregnancy drives the growth of the foetus: it stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, and increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta, which supplies the foetus.
[bio23-s6.5.1] Oestrogen also develops the milk ducts of the breasts, so they are ready to carry milk after birth.
[bio23-s6.5.2] Late in pregnancy oestrogen makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
[bio23-s6.5.3] Level summary: progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.
[bio23-s6.6.b] Birth needs the powerful uterus muscle to contract strongly. A shift in hormone balance and the hormone oxytocin make this happen.
[bio23-s6.6.0] Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
[bio23-s6.6.1] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus (a part of the brain just above the pituitary) and released by the pituitary into the blood.
[bio23-s6.6.2] Oxytocin causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour, the process of giving birth.
[bio23-s6.7.b] Labour speeds itself up. Each contraction causes more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, a pattern called positive feedback, where a change leads to more of the same change.
[bio23-s6.7.0] 1. The baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix.
[bio23-s6.7.1] 2. Stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain.
[bio23-s6.7.2] 3. The pituitary releases more oxytocin.
[bio23-s6.7.3] 4. Stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more.
[bio23-s6.7.4] 5. The cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus (the stretching), so the loop stops.
[bio23-s6.7.5] A nervous pathway (the impulses) and a hormonal pathway (oxytocin in the blood) work together here. There is more on this in Nervous vs hormonal coordination.
[bio23-s6.7.6] Diagram: the positive feedback loop as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → back to the start; birth ends the loop.
[bio23-s6.8.b] Oxytocin's work does not stop when the baby arrives. It also helps the uterus recover, and doctors use a man-made version of it.
[bio23-s6.8.0] After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding.
[bio23-s6.8.1] Oxytocin also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size, because its contractions squeeze the muscle smaller.
[bio23-s6.8.2] Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour, and to reduce bleeding after birth.
[bio23-s6.9.b] The breasts are prepared during pregnancy, but milk only starts once the placenta has gone. The hormone that actually makes milk is prolactin.
[bio23-s6.9.0] During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises.
[bio23-s6.9.1] The high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced, even though prolactin is already rising.
[bio23-s6.9.2] When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
[bio23-s6.9.3] Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk, which is why it is called the milk-making hormone.
[bio23-s6.9.4] Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made: supply matches demand.
[bio23-s6.9.5] While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often do not return for some months; this is not reliable contraception. There is more on this in Fertility control and infertility.
[bio23-s6.10.b] Making milk is not the same as delivering it. Oxytocin does the second job, so a feed needs both hormones.
[bio23-s6.10.0] Oxytocin causes the milk to be released (called let-down, or milk ejection).
[bio23-s6.10.1] Suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple.
[bio23-s6.10.2] Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
[bio23-s6.11.b] The first milk a baby gets is different from the milk that follows. It is tuned to protect a newborn rather than to fatten it.
[bio23-s6.11.0] Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth.
[bio23-s6.11.1] Colostrum is a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar.
[bio23-s6.11.2] Its antibodies give the newborn passive immunity, which means protection made by the mother rather than by the baby's own immune system. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s6.11.3] Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose (milk sugar) for energy and growth.
[bio23-s6.12.b] A woman's fertility does not last for life. Menopause is the point where the ovaries stop responding, and the fall in hormones has effects across the body.
[bio23-s6.12.0] Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months.
[bio23-s6.12.1] It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
[bio23-s6.12.2] Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone. It is not simply that the eggs are used up by ovulation, because most follicles degenerate without ever ovulating.
[bio23-s6.12.3] Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, so there is nothing to shed and periods stop.
[bio23-s6.12.4] Effects of falling oestrogen in the short term: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, and vaginal dryness.
[bio23-s6.12.5] Effects of falling oestrogen in the longer term: loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease.
[bio23-s6.12.6] Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.
[bio23-s6.12.7] There is more on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.13.b] One bullet per hormone: its gland, its roles and how its level changes through life.
[bio23-s6.13.0] Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth, puberty, sperm production and sex drive. There is more on development before birth in Sex determination.
[bio23-s6.13.1] Testosterone level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, and declines slowly in later life.
[bio23-s6.13.2] Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, and keeping bones strong.
[bio23-s6.13.3] Oestrogen level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, and falls at menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.4] Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares the milk glands and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.13.5] Progesterone level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.6] Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth, and milk let-down. Its level rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
[bio23-s6.13.7] Prolactin (pituitary): milk production. Its level rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed, and falls when breastfeeding stops.
[bio23-s6.14.b] FSH and LH are the two pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads. In males they hold testosterone steady through negative feedback, and in females they drive the monthly cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.0] At puberty the hypothalamus, the part of the brain above the pituitary, signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.14.1] In males these hormones stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
[bio23-s6.14.2] In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, working with testosterone, so without FSH sperm output falls.
[bio23-s6.14.3] In males, LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
[bio23-s6.14.4] Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH and FSH from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again.
[bio23-s6.14.5] This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.6] Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone) have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.14.7] In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s6.15.b] FSH and LH levels follow the state of the ovaries and the other hormones. When oestrogen and progesterone are high they stay low, and when these hormones are lost they rise.
[bio23-s6.15.0] In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens.
[bio23-s6.15.1] While breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.15.2] After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
[bio23-s6.15.3] Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood and rise at puberty.
[bio23-s6.15.4] In men FSH and LH are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle, with an LH surge at ovulation.
[bio23-s6.15.5] In women FSH and LH are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and high after the menopause. The levels and feedback through the month are in The menstrual cycle.

[bio23-s6.16.0] A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback, with − for inhibition and + for stimulation.
[bio23-s6.16.1] Use the model to predict effects: blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone, as in the pill, keeps FSH and LH low.

[bio23-s6.16.3] Female flow chart: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with “−” arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a “+” arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge.

CANDIDATES bio24-s1: Flower structure and pollination
[bio24-s1.0.b] Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers, which produce the gametes (sex cells) and bring about pollination (the transfer of pollen) and fertilisation (the joining of male and female gametes). Three structures carry the process from gamete to new plant: the flower, the seed and the fruit.
[bio24-s1.0.0] The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle, the tip of the flower stalk. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
[bio24-s1.0.1] The flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination. It is the only one of the three structures that exists before fertilisation, because the seed and fruit develop from flower parts afterwards.
[bio24-s1.0.2] The seed, a fertilised and developed ovule, contains the embryo (a new plant) with a food store and a protective coat. The fruit, a developed ovary, protects the seeds and helps to disperse them, that is, spread them away from the parent plant.
[bio24-s1.0.3] Seed detail is in Seeds; dispersal is in Fruit and seed dispersal.
[bio24-s1.0.4] Many flowers are bisexual (hermaphrodite), meaning they have both male and female parts in the same flower, e.g. buttercup.
[bio24-s1.0.5] Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel, whose male flowers hang in catkins and whose small female flowers have red stigmas. In some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly.
[bio24-s1.1.b] A flower is built in rings, listed here from the outside inwards. The outer parts hold the flower together, protect it and, in many species, attract the animals that carry its pollen.
[bio24-s1.1.0] Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached, so it holds the flower together. For example, in strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
[bio24-s1.1.1] Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud, and some photosynthesise (make food using light).
[bio24-s1.1.2] Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, which attracts insects.
[bio24-s1.1.3] Many insect-pollinated petals have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent, because there is no need to attract animals.
[bio24-s1.1.4] Nectary: a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects so that they keep coming back. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
[bio24-s1.2.b] The male part of the flower is the stamen. Its job is to make pollen, the package that carries the male gametes, and to hold it where a pollinator can pick it up.
[bio24-s1.2.0] Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament, so that the pollen is held up where it can be picked up. The stamens together form the androecium.
[bio24-s1.2.1] Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis (a cell division that halves the chromosome number), so the pollen grains are haploid, with one set of chromosomes. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
[bio24-s1.2.2] Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position, where insects brush against it or out in the wind. It also carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand (a thin tube of transport tissue).
[bio24-s1.2.3] Diagram: a stamen drawn on its own. Labels: anther, filament.
[bio24-s1.3.b] The female part of the flower is the carpel. It catches pollen, provides a route for the male gametes to reach the egg cell, and protects the ovules that become seeds.
[bio24-s1.3.0] Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
[bio24-s1.3.1] Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers, so pollen carried on an insect adheres, and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses, so it can catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s1.3.2] Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube (the thin tube a pollen grain grows) grows down through it to the ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.3] Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them, so the developing seeds are not exposed. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
[bio24-s1.3.4] Ovule: a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis, so it is haploid. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
[bio24-s1.3.5] Diagram: a carpel drawn on its own. Labels: stigma, style, ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.6] Diagram: a half-flower (vertical section) of an insect-pollinated flower such as buttercup or wallflower. Labels: receptacle, sepal, petal, nectary, stamen (anther, filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), ovule.
[bio24-s1.4.b] Pollination must happen before fertilisation can take place. Flowers can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated, and several features favour cross-pollination.
[bio24-s1.4.0] Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. It is only the arrival of pollen: fertilisation, the fusion of gamete nuclei in the ovule, comes later.
[bio24-s1.4.1] Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
[bio24-s1.4.2] Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation. The species is better able to adapt to change, such as new diseases or climate, and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding (breeding between close relatives).
[bio24-s1.4.3] Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but it gives less variation. It suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
[bio24-s1.4.4] Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly).
[bio24-s1.4.5] In primrose two flower forms exist: pin flowers, with a long style and low anthers, and thrum flowers, with a short style and high anthers. Insects carry pollen between the two forms, so the pollen reaches a different plant.
[bio24-s1.4.6] Agents of pollination (the carriers of pollen): insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak and birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. There is more on this in Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants.
[bio24-s1.5.b] The flower's structure matches the agent of pollination, whether an animal or the wind. Each type of flower is built to make the transfer of pollen as reliable as possible.
[bio24-s1.5.0] In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them.
[bio24-s1.5.1] Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
[bio24-s1.5.2] Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip, having learned the flower they are faithful to, so pollen is carried to the right species.
[bio24-s1.5.3] Plant and pollinator both benefit, because the plant is pollinated and the insect is fed: this is a mutualism (a relationship in which both partners gain). There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio24-s1.5.4] In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma. There is nothing to attract animals, so there are no showy petals, scent or nectar.
[bio24-s1.5.5] Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries and oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease.
[bio24-s1.5.6] Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre. It encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators, e.g. by leaving wildflowers and mowing less often.
[bio24-s1.5.7] Health link: wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.
[bio24-s1.6.b] Once pollen reaches a stigma of the right species, a sequence of events leads to fertilisation. Afterwards the flower parts change into the seed and fruit.
[bio24-s1.6.0] Fertilisation in five steps: 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma.
[bio24-s1.6.1] 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes.
[bio24-s1.6.2] 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it.
[bio24-s1.6.3] 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes, the first cell of the new plant).
[bio24-s1.6.4] 5. The zygote divides by mitosis (ordinary cell division that makes identical cells) to form the embryo.
[bio24-s1.6.5] Double fertilisation: a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store.
[bio24-s1.6.6] After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed, and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa, the seed coat. The ovary becomes the fruit, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp). The petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.
[bio24-s1.6.7] Diagram: a carpel in section, with a pollen grain on the stigma, the pollen tube growing down the style and entering an ovule through the micropyle. Labels: pollen grain, pollen tube, style, ovary, ovule, egg cell, micropyle.

CANDIDATES bio24-s2: Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants
[bio24-s2.0.b] A flower's structure follows from how its pollen travels. Insects must be lured in and rewarded, so insect-pollinated flowers advertise; the wind needs no persuading, so wind-pollinated flowers drop the advertising. Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second, and the flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio24-s2.0.0] Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides (markings that lead the insect towards the nectar), to attract insects.
[bio24-s2.0.1] Wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, because there is no animal to attract and large petals would block pollen in the wind.
[bio24-s2.0.2] Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries (the glands that produce nectar, a sugary liquid), so the insect is rewarded for visiting.
[bio24-s2.0.3] Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries, because there is no visitor to reward.
[bio24-s2.0.4] Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters, so that insects can spot them.
[bio24-s2.0.5] Wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins (dangling spikes of tiny flowers) or spikes held high above the leaves, where the wind can reach them.
[bio24-s2.0.6] Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
[bio24-s2.0.7] Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
[bio24-s2.1.b] The anther (the pollen-producing part of the stamen, held up on a stalk called the filament) and the stigma (the sticky or feathery top of the carpel that receives pollen) are positioned to suit the carrier. Insects touch the flower's parts; the wind blows past them.
[bio24-s2.1.0] Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them, so pollen rubs onto its body.
[bio24-s2.1.1] Wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
[bio24-s2.1.2] Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them and pollen sticks to them.
[bio24-s2.1.3] Wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s2.1.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of a buttercup or wallflower half-flower (large petal, nectary, anther inside, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas, anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
[bio24-s2.2.b] The pollen itself also differs. Insects deliver pollen accurately, whereas the wind scatters it at random, and the grains are built for the different journeys.
[bio24-s2.2.0] Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower.
[bio24-s2.2.1] Wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species, so many grains are needed for a few to land.
[bio24-s2.2.2] Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs.
[bio24-s2.2.3] Wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump together.
[bio24-s2.2.4] Diagram: an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.
[bio24-s2.3.b] This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.
[bio24-s2.3.0] Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
[bio24-s2.3.1] Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
[bio24-s2.3.2] What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features (recorded in words) are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface.
[bio24-s2.3.3] Quantitative features (measured as numbers) are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
[bio24-s2.3.4] Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen, so every flower is at the same stage. Examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements, to reduce the effect of one unusual flower.
[bio24-s2.3.5] Measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification, so the two types can be compared fairly.
[bio24-s2.3.6] Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.3.7] Also needed: microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.
[bio24-s2.4.b] The method moves from the whole flower, to its separated parts, to the pollen under the microscope, and repeats everything for the second flower type.
[bio24-s2.4.0] 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
[bio24-s2.4.1] 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
[bio24-s2.4.2] 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
[bio24-s2.4.3] 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
[bio24-s2.4.4] 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
[bio24-s2.4.5] 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
[bio24-s2.4.6] 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power.
[bio24-s2.4.7] 8. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
[bio24-s2.5.b] Scalpels, pollen, some plants and glass slides each carry a risk, so the practical needs a precaution for each.
[bio24-s2.5.0] Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely, so nobody is cut.
[bio24-s2.5.1] Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
[bio24-s2.5.2] Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
[bio24-s2.5.3] Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
[bio24-s2.5.4] Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.
[bio24-s2.6.b] Recording results in a table puts the two flowers side by side, so each difference can be seen and then explained.
[bio24-s2.6.0] Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
[bio24-s2.6.1] Expected primary data (collected by you) for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.6.2] Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.7.b] A conclusion links what was seen to why it matters for pollination, and secondary data add evidence the class could not collect itself.
[bio24-s2.7.0] Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations above. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio24-s2.7.1] Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen.
[bio24-s2.7.2] Other secondary data: published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; and labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
[bio24-s2.7.3] Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio24-s2.8.b] Every practical has weaknesses. Each limitation below has a matching improvement, and you should be able to give both.
[bio24-s2.8.0] Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
[bio24-s2.8.1] Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
[bio24-s2.8.2] Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.8.3] Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes.
[bio24-s2.8.4] The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
[bio24-s2.8.5] Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective, because different people judge differently; have two people record them.
[bio24-s2.8.6] Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

CANDIDATES bio7-s1: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
[bio7-s1.0.b] Biologists sort all cells into two types, depending on whether the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. The names themselves tell you the difference, and you need the units to talk about how big cells are.
[bio7-s1.0.0] A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments wrapped in their own membrane). Its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. “Pro” means before and “karyon” means kernel or nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.1] A eukaryotic cell has its DNA enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and it has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. “Eu” means true, so eukaryote means “true nucleus”. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.2] Cells are far too small for millimetres to be a handy unit, so their sizes are given in micrometres. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres) and 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.
[bio7-s1.1.b] Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells look very different, but both are living cells that must do the same basic jobs. So they share a core set of features.
[bio7-s1.1.0] Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves the cell, and cytoplasm, the fluid where many of the cell's reactions take place.
[bio7-s1.1.1] Both have ribosomes (the tiny structures that make proteins), and both use DNA as the genetic material, reading it with the same genetic code.
[bio7-s1.1.2] Both respire, releasing energy from food, and both use ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy) as the energy currency.
[bio7-s1.1.3] These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor, because such an unusual set of identical features is unlikely to have arisen separately.
[bio7-s1.2.b] The most important difference between the two cell types is how the DNA is stored. This affects where the DNA sits, what shape it has and what it is packed with.
[bio7-s1.2.0] Nucleus: prokaryotes have none. Their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Eukaryotes have a true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores (small channels through the envelope).
[bio7-s1.2.1] Number and shape of chromosomes: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome (a closed loop of DNA). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes (DNA with two ends).
[bio7-s1.2.2] Packaging: eukaryotic DNA is wound around histone proteins, which help to pack it. In bacteria the circular chromosome is not wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s1.2.3] Prokaryotes often also have plasmids, small extra rings of DNA separate from the main chromosome. Plasmids can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance, which is why resistance can spread between bacteria.
[bio7-s1.2.4] Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast), not in all eukaryotes, so do not say that eukaryotes have none or that all have them.
[bio7-s1.3.b] Eukaryotic cells divide their work between membrane-bound organelles, whereas prokaryotes have to do everything without them. Ribosomes are the one structure both have, but they differ in size.
[bio7-s1.3.0] Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none, so no mitochondria, no chloroplasts and no other membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.3.1] Without organelles, prokaryotes carry out respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it (folds of the membrane that increase its area).
[bio7-s1.3.2] Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. This is one more way these organelles resemble prokaryotic cells.
[bio7-s1.3.3] Ribosomes: both cell types have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). The S number is a measure of size.
[bio7-s1.3.4] Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they stop bacteria making proteins and harm bacteria but not human cells, which use 80S ribosomes in their cytoplasm.
[bio7-s1.4.b] The remaining differences are about the whole cell: how big it is, what surrounds it, and how the organism is built and reproduces.
[bio7-s1.4.0] Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so they are roughly ten times larger in length.
[bio7-s1.4.1] Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one, made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells have chitin walls, and animal cells have none.
[bio7-s1.4.2] Some prokaryotes have extra outer structures: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum, a whip-like tail, for movement. There is more on the full bacterial cell in Microorganisms.
[bio7-s1.4.3] Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular (made of a single cell) and divide by binary fission, in which one cell splits in two. Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis.
[bio7-s1.4.4] Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotes are protists, fungi, plants and animals.
[bio7-s1.5.b] How a cell looks in a micrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope) depends on the microscope, because a light microscope cannot show very small structures but an electron microscope can. Knowing what each shows lets you identify a cell.
[bio7-s1.5.0] Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure. It is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
[bio7-s1.5.1] Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm and the cell membrane as a line. Plant cells also show the cell wall, a large vacuole (a fluid-filled sac) and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.5.2] Electron micrographs show what the light microscope cannot. In prokaryotes they show the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall.
[bio7-s1.5.3] In eukaryotes, electron micrographs show the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
[bio7-s1.5.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule, flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), with the eukaryotic cell drawn much larger.
[bio7-s1.6.b] You may be given a micrograph and asked whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and to justify the answer. Work through the same checks in order.
[bio7-s1.6.0] 1. Check for a nucleus. If one is present, the cell is eukaryotic, because only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nucleus.
[bio7-s1.6.1] 2. Check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size. A cell of about 1–10 µm suggests a prokaryote, since eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm.
[bio7-s1.6.2] 3. Look for membrane-bound organelles, which only eukaryotes have.
[bio7-s1.6.3] 4. For a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts, which mean a plant cell, or their absence, which suggests an animal cell. There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
