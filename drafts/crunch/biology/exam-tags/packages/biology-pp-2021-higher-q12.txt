CANDIDATES bio11-s1: Chromosome structure and genes
[bio11-s1.0.b] A chromosome is DNA together with protein, and it carries genes in a fixed linear order. Knowing what it is made of, how it looks in a resting and a dividing cell, and how big it is explains why cells need to pack their DNA.
[bio11-s1.0.0] A chromosome is a single, very long molecule of DNA together with proteins. It is found in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell (a cell with a nucleus) and carries many genes in a fixed linear order along its length.
[bio11-s1.0.1] Chromatin is the name for the DNA and protein material of the chromosomes when the cell is not dividing. The chromosomes are then long, thin and uncoiled, spread through the nucleus, so they cannot be seen as separate threads.
[bio11-s1.0.2] Before a cell divides, the chromatin coils and folds up tightly (condenses), so each chromosome becomes short and thick and is visible under the light microscope when stained. In other words, chromatin and chromosomes are the same material in two states.
[bio11-s1.0.3] Human body cells are diploid (two sets of chromosomes), with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes (chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes) and one pair of sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio11-s1.0.4] Homologous chromosomes are the two chromosomes of a pair. They are the same length, have the centromere (the narrowed region of the chromosome) in the same position and carry the same genes at the same positions, with one chromosome of each pair coming from each parent.
[bio11-s1.0.5] The chromosome number is fixed for a species (46 in humans); a different number does not mean a more complex organism, so you cannot judge complexity by counting chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.0.6] The human genome (all the DNA in one set of 23 chromosomes) is about 3 billion base pairs. The DNA in one human body cell, stretched out, would be about 2 m long, yet it fits in a nucleus a few micrometres across, which is why DNA must be packed.
[bio11-s1.0.7] Prokaryotes (bacteria) differ: a bacterial cell has one circular chromosome in the cytoplasm, not wound around histones (the packing proteins of eukaryotes), plus plasmids (small extra rings of DNA).
[bio11-s1.1.b] Histones are the proteins that pack eukaryotic DNA into a small space. Without them, about 2 m of DNA per cell could not fit in the nucleus or be moved safely when the cell divides.
[bio11-s1.1.0] Histones are small proteins around which the DNA of a eukaryotic chromosome is wound. They are positively charged, while the phosphate groups of the DNA backbone are negatively charged, so DNA binds tightly to them.
[bio11-s1.1.1] The DNA wraps nearly twice around a core of eight histone proteins, forming a bead called a nucleosome. Nucleosomes are joined by short stretches of linker DNA, like beads on a string.
[bio11-s1.1.2] The string of nucleosomes coils and loops further, and before division it condenses into the visible chromosome.
[bio11-s1.1.3] Histones do three jobs: they pack a very long DNA molecule into a small nucleus, they protect the DNA and keep it untangled during division, and they help control which genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.4] DNA wound tightly around histones cannot be reached by the enzymes that read genes, so those genes are switched off; loosely packed regions are active, which means their genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.5] Chemical changes to histones are one of the epigenetic mechanisms (ways of switching genes on or off without changing the DNA sequence). There is more on this in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s1.1.6] Diagram: DNA double helix wound around histone cores to form nucleosomes, then the coiled chromatin fibre, then a condensed chromosome; label DNA, histone, nucleosome, chromatin and chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.b] Before a cell divides it copies its DNA, so each chromosome ends up as two joined copies. Chromatids and the centromere are the names for those copies and for the point that holds them together.
[bio11-s1.2.0] Before a cell divides, each chromosome's DNA is copied, a process called replication. The two identical copies stay joined together; each copy is a chromatid. There is more on this in DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle.
[bio11-s1.2.1] A replicated chromosome therefore consists of two identical sister chromatids, each containing one complete DNA molecule with the same genes and the same alleles (versions of a gene).
[bio11-s1.2.2] The centromere is the constricted (narrowed) region of a chromosome where the two sister chromatids are held together. Spindle fibres (protein threads that move chromosomes in division) attach at the centromere and pull the chromatids apart to opposite poles of the cell.
[bio11-s1.2.3] Once separated, each chromatid is a chromosome in its own right.
[bio11-s1.2.4] The centromere is in a fixed position for each chromosome, dividing it into two arms of equal or different length, so it is not always in the middle. Its position, together with length and banding pattern, is used to identify each chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.5] Before replication, a chromosome is one DNA molecule and one chromatid. After replication it is still one chromosome but has two chromatids, the X shape usually drawn; the two arms of the X are chromatids, not separate chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.2.6] Homologous chromosomes (a pair, one from each parent) may carry different alleles, whereas sister chromatids are identical copies of each other, so they always carry identical alleles.
[bio11-s1.2.7] Diagram: an unreplicated chromosome (single rod) beside a replicated chromosome (X shape); label chromatid, sister chromatids, centromere, arm, and a gene locus marked as a band at the same position on both chromatids. Optionally add a homologous pair of replicated chromosomes, same locus marked on each, labelled homologous chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.3.b] A gene is the unit of heredity, the piece of information that is passed on from cell to cell and parent to offspring. Genes sit at fixed places on chromosomes and, through the proteins they code for, control traits.
[bio11-s1.3.0] A gene is a section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a protein. This means a chromosome is not simply a string of genes: genes are sections of its DNA.
[bio11-s1.3.1] Each gene has a fixed position on a particular chromosome, called its locus (plural loci). The locus of a gene is the same in every member of a species.
[bio11-s1.3.2] A gene carries the information to make one protein, although some genes code instead for RNA molecules that are used directly, such as ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA. So not every gene codes for a protein.
[bio11-s1.3.3] Through the protein it codes for, a gene controls a trait (a characteristic of the organism). The gene is copied and passed to daughter cells in division and to offspring in gametes (sex cells).
[bio11-s1.3.4] Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes spread across the 23 kinds of chromosome; each chromosome carries hundreds to thousands of genes.
[bio11-s1.3.5] Because homologous chromosomes carry the same genes, a body cell has two copies of each autosomal gene. These may be the same or different versions, called alleles. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio11-s1.4.b] Most of the DNA in a human cell does not code for protein. Separating the small coding part from the large non-coding part shows that a chromosome is far more than a list of protein recipes.
[bio11-s1.4.0] Coding DNA is DNA whose base sequence is used to make a protein: the protein-coding parts of genes. Only about 1–2% of human DNA is coding DNA.
[bio11-s1.4.1] Non-coding DNA is all DNA that does not code for protein, about 98% of the human genome. It includes several kinds of sequence, listed in the next points.
[bio11-s1.4.2] Regulatory sequences beside genes act as switches, controlling when, where and how much a gene is expressed (used to make its product).
[bio11-s1.4.3] Genes for functional RNA (rRNA and tRNA) are non-coding DNA, as are introns, the non-coding sections within genes, which are copied into mRNA but removed before it is used to make protein.
[bio11-s1.4.4] Repeated sequences, such as the DNA at centromeres and at the ends of chromosomes (telomeres), protect the chromosome and help it divide correctly. Some non-coding DNA has a function that is still unknown.
[bio11-s1.4.5] Non-coding DNA was once called 'junk DNA', but much of it is now known to have functions, especially in regulating genes. Changes in regulatory DNA can change a trait without changing any protein.
[bio11-s1.4.6] Repeated non-coding sequences vary greatly in length between people, which is why they are used in DNA profiling (identifying individuals from their DNA).
[bio11-s1.4.7] Diagram: a stretch of a chromosome's DNA drawn as a line, with two genes shown as boxes (each with a regulatory region at one end and coding sections separated by an intron), and the DNA between genes labelled non-coding; label gene, coding DNA, non-coding DNA, regulatory sequence and intron.

CANDIDATES bio11-s11: Sex-linked traits
[bio11-s11.0.b] Some genes sit on the sex chromosomes rather than on the other chromosomes, and this changes how they are inherited. Because males and females carry different sex chromosomes, the same allele can have a different effect in each sex.
[bio11-s11.0.0] Sex-linked genes are almost all on the X chromosome (X-linked), because the Y carries very few genes.
[bio11-s11.0.1] A sex-linked trait is a trait controlled by such a gene. The Y has no matching allele for most X-linked genes, so in males the X-linked gene has no second copy to pair with.
[bio11-s11.0.2] Females (XX) have two copies of each X-linked gene, so they can be homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous (two different alleles).
[bio11-s11.0.3] A heterozygous female with one recessive allele is a carrier (she carries the allele but does not show the trait), because her dominant allele masks it.
[bio11-s11.0.4] Males (XY) have only one copy, so whatever allele their single X carries is expressed, with no second allele to mask it.
[bio11-s11.0.5] This is why a male cannot be a carrier, and why one recessive allele is enough for him to show the trait.
[bio11-s11.0.6] X-linked recessive conditions are therefore much more common in males than in females.
[bio11-s11.0.7] A female shows the condition only if she inherits the recessive allele from both parents, which means an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
[bio11-s11.1.b] To predict who inherits an X-linked allele, follow the sex chromosomes. A child's sex is decided by the sex chromosome the father contributes, and that also decides where the X-linked allele comes from.
[bio11-s11.1.0] A father passes his X to all his daughters and his Y to all his sons.
[bio11-s11.1.1] So an affected father never passes an X-linked condition to his sons, because sons get his Y, which carries no allele for the gene.
[bio11-s11.1.2] All his daughters are at least carriers, because each receives his X with the recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.1.3] A son's X-linked allele always comes from his mother, since his only X is the one she gave him.
[bio11-s11.2.b] Genetics questions use a fixed way of writing X-linked genotypes so that the sex and the alleles are both clear at a glance.
[bio11-s11.2.0] Write the allele as a superscript on the X, and the Y with no allele, because the Y has no matching allele for these genes.
[bio11-s11.2.1] Colour blindness: Xᴮ = normal colour vision, Xᵇ = colour blind. Haemophilia: Xᴴ = normal clotting, Xʰ = haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.2.2] Colour-blindness genotypes: female XᴮXᴮ (normal), XᴮXᵇ (carrier), XᵇXᵇ (colour blind); male XᴮY (normal), XᵇY (colour blind).
[bio11-s11.2.3] Use the letters a question gives.
[bio11-s11.3.b] Red-green colour blindness is the standard example of an X-linked recessive condition, and the one most often used in crosses.
[bio11-s11.3.0] Red-green colour blindness is an X-linked recessive condition in which a person has difficulty telling red and green apart.
[bio11-s11.3.1] The genes for the red- and green-sensitive light-absorbing pigments of the cone cells of the retina (the colour-detecting cells at the back of the eye) are on the X chromosome. The recessive allele gives a pigment that is missing or works abnormally.
[bio11-s11.3.2] It affects about 1 in 12 men (8%) and about 1 in 200 women (0.5%) of northern European ancestry. The difference fits the pattern that males need only one recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.3.3] It is detected with coloured-dot test charts (Ishihara plates).
[bio11-s11.3.4] It is not treatable, but most people adapt to it.
[bio11-s11.4.b] Haemophilia is the second named X-linked recessive condition. It shows how a single faulty gene can affect the whole body, because it removes one step in blood clotting.
[bio11-s11.4.0] Haemophilia is an X-linked recessive condition in which blood does not clot properly because a clotting protein is missing or faulty.
[bio11-s11.4.1] Haemophilia A lacks clotting factor VIII; haemophilia B lacks factor IX.
[bio11-s11.4.2] Bleeding after injury lasts much longer, and bleeding can occur into joints and muscles.
[bio11-s11.4.3] Haemophilia A affects about 1 in 5,000 male births.
[bio11-s11.4.4] It is treated by injections of the missing clotting factor, now mostly produced by genetically engineered cells. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio11-s11.5.b] Haemophilia in European royal families is a well-known real case of an X-linked allele passing through carrier daughters to affected grandsons.
[bio11-s11.5.0] Queen Victoria (1819–1901) was a carrier of haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.5.1] Her son Leopold had haemophilia. Through two of her daughters, who were carriers, it passed into the royal families of Russia (Tsarevich Alexei, son of Tsar Nicholas II) and Spain.
[bio11-s11.6.b] Haemophilia and colour blindness are not the only X-linked recessive conditions. A third example is useful when a question asks for one other than haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.6.0] Duchenne muscular dystrophy is another X-linked recessive condition, caused by a lack of the muscle protein dystrophin, which causes progressive muscle weakness (muscles get steadily weaker over time).
[bio11-s11.6.1] It is almost always in boys, which fits the pattern for X-linked recessive conditions.
[bio11-s11.7.b] A mother who is a carrier of colour blindness has a partner with normal vision, so we can work out what their children could inherit.
[bio11-s11.7.0] Parents' phenotypes: normal-vision (carrier) female × normal-vision male.
[bio11-s11.7.1] Parents' genotypes: carrier female XᴮXᵇ × normal male XᴮY.
[bio11-s11.7.2] Gametes (each carries one sex chromosome with its allele): carrier female Xᴮ, Xᵇ × normal male Xᴮ, Y.
[bio11-s11.7.3] Punnett square: XᴮXᴮ, XᴮY / XᴮXᵇ, XᵇY.
[bio11-s11.7.4] Offspring genotypes and phenotypes: 1 normal female (XᴮXᴮ) : 1 carrier female (XᴮXᵇ) : 1 normal male (XᴮY) : 1 colour-blind male (XᵇY).
[bio11-s11.7.5] Half the sons are expected to be colour blind and half the daughters carriers; no daughters are colour blind, because every daughter gets the father's normal Xᴮ. 25% of all children are colour blind.
[bio11-s11.7.6] Diagram: the Punnett square for this cross, with the mother's gametes Xᴮ and Xᵇ on one side and the father's Xᴮ and Y on the other, each box labelled with genotype and phenotype (normal female, carrier female, normal male, colour-blind male).
[bio11-s11.8.b] Here the father has haemophilia and the mother has two normal alleles. This cross shows what an affected father passes on.
[bio11-s11.8.0] Parents' phenotypes: normal female × affected male.
[bio11-s11.8.1] Parents' genotypes: normal female XᴴXᴴ × affected male XʰY.
[bio11-s11.8.2] Gametes: normal female Xᴴ × affected male Xʰ, Y.
[bio11-s11.8.3] Offspring: XᴴXʰ (carrier daughters) and XᴴY (normal sons), 1 : 1.
[bio11-s11.8.4] All daughters are carriers, because each gets the father's Xʰ. No children have haemophilia, because the sons get his Y and the mother's normal Xᴴ.
[bio11-s11.9.b] When both parents contribute a recessive allele, a daughter can be affected. This cross shows how that happens.
[bio11-s11.9.0] Parents' phenotypes: carrier female × colour-blind male.
[bio11-s11.9.1] Parents' genotypes: carrier female XᴮXᵇ × colour-blind male XᵇY.
[bio11-s11.9.2] Gametes: carrier female Xᴮ, Xᵇ × colour-blind male Xᵇ, Y.
[bio11-s11.9.3] Offspring: XᴮXᵇ (carrier female), XᵇXᵇ (colour-blind female), XᴮY (normal male), XᵇY (colour-blind male), 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
[bio11-s11.9.4] Half the daughters and half the sons are colour blind. This is how a colour-blind daughter can arise: she inherits Xᵇ from both her mother and her father.
[bio11-s11.10.b] This cross reverses the sexes of the affected parent. It shows that an affected mother passes the condition to every son.
[bio11-s11.10.0] Parents' phenotypes: affected female × normal male.
[bio11-s11.10.1] Parents' genotypes: affected female XʰXʰ × normal male XᴴY.
[bio11-s11.10.2] Gametes: affected female Xʰ × normal male Xᴴ, Y.
[bio11-s11.10.3] Offspring: XᴴXʰ (carrier daughters) and XʰY (sons with haemophilia).
[bio11-s11.10.4] All sons are affected, because their only X comes from the mother; all daughters are carriers, because they also get the father's normal Xᴴ.
[bio11-s11.10.5] Females with haemophilia are very rare; this cross is used to show the pattern.
[bio11-s11.11.b] A pedigree shows how a trait passes through several generations of a family. Before you can use one, you need to know what its symbols and lines mean.
[bio11-s11.11.0] A pedigree is a family-tree diagram showing which members of a family have a trait.
[bio11-s11.11.1] Symbols: square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected; half-shaded (or a dot in the centre) = carrier.
[bio11-s11.11.2] A horizontal line joining a male and a female = parents; a vertical line down to a horizontal line = their children, in birth order from left to right.
[bio11-s11.11.3] Generations are numbered I, II, III and individuals 1, 2, 3 within each, so every person can be named, for example II-2.
[bio11-s11.12.b] Four features of a pedigree point to an X-linked recessive trait. Each follows from the rules on how X chromosomes are passed on.
[bio11-s11.12.0] Many more affected males than females, because a male needs only one recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.12.1] Affected males usually have unaffected parents, because the mother is a carrier.
[bio11-s11.12.2] The trait can skip a generation, passing from an affected man through his carrier daughters to his grandsons.
[bio11-s11.12.3] It is never passed from father to son, because a son gets his father's Y, not his X.
[bio11-s11.13.b] To give the genotype of a named person, or the chance that a child is affected or a carrier, work through the pedigree in a fixed order. This starts with the people whose genotype is certain.
[bio11-s11.13.0] 1. Write XY for every male and XX for every female.
[bio11-s11.13.1] 2. Fill in the alleles of affected males (XʰY) and unaffected males (XᴴY), which follow directly from their phenotype.
[bio11-s11.13.2] 3. Any daughter of an affected male is at least a carrier; any mother of an affected son is a carrier or affected.
[bio11-s11.13.3] 4. Use these to fill in the remaining females.
[bio11-s11.13.4] 5. Then work any further cross with a Punnett square.

Generation III: the carrier daughter's children with a normal man, including an affected grandson. Label symbols and generations.
[bio11-s11.14.b] Genes on the Y chromosome follow a different pattern from X-linked genes, because only males have a Y.
[bio11-s11.14.0] Genes on the Y chromosome (for example SRY) are passed only from father to son and are found only in males. There is more on SRY in Sex determination.

CANDIDATES bio11-s7: Mendel's Laws
[bio11-s7.0.b] Mendel bred pea plants in a monastery garden and worked out the basic rules of inheritance long before anyone knew about genes, chromosomes or DNA.
[bio11-s7.0.0] Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was an Augustinian friar, later abbot, at the monastery of St Thomas in Brno (then Brünn, in the Austrian Empire; now in the Czech Republic).
[bio11-s7.0.1] He bred garden pea plants (Pisum sativum) between 1856 and 1863, presented his results in 1865 and published them in 1866.
[bio11-s7.0.2] His work was largely ignored until 1900, when it was rediscovered independently by other botanists, including Hugo de Vries and Carl Correns.
[bio11-s7.0.3] He worked before chromosomes, meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes) or DNA were understood, so he called the units of inheritance “factors”. We now call them genes, and their different versions alleles.
[bio11-s7.1.b] Mendel's choice of organism was part of why his experiments worked. Garden peas had four features that made clean, countable results possible.
[bio11-s7.1.0] Peas had many varieties with clear contrasting forms of a trait (for example tall or dwarf, with nothing in between), so every offspring could be sorted into one group or the other.
[bio11-s7.1.1] Peas normally self-pollinate (a flower pollinates itself), so true-breeding lines were available. True-breeding means offspring always look like their parents, generation after generation.
[bio11-s7.1.2] They can be cross-pollinated by hand: the anthers (pollen-producing parts) of one flower are removed and its stigma (the pollen-receiving part) is dusted with pollen from another. This lets the experimenter choose the parents.
[bio11-s7.1.3] They grow quickly and produce many seeds, giving large numbers to count, which is needed to see clear ratios.
[bio11-s7.2.b] Mendel's method was careful and quantitative, which is why his results were clear. He followed simple, clear-cut traits and treated inheritance as something to be counted and calculated.
[bio11-s7.2.0] The seven traits he studied, dominant form first: seed shape (round / wrinkled), seed colour (yellow / green), flower colour (purple / white), pod shape (inflated / constricted), pod colour (green / yellow), flower position (axial, along the stem / terminal, at the tip), stem length (tall / dwarf).
[bio11-s7.2.1] He started with true-breeding parents and studied one trait at a time, then two. Looking at one trait at a time kept the results simple enough to interpret.
[bio11-s7.2.2] He counted large numbers of offspring, because small samples can give misleading ratios by chance.
[bio11-s7.2.3] He recorded results over several generations: P (the parents), F1 (their offspring) and F2 (the offspring of the F1 crossed with each other).
[bio11-s7.2.4] He used mathematics (ratios) to analyse the results, and repeated each cross for several traits to check that the pattern held.
[bio11-s7.3.b] Mendel's crosses gave the same pattern every time. That pattern led him to the idea that each trait is controlled by two factors, one of which can hide the other.
[bio11-s7.3.0] Crossing true-breeding tall with true-breeding dwarf gave an F1 that was all tall; none were dwarf and none were in between.
[bio11-s7.3.1] Self-pollinating the F1 gave an F2 of tall and dwarf in about 3 : 1.
[bio11-s7.3.2] F2 figures: 787 tall : 277 dwarf (2.84 : 1); 5474 round : 1850 wrinkled seeds (2.96 : 1); 6022 yellow : 2001 green seeds (3.01 : 1). Large numbers bring the ratio very close to 3 : 1.
[bio11-s7.3.3] The dwarf trait disappeared in the F1 and reappeared in the F2, so the dwarf “factor” must have been present, hidden, in the F1.
[bio11-s7.3.4] He concluded that each plant has two factors for each trait, one from each parent, and that one (dominant) can mask the other (recessive).
[bio11-s7.4.b] Mendel's first law explains the 3 : 1 ratio. It is about the two alleles of a single gene, and what happens to them when gametes (sex cells) are made.
[bio11-s7.4.0] Statement: “The two alleles (factors) of a gene separate (segregate) from each other during gamete formation, so that each gamete receives only one allele of each gene.”
[bio11-s7.4.1] Shorter accepted form: “Inherited traits are controlled by pairs of factors (alleles), which separate during gamete formation.”
[bio11-s7.4.2] A heterozygous tall plant (Tt, carrying two different alleles) forms two types of gamete, T and t, in equal numbers, so half the gametes carry T and half carry t.
[bio11-s7.4.3] Random fertilisation of these gametes in Tt × Tt gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt and 3 tall : 1 dwarf, the 3 : 1 Mendel observed. There is more on this in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s7.4.4] Chromosome basis (Mendel did not know this): the two alleles of a gene lie at the same locus (position) on the two chromosomes of a homologous pair, the matching pair of chromosomes, one from each parent.
[bio11-s7.4.5] In meiosis the homologous chromosomes separate into different cells, carrying one allele each into each gamete. This is why the alleles segregate.
[bio11-s7.4.6] Diagram: a diploid cell with one homologous pair, one chromosome carrying T and the other t at the same position, dividing by meiosis into gametes, half with T and half with t. Labels: homologous pair, allele T, allele t, locus, meiosis, gametes (haploid).
[bio11-s7.5.b] Mendel's second law deals with two different genes at once. It says that how one gene's alleles are shared out into gametes does not affect how another gene's alleles are shared out.
[bio11-s7.5.0] Statement: “When gametes form, the alleles of one gene separate independently of the alleles of another gene, so either allele of one gene can combine at random with either allele of another gene.”
[bio11-s7.5.1] Equivalent: “Each member of a pair of factors can combine randomly with either member of another pair.”
[bio11-s7.5.2] The law applies to genes on different chromosomes (unlinked genes). Genes close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together and break this rule.
[bio11-s7.5.3] A plant heterozygous for seed shape and seed colour (RrYy) forms four types of gamete, RY, Ry, rY and ry, in equal numbers (1/4 each).
[bio11-s7.5.4] This is because whether a gamete gets R or r has no effect on whether it gets Y or y.
[bio11-s7.5.5] Chromosome basis: when homologous pairs line up in meiosis, the way one pair lines up is independent of the way another pair lines up.
[bio11-s7.5.6] With R and Y on different chromosome pairs, half the cells put R with Y (giving RY and ry gametes) and half put R with y (giving Ry and rY gametes), so all four types are made in equal numbers.
[bio11-s7.5.7] Diagram: a cell with two homologous pairs (a long pair carrying R and r, a short pair carrying Y and y) in the two possible arrangements. Arrangement 1 gives RY and ry; arrangement 2 gives Ry and rY. Labels: homologous pairs, alleles, two equally likely arrangements, four gamete types.
[bio11-s7.6.b] A dihybrid cross follows two traits at the same time. Mendel's results from one showed that the two traits were inherited independently of each other.
[bio11-s7.6.0] Mendel's dihybrid cross: true-breeding round yellow × wrinkled green gave an F1 that was all round yellow.
[bio11-s7.6.1] Self-pollinating the F1 gave an F2 of 315 round yellow : 108 round green : 101 wrinkled yellow : 32 wrinkled green (total 556), close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
[bio11-s7.6.2] The new combinations (round green and wrinkled yellow) can only arise if the two genes are inherited independently, because neither parent had those pairings of traits. There is more on this in Dihybrid crosses.
[bio11-s7.7.b] Mendel's biggest contribution was a new way of thinking about heredity. His laws still underpin how we predict the outcome of genetic crosses.
[bio11-s7.7.0] Mendel showed that inheritance is particulate: traits are passed on by separate units that stay unchanged from generation to generation, rather than by blending of the parents' characteristics.
[bio11-s7.7.1] A blended trait could never reappear unchanged, as the dwarf trait did in the F2, so the reappearance is evidence for separate units.
[bio11-s7.7.2] His laws are the basis of the predictions in Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics.

CANDIDATES bio11-s8: Dihybrid crosses
[bio11-s8.0.b] A monohybrid cross follows one gene. A dihybrid cross follows two genes at once, which is how you predict offspring when two traits are inherited together.
[bio11-s8.0.0] A dihybrid cross follows the inheritance of two different traits (two genes) at the same time, e.g. seed shape and seed colour in peas.
[bio11-s8.0.1] “Unlinked” means the two genes are on different chromosomes, so they assort independently (are sorted into gametes without affecting each other) and every combination of their alleles is equally likely in the gametes. There is more on genes on the same chromosome in Linkage.
[bio11-s8.0.2] Key for the worked examples: R = round seed, r = wrinkled; Y = yellow seed, y = green. Capital letters are dominant alleles (the version that shows when present), lower case are recessive.
[bio11-s8.0.3] A genotype always has two letters for each gene (four letters in all), written with the two alleles of one gene together and the dominant first: RrYy, not RYry. Keeping each gene's pair together stops you mixing the genes up.
[bio11-s8.1.b] Before you can fill in a Punnett square you need the gametes (sex cells) each parent can make. This is a short method you can use on any two-gene genotype.
[bio11-s8.1.0] Each gamete gets one allele of each gene, so every gamete has two letters, one for each gene; never two of the same gene: RY, not Rr.
[bio11-s8.1.1] Method: pair each allele of the first gene with each allele of the second gene.
[bio11-s8.1.2] For RrYy: R with Y, R with y, r with Y, r with y, giving RY, Ry, rY, ry, in equal numbers. This equal mixing is the Law of Independent Assortment: the alleles of one gene are sorted into gametes independently of the other gene.
[bio11-s8.1.3] The number of gamete types depends on how many genes are heterozygous (have two different alleles): RRYY gives one type (RY); RRYy gives two (RY, Ry); Rryy gives two (Ry, ry).
[bio11-s8.1.4] RrYy, heterozygous for both genes, gives four (RY, Ry, rY, ry); rryy gives one (ry). Write each different gamete once, however many times it could be made.
[bio11-s8.2.b] The first step in Mendel's style of cross is to breed two pure lines together. The offspring, the F1 (first filial) generation, are all identical, which sets up the F2.
[bio11-s8.2.0] Parents' phenotypes (the visible traits): round yellow × wrinkled green (both true-breeding, meaning homozygous, so they always pass on the same alleles).
[bio11-s8.2.1] Parents' genotypes: RRYY × rryy, so each parent is homozygous for both genes.
[bio11-s8.2.2] Gametes: RY × ry, because each parent has only one type of gamete.
[bio11-s8.2.3] F1 genotype: all RrYy, as every offspring gets one dominant and one recessive allele of each gene.
[bio11-s8.2.4] F1 phenotype: all round yellow (both dominant traits), because the dominant alleles hide the recessive ones.
[bio11-s8.3.b] Crossing two F1 plants is the core dihybrid cross. Both parents are heterozygous for both genes, so each makes four types of gamete and the Punnett square (a grid showing every possible gamete pairing) is 4 × 4.
[bio11-s8.3.0] Parents (F1): round yellow × round yellow. Genotypes: RrYy × RrYy, so both are heterozygous for both genes.
[bio11-s8.3.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × RY, Ry, rY, ry.
[bio11-s8.3.2] Punnett square: 4 × 4 = 16 boxes, each a 1/16 chance, because every gamete pairing is equally likely.
[bio11-s8.3.3] Punnett square, row by row (gametes RY, Ry, rY, ry across the top and down the side). Row 1 (gamete RY): RRYY, RRYy, RrYY, RrYy. Row 2 (gamete Ry): RRYy, RRyy, RrYy, Rryy.
[bio11-s8.3.4] Row 3 (gamete rY): RrYY, RrYy, rrYY, rrYy. Row 4 (gamete ry): RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy.
[bio11-s8.3.5] F2 genotypes (nine kinds): 1 RRYY, 2 RRYy, 2 RrYY, 4 RrYy, 1 RRyy, 2 Rryy, 1 rrYY, 2 rrYy, 1 rryy (total 16). Count repeated genotypes from the squares to get these numbers.
[bio11-s8.4.b] The 16 boxes contain nine genotypes but only four phenotypes, because a dominant allele hides the recessive one. Counting those phenotypes gives the famous dihybrid ratio.
[bio11-s8.4.0] F2 phenotypes: 9 round yellow (at least one R and at least one Y) : 3 round green (at least one R, yy) : 3 wrinkled yellow (rr, at least one Y) : 1 wrinkled green (rryy).
[bio11-s8.4.1] The ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is the signature of a dihybrid F2 with two unlinked genes, each with complete dominance (the heterozygote looks the same as the dominant homozygote). There is more on Mendel's F2 counts, close to this ratio, in Mendel's Laws.
[bio11-s8.5.b] Look at the F2 phenotypes against the original parents. Some combinations of traits appear that neither the P nor the F1 plants showed.
[bio11-s8.5.0] Two of the four F2 phenotypes (round green and wrinkled yellow) are new combinations, found in neither the P nor the F1 generation.
[bio11-s8.5.1] They appear because the genes assort independently, so any seed shape allele can end up with any seed colour allele. They are a source of variation in offspring.
[bio11-s8.5.2] Diagram: the full 4 × 4 Punnett square of the F1 × F1 cross, with gametes labelled on both sides, and the boxes coloured or marked by phenotype (9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, 1 wrinkled green).
[bio11-s8.6.b] A 16-box square is slow and easy to miscount. Because the two genes are independent, you can work out each gene separately and multiply, which gives a quick check.
[bio11-s8.6.0] Because the genes are independent, the dihybrid result is the two monohybrid results multiplied.
[bio11-s8.6.1] Rr × Rr gives 3/4 round and 1/4 wrinkled; Yy × Yy gives 3/4 yellow and 1/4 green, so each gene is treated as its own monohybrid cross.
[bio11-s8.6.2] So round yellow = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16, round green = 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16, wrinkled yellow = 3/16, wrinkled green = 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.
[bio11-s8.6.3] Use this to check a Punnett square; show the full square in an exam answer when asked for a genetic cross.
[bio11-s8.7.b] A test cross crosses an individual showing the dominant traits with one that is homozygous recessive for both genes. The recessive parent makes only one type of gamete, so the offspring reveal exactly which gametes the other parent makes.
[bio11-s8.7.0] Round yellow (RrYy) × wrinkled green (rryy) is the dihybrid test cross.
[bio11-s8.7.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × ry, because rryy can only make ry.
[bio11-s8.7.2] Offspring genotypes: RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy (1 : 1 : 1 : 1).
[bio11-s8.7.3] Phenotypes: 1 round yellow : 1 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green (25% each), because each of the four gametes has a 1/4 chance.
[bio11-s8.7.4] A 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 result shows that the dominant-looking parent was heterozygous for both genes and that the genes assort independently. There is more on the same cross with linked genes in Linkage.
[bio11-s8.7.5] The same pattern with other traits: in peas with tall (T) dominant to dwarf (t) and yellow (Y) dominant to green (y), TtYy × ttyy gives 1 tall yellow (TtYy) : 1 tall green (Ttyy) : 1 dwarf yellow (ttYy) : 1 dwarf green (ttyy).
[bio11-s8.8.b] Not every cross has two double heterozygotes. Here one parent is heterozygous for only one gene, so it makes just two gamete types and the square is 4 × 2.
[bio11-s8.8.0] Round yellow × round green: RrYy × Rryy, so the second parent is homozygous recessive for colour and heterozygous for shape.
[bio11-s8.8.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × Ry, ry.
[bio11-s8.8.2] Punnett square: 4 × 2 = 8 boxes; column Ry: RRYy, RRyy, RrYy, Rryy; column ry: RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy.
[bio11-s8.8.3] Phenotypes: 3 round yellow : 3 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green (3 : 3 : 1 : 1).
[bio11-s8.8.4] Check: 3/4 round × 1/2 yellow = 3/8 round yellow, matching the 3 round yellow out of 8 boxes.
[bio11-s8.9.b] When one gene shows incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has a blend in between the two homozygotes. This changes the phenotype count but the method for crossing two genes stays the same.
[bio11-s8.9.0] Snapdragons: tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t); red (R) and white (W) flower colour show incomplete dominance (RW = pink).
[bio11-s8.9.1] Parents' phenotypes: homozygous dwarf red × tall pink (heterozygous for height). Parents' genotypes: ttRR × TtRW.
[bio11-s8.9.2] Gametes: tR × TR, TW, tR, tW, because the ttRR parent can only make tR while the TtRW parent makes four types.
[bio11-s8.9.3] Offspring: TtRR (tall, red), TtRW (tall, pink), ttRR (dwarf, red), ttRW (dwarf, pink), in a 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio.
[bio11-s8.9.4] So 50% of the offspring have pink flowers (two of four genotypes) and 50% are tall.
[bio11-s8.9.5] To the second generation: TTRR × ttWW gives an F1 of all TtRW (tall pink).
[bio11-s8.9.6] F1 × F1 gives 3 tall red : 6 tall pink : 3 tall white : 1 dwarf red : 2 dwarf pink : 1 dwarf white (out of 16).
[bio11-s8.9.7] These are found by multiplying 3/4 tall and 1/4 dwarf by 1/4 red, 1/2 pink and 1/4 white, because the two genes are independent.

CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio17-s3: Mutations
[bio17-s3.0.b] DNA is copied every time a cell divides, and the copying is not perfect. A mutation is what is left when a change in the DNA is not put right, and it matters because it is the raw material for new traits.
[bio17-s3.0.0] A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA: either in the base sequence of a gene, called a point or gene mutation, or in the structure of a chromosome, called a chromosomal mutation.
[bio17-s3.0.1] A mutation that changes a gene produces a new allele (a different version of that gene).
[bio17-s3.0.2] Mutations arise spontaneously, mainly as uncorrected errors in base pairing during DNA replication (the copying of DNA before a cell divides), when a wrong base is paired and nobody fixes it.
[bio17-s3.0.3] Mutagens (agents that increase the rate of mutation) include ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive substances) and ultraviolet light, which damage DNA directly.
[bio17-s3.0.4] Other mutagens are chemicals such as those in tobacco smoke, and some viruses.
[bio17-s3.0.5] Repair enzymes correct most damage, so only the errors that escape repair become permanent mutations. There is more on mutagens and cancer in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.b] Whether a mutation matters to a family depends on which cell it happens in. What it does to the organism depends on whether it changes a protein, and for evolution it matters because mutation creates new alleles.
[bio17-s3.1.0] Body-cell (somatic) mutations affect only the individual and the cells descended from the mutated cell, so they are not inherited; they can lead to cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.1] Mutations in cells that form gametes (germ-line mutations) can be passed to offspring, and are then present in every cell of the child because every cell grows from the fertilised egg.
[bio17-s3.1.2] Most mutations are neutral, either because they fall in non-coding DNA (DNA that does not code for a protein) or because they are silent, meaning the protein is not changed.
[bio17-s3.1.3] Many mutations are harmful, because they change or stop a protein.
[bio17-s3.1.4] A few mutations are beneficial in a particular environment, for example lactase persistence (being able to digest milk sugar as an adult).
[bio17-s3.1.5] Mutations giving antibiotic resistance help bacteria survive antibiotics, so they are beneficial to the bacteria in the presence of the drug.
[bio17-s3.1.6] Mutation is the only source of new alleles, and so the ultimate source of variation for natural selection to act on. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio17-s3.2.b] A point mutation is a tiny change at one spot in a gene. Because the genetic code is read in codons (groups of three bases, each coding for one amino acid), the effect depends on exactly what changes. The simplest case is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.2.0] A point mutation is a change in the nucleotide sequence at one point in a gene, usually of one or a few base pairs. This includes small deletions and insertions, not only substitutions.
[bio17-s3.2.1] It occurs when a wrong nucleotide pairs during replication and is not corrected, or when a nucleotide is added or missed out.
[bio17-s3.2.2] At the next replication the change is copied into both strands, so it becomes permanent.
[bio17-s3.2.3] In a substitution one base is replaced by another, so only one codon changes.
[bio17-s3.2.4] Silent substitution: the new codon codes for the same amino acid, because the code is degenerate (several codons can code for the same amino acid), so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.2.5] Missense substitution: the new codon codes for a different amino acid, which may or may not change the protein's shape and function.
[bio17-s3.2.6] Nonsense substitution: the codon becomes a stop codon (a signal to end translation), so the polypeptide is cut short and usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.b] Adding or losing bases is more dangerous than swapping one, because it can shift the way the whole rest of the gene is read.
[bio17-s3.3.0] Insertion or deletion of one base, or of any number of bases that is not a multiple of three, is a frameshift: every codon after the change is read differently, because the code is read in threes without gaps from the start codon.
[bio17-s3.3.1] After a frameshift many amino acids are wrong, and a stop codon often appears early or is lost, so the protein usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.2] A frameshift near the start of a gene is more damaging than one near the end, because more of the protein is changed.
[bio17-s3.3.3] Insertion or deletion of three bases adds or removes one amino acid without a frameshift, so the rest of the protein is normal, but even one missing amino acid can change the folding, as in cystic fibrosis.
[bio17-s3.4.b] To model a mutation, write the mRNA in codons, apply the change, then read each new codon with the codon table. Start with the normal mRNA AUG UUU GGC AAA UAA, which codes for methionine, phenylalanine, glycine and lysine, then stops.
[bio17-s3.4.0] Substitution, silent: GGC changed to GGA; still glycine, so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.4.1] Substitution, missense: UUU changed to UCU; serine instead of phenylalanine.
[bio17-s3.4.2] Substitution, nonsense: AAA changed to UAA, a stop codon; the chain ends after glycine.
[bio17-s3.4.3] Insertion of G after AUG: AUG GUU UGG CAA AUA A… gives methionine, valine, tryptophan, glutamine, isoleucine…; every amino acid after the start is changed and the original stop codon is lost from the reading frame.
[bio17-s3.4.4] Deletion of the first U of UUU: AUG UUG GCA AAU AA… gives methionine, leucine, alanine, asparagine…; this is a frameshift, because the groups of three have moved along.
[bio17-s3.4.5] Deletion of the whole codon UUU: AUG GGC AAA UAA gives methionine, glycine, lysine; one amino acid is missing and the rest is unchanged, so there is no frameshift.
[bio17-s3.4.6] Word analogy for revision: THE CAT ATE THE RAT; substitution THE CAT ATE THE HAT changes one word; insertion THE CXA TAT ETH ERA T gives nonsense words after the change; deletion THE ATA TET HER AT shifts every word after it.
[bio17-s3.5.b] Sickle-cell anaemia shows how a change in a single base can alter a protein and so a whole disease. It is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.5.0] Sickle-cell anaemia is a substitution in the gene for beta-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin, on chromosome 11.
[bio17-s3.5.1] One base change alters the codon for the sixth amino acid from GAG to GUG in mRNA (CTC to CAC in the DNA strand being read), so valine replaces glutamic acid.
[bio17-s3.5.2] The changed haemoglobin, haemoglobin S, molecules stick together into long fibres when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a rigid sickle shape.
[bio17-s3.5.3] Sickled cells block small blood vessels, causing pain and organ damage, and are destroyed quickly, causing anaemia.
[bio17-s3.5.4] Sickle-cell is recessive: people with two copies have the disease; people with one copy (sickle-cell trait) are usually healthy and are more resistant to malaria.
[bio17-s3.5.5] This is why the sickle-cell allele is common in parts of Africa where malaria is common, an example of natural selection.
[bio17-s3.6.b] Cystic fibrosis is the example of a point mutation that is a small deletion. It shows that losing even one amino acid can ruin a protein.
[bio17-s3.6.0] Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7, which codes for a chloride-ion channel protein in cell membranes.
[bio17-s3.6.1] The most common cystic fibrosis mutation, F508del (delta F508), is a deletion of three bases, which removes one amino acid, phenylalanine, at position 508 of the protein.
[bio17-s3.6.2] The faulty protein folds wrongly, is broken down by the cell and does not reach the membrane, giving thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas.
[bio17-s3.6.3] Cystic fibrosis is recessive, and Ireland has the highest rate of cystic fibrosis in the world.
[bio17-s3.6.4] Newer CFTR modulator drugs help the faulty protein fold and work, which shows how knowing the mutation leads to treatment.
[bio17-s3.6.5] Cystic fibrosis is one of the conditions tested for in the newborn heel-prick screening offered to every baby in Ireland. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio17-s3.7.b] A chromosomal mutation changes a large piece of a chromosome, so many genes are affected at once, unlike a point mutation. A letter model makes the four types easy to follow.
[bio17-s3.7.0] A chromosomal mutation is a change in the structure of one or more chromosomes that affects many genes at once.
[bio17-s3.7.1] It occurs when chromosomes break, for example from ionising radiation or chemicals, or from errors when chromosomes are copied or separated in meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes), and the broken pieces are lost or rejoin in the wrong place.
[bio17-s3.7.2] Large changes can be seen under the light microscope in a karyotype (a picture of a cell's chromosomes).
[bio17-s3.7.3] Model: write a chromosome as A B C • D E F G H, where the letters are genes or groups of genes and • is the centromere (the constricted point where the two halves of a chromosome are joined).
[bio17-s3.7.4] A different, non-homologous chromosome (one that is not its partner in a pair) is written M N O • P Q R.
[bio17-s3.8.b] Two of the four types change how many copies of a section there are, one by losing it and one by repeating it.
[bio17-s3.8.0] Deletion: a section breaks off and is lost, so its genes are missing: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E H, with F and G lost.
[bio17-s3.8.1] Deletion is usually harmful, because a whole set of genes is missing.
[bio17-s3.8.2] Duplication: a section is copied twice, so its genes are repeated: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E F E F G H.
[bio17-s3.8.3] Extra copies can upset the balance of gene products (the proteins the genes make); over evolutionary time, duplicated genes can mutate to take on new functions.
[bio17-s3.9.b] The other two types move genes without necessarily losing any. An inversion turns a section around in the same chromosome; a translocation moves a section to another chromosome.
[bio17-s3.9.0] Inversion: a section breaks out, turns through 180 degrees and rejoins, so its genes are in reverse order: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D G F E H.
[bio17-s3.9.1] No genes are lost, so an inversion often has no effect on the person, unless a break falls inside a gene; it can cause problems in meiosis, leading to gametes with missing or extra genes.
[bio17-s3.9.2] Translocation: a section of one chromosome breaks off and joins a different, non-homologous chromosome; in a reciprocal translocation two chromosomes swap sections.
[bio17-s3.9.3] In a reciprocal translocation, A B C • D E F G H and M N O • P Q R become A B C • D E F Q R and M N O • P G H.
[bio17-s3.9.4] If no genes are lost the person may be healthy, but a break can join two genes into a new hybrid (fusion) gene, and carriers can produce gametes with unbalanced sets of genes.
[bio17-s3.9.5] Diagram: each of the four mutations drawn as a chromosome with lettered bands and a centromere, before and after, with the translocation shown between two chromosomes of different sizes.
[bio17-s3.10.b] Each structural type has a real disorder that illustrates it.
[bio17-s3.10.0] Deletion: cri du chat syndrome (French for “cry of the cat”) is caused by deletion of part of the short arm of chromosome 5.
[bio17-s3.10.1] Babies with cri du chat have a high-pitched cry like a cat's, a small head and low birth weight, and later intellectual disability and delayed development.
[bio17-s3.10.2] Duplication: Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A is caused by duplication of a small section of chromosome 17 containing the PMP22 gene.
[bio17-s3.10.3] The extra copy disrupts the insulating myelin sheath of nerves in the limbs, causing gradual weakness of the muscles of the feet and lower legs.
[bio17-s3.10.4] Inversion: an inversion within the gene for blood-clotting factor VIII on the X chromosome breaks the gene and causes nearly half of all cases of severe haemophilia A.
[bio17-s3.10.5] By contrast, a common inversion in chromosome 9 usually has no effect, because no genes are broken. There is more on haemophilia as a sex-linked trait in Sex-linked traits.
[bio17-s3.11.b] The Philadelphia chromosome shows how swapping pieces between chromosomes can create a gene that causes cancer.
[bio17-s3.11.0] The Philadelphia chromosome is found in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), a cancer of white blood cells.
[bio17-s3.11.1] A reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, in a bone-marrow stem cell, joins part of the ABL gene from chromosome 9 to the BCR gene on chromosome 22.
[bio17-s3.11.2] This forms a BCR-ABL fusion gene on a shortened chromosome 22, the Philadelphia chromosome.
[bio17-s3.11.3] The fusion gene codes for an enzyme that is permanently switched on and drives the cell to divide uncontrollably, so it is an oncogene (a gene that causes cancer).
[bio17-s3.11.4] CML is caused by a somatic mutation and is not inherited, because it happens in a body cell and not in a gamete-forming cell.
[bio17-s3.11.5] The Philadelphia chromosome was discovered in Philadelphia, USA, in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford; Janet Rowley showed in 1973 that it arises by translocation.
[bio17-s3.11.6] The drug imatinib blocks the BCR-ABL enzyme. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.12.b] Some conditions come from having the wrong number of chromosomes, not from a change in structure. They are therefore not examples of deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.0] Some conditions are caused by a change in the number of chromosomes, not in their structure, so they are not deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.1] They usually result from non-disjunction: a homologous pair, or two copies of a chromosome, fails to separate during meiosis, so a gamete has one chromosome too many or too few.
[bio17-s3.12.2] Down syndrome: in about 95% of cases there are three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), giving 47 chromosomes, after non-disjunction, usually during egg formation.
[bio17-s3.12.3] The risk of Down syndrome rises with the mother's age.
[bio17-s3.12.4] Features of Down syndrome include a characteristic facial appearance, some degree of intellectual disability and a higher risk of heart defects.
[bio17-s3.12.5] A few per cent of Down syndrome cases are caused instead by a translocation that attaches extra chromosome-21 material to another chromosome; only those cases involve a structural change.
[bio17-s3.13.b] Sex chromosomes can also be gained or lost, and a karyotype is the tool that shows number changes and large structural changes.
[bio17-s3.13.0] Turner syndrome is a change in number with 45 chromosomes, X: one X and no second sex chromosome, female.
[bio17-s3.13.1] Klinefelter syndrome is a change in number with 47 chromosomes, XXY, male.
[bio17-s3.13.2] A karyotype is a photograph of a cell's chromosomes, taken during mitosis when they are condensed, and arranged in homologous pairs by size and shape.
[bio17-s3.13.3] A karyotype shows changes in number (for example three chromosome 21s) and large structural changes (for example the shortened chromosome 22).
[bio17-s3.14.b] The two kinds of mutation differ in size, in how they arise, and in how they are detected. Comparing them in pairs is the quickest way to answer a distinguish question.
[bio17-s3.14.0] Scale: a point mutation changes one or a few bases in one gene; a chromosomal mutation changes the arrangement or number of copies of a large section with many genes.
[bio17-s3.14.1] How they arise: point mutations mainly from mispairing during replication; chromosomal mutations from breakage and wrong rejoining of chromosomes, often during meiosis.
[bio17-s3.14.2] Detection: point mutations need DNA sequencing (reading the order of bases), because they are too small to see; large chromosomal mutations can be seen in a karyotype under the light microscope.
[bio17-s3.14.3] Examples: sickle-cell anaemia (substitution) and cystic fibrosis (three-base deletion); cri du chat (deletion), Charcot–Marie–Tooth type 1A (duplication), severe haemophilia A (inversion), and the Philadelphia chromosome in CML (translocation).

CANDIDATES bio35-s1: Genetic engineering
[bio35-s1.0.b] Genetic engineering lets scientists give an organism a new trait by changing its DNA directly, rather than waiting for the trait to arise by breeding. Three terms name the technique, its products and the DNA it creates.
[bio35-s1.0.0] Genetic engineering (genetic modification, GM) is the deliberate change of an organism’s DNA by artificial means, usually by cutting a gene out of one organism and inserting it into the DNA of another, often a different species.
[bio35-s1.0.1] The organism produced is a genetically modified organism (GMO). One carrying a gene from another species is transgenic, which means it holds DNA that did not come from its own species.
[bio35-s1.0.2] DNA made by joining DNA from two different sources is recombinant DNA, because the pieces have been recombined into a new combination that did not exist before.
[bio35-s1.1.b] A gene from one species can work in another, and that is what separates genetic engineering from older methods of improving organisms. The technique is also quite recent.
[bio35-s1.1.0] It works because the genetic code is universal: every organism uses the same code to read DNA, so a host cell reads a gene from any species and makes the same protein.
[bio35-s1.1.1] The inserted gene must include the instructions the host needs to switch it on, so that it is transcribed (copied into mRNA) and translated (built into a protein) in the host.
[bio35-s1.1.2] Selective breeding (artificial selection, where humans choose which organisms reproduce) mixes whole sets of genes between members of the same or closely related species, and takes many generations.
[bio35-s1.1.3] Genetic engineering instead moves one chosen gene, even between unrelated species (for example bacterium to plant, or human to bacterium), in one generation. This is why selective breeding is not genetic engineering.
[bio35-s1.1.4] In 1973 Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer in the USA inserted foreign DNA into a plasmid (a small ring of DNA found in bacteria) and put it into bacteria, which copied it: the first genetically engineered organism.
[bio35-s1.1.5] Bacterial restriction enzymes (enzymes that cut DNA), discovered in the late 1960s and early 1970s, made this possible.
[bio35-s1.2.b] A gene cannot get into a host cell on its own, so it is carried in. The carrier is called a vector, and several kinds are used depending on the host.
[bio35-s1.2.0] A vector is a carrier that takes the gene into the host cell.
[bio35-s1.2.1] A plasmid is a small circle of double-stranded DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main chromosome. It copies itself independently and is passed on when the bacterium divides, so the new gene is copied too.
[bio35-s1.2.2] Plasmids are the most common vector for bacteria and yeast.
[bio35-s1.2.3] Plasmids used as vectors usually carry a marker gene (for example for resistance to an antibiotic) so that cells which have taken them up can be identified.
[bio35-s1.2.4] Modified viruses are also vectors, because viruses naturally inject their DNA into cells. Bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) are used for bacteria. Harmless modified viruses are used for human and animal cells in gene therapy and some vaccines.
[bio35-s1.2.5] Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a soil bacterium, naturally transfers part of its plasmid (the Ti plasmid) into plant cells, so it is used to modify plants.
[bio35-s1.2.6] Some methods use no vector. In microinjection, DNA is injected into a fertilised animal egg with a fine needle. A “gene gun” fires tiny metal particles coated with DNA into plant cells.
[bio35-s1.3.b] To move a gene, scientists first need a way to cut DNA at exact places. Restriction enzymes do this, and the way they cut makes it easy to join pieces back together.
[bio35-s1.3.0] Restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases) cut DNA at a specific short base sequence, the recognition site, usually 4 to 8 base pairs long.
[bio35-s1.3.1] In nature restriction enzymes protect bacteria by cutting up the DNA of invading viruses.
[bio35-s1.3.2] Many restriction enzymes cut the two strands at slightly different points, leaving short single-stranded overhangs called sticky ends. Others cut straight across, leaving blunt ends.
[bio35-s1.3.3] Worked example: the restriction enzyme EcoRI (from the bacterium Escherichia coli) recognises GAATTC and cuts between G and A on each strand: …G | AATTC… on one strand and …CTTAA | G… on the other, leaving the four-base sticky ends AATT.
[bio35-s1.3.4] Any two pieces of DNA cut with the same enzyme have complementary sticky ends, so they can base-pair (A with T, C with G). This is why the gene and the plasmid are cut with the same enzyme.
[bio35-s1.3.5] Diagram: a double strand with GAATTC/CTTAAG, the staggered cut shown, and the two fragments with their AATT overhangs labelled “sticky end”.
[bio35-s1.4.b] Sticky ends hold two fragments together only weakly. A second enzyme is needed to make the join permanent.
[bio35-s1.4.0] DNA ligase is the enzyme that joins DNA fragments by re-forming the sugar–phosphate backbone (the long chain on the outside of each DNA strand) where the sticky ends have paired.
[bio35-s1.4.1] The sticky ends pair by hydrogen bonds between bases, which are weak. DNA ligase makes the strong backbone bonds, so it is sometimes called “molecular glue”.
[bio35-s1.5.b] Making a recombinant plasmid has three stages: get the DNA, cut it, then join the gene into the plasmid. Human insulin is the standard example.
[bio35-s1.5.0] 1. DNA isolation: the donor DNA containing the gene is extracted from the donor cells (for example human cells for the insulin gene). Separately, plasmids are extracted from bacteria by breaking the cells open.
[bio35-s1.5.1] 2. Cutting: the donor DNA and the plasmid are cut with the same restriction enzyme.
[bio35-s1.5.2] This cuts the gene out of the donor DNA and opens the plasmid ring once, so both have matching sticky ends.
[bio35-s1.5.3] 3. Ligation: the gene and the opened plasmids are mixed. Their complementary sticky ends base-pair, and DNA ligase seals the backbone, giving a recombinant plasmid that contains the new gene.
[bio35-s1.6.b] The recombinant plasmid now has to get into bacteria and be put to work. The bacteria then become small factories for the protein.
[bio35-s1.6.0] 4. Transformation: bacteria take up the recombinant plasmid from their surroundings. Transformation means a cell taking up and keeping foreign DNA.
[bio35-s1.6.1] In the laboratory, uptake is increased by treating the bacteria with calcium chloride and a brief heat shock.
[bio35-s1.6.2] Only a small fraction of bacteria take up a plasmid, so they are selected. They are grown on agar containing the antibiotic, and only bacteria carrying the plasmid’s resistance marker gene survive.
[bio35-s1.6.3] 5. Expression: the transformed bacteria are cloned, each dividing repeatedly by binary fission (splitting in two) to produce millions of identical copies, each with the plasmid.
[bio35-s1.6.4] The bacteria transcribe and translate the inserted gene and make the protein; the gene is “expressed”.
[bio35-s1.6.5] They are grown in large numbers in a fermenter (bioreactor, a large vessel for growing microorganisms), and the protein is extracted and purified.
[bio35-s1.6.6] Diagram: flow chart: donor cell → gene cut out by restriction enzyme; bacterium → plasmid cut by the same enzyme; gene + plasmid joined by DNA ligase → recombinant plasmid; recombinant plasmid taken up by a bacterium (transformation); bacteria multiply and make the protein (expression).
[bio35-s1.6.7] Diagram labels: donor DNA, gene, restriction enzyme, sticky ends, plasmid, DNA ligase, recombinant plasmid, host bacterium, marker gene.
[bio35-s1.7.b] Bacteria, yeast and other cells that carry a human gene can make human proteins cheaply and in large amounts.
[bio35-s1.7.0] Human insulin: the human insulin gene is inserted into bacteria (or yeast), which make human insulin for people with type 1 diabetes.
[bio35-s1.7.1] In 1982 human insulin became the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use.
[bio35-s1.7.2] Advantages over insulin from cattle and pig pancreases: it is identical to human insulin, so there are fewer allergic reactions; the supply is unlimited and consistent; and no animal slaughter is needed, avoiding religious and vegetarian objections.
[bio35-s1.7.3] Other medicines are made by genetically engineered cells, such as human growth hormone, for children who lack it.
[bio35-s1.7.4] Clotting factor VIII for people with haemophilia A (a condition where blood does not clot properly) is made by genetically engineered mammalian cells grown in culture, which removes the risk of infection from donated blood.
[bio35-s1.7.5] The hepatitis B vaccine is made from a viral protein produced by genetically modified yeast.
[bio35-s1.7.6] Many industrial and food enzymes are made by genetically modified microorganisms carrying the gene for the enzyme, for example chymosin for cheese-making, replacing rennet from calves’ stomachs, and enzymes in biological detergents.
[bio35-s1.8.b] Plants are modified to protect themselves from pests, to tolerate weed-killers and to carry more nutrients. Each example below inserts a gene from another organism.
[bio35-s1.8.0] Insect-resistant crops: a gene from the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for a protein toxic to certain insect larvae (for example caterpillars and corn borers) is inserted into maize or cotton, giving Bt maize and Bt cotton.
[bio35-s1.8.1] Larvae that eat the plant die, so farmers spray less insecticide. The protein is harmless to mammals, birds and most other insects.
[bio35-s1.8.2] Herbicide-tolerant crops: a gene for resistance to a herbicide (weed-killer) such as glyphosate is inserted into crops such as soya and maize, so the field can be sprayed to kill weeds without harming the crop.
[bio35-s1.8.3] Improved nutrition: Golden Rice has genes from other organisms inserted so that its grain makes beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A. The beta-carotene gives the rice its yellow colour.
[bio35-s1.8.4] Golden Rice was developed to reduce vitamin A deficiency, a cause of childhood blindness and death where rice is the main food. It was approved for growing in the Philippines in 2021.
[bio35-s1.8.5] The EU regulates GM crops strictly. The only GM crop grown commercially in the EU is a Bt maize, grown mostly in Spain.
[bio35-s1.8.6] No GM crops are grown commercially in Ireland, but GM soya and maize are imported, mainly for animal feed.
[bio35-s1.9.b] Genes can also be added to animals, and to human cells to treat disease. The effect depends on which cells are changed.
[bio35-s1.9.0] GM animals: AquAdvantage salmon carries a growth-hormone gene from another salmon species (the Chinook salmon), so it grows to market size faster.
[bio35-s1.9.1] AquAdvantage salmon was approved for food in the USA in 2015, the first GM animal approved for food there.
[bio35-s1.9.2] GM animals, especially mice, are also used in medical research as models of human diseases.
[bio35-s1.9.3] Gene therapy: a working copy of a gene is inserted into a patient’s own cells to treat a genetic disease caused by a faulty allele (version of a gene), usually using a harmless modified virus as the vector.
[bio35-s1.9.4] Somatic gene therapy changes body cells only, so the change is not inherited.
[bio35-s1.9.5] Germline changes (in eggs, sperm or embryos) would be inherited by the patient’s children, and are not allowed in most countries.
[bio35-s1.10.b] Plasmid methods add a new gene. Gene editing is a newer approach that changes a gene exactly where it already sits in the genome (all of an organism’s DNA).
[bio35-s1.10.0] CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing tool adapted from a system that bacteria use to defend themselves against viruses.
[bio35-s1.10.1] A short guide RNA, made to be complementary to the target DNA sequence, leads the enzyme Cas9 to that exact place in the genome, where Cas9 cuts both DNA strands.
[bio35-s1.10.2] When the cell repairs the cut, the gene can be switched off, or a corrected sequence supplied by scientists can be built in.
[bio35-s1.10.3] Unlike the earlier plasmid method, which adds a new gene carried in a vector, CRISPR changes a gene in its own place in the organism’s DNA (editing). It is faster, cheaper and more precise.
[bio35-s1.10.4] Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing it.
[bio35-s1.10.5] In 2023 the UK became the first country to approve a CRISPR-based treatment, for sickle-cell disease and beta thalassaemia. The patient’s own blood stem cells are edited outside the body and returned.
[bio35-s1.11.b] The uses of genetic engineering fall into three themes, which is a useful way to organise an answer.
[bio35-s1.11.0] Health: medicines (insulin, growth hormone, clotting factors, vaccines), gene therapy and gene editing.
[bio35-s1.11.1] Sustainability: crops needing less insecticide, nutrition-enhanced crops, and enzymes replacing harsh chemical processes.
[bio35-s1.11.2] Technology: genetic engineering is a core biotechnology (the use of living organisms to make useful products).
