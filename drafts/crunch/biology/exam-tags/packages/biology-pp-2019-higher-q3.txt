CANDIDATES bio5-s2: Levels of organisation
[bio5-s2.0.b] Living things are organised in levels of increasing complexity. Each level is made of units of the level below working together, so a whole organism can be understood by building up from its cells.
[bio5-s2.0.0] The order is cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Learn it as a ladder: each step up combines the units of the step below.
[bio5-s2.0.1] Below the cell, cells are built of organelles (small structures inside the cell, each with a job), which are built of biomolecules (e.g. proteins, lipids). There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
[bio5-s2.0.2] Cell: the basic unit of life. In a multicellular organism (one made of many cells) cells are specialised (differentiated), so that the structure of each cell suits one job.
[bio5-s2.0.3] Tissue: a group of similar cells with the same structure, working together to carry out a particular function. Because the cells are alike, the tissue does one job well.
[bio5-s2.0.4] Organ: a structure made of several different tissues working together to carry out one or more functions. Having different tissues is what separates an organ from a tissue.
[bio5-s2.0.5] Organ system: a group of organs working together to carry out a major function, such as digesting food.
[bio5-s2.0.6] Organism: an individual living thing, whose organ systems work together to keep it alive.
[bio5-s2.0.7] Diagram: a five-step flow chart from cell to organism, with a human example at each level (muscle cell → muscle tissue → stomach → digestive system → human) and a plant example (xylem vessel → vascular tissue → leaf → shoot system → plant).
[bio5-s2.1.b] A specialised cell has a structure that suits its particular job. Cells in a multicellular organism are specialised, and each example below links a feature of the cell to its function.
[bio5-s2.1.0] Unicellular organisms (those made of a single cell), e.g. Amoeba, yeast and bacteria, carry out every life process in one cell, so that cell is not specialised for a single job.
[bio5-s2.1.1] Differentiation: in a multicellular organism, all body cells contain the same DNA, but each cell type uses (expresses) only some of its genes, so cells become specialised.
[bio5-s2.1.2] Unspecialised cells that can still become several cell types are called stem cells. There is more on their uses in Genetic Engineering.
[bio5-s2.1.3] Red blood cell: no nucleus and a biconcave disc shape (a disc dipped in on both faces), giving more room for haemoglobin (the pigment that carries oxygen) and a large surface area for carrying oxygen.
[bio5-s2.1.4] Nerve cell (neuron): long thin extensions carry impulses (electrical signals) over long distances quickly, which suits a cell that links distant parts of the body.
[bio5-s2.1.5] Sperm cell: a tail for swimming, and many mitochondria in the middle section to release the energy that powers the tail.
[bio5-s2.1.6] Root hair cell: a long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water and minerals from the soil.
[bio5-s2.1.7] Palisade cell of a leaf: packed with chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens) for photosynthesis. Guard cells change shape to open and close the stomata (the pores in the leaf surface).
[bio5-s2.2.b] Animal bodies are built from four main tissue types. Each is a group of similar cells doing one kind of job.
[bio5-s2.2.0] Epithelial tissue lines and covers surfaces, e.g. the lining of the gut and the skin surface.
[bio5-s2.2.1] Connective tissue supports and connects other tissues, e.g. bone, cartilage and blood. Blood counts as a connective tissue.
[bio5-s2.2.2] Muscle tissue contracts (shortens) to cause movement. There are three types: skeletal, smooth and cardiac (heart muscle).
[bio5-s2.2.3] Nervous tissue carries impulses, e.g. in the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
[bio5-s2.3.b] Plants also have tissue types, each a group of similar cells with one job. Together they build plant organs such as the leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.0] Dermal tissue is the epidermis, a protective outer layer covering the plant.
[bio5-s2.3.1] Vascular tissue carries materials around the plant: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries food.
[bio5-s2.3.2] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant, carrying out photosynthesis and storage, e.g. the mesophyll (the inner tissue) of a leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.3] Meristematic tissue consists of dividing cells at root and shoot tips, where growth happens, so new cells for growth are made there.
[bio5-s2.3.4] A leaf contains dermal, ground and vascular tissue, which shows that an organ is made of several tissue types. Plant organs are root, stem, leaf and flower.
[bio5-s2.4.b] Organs are made of several tissue types, and organs that cooperate form organ systems. Each system has one major function.
[bio5-s2.4.0] The stomach is an organ containing epithelial tissue lining it, smooth muscle that churns food, connective tissue and nervous tissue. Four different tissues working together make it an organ.
[bio5-s2.4.1] Other human organs include the heart, lungs, kidneys and brain.
[bio5-s2.4.2] Digestive system: breaks down and absorbs food. Circulatory system: transports materials around the body.
[bio5-s2.4.3] Breathing system: gas exchange (taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide). Nervous system: fast coordination.
[bio5-s2.4.4] Endocrine system: hormonal coordination. Musculoskeletal system: support and movement.
[bio5-s2.4.5] Urinary system: excretion (removing wastes) and water balance. Reproductive system: reproduction. Immune system: defence.
[bio5-s2.4.6] Plants have two systems. The root system anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals.
[bio5-s2.4.7] The shoot system (stem, leaves and flowers) carries out photosynthesis, transport and reproduction.
[bio5-s2.5.b] Size changes how a living thing gets what it needs. The bigger an organism, the harder it is to supply every cell, which is why large organisms need specialised systems.
[bio5-s2.5.0] As an organism gets bigger, its volume increases faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio falls.
[bio5-s2.5.1] Worked example: a 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm² and a volume of 1 cm³, a ratio of 6:1. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of 24 cm² and a volume of 8 cm³, a ratio of 3:1.
[bio5-s2.5.2] A unicellular organism has a large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane is enough to supply oxygen and food and to remove wastes.
[bio5-s2.5.3] In a large multicellular organism most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow to reach them.
[bio5-s2.5.4] Multicellular organisms therefore need specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gut lining, root hairs, leaves) to take in materials, and transport systems (blood and heart; xylem and phloem) to carry them to every cell.
[bio5-s2.5.5] They also need coordination systems (nervous and hormonal) that link all the parts and keep conditions constant (homeostasis).
[bio5-s2.5.6] Division of labour: each cell type does one job very efficiently, but no cell can survive alone, so the cells depend on each other.
[bio5-s2.6.b] Being made of many specialised cells brings benefits, but it also has costs. Comparing the two shows why both unicellular and multicellular life exist.
[bio5-s2.6.0] Advantage: a larger size, which means fewer predators and access to light or food.
[bio5-s2.6.1] Advantage: cells inside the body are protected from the environment, because they are surrounded by other cells.
[bio5-s2.6.2] Advantage: efficiency from specialisation, since each cell type does one job well.
[bio5-s2.6.3] Advantage: the organism can survive the death of individual cells, which are replaced.
[bio5-s2.6.4] Disadvantage: more energy and resources are needed to build and maintain the body.
[bio5-s2.6.5] Disadvantage: damage to one vital organ or system can kill the whole organism, because the other cells depend on it.
[bio5-s2.6.6] Disadvantage: growth to reproductive age takes longer than for unicellular organisms, which can divide within hours.
