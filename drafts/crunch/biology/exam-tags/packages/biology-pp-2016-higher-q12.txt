CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio20-s1.10.5] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; oestrogen; female secondary sexual characteristics and thickening of the endometrium.
[bio20-s1.10.6] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; progesterone; maintains the endometrium and pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio26-s1: The urinary system
[bio26-s1.0.b] Every cell produces waste as it carries out its chemical reactions, and some of that waste is harmful if it stays in the body. The kidneys are the main organs that clear it away, and they do a second job at the same time: keeping the body's fluids balanced.
[bio26-s1.0.0] Excretion is the removal of the waste products of metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells). Faeces are not excreted: they are undigested food that was never absorbed, so passing them out is egestion.
[bio26-s1.0.1] The kidneys excrete urea, excess water, excess salts and excess hydrogen ions in urine. Other organs help: the lungs excrete carbon dioxide, and sweat removes some water, salts and a little urea.
[bio26-s1.0.2] Urea is made in the liver from excess amino acids (the units of proteins) by deamination, the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of an amino acid. It is carried dissolved in the blood plasma to the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.0.3] Urea is toxic if it builds up, so it must be removed continuously rather than in occasional bursts.
[bio26-s1.0.4] Transport between environments: blood (the internal environment) carries wastes to the kidneys, the kidneys transfer them from the blood into urine, and urine is passed out of the body (the external environment).
[bio26-s1.0.5] At the same time the kidneys return useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most of the water and most of the salts) to the blood, so nutrients are not lost in the urine.
[bio26-s1.0.6] The kidneys have two roles: excretion (removing urea and other wastes), and osmoregulation and homeostasis, which regulate the volume, water content, salt concentration and pH of the body fluids.
[bio26-s1.0.7] Both roles use the same two processes: filtration of the blood (squeezing small molecules out of it), then reabsorption (taking back what the body needs). What is not reabsorbed is urine.
[bio26-s1.1.b] The kidneys sit well protected at the back of the body and are supplied directly by a large artery. That position and supply let them filter a large volume of blood at high pressure.
[bio26-s1.1.0] The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs at the back of the abdomen, one on each side of the vertebral column (backbone), just below the diaphragm. The lowest ribs partly protect them.
[bio26-s1.1.1] The right kidney is slightly lower than the left because the liver lies above it and takes up space.
[bio26-s1.1.2] Each kidney is embedded in a layer of fat that cushions it against physical damage and helps hold it in place, and is covered by a tough outer membrane, the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.1.3] The adrenal glands (small hormone-producing glands) sit on top of the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.1.4] Each kidney receives blood from a renal artery, a short wide branch of the aorta (the body's main artery), so blood arrives at high pressure and in large volume, which suits filtration.
[bio26-s1.1.5] Filtered blood leaves by the renal vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart).
[bio26-s1.1.6] Blood in the renal vein has less urea, less oxygen and less glucose than blood in the renal artery, and more carbon dioxide because the kidney cells respire. It is the blood vessel with the lowest urea concentration in the body.
[bio26-s1.1.7] Diagram: front view of the urinary system. Label two kidneys, renal arteries from the aorta, renal veins to the vena cava, ureters, bladder, sphincter muscles and urethra. The adrenal glands on top of the kidneys may be shown.
[bio26-s1.2.b] Once urine has formed it must be carried away, stored and released at a suitable moment. Three structures do this in turn: the ureters carry it, the bladder stores it and the urethra lets it out.
[bio26-s1.2.0] There are two ureters: narrow muscular tubes, one from the renal pelvis (the funnel-shaped collecting space inside each kidney) down to the bladder.
[bio26-s1.2.1] The ureter walls contain smooth muscle that moves urine by peristalsis (waves of contraction), so urine reaches the bladder whatever the body position, not just by gravity.
[bio26-s1.2.2] The urinary bladder is a hollow muscular bag in the lower abdomen (pelvis) that stores urine.
[bio26-s1.2.3] The bladder wall is elastic and folded, so it can stretch to hold a large volume and contract to empty.
[bio26-s1.2.4] Two rings of sphincter muscle (muscle that closes an opening) at the bladder outlet keep it closed: an inner one that works automatically (involuntary), and an outer one under conscious (voluntary) control, which young children learn to control.
[bio26-s1.2.5] The urethra is the single tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
[bio26-s1.2.6] In males the urethra is longer, runs through the penis and also carries semen. In females it is short and opens just in front of the vagina.
[bio26-s1.3.b] Cut a kidney lengthways and you can see that it is not uniform inside. The three regions each have a different role in making and collecting urine.
[bio26-s1.3.0] A kidney cut lengthways shows three regions: cortex, medulla and renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.3.1] Cortex: the dark outer layer, under the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.3.2] Medulla: the paler inner layer, divided into cone-shaped renal pyramids.
[bio26-s1.3.3] Renal pelvis: a funnel-shaped space on the inner (concave) side that collects urine and leads into the ureter. No filtration happens in the pelvis and urine is not changed there, so it is only a collecting space.
[bio26-s1.4.b] The real work of the kidney is done by microscopic tubes called nephrons. Each one first filters the blood, then takes back what the body needs, and the leftover liquid becomes urine.
[bio26-s1.4.0] Each kidney contains about one million nephrons, microscopic tubules that are the kidney's filtering units, so together they give a huge filtering surface. Each runs from the cortex down into the medulla and back.
[bio26-s1.4.1] The nephron tubules drain into collecting ducts that run through the pyramids and open into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.4.2] Filtration takes place in the cortex: blood is forced at high pressure through knots of capillaries (glomeruli) into the cup-shaped tops of the nephrons (Bowman's capsules).
[bio26-s1.4.3] Small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea) are filtered out of the blood. Blood cells and plasma proteins are too large, so they stay in the blood. The filtered liquid is called the filtrate.
[bio26-s1.4.4] Reabsorption (taking useful substances back into the blood) takes place in the cortex, in the coiled tubules, and in the medulla, in the loops of Henle and the collecting ducts.
[bio26-s1.4.5] All the glucose and amino acids, most of the salts and most of the water are taken back into the blood. The remaining liquid, carrying urea and excess salts and water, is urine.
[bio26-s1.4.6] Diagram: longitudinal section of a kidney. Label renal capsule, cortex, medulla, renal pyramid, renal pelvis, ureter, renal artery and renal vein. Draw one nephron from cortex into medulla to show where filtration and reabsorption happen.
[bio26-s1.5.b] Urine is what is left of the filtrate once the body has taken back what it needs. Its volume and concentration change from hour to hour, which is how the kidneys keep the water content of the blood steady.
[bio26-s1.5.0] Urine is mostly water, with urea, salts (mainly sodium chloride) and other wastes dissolved in it.
[bio26-s1.5.1] Healthy urine contains no protein and no glucose, because protein is not filtered out of the blood and all the glucose is reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.5.2] After drinking a lot of water, the kidneys produce a large volume of dilute (pale) urine, which gets rid of the extra water.
[bio26-s1.5.3] When water is short (little drinking, heavy sweating in exercise or hot weather, or diarrhoea), a small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine is produced, so the body saves water.
[bio26-s1.5.4] The control is by the hormone ADH. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation; here you only need the effect on urine.
[bio26-s1.6.b] Water is not the only thing the kidneys balance. They also adjust salts and acidity, and together this keeps the body's internal conditions steady.
[bio26-s1.6.0] Salt balance: after a salty meal the kidneys excrete more salt, and when salt is short they reabsorb more.
[bio26-s1.6.1] Keeping the salt and water concentration of the blood steady stops body cells from gaining or losing water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio26-s1.6.2] pH: the kidneys remove excess hydrogen ions from the blood in urine, helping to keep blood pH at about 7.4. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio26-s1.6.3] This is homeostasis: the kidneys keep the internal environment constant whatever is eaten or drunk, much as a thermostat holds a room at one temperature. This works by negative feedback, which is taught in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio26-s1.7.b] Urine is made all the time, but it is passed out only now and then. This sequence shows how it travels and why release can be delayed.
[bio26-s1.7.0] 1. Urine forms continuously in the nephrons and drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.7.1] 2. Peristalsis in the ureter carries it to the bladder in small spurts.
[bio26-s1.7.2] 3. The bladder fills and its wall stretches. Stretch receptors in the wall send nerve impulses that give the urge to urinate.
[bio26-s1.7.3] 4. To urinate, the bladder's muscular wall contracts and the sphincters relax. The outer sphincter relaxes under voluntary control, which is learned after early childhood.
[bio26-s1.7.4] 5. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.
[bio26-s1.8.b] Each part of the urinary system has features that suit its job. Examiners ask you to link structure to function, so learn these as pairs.
[bio26-s1.8.0] Fat layer around each kidney: protects it from physical damage.
[bio26-s1.8.1] Renal artery branching directly from the aorta: delivers high-pressure blood for filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.2] About one million nephrons: a huge filtering surface.
[bio26-s1.8.3] Cortex full of capillary knots: the site of filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.4] Long tubules and loops in the medulla: the site of reabsorption.
[bio26-s1.8.5] Funnel-shaped renal pelvis: collects urine.
[bio26-s1.8.6] Muscular ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristalsis.
[bio26-s1.8.7] Elastic bladder with sphincters: stores urine and allows controlled release. The urethra is the exit for urine from the body.
[bio26-s1.9.b] Because healthy urine contains no glucose or protein, finding either one in a urine sample tells a doctor that something is wrong.
[bio26-s1.9.0] Glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes, because blood glucose is so high that not all of it can be reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.9.1] Protein in urine is a sign of damage to the kidney's filters, because healthy filters do not let protein through.
[bio26-s1.9.2] Urine tests are also used to detect drugs, for example in sport.
[bio26-s1.10.b] If the kidneys stop working, the body can no longer clear its wastes or balance its fluids. There are two treatments: a machine that does the kidney's job, or a replacement kidney.
[bio26-s1.10.0] If the kidneys stop working, urea, salts and water build up in the blood and the person will die without treatment.
[bio26-s1.10.1] Treatment is by dialysis or by a kidney transplant.
[bio26-s1.10.2] A transplant gives a working kidney, but the patient must take drugs to stop the immune system rejecting it, because the body treats the new kidney as foreign. There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio26-s1.11.b] A dialysis machine copies the kidney's filtering. It uses diffusion across a membrane to clean the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.0] The patient's blood is pumped through a machine where it flows past a selectively permeable membrane (one that lets only some substances through), with dialysis fluid on the other side.
[bio26-s1.11.1] Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid, because their concentration is higher in the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.2] The fluid contains glucose and salts at normal blood concentrations, so these are not lost from the blood. Blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane.
[bio26-s1.11.3] Dialysis must be repeated several times a week, because it does not work continuously the way healthy kidneys do.

CANDIDATES bio26-s3: ADH and osmoregulation
[bio26-s3.0.b] The water content of the body is never constant on its own: we take water in and lose it all day. Osmoregulation (keeping the water and solute balance of the body fluids steady) stops these swings from harming our cells.
[bio26-s3.0.0] Osmoregulation in humans is mainly done by the kidneys, which change how much water is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood) from the collecting ducts, the final tubes that carry urine out of the kidney. The variable being controlled is the concentration of the blood plasma.
[bio26-s3.0.1] Water is gained from drink, food and respiration, and lost in urine, sweat, breath (as water vapour) and faeces. Gains and losses change from hour to hour, so the kidney must adjust the urine to match.
[bio26-s3.0.2] If the blood becomes too concentrated, body cells lose water by osmosis (the movement of water from a dilute region to a more concentrated one) and shrink. If it becomes too dilute, cells gain water and swell. Either upsets cell function, especially in the brain.
[bio26-s3.1.b] The kidney needs a message telling it how much water to save. That message is a hormone, ADH, which changes how leaky the collecting duct wall is to water.
[bio26-s3.1.0] ADH stands for antidiuretic hormone. Diuresis is the production of a large volume of dilute urine, so ADH works against it and reduces urine volume. ADH is released from the pituitary gland.
[bio26-s3.1.1] The target cells (the cells that respond to the hormone) line the collecting ducts and the last part of the distal convoluted tubule.
[bio26-s3.1.2] ADH binds to receptors on these cells, which causes water channel proteins, called aquaporins, to be inserted into their membranes. This makes the duct wall more permeable to water.
[bio26-s3.1.3] The collecting duct passes through the salty medulla (the inner region of the kidney), so water moves out of the more permeable duct by osmosis into the medulla and is carried away in the blood capillaries.
[bio26-s3.1.4] More ADH means more water reabsorbed, less water left in the duct, and a small volume of concentrated urine.
[bio26-s3.1.5] Less ADH means fewer water channels, so the duct wall is less permeable and less water is reabsorbed. A large volume of dilute urine is the result.
[bio26-s3.1.6] ADH changes water reabsorption only. It does not change the amount of salt or urea (the waste made from the breakdown of proteins) excreted. There is more on channel proteins in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio26-s3.2.b] When the blood becomes too concentrated, the body responds in a fixed sequence that saves water and brings the concentration back to normal. Follow the steps in order.
[bio26-s3.2.0] 1. Stimulus: the water concentration of the blood falls (its solute concentration rises), for example after heavy sweating, not drinking enough, eating salty food, or diarrhoea.
[bio26-s3.2.1] 2. Receptors: osmoreceptors, special cells in the hypothalamus of the brain that detect the concentration of the blood, notice the change.
[bio26-s3.2.2] 3. The hypothalamus causes the pituitary gland to release more ADH into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.3] 4. ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys.
[bio26-s3.2.4] 5. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.5] 6. A small volume of concentrated, dark-yellow urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.2.6] 7. The water concentration of the blood rises back towards normal, and ADH release falls again. This is negative feedback, because the response reverses the original change.
[bio26-s3.2.7] At the same time the hypothalamus triggers thirst, so the person drinks more. Drinking is the only way to replace lost water, because the kidneys can only reduce further loss.
[bio26-s3.3.b] The same system works in reverse when the blood becomes too dilute. The kidney then lets more water go, so the concentration returns to normal.
[bio26-s3.3.0] 1. Stimulus: after drinking a large volume of water, the water concentration of the blood rises (it becomes more dilute).
[bio26-s3.3.1] 2. The hypothalamus detects the change.
[bio26-s3.3.2] 3. The pituitary releases less ADH.
[bio26-s3.3.3] 4. The collecting ducts become less permeable to water, because fewer water channels are present in their walls.
[bio26-s3.3.4] 5. Less water is reabsorbed, so more stays in the duct.
[bio26-s3.3.5] 6. A large volume of dilute, pale urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.3.6] 7. The blood's water concentration falls back to normal and ADH release returns to its usual level.
[bio26-s3.4.b] Everyday situations change the concentration of the blood, and ADH release changes with them. Each example below follows the same chain from blood concentration to urine volume.
[bio26-s3.4.0] On a hot day or during exercise, much water is lost in sweat, so more ADH is released and the urine is small in volume and dark.
[bio26-s3.4.1] A high salt intake raises the solute concentration of the blood, so more ADH is released, more water is reabsorbed and a smaller volume of urine is produced. The extra salt is excreted in this smaller volume, and the person also feels thirsty.
[bio26-s3.4.2] Alcohol reduces the release of ADH, so the collecting ducts reabsorb less water and a large volume of dilute urine is produced. This causes dehydration (loss of body water), which contributes to the headache and thirst of a hangover.
[bio26-s3.5.b] The two feedback responses can be drawn as one negative-feedback loop around the normal blood water concentration. Drawing it shows that every change is pushed back towards normal.
[bio26-s3.5.0] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop for water balance, with normal blood water concentration at the centre. One branch shows the water concentration falling, the hypothalamus detecting it, the pituitary releasing more ADH, the collecting ducts becoming more permeable, more water being reabsorbed, a small volume of concentrated urine being produced, and the concentration returning to normal. The other branch shows the water concentration rising, less ADH, less permeable collecting ducts, less water reabsorbed, a large volume of dilute urine, and a return to normal. CANDIDATES bio27-s1: The digestive system
[bio27-s1.0.b] Humans can't make their own food, so every meal has to be taken in, broken down and moved into the body before cells can use it. This happens in four stages: ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.
[bio27-s1.0.0] Humans are heterotrophs, meaning they obtain food from other organisms rather than making it. They are also omnivores: they eat both plant and animal material.
[bio27-s1.0.1] Ingestion is taking food into the body through the mouth, which is where every meal enters the gut.
[bio27-s1.0.2] Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. It is both mechanical (physical breakdown into smaller pieces) and chemical (hydrolysis, the splitting of large molecules by enzymes).
[bio27-s1.0.3] Digestion is necessary because large molecules such as starch, proteins and fats cannot pass through the wall of the gut or through cell membranes. It converts them to small soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.
[bio27-s1.0.4] Cells then use the absorbed molecules to build the body's own molecules or for respiration. This use of absorbed molecules is called assimilation: for example, amino acids are built into proteins and glucose is respired to release energy.
[bio27-s1.0.5] Absorption is the passage of the small soluble products of digestion, together with water, vitamins and minerals, through the wall of the gut into the blood or lymph. It happens mainly in the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.0.6] Egestion is the removal of undigested and unabsorbed material, mainly dietary fibre, as faeces through the anus. This material is left over because the body cannot digest or absorb it.
[bio27-s1.0.7] Egestion is not excretion. Faeces were never part of the body's metabolism (the chemical reactions inside cells), except for bile pigments, whereas excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells.
[bio27-s1.1.b] The alimentary canal, or gut, is the pathway food follows through the body. Its muscular wall and lining are built to move food along and to protect the gut itself.
[bio27-s1.1.0] The alimentary canal, or gut, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. Food passes in order through the mouth, pharynx (throat), oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus.
[bio27-s1.1.1] The gut wall contains two layers of smooth muscle, circular and longitudinal, which move food along by peristalsis (waves of muscle contraction that push food onwards).
[bio27-s1.1.2] The inner lining of the gut makes mucus. Mucus lubricates the food so it slides along, and protects the lining from digestive enzymes and acid, which would otherwise damage the gut's own cells.
[bio27-s1.1.3] Sphincters are rings of muscle that control movement between parts of the gut, for example at the entrance and exit of the stomach (the exit one is the pyloric sphincter) and at the anus.
[bio27-s1.2.b] The first three organs prepare food and carry it onwards. The mouth breaks it up and starts starch digestion, the oesophagus transports it, and the stomach stores and churns it while starting protein digestion.
[bio27-s1.2.0] The mouth is where food is ingested and chewed by the teeth, then mixed with saliva from three pairs of salivary glands. The tongue shapes it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. Starch digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.1] The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries food by peristalsis from the pharynx, down through the chest behind the trachea (windpipe) and through the diaphragm (the sheet of muscle below the lungs), to the stomach. No digestion or absorption happens in it.
[bio27-s1.2.2] During swallowing the epiglottis (a flap) covers the entrance to the trachea, so food goes down the oesophagus and not into the lungs.
[bio27-s1.2.3] The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the upper left of the abdomen, just below the diaphragm. It stores food for a few hours, which means a meal can be eaten quickly and released to the intestine gradually.
[bio27-s1.2.4] The stomach churns food with gastric juice (acid and a protease, an enzyme that digests protein) into a thick liquid called chyme. The acid kills most bacteria in food, and protein digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.5] The pyloric sphincter at the stomach's exit releases chyme a little at a time into the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.3.b] After the stomach, chyme enters the small intestine, where digestion is finished and most absorption happens. What is left passes to the large intestine, which recovers water and stores the waste until it leaves the body.
[bio27-s1.3.0] The small intestine is the longest part of the gut, coiled in the abdomen. Its first part, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas, and most chemical digestion happens there.
[bio27-s1.3.1] The later parts, the jejunum and the ileum, complete digestion and absorb most digested food and water through millions of villi (tiny finger-like projections of the lining that give a large surface for absorption).
[bio27-s1.3.2] The large intestine is wider but shorter than the small intestine; "large" refers to its width, not its length. Its parts are the caecum, with the appendix attached, the colon and the rectum.
[bio27-s1.3.3] The appendix is a small dead-end tube with no digestive role in humans, so food is not digested in it.
[bio27-s1.3.4] The colon absorbs water and some minerals from the remaining material, and is home to many bacteria. It is not an excretory organ, because what leaves is egested, not excreted.
[bio27-s1.3.5] The rectum is the last part of the large intestine, which stores faeces until egestion, so waste does not have to leave the body continuously.
[bio27-s1.3.6] The anus is the opening at the end of the gut, closed by sphincter muscles. The outer sphincter is under voluntary control, so egestion can be delayed until it is convenient.
[bio27-s1.4.b] Some organs help digestion without food ever entering them. They make secretions that reach the gut through ducts (narrow tubes), so they are called associated organs.
[bio27-s1.4.0] The salivary glands are three pairs, around the mouth and below the jaw, releasing saliva into the mouth through ducts. Saliva contains water, mucus and the enzyme salivary amylase.
[bio27-s1.4.1] The liver is the largest internal organ, in the upper right of the abdomen just below the diaphragm. Its digestive role is to make bile.
[bio27-s1.4.2] The liver also receives the absorbed products of digestion through the hepatic portal vein (the vein carrying blood from the gut to the liver), stores glycogen and deaminates amino acids (removes their nitrogen-containing part).
[bio27-s1.4.3] The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile. It squeezes bile through the bile duct into the duodenum when fatty food arrives there. It makes no bile itself.
[bio27-s1.4.4] The pancreas is a soft, pinkish gland behind the stomach, lying in the curve of the duodenum. It makes pancreatic juice, which contains enzymes for starch, protein and fat and sodium hydrogencarbonate, and this flows through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum.
[bio27-s1.4.5] The pancreas is also an endocrine gland (a ductless gland that releases hormones into the blood) making insulin, so it has both ducted (exocrine) and ductless (endocrine) roles.
[bio27-s1.4.6] The liver, gallbladder and pancreas are associated organs: food never passes through them, and their secretions reach the gut through ducts. Food does pass through the mouth, stomach and intestines.
[bio27-s1.5.b] A labelled diagram shows how the gut and its associated organs sit in relation to each other. Getting the positions right matters as much as the labels.
[bio27-s1.5.0] Diagram: alimentary canal and associated glands, front view, as one continuous tube. Labels: mouth, salivary glands, tongue, oesophagus, diaphragm, liver (above stomach, body's right), gallbladder (under liver), bile duct, stomach, pyloric sphincter, pancreas (in duodenum's loop), pancreatic duct, duodenum, ileum, caecum, appendix, colon, rectum, anus.
[bio27-s1.6.b] Following one mouthful from start to finish pulls the stages of nutrition together. Each part of the gut has its own main job.
[bio27-s1.6.0] Mouth: ingestion; mechanical and chemical digestion begin.
[bio27-s1.6.1] Oesophagus: transport of food to the stomach, because the mouth and stomach are too far apart for food to move unaided.
[bio27-s1.6.2] Stomach: storage and churning; protein digestion begins.
[bio27-s1.6.3] Duodenum: most chemical digestion, with bile and pancreatic juice.
[bio27-s1.6.4] Ileum: digestion is completed and absorption takes place.
[bio27-s1.6.5] Colon: absorption of water, so the remaining waste becomes more solid.
[bio27-s1.6.6] Rectum: storage of faeces.
[bio27-s1.6.7] Anus: egestion, the removal of faeces from the body.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio30-s2: Transport of water and minerals
[bio30-s2.0.b] Water moves in one continuous flow, from the soil through the plant and out into the air. This flow is the transpiration stream, and its first part carries water from the soil into the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.0] Overview: water moves from the soil into the root hairs (tiny outgrowths of root cells that give a large surface for absorption), across the root cortex (the layer of cells between the surface and the centre), into the xylem (the water-carrying tubes), up the stem and into the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.1] It then leaves the leaves as water vapour through the stomata (pores in the leaf surface), completing the continuous flow called the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.0.2] Stage 1, into the root hair by osmosis: soil water is a dilute solution with a higher water concentration, while the cell sap in the root hair's vacuole is more concentrated, with a lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.3] Water therefore passes in through the selectively permeable cell membrane, moving from higher to lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.4] Stage 2, across the cortex: water moves from cell to cell by osmosis, each cell inwards being slightly more concentrated than the one before, so the water keeps flowing inwards. It also seeps along the cellulose cell walls and through the spaces between cells.
[bio30-s2.0.5] Water then passes through the endodermis (the ring of cells around the centre of the root) into the xylem at the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.6] Stage 3, up the xylem: water travels up the root and stem xylem as continuous columns, which carry it into the veins of the leaves. The force that moves it is the transpiration pull, which comes from evaporation in the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.7] At Higher Level, root pressure and the cohesion–tension model give a fuller account of how water rises, in their own blocks below.
[bio30-s2.1.b] Once water reaches the leaf it has to get out again, and the way it leaves is what pulls the whole column up from the roots. This part of the route also shows that the plant itself does no work to lift the water.
[bio30-s2.1.0] Stage 4, into the leaf cells: water leaves the xylem in the veins and passes by osmosis into the mesophyll cells (the cells in the middle layer of the leaf), keeping them turgid (firm and full of water) and supplying a small amount for photosynthesis.
[bio30-s2.1.1] Stage 5, out of the leaf: water evaporates from the moist walls of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, which become saturated with water vapour. The vapour then diffuses out through the open stomata into the drier air outside, from a higher to a lower concentration of water vapour.
[bio30-s2.1.2] Transpiration pull: as water evaporates from the leaves, more water is drawn out of the xylem to replace it, so the whole column of water in the xylem is pulled up from the roots, like drinking through a straw.
[bio30-s2.1.3] The energy for the transpiration pull comes from the sun's heat, which causes evaporation; the plant uses none of its own ATP (its cell energy currency) to move water up, so the pull is a passive process.
[bio30-s2.1.4] Diagram: an outline of a whole plant with arrows showing the route soil water, root hair, cortex, root xylem, stem xylem, leaf vein, mesophyll cell, air space, stoma, air; label osmosis (root hair, cortex), evaporation (mesophyll cell walls) and diffusion (stoma).
[bio30-s2.2.b] Transpiration is the process that drives the water flow described above. It brings real benefits, but it is also a cost the plant cannot avoid, so you need to be able to give both sides.
[bio30-s2.2.0] Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the parts of a plant above the ground, mainly through the stomata of the leaves. Small amounts are lost through the cuticle (the waxy surface layer) and the lenticels (small pores in woody stems).
[bio30-s2.2.1] Most of the water a plant absorbs is lost by transpiration; only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis or kept in the cells. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio30-s2.2.2] Transpiration benefits the plant by pulling water and dissolved minerals up from the roots to the leaves, the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.2.3] It keeps the leaf cells supplied with water, so they stay turgid and the leaves stay firm and spread out to the light. Evaporation also cools the leaves in strong sunlight, because evaporating water carries heat away.
[bio30-s2.2.4] Transpiration is unavoidable. The stomata must be open to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis, and water vapour escapes through the same pores.
[bio30-s2.2.5] If water is lost faster than the roots can take it in, the cells lose turgor (they go limp) and the plant wilts. Prolonged wilting stops photosynthesis and can kill the plant, so the plant balances gas exchange against water loss.
[bio30-s2.2.6] Conditions that increase transpiration are bright light, high temperature, wind, low humidity, a large leaf area and a thin cuticle. Each is explained and investigated in Investigation: rate of transpiration.
[bio30-s2.3.b] A stoma is a pore that lets CO₂ in and water vapour out, and the plant can open or close it. The guard cells on either side of the pore do this by changing how much water they hold.
[bio30-s2.3.0] Each stoma is a pore between two guard cells. The wall of a guard cell next to the pore, the inner wall, is thicker and less stretchy than the outer wall, and this difference is what makes the cell bend when it swells.
[bio30-s2.3.1] Opening: in the light, guard cells actively take in potassium ions (K⁺) from neighbouring epidermal cells (the ordinary cells of the leaf surface), using ATP. This raises the solute concentration in the guard cells, so water enters them by osmosis from the surrounding cells.
[bio30-s2.3.2] The guard cells swell and become turgid. Because the thick inner wall stretches less than the thin outer wall, the cells bend outwards and curve apart, and the pore opens.
[bio30-s2.3.3] Closing: potassium ions leave the guard cells, water follows by osmosis, the guard cells become flaccid (limp) and straighten, and the pore closes.
[bio30-s2.3.4] Diagram: a pair of guard cells drawn twice, once open (turgid, curved apart, pore open) and once closed (flaccid, straight, pore closed); label guard cell, thick inner wall, thin outer wall, chloroplast, nucleus and stoma (pore); add arrows for K⁺ and water entering when the stoma opens.
[bio30-s2.4.b] The plant does not open its stomata at random. It opens them when CO₂ is needed and closes them when water must be saved, and it places them where least water is lost.
[bio30-s2.4.0] Stomata are usually open in daylight, when CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis, and when the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is low. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s2.4.1] Stomata close at night, when there is no photosynthesis and so no need for CO₂, and in very hot, dry conditions. They also close when CO₂ concentration inside the leaf is high.
[bio30-s2.4.2] When water is short and the plant begins to wilt, a chemical signal from the roots closes the stomata, saving water even though photosynthesis slows.
[bio30-s2.4.3] Stomata are mostly on the lower surface of a broad leaf, where it is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates from them. Leaves floating on water, such as the water lily, have them on the upper surface instead, because the lower surface touches the water.
[bio30-s2.5.b] Because transpiration cannot be stopped, plants survive by limiting it. Their leaves and habits are adapted to lose less water, most of all in dry, windy or cold places.
[bio30-s2.5.0] Leaf features that reduce water loss include a waxy, waterproof cuticle, thicker on the leaves of plants in dry or windy places; most stomata on the lower, shaded surface; and stomata that close at night and in drought.
[bio30-s2.5.1] Deciduous trees (trees that drop their leaves each year) shed their leaves in autumn, so they do not lose water in winter, when cold soil water is hard to absorb.
[bio30-s2.5.2] Evergreen conifers have needle-shaped leaves with a small surface area and a thick cuticle, which means little water evaporates even though the leaves stay on all year.
[bio30-s2.5.3] Plants of dry habitats (xerophytes) have extra adaptations. Marram grass of sand dunes rolls its leaves into a tube, with the stomata on the inside surface, trapping humid air. Cacti have leaves reduced to spines and store water in a swollen green stem.
[bio30-s2.6.b] Water is not the only thing roots take in. They also absorb dissolved minerals from the soil, but minerals enter by a different process from water, and that process costs the plant energy.
[bio30-s2.6.0] Plants need minerals such as nitrate, phosphate, potassium, magnesium and calcium. They are taken up from the soil water as dissolved ions, for example nitrate (NO₃⁻), potassium (K⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺).
[bio30-s2.6.1] Mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside the root hair cells than in the soil water, so they must be pumped in against their concentration gradient by carrier proteins in the cell membrane, using ATP from respiration. This is active transport.
[bio30-s2.6.2] Where the soil concentration is higher than in the cell, some ions also enter by diffusion.
[bio30-s2.6.3] Because active transport needs ATP, roots need oxygen for aerobic respiration. In waterlogged soil the air spaces fill with water, the roots get too little oxygen, mineral uptake falls and the plant grows poorly.
[bio30-s2.6.4] This is one reason soil must be well drained, and why roots have air spaces in the cortex.
[bio30-s2.6.5] The ions move across the cortex to the xylem and are carried up, dissolved in the water of the transpiration stream, to the leaves, growing tips and other parts, where they are taken into cells. The faster the transpiration, the faster minerals reach the leaves.
[bio30-s2.6.6] Pumping mineral ions into the root also makes the root cells more concentrated than the soil water, which keeps water entering by osmosis.
[bio30-s2.7.b] Transpiration pulls water up from above, but roots can also push it up from below. This push is called root pressure, and it is an active process that needs living root cells.
[bio30-s2.7.0] Root pressure is a pressure in the root xylem that pushes water a short distance up the stem from below.
[bio30-s2.7.1] Living root cells actively pump mineral ions into the xylem, using ATP. This makes the xylem sap more concentrated than the surrounding cells, so water passes into the xylem by osmosis, building up a pressure that pushes the water upwards.
[bio30-s2.7.2] Evidence: when a stem is cut off near ground level, sap oozes from the cut stump for some time, pushed out by the roots. Grapevines pruned in spring bleed sap in this way.
[bio30-s2.7.3] Guttation: on cool, humid nights or early mornings, when transpiration is very slow, drops of water are forced out through special pores at the tips and edges of leaves, for example on grass and strawberry leaves.
[bio30-s2.7.4] Guttation drops are not dew, because they appear at the leaf edges, not all over the surface.
[bio30-s2.7.5] Root pressure needs living, respiring root cells. It stops if the roots are killed, cooled or deprived of oxygen.
[bio30-s2.7.6] That it stops in these conditions shows that root pressure is an active process.
[bio30-s2.7.7] Root pressure is most significant at night and in spring, before the leaves have opened and when transpiration is low, and it can refill xylem columns that contain air bubbles.
[bio30-s2.7.8] Root pressure is far too weak to raise water to the top of a tall tree, and some plants show little or none, so it is not the main mechanism.
[bio30-s2.8.b] Root pressure is too weak to lift water to the top of a tall tree, so something else must do it. The cohesion–tension model explains how a pulling force from the leaves, and water's tendency to stick together, raises an unbroken column of water.
[bio30-s2.8.0] The cohesion–tension model explains how water rises to the tops of the tallest trees. It was proposed in 1894 by two Irish scientists at Trinity College Dublin, the botanist Henry Dixon and the physicist John Joly.
[bio30-s2.8.1] 1. Transpiration: water evaporates from the walls of the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata.
[bio30-s2.8.2] 2. The mesophyll cells replace the lost water by osmosis from the cells deeper in the leaf, and these take water from the xylem in the veins.
[bio30-s2.8.3] 3. Tension: removing water from the top of the xylem puts the column of water in the xylem under tension, a pulling force or negative pressure, which is passed all the way down the stem to the roots. This is the transpiration pull.
[bio30-s2.8.4] 4. Cohesion: water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, so they stick together and the column does not break. Each molecule pulled up drags the one behind it, so the whole unbroken column moves up as one.
[bio30-s2.8.5] 5. Adhesion: water molecules are also attracted to the walls of the narrow xylem vessels. This helps hold the column up against gravity and stops it pulling away from the walls.
[bio30-s2.8.6] 6. At the bottom, water is drawn into the root xylem from the cortex, and into the root hairs from the soil, to replace it.
[bio30-s2.8.7] The process is passive, so the plant spends no ATP on it. The lignified (stiffened with lignin) walls of the xylem stop the vessels collapsing inwards under the tension.
[bio30-s2.9.b] A model is only convincing if observations support it. Two observations show that the water in the xylem really is under tension, and one other factor is too weak to matter.
[bio30-s2.9.0] The trunk of a tree becomes very slightly narrower during the day, when transpiration is fastest, as the tension pulls the xylem walls inwards.
[bio30-s2.9.1] When a xylem vessel under tension is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out. If air enters the xylem the column breaks and water flow in that vessel stops.
[bio30-s2.9.2] This is why the stems of cut flowers should be cut again under water before being placed in a vase.
[bio30-s2.9.3] Capillarity, the rise of water up narrow tubes by adhesion, raises water only a short way and is a minor factor.
[bio30-s2.10.b] The two mechanisms work together to move water, but they differ in direction, energy source, strength and timing. Comparing them in pairs is the clearest way to keep them apart.
[bio30-s2.10.0] Root pressure pushes water from below; cohesion–tension pulls it from above.
[bio30-s2.10.1] Root pressure is active, using ATP in the root cells; cohesion–tension is passive, powered by the sun's energy, which evaporates the water.
[bio30-s2.10.2] Root pressure raises water only a short distance; cohesion–tension is the main mechanism and can lift water to the top of the tallest trees.
[bio30-s2.10.3] Root pressure is most important at night and in spring, when transpiration is low; cohesion–tension works when the stomata are open and transpiration is fast, mainly by day.

CANDIDATES bio5-s1: Characteristics of living things
[bio5-s1.0.b] Biology is the study of living things, called organisms. No single feature separates living from non-living, so the specification lists eleven characteristics: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis. Together they define life.
[bio5-s1.0.0] An organism shows all eleven characteristics at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once. A dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but it is still alive.
[bio5-s1.0.1] Some non-living things show one or two characteristics. A crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells.
[bio5-s1.0.2] A fire seems to 'use oxygen', 'release energy' and 'spread', but it has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions, so it is not alive.
[bio5-s1.0.3] Something is alive only if it shows the full set, not just one or two features. Viruses are the main borderline case. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio5-s1.1.b] Living things are not random mixtures of chemicals: they are built in an ordered way, and the basic building block is the cell. Two of the eleven characteristics, organisation and cellular basis, describe this.
[bio5-s1.1.0] Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms (those made of many cells), cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs.
[bio5-s1.1.1] Even a single cell has organised parts called organelles (small structures inside a cell, each with its own job). There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio5-s1.1.2] Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life, so it is the basic unit of structure and function.
[bio5-s1.1.3] Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process. Multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells, each adapted to one job.
[bio5-s1.1.4] All cells share a cell membrane, cytoplasm (the jelly-like fluid inside the cell), ribosomes (which make proteins) and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
[bio5-s1.1.5] All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division. New cells are never made from non-living material.
[bio5-s1.1.6] These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century. Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
[bio5-s1.2.b] Every organism needs a supply of food to stay alive and to build and repair its body. Nutrition is how it gets that food, and organisms fall into two groups depending on whether they make it or take it in.
[bio5-s1.2.0] Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
[bio5-s1.2.1] Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances (ones not made by living things, such as carbon dioxide and water). Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio5-s1.2.2] A few bacteria are chemosynthetic: instead of light, they use energy from chemical reactions to make their food, so they can live where there is no sunlight.
[bio5-s1.2.3] Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals.
[bio5-s1.2.4] Fungi and many bacteria are also heterotrophs: they absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio5-s1.3.b] Food is only useful once its stored energy can be released in a form cells can use. Respiration does this inside every living cell, and it is easily confused with breathing.
[bio5-s1.3.0] Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food, mainly glucose, inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy), which powers the cell's activities.
[bio5-s1.3.1] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
[bio5-s1.3.2] Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio5-s1.3.3] In human muscle, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid. In yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[bio5-s1.3.4] Respiration is not the same as breathing. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange), while respiration is a chemical process in cells.
[bio5-s1.3.5] Plants respire all the time, day and night, because every living cell needs energy continuously. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio5-s1.4.b] Living cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Metabolism is the name for all of them, and they fall into two types: those that build up and those that break down.
[bio5-s1.4.0] Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction).
[bio5-s1.4.1] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose made from carbon dioxide and water) and protein synthesis from amino acids.
[bio5-s1.4.2] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, and the digestion of starch to maltose.
[bio5-s1.4.3] In short, anabolic means building up and using energy, while catabolic means breaking down and releasing energy. There is more on this in Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life.
[bio5-s1.5.b] Chemical reactions in cells make some products that the body cannot use. Excretion removes them before they cause harm.
[bio5-s1.5.0] Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism, which are substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed. If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
[bio5-s1.5.1] Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs. Urea, made in the liver from excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in urine. Excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
[bio5-s1.5.2] Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata (the tiny pores in leaves). Some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
[bio5-s1.5.3] Excretion is not egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.b] Cells work properly only when the conditions around them stay within narrow limits, even if the outside world changes. Homeostasis is the control that keeps them steady.
[bio5-s1.6.0] Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, such as body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance and pH, despite changes outside or inside the organism.
[bio5-s1.6.1] It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH, so a large change would slow or stop the reactions of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.2] For example, human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C, and insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. There is more on the mechanism, negative feedback, in Homeostasis.
[bio5-s1.7.b] Organisms must cope with a changing environment and must also produce new individuals, because every organism eventually dies. Response and reproduction cover these two needs.
[bio5-s1.7.0] Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
[bio5-s1.7.1] Animals respond quickly through the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio5-s1.7.2] Plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure, e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short, which reduces water loss. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio5-s1.7.3] Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
[bio5-s1.7.4] Asexual reproduction: one parent and no gametes (sex cells). The offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria and budding in yeast.
[bio5-s1.7.5] Sexual reproduction: two gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote (the first cell of the new organism). The offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans and flowering plants.
[bio5-s1.8.b] Organisms get bigger as they develop, and they pass their features on to the next generation. Growth and heredity describe these two processes.
[bio5-s1.8.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism, the building reactions of metabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
[bio5-s1.8.1] Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips. Most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
[bio5-s1.8.2] Heredity: the passing of genetic information, carried by genes made of DNA, from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. 'Like produces like', e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
[bio5-s1.8.3] Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits (its characteristics). Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance.
[bio5-s1.9.b] Here is the full set in one place for quick revision. An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life.
[bio5-s1.9.0] Organisation: highly ordered structure, from cells up to organ systems.
[bio5-s1.9.1] Response: detecting a stimulus and reacting to it.
[bio5-s1.9.2] Respiration: releasing energy from food inside cells.
[bio5-s1.9.3] Nutrition: obtaining food for energy and raw materials.
[bio5-s1.9.4] Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.9.5] Reproduction: producing new individuals of the same species.
[bio5-s1.10.b] These are the remaining five characteristics, covering growth, chemistry, stability, inheritance and cells.
[bio5-s1.10.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass.
[bio5-s1.10.1] Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism.
[bio5-s1.10.2] Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment relatively constant.
[bio5-s1.10.3] Heredity: passing genetic information from parents to offspring.
[bio5-s1.10.4] Cellular basis: made of cells, which arise only from pre-existing cells.

CANDIDATES bio5-s2: Levels of organisation
[bio5-s2.0.b] Living things are organised in levels of increasing complexity. Each level is made of units of the level below working together, so a whole organism can be understood by building up from its cells.
[bio5-s2.0.0] The order is cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Learn it as a ladder: each step up combines the units of the step below.
[bio5-s2.0.1] Below the cell, cells are built of organelles (small structures inside the cell, each with a job), which are built of biomolecules (e.g. proteins, lipids). There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
[bio5-s2.0.2] Cell: the basic unit of life. In a multicellular organism (one made of many cells) cells are specialised (differentiated), so that the structure of each cell suits one job.
[bio5-s2.0.3] Tissue: a group of similar cells with the same structure, working together to carry out a particular function. Because the cells are alike, the tissue does one job well.
[bio5-s2.0.4] Organ: a structure made of several different tissues working together to carry out one or more functions. Having different tissues is what separates an organ from a tissue.
[bio5-s2.0.5] Organ system: a group of organs working together to carry out a major function, such as digesting food.
[bio5-s2.0.6] Organism: an individual living thing, whose organ systems work together to keep it alive.
[bio5-s2.0.7] Diagram: a five-step flow chart from cell to organism, with a human example at each level (muscle cell → muscle tissue → stomach → digestive system → human) and a plant example (xylem vessel → vascular tissue → leaf → shoot system → plant).
[bio5-s2.1.b] A specialised cell has a structure that suits its particular job. Cells in a multicellular organism are specialised, and each example below links a feature of the cell to its function.
[bio5-s2.1.0] Unicellular organisms (those made of a single cell), e.g. Amoeba, yeast and bacteria, carry out every life process in one cell, so that cell is not specialised for a single job.
[bio5-s2.1.1] Differentiation: in a multicellular organism, all body cells contain the same DNA, but each cell type uses (expresses) only some of its genes, so cells become specialised.
[bio5-s2.1.2] Unspecialised cells that can still become several cell types are called stem cells. There is more on their uses in Genetic Engineering.
[bio5-s2.1.3] Red blood cell: no nucleus and a biconcave disc shape (a disc dipped in on both faces), giving more room for haemoglobin (the pigment that carries oxygen) and a large surface area for carrying oxygen.
[bio5-s2.1.4] Nerve cell (neuron): long thin extensions carry impulses (electrical signals) over long distances quickly, which suits a cell that links distant parts of the body.
[bio5-s2.1.5] Sperm cell: a tail for swimming, and many mitochondria in the middle section to release the energy that powers the tail.
[bio5-s2.1.6] Root hair cell: a long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water and minerals from the soil.
[bio5-s2.1.7] Palisade cell of a leaf: packed with chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens) for photosynthesis. Guard cells change shape to open and close the stomata (the pores in the leaf surface).
[bio5-s2.2.b] Animal bodies are built from four main tissue types. Each is a group of similar cells doing one kind of job.
[bio5-s2.2.0] Epithelial tissue lines and covers surfaces, e.g. the lining of the gut and the skin surface.
[bio5-s2.2.1] Connective tissue supports and connects other tissues, e.g. bone, cartilage and blood. Blood counts as a connective tissue.
[bio5-s2.2.2] Muscle tissue contracts (shortens) to cause movement. There are three types: skeletal, smooth and cardiac (heart muscle).
[bio5-s2.2.3] Nervous tissue carries impulses, e.g. in the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
[bio5-s2.3.b] Plants also have tissue types, each a group of similar cells with one job. Together they build plant organs such as the leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.0] Dermal tissue is the epidermis, a protective outer layer covering the plant.
[bio5-s2.3.1] Vascular tissue carries materials around the plant: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries food.
[bio5-s2.3.2] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant, carrying out photosynthesis and storage, e.g. the mesophyll (the inner tissue) of a leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.3] Meristematic tissue consists of dividing cells at root and shoot tips, where growth happens, so new cells for growth are made there.
[bio5-s2.3.4] A leaf contains dermal, ground and vascular tissue, which shows that an organ is made of several tissue types. Plant organs are root, stem, leaf and flower.
[bio5-s2.4.b] Organs are made of several tissue types, and organs that cooperate form organ systems. Each system has one major function.
[bio5-s2.4.0] The stomach is an organ containing epithelial tissue lining it, smooth muscle that churns food, connective tissue and nervous tissue. Four different tissues working together make it an organ.
[bio5-s2.4.1] Other human organs include the heart, lungs, kidneys and brain.
[bio5-s2.4.2] Digestive system: breaks down and absorbs food. Circulatory system: transports materials around the body.
[bio5-s2.4.3] Breathing system: gas exchange (taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide). Nervous system: fast coordination.
[bio5-s2.4.4] Endocrine system: hormonal coordination. Musculoskeletal system: support and movement.
[bio5-s2.4.5] Urinary system: excretion (removing wastes) and water balance. Reproductive system: reproduction. Immune system: defence.
[bio5-s2.4.6] Plants have two systems. The root system anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals.
[bio5-s2.4.7] The shoot system (stem, leaves and flowers) carries out photosynthesis, transport and reproduction.
[bio5-s2.5.b] Size changes how a living thing gets what it needs. The bigger an organism, the harder it is to supply every cell, which is why large organisms need specialised systems.
[bio5-s2.5.0] As an organism gets bigger, its volume increases faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio falls.
[bio5-s2.5.1] Worked example: a 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm² and a volume of 1 cm³, a ratio of 6:1. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of 24 cm² and a volume of 8 cm³, a ratio of 3:1.
[bio5-s2.5.2] A unicellular organism has a large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane is enough to supply oxygen and food and to remove wastes.
[bio5-s2.5.3] In a large multicellular organism most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow to reach them.
[bio5-s2.5.4] Multicellular organisms therefore need specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gut lining, root hairs, leaves) to take in materials, and transport systems (blood and heart; xylem and phloem) to carry them to every cell.
[bio5-s2.5.5] They also need coordination systems (nervous and hormonal) that link all the parts and keep conditions constant (homeostasis).
[bio5-s2.5.6] Division of labour: each cell type does one job very efficiently, but no cell can survive alone, so the cells depend on each other.
[bio5-s2.6.b] Being made of many specialised cells brings benefits, but it also has costs. Comparing the two shows why both unicellular and multicellular life exist.
[bio5-s2.6.0] Advantage: a larger size, which means fewer predators and access to light or food.
[bio5-s2.6.1] Advantage: cells inside the body are protected from the environment, because they are surrounded by other cells.
[bio5-s2.6.2] Advantage: efficiency from specialisation, since each cell type does one job well.
[bio5-s2.6.3] Advantage: the organism can survive the death of individual cells, which are replaced.
[bio5-s2.6.4] Disadvantage: more energy and resources are needed to build and maintain the body.
[bio5-s2.6.5] Disadvantage: damage to one vital organ or system can kill the whole organism, because the other cells depend on it.
[bio5-s2.6.6] Disadvantage: growth to reproductive age takes longer than for unicellular organisms, which can divide within hours.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.

CANDIDATES bio8-s1: Biomolecules and metabolism
[bio8-s1.0.b] Living things are built from and run by a small set of chemical families. Knowing which family a molecule belongs to, and what food provides, is the starting point for all of cell chemistry.
[bio8-s1.0.0] A biomolecule is a molecule made by living organisms. The four main groups are carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids.
[bio8-s1.0.1] All four are organic, which means they are built around chains or rings of carbon atoms bonded to hydrogen. Water and minerals are also chemicals of life but are inorganic, so they do not count as biomolecules. There is more on this in Water and Minerals.
[bio8-s1.0.2] The particular elements found in each biomolecule are listed in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio8-s1.0.3] Food supplies three things: energy, released in respiration (the chemical reactions in cells that release energy from food) mainly from carbohydrates and lipids; raw materials for growth and repair, mainly proteins; and substances that regulate body processes, namely vitamins and minerals.
[bio8-s1.0.4] The body does not take in enzymes and hormones ready made. It makes its own enzymes and hormones from the food it takes in.
[bio8-s1.1.b] Cells build big molecules by joining small ones together, and break big molecules down again by splitting them. The words monomer, polymer, condensation and hydrolysis name these units and these two opposite reactions.
[bio8-s1.1.0] A monomer is a small unit molecule. A polymer is a large molecule made of many monomers joined in a chain.
[bio8-s1.1.1] Monosaccharides (single sugar units) are the monomers of polysaccharides. Amino acids are the monomers of polypeptides and proteins. Nucleotides are the monomers of nucleic acids.
[bio8-s1.1.2] A triglyceride (a fat or oil) is built from glycerol and three fatty acids, but it is not a repeating polymer, because it is not a long chain of identical repeating units.
[bio8-s1.1.3] Condensation: two molecules join and a molecule of water is removed. Glucose units join in this way to form starch, glycogen and cellulose, and amino acids join to form proteins.
[bio8-s1.1.4] Hydrolysis is the reverse: a large molecule is split into smaller ones by adding water, for example starch is hydrolysed to maltose and proteins are hydrolysed to amino acids. Condensation removes water; hydrolysis adds it.
[bio8-s1.1.5] Digestion is hydrolysis catalysed by enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), which is how food molecules are split into units small enough to absorb. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio8-s1.1.6] Cells assemble biomolecules into the structures they are made of. Cellulose fibres form plant cell walls; phospholipids and proteins form every cell membrane; keratin forms hair and nails; collagen forms skin, tendons and the framework of bone.
[bio8-s1.1.7] Diagram: a chain of linked glucose units. Draw an arrow labelled condensation (water out) joining two units, and an arrow labelled hydrolysis (water in) splitting them. Label monomer and polymer.
[bio8-s1.2.b] Inside every cell many reactions are going on at once. Together they are called metabolism, and they fall into two opposite kinds: reactions that build up and reactions that break down.
[bio8-s1.2.0] Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Each reaction is controlled by a specific enzyme. Metabolism is one of the characteristics of life.
[bio8-s1.2.1] Anabolism: reactions that build larger, more complex molecules from smaller ones. They need (absorb) energy, because forming bigger molecules is an uphill process.
[bio8-s1.2.2] Anabolic examples: photosynthesis, in which glucose is made from carbon dioxide and water; joining glucose into starch in plants or glycogen in the liver and muscles; and joining amino acids into proteins.
[bio8-s1.2.3] Catabolism: reactions that break larger molecules into smaller ones. They release energy.
[bio8-s1.2.4] Catabolic examples: respiration, in which glucose is broken down to carbon dioxide and water; digestion of starch to maltose and glucose; and breakdown of glycogen to glucose.
[bio8-s1.2.5] Energy transfer: energy released by catabolic reactions such as respiration is used to drive anabolic reactions. It is carried between them by the transfer molecule ATP, a small molecule that delivers energy where the cell needs it.
[bio8-s1.2.6] Catabolism and anabolism therefore depend on each other: catabolism supplies the energy that anabolism needs. There is more on this in Transfer Molecules.
[bio8-s1.3.b] Cells do not only build and break down. They also convert one biomolecule into another, so that a single food or product can supply whatever the cell is short of.
[bio8-s1.3.0] Cells convert one biomolecule into another to meet their needs.
[bio8-s1.3.1] Plants make starch for storage, cellulose for cell walls, sucrose for transport and oils (for example stored in seeds) from the glucose made in photosynthesis.
[bio8-s1.3.2] Plants absorb nitrogen from the soil as nitrate. Glucose contains no nitrogen, so they use the nitrate, together with glucose, to make amino acids and proteins.
[bio8-s1.3.3] In humans the liver converts excess glucose to glycogen, a storage carbohydrate, and excess carbohydrate is converted to fat for storage.
[bio8-s1.4.b] The two types of metabolism are mirror images of each other. Setting them side by side makes the differences easy to remember.
[bio8-s1.4.0] Direction: anabolism joins small molecules into large ones; catabolism breaks large molecules into small ones.
[bio8-s1.4.1] Energy: anabolism needs an input of energy; catabolism releases energy, which is why the two are linked.
[bio8-s1.4.2] Examples: photosynthesis and protein synthesis are anabolic; respiration and digestion are catabolic.
