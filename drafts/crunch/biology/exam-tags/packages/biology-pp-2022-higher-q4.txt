CANDIDATES bio15-s1: Respiration as a catabolic process
[bio15-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a constant supply of usable energy. Respiration is the process that gets it, by breaking down food molecules and transferring the energy they hold to ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio15-s1.0.0] Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food, mainly glucose, in every living cell. The energy released is transferred to ATP, so respiration releases energy rather than making it.
[bio15-s1.0.1] Respiration is catabolic, meaning a large molecule such as glucose is broken down into smaller molecules and energy is released. The products are CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂, depending on the conditions.
[bio15-s1.0.2] Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process, because it builds small molecules up into large ones and takes in energy, whereas respiration breaks large ones down.
[bio15-s1.0.3] The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once, as in burning. This is why much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. Energy not trapped as ATP is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
[bio15-s1.0.4] The ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport (moving substances across a membrane using energy) and building large molecules.
[bio15-s1.0.5] Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed (built into sugar).
[bio15-s1.0.6] Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake of carbon by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle. There is more on this in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio15-s1.0.7] Glucose is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down). Glycogen (in animals) and starch (in plants) are first broken down to glucose, and fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired.
[bio15-s1.1.b] Aerobic (with oxygen) respiration breaks glucose down completely, releasing energy. Knowing where the glucose and oxygen come from, and where the carbon dioxide and water go, shows how organisms and their surroundings are linked.
[bio15-s1.1.0] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
[bio15-s1.1.1] Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Its chemicals are those of the photosynthesis equation reversed, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
[bio15-s1.1.2] Source of glucose in plants: photosynthesis, or stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant). In animals: digested food absorbed into the blood, or glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
[bio15-s1.1.3] Source of oxygen in humans: the air, taken in through the lungs and carried to the cells by red blood cells, which is how it reaches every cell.
[bio15-s1.1.4] Source of oxygen in plants: the air, entering through stomata (leaf pores) and lenticels (pores in woody stems); soil air, taken in by the roots; and the plant's own photosynthesis. In aquatic organisms it is oxygen dissolved in water.
[bio15-s1.1.5] Fate of carbon dioxide: it diffuses out of cells. In humans it is carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
[bio15-s1.1.6] Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted. Some is breathed out as water vapour.
[bio15-s1.2.b] When oxygen is scarce or absent, cells can still release some energy from glucose, but not nearly as much. The products depend on the organism.
[bio15-s1.2.0] Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. Because the glucose is only partly broken down, much of the energy stays in the products, so much less energy is released per glucose and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.2.1] In animals, such as human muscle during intense exercise, and in some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
[bio15-s1.2.2] In yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen such as roots in waterlogged soil, the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
[bio15-s1.2.3] Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. In baking, yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise and the ethanol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and wine-making, yeast's ethanol is the useful product. In making yogurt and cheese, bacteria's lactic acid is the useful product.
[bio15-s1.2.4] In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire.
[bio15-s1.2.5] Oxygen debt: after exercise, extra oxygen is needed to remove the lactic acid. It is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose, and the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
[bio15-s1.2.6] Obligate anaerobes respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen, for example the bacteria that cause tetanus. Yeast can respire either way, so it is a facultative anaerobe.
[bio15-s1.3.b] Respiration happens in two stages in different parts of the cell. Stage 1 is the same whether or not oxygen is present, and what happens next depends on oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid inside the cell) of every cell and does not need oxygen. 1. Glucose, with 6 carbon atoms, is split into two molecules of pyruvate, each with 3 carbon atoms. 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
[bio15-s1.3.1] With oxygen, stage 2 takes place in the mitochondria (the organelles where aerobic respiration is completed). 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water.
[bio15-s1.3.2] 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.3] Without oxygen, pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (in animals and some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast and plants). No further ATP is made, so anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s1.3.4] Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria, so their aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane.
[bio15-s1.3.5] Diagram: flow diagram. Glucose (cytoplasm), then glycolysis (small ATP), then 2 pyruvate. Oxygen present: mitochondrion, then CO₂, water, large ATP. No oxygen: cytoplasm, then lactic acid (animals) or ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1, stage 2.
[bio15-s1.4.b] Aerobic and anaerobic respiration start the same way but differ in how far glucose is broken down and how much ATP results. Respiration also contrasts with photosynthesis: respiration is catabolic and happens in all living cells, while photosynthesis is anabolic and happens only in cells with chloroplasts.
[bio15-s1.4.0] Oxygen: needed in aerobic respiration; not needed in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.1] Location: aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria; anaerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm only.
[bio15-s1.4.2] Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O, in aerobic respiration; incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂, in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.3] Energy: a large amount of ATP in aerobic respiration; a small amount, only the ATP made in glycolysis, in anaerobic respiration, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio15-s1.4.4] Stages: stage 1 followed by stage 2 in aerobic respiration; stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.5] Respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂. It happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
[bio15-s1.4.6] Photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂. It happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only in the light.

CANDIDATES bio15-s3: First-stage process
[bio15-s3.0.b] Respiration starts with glycolysis, a short pathway that partly breaks down glucose before the cell knows whether oxygen is available. It is the first stage of both aerobic respiration (with oxygen) and anaerobic respiration (without oxygen).
[bio15-s3.0.0] Glycolysis means 'sugar splitting'. It takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid part of the cell outside the organelles), is controlled by about ten enzymes working in sequence, and needs no oxygen.
[bio15-s3.0.1] 1. Activation: two ATP molecules are used to add phosphate groups to the glucose (C₆, a six-carbon sugar). This makes the glucose more reactive, so it is an investment of energy that is repaid later.
[bio15-s3.0.2] 2. Splitting: the six-carbon sugar is split into two three-carbon molecules.
[bio15-s3.0.3] 3. Oxidation: hydrogen, as electrons and protons, is removed from each three-carbon molecule by dehydrogenase enzymes and picked up by NAD⁺ (a carrier molecule that accepts hydrogen), forming NADH. Two NADH are made per glucose.
[bio15-s3.0.4] 4. ATP generation: four ATP are made as phosphate groups are transferred to ADP. Net gain: 4 made minus 2 used, so 2 ATP per glucose.
[bio15-s3.0.5] 5. Product: two molecules of pyruvate (pyruvic acid, C₃) per glucose. Pyruvate still holds most of the energy of the glucose, which is why much more energy can be released from it later.
[bio15-s3.0.6] Summary: glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pi + 2 NAD⁺ → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH. No CO₂ is released in glycolysis, because the carbons all stay in the two pyruvate molecules.
[bio15-s3.1.b] Glycolysis happens in almost every living cell, and it is not limited to glucose from food.
[bio15-s3.1.0] Glycolysis happens in almost every living cell, prokaryotic (no nucleus) and eukaryotic (with a nucleus). This suggests it evolved early in the history of life, before there was oxygen in the atmosphere, which fits with it needing no oxygen.
[bio15-s3.1.1] Other six-carbon sugars, such as fructose, and the glucose from glycogen or starch enter the same pathway.
[bio15-s3.2.b] Two transfer molecules, ATP and NAD⁺, carry energy and hydrogen through glycolysis. How each one is used explains why the cell must have a way to carry on without oxygen.
[bio15-s3.2.0] ATP: two are used to activate the glucose and four are made by transferring phosphate from the sugar molecules directly to ADP. The net 2 ATP is all the energy anaerobic respiration releases per glucose.
[bio15-s3.2.1] NAD⁺: the cell has only a small supply. Glycolysis converts NAD⁺ to NADH, so glycolysis stops if NADH is not turned back into NAD⁺, because there would be no NAD⁺ left to accept hydrogen.
[bio15-s3.2.2] The fate of NADH depends on oxygen. With oxygen, it passes its electrons to the electron transport chain in the mitochondrion (the organelle where the second stage of respiration happens). There is more on this in Second-stage process.
[bio15-s3.2.3] Without oxygen, NADH passes its hydrogen to pyruvate instead, a process called fermentation. Either way, NAD⁺ is regenerated, so glycolysis can carry on.
[bio15-s3.3.b] Without oxygen, pyruvate is not passed to the mitochondrion, and glycolysis would stop if NADH were not turned back into NAD⁺. Fermentation solves this problem, and it works in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s3.3.0] When oxygen is absent or too scarce, pyruvate is not passed to the mitochondrion. Instead, in the cytoplasm, NADH passes its hydrogen to pyruvate, or to a product made from it. This regenerates NAD⁺, so glycolysis can keep running and keep making its 2 ATP per glucose.
[bio15-s3.3.1] The purpose of fermentation is to regenerate NAD⁺, not to make more ATP. No ATP is made after glycolysis.
[bio15-s3.3.2] Lactic acid fermentation, in animal muscle cells and in lactic acid bacteria such as those used to make yogurt: pyruvate (C₃) takes hydrogen from NADH and becomes lactic acid (lactate, C₃). This is one step, and no CO₂ is released.
[bio15-s3.3.3] Overall for lactic acid fermentation: glucose → 2 lactic acid, with a net 2 ATP.
[bio15-s3.3.4] Alcoholic (ethanol) fermentation, in yeast and in plant cells short of oxygen, such as waterlogged roots: pyruvate (C₃) first loses one carbon as CO₂, forming a two-carbon compound. This then takes hydrogen from NADH and becomes ethanol (C₂).
[bio15-s3.3.5] Alcoholic fermentation is two steps, with one CO₂ released per pyruvate. Overall: glucose → 2 ethanol + 2 CO₂, with a net 2 ATP.
[bio15-s3.3.6] Lactic acid has three carbons, like pyruvate; ethanol has two, because one carbon is lost as CO₂.
[bio15-s3.4.b] Whether fermentation happens, and what it produces, depends on the organism and on the conditions. Fermentation also releases far less energy than aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s3.4.0] The product depends on the organism's enzymes. Animals and many bacteria make lactic acid, while yeast and plants make ethanol.
[bio15-s3.4.1] Whether fermentation happens at all depends on conditions. Yeast and muscle respire aerobically when oxygen is available and switch to fermentation when it is not. There is more on the conditions in Investigation: conditions for fermentation.
[bio15-s3.4.2] Fermentation releases little energy because glucose is only partly broken down. Lactic acid and ethanol still contain much of the original energy; ethanol is a fuel, for example in biofuels.
[bio15-s3.4.3] Diagram: flow chart. Glucose (C₆), 2 ATP used, 4 ATP made, 2 NAD⁺ → 2 NADH, gives 2 pyruvate (C₃). Branches: (a) oxygen: mitochondrion; (b) no oxygen, animals, some bacteria: lactic acid (C₃); (c) no oxygen, yeast, plants: CO₂, ethanol (C₂). NADH → NAD⁺ loops to glycolysis. Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion.

CANDIDATES bio15-s4: Second-stage process
[bio15-s4.0.b] Glycolysis (the first stage of respiration, in the cytoplasm) only splits glucose part of the way and ends with pyruvate. If oxygen is available, the cell passes pyruvate on to the second stage, and the first job there is a short link step that prepares it for the citric acid cycle.
[bio15-s4.0.0] When oxygen is present, each pyruvate (a 3-carbon molecule, C₃) made in glycolysis moves from the cytoplasm into the matrix of a mitochondrion. The matrix is the fluid-filled space inside the inner membrane.
[bio15-s4.0.1] In the matrix, pyruvate is broken down. One carbon atom is removed as one molecule of carbon dioxide, and hydrogen is removed and picked up by NAD⁺ (a hydrogen carrier), which turns it into NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.2] The remaining two-carbon (acetyl) group is joined to coenzyme A, a carrier molecule, forming acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA, C₂). Per pyruvate: pyruvate + coenzyme A + NAD⁺ → acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.3] Each glucose gives two pyruvate, so this link step happens twice per glucose, producing 2 CO₂ and 2 NADH.
[bio15-s4.1.b] Acetyl CoA is the fuel for the citric acid cycle, which strips the carbon from the acetyl group off as CO₂ and loads hydrogen onto carriers. Because the cycle rebuilds its starting compound every turn, it can keep running as long as acetyl CoA keeps arriving.
[bio15-s4.1.0] The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, is a cycle of enzyme-controlled reactions in the matrix of the mitochondrion, not on the cristae (the folds of the inner membrane).
[bio15-s4.1.1] 1. The acetyl CoA passes its two-carbon acetyl group into the cycle, where it joins a four-carbon compound to form citric acid (C₆), which gives the cycle its name. Coenzyme A is released to collect another acetyl group.
[bio15-s4.1.2] 2. In a series of steps, citric acid is broken down back to the four-carbon compound. This is regenerated to accept the next acetyl group, which is why it is a cycle.
[bio15-s4.1.3] 3. During each turn, two carbon atoms are removed as two molecules of CO₂, so the carbon of the acetyl group leaves as CO₂.
[bio15-s4.1.4] 4. Hydrogen, as protons and electrons, is removed at several steps and loaded onto NAD⁺, forming NADH. A small amount of ATP, one per turn, is also made directly.
[bio15-s4.1.5] The cycle turns twice for each glucose, once per acetyl CoA. With the link step, all six carbon atoms of glucose have now left as six molecules of CO₂, matching the 6CO₂ in the respiration equation. Glycolysis releases none.
[bio15-s4.1.6] The specification's products are CO₂ and H₂O. The CO₂ is released directly. The hydrogen removed is carried by NADH to the electron transport chain, where it ends up in water, so water is not made in the cycle itself.
[bio15-s4.1.7] The cycle does not need oxygen directly, but it stops without oxygen, because NADH cannot be unloaded and NAD⁺ runs out.
[bio15-s4.2.b] Most of the energy in glucose is still locked up in the hydrogen carried by NADH. The electron transport chain releases it in small steps and uses it to make ATP, which is why far more ATP per glucose is made in the second stage than the net 2 ATP of glycolysis. Aerobic respiration (respiration with oxygen) therefore releases many times more energy per glucose than fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.0] The electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers (protein complexes and mobile carriers) in the inner mitochondrial membrane, especially on the folds called cristae.
[bio15-s4.2.1] 1. NADH from glycolysis, the link step and the citric acid cycle delivers its high-energy electrons and protons to the start of the chain. NAD⁺ is released to collect more.
[bio15-s4.2.2] 2. The electrons are passed from carrier to carrier, each at a lower energy level. The energy released at each transfer is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi (phosphate).
[bio15-s4.2.3] 3. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor. At the end of the chain, low-energy electrons combine with oxygen and protons (H⁺) to form water: 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ + O₂ → 2H₂O. It is the only step of respiration using oxygen directly, and the source of the 6H₂O in the equation.
[bio15-s4.2.4] 4. If oxygen is absent, electrons cannot leave the end of the chain, so the whole chain stops. NADH cannot be unloaded, so the citric acid cycle and the link step stop too, and the cell can then only carry out glycolysis and fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.5] Cyanide is a poison because it blocks the last complex of the chain, which has the same effect as having no oxygen: the chain stops and aerobic ATP production collapses.
[bio15-s4.2.6] 5. The ATP made is exported from the mitochondrion to the cytoplasm and used for other biochemical reactions in the cell, such as synthesis of proteins, active transport and muscle contraction.
[bio15-s4.2.7] Most of the ATP from aerobic respiration is made here, via a proton gradient and ATP synthase. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.b] Respiration can be modelled as two stages joined by carrier molecules that shuttle electrons, hydrogen, acetyl groups and energy around the cell. Putting the stages together shows where each product comes from and where each reactant is used. There is more on the membrane comparison with photosynthesis in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm: glucose becomes 2 pyruvate, a net 2 ATP is made directly, and NAD⁺ is converted to NADH.
[bio15-s4.3.1] Stage 2 takes place in the mitochondrion. The link step and the citric acid cycle, in the matrix, turn pyruvate into acetyl CoA and then into CO₂, converting NAD⁺ to NADH and making a little ATP directly.
[bio15-s4.3.2] The electron transport chain, on the inner membrane (cristae), unloads NADH to NAD⁺, passes electrons to oxygen to form water, and makes most of the ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio15-s4.3.3] Transfer molecules carry energy and electrons between the stages. NAD⁺/NADH carries electrons and hydrogen from the breakdown of glucose (glycolysis, link step and cycle) to the electron transport chain, and returns empty. Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group into the cycle and returns.
[bio15-s4.3.4] ATP carries energy from the mitochondrion and cytoplasm to the cell's activities, returning as ADP + Pi to be reloaded. All the transfer molecules are recycled, so only small amounts are needed.
[bio15-s4.3.5] Tracing the atoms: the carbon of glucose leaves as CO₂ in the link step and the cycle. The hydrogen of glucose ends up in water, formed by combining with oxygen breathed in at the electron transport chain. The oxygen breathed in therefore ends up in water, not in CO₂.
[bio15-s4.3.6] Comparison with photosynthesis: in photosynthesis, NADPH and ATP are made in stage 1, using light energy, and used in stage 2 to build glucose. In respiration, NADH is made as glucose is broken down and is used at the end of stage 2 to make ATP.
[bio15-s4.3.7] Diagram: cytoplasm and a mitochondrion (outer membrane, inner membrane with cristae, matrix). Label: glucose, 2 pyruvate, NAD⁺ → NADH, acetyl CoA, CO₂, citric acid cycle (acetyl group + C₄ → citric acid (C₆) → back to C₄), electron transport chain, O₂ + H⁺ → H₂O, ATP, NAD⁺; what each carrier carries.

CANDIDATES bio29-s4: Blood
[bio29-s4.0.b] Blood is a tissue, like muscle or bone, but a liquid one. It is made of cells and cell fragments carried in a fluid, and each part has its own job.
[bio29-s4.0.0] Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
[bio29-s4.0.1] If blood, with a chemical added to stop it clotting, is spun in a centrifuge (a machine that spins samples so the heaviest parts sink), it separates into about 55% plasma, a straw-coloured liquid on top, and about 45% cells.
[bio29-s4.0.2] The cells form a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
[bio29-s4.0.3] Numbers in a healthy adult: red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. Red cells therefore greatly outnumber white cells, by several hundred to one.
[bio29-s4.0.4] All blood cells and platelets are made in the red bone marrow, found in spongy bone such as the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones. They come from stem cells (unspecialised cells that can become other cell types) that divide by mitosis.
[bio29-s4.0.5] Functions of blood: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature and water balance).
[bio29-s4.0.6] Diagram: a stained blood smear under a light microscope. Draw many red blood cells (pale centres, no nucleus), one or two white blood cells (larger, with a stained nucleus: a phagocyte with a lobed nucleus and a lymphocyte with a large round nucleus) and small platelets. Label each.
[bio29-s4.0.7] Diagram: draw a centrifuged tube labelled plasma, white cells and platelets layer, and red cells layer, with percentages.
[bio29-s4.1.b] Plasma is the liquid part of blood, and everything else in blood is carried in it or by it. Because it is mostly water, it can dissolve and transport a huge range of substances around the body.
[bio29-s4.1.0] Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it. Water is a good solvent, so many substances dissolve in it, and it also carries heat.
[bio29-s4.1.1] Dissolved substances transported in plasma: nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals); wastes (urea, from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; and salts, such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio29-s4.1.2] Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻). They form in red blood cells, by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻).
[bio29-s4.1.3] A small amount of carbon dioxide is simply dissolved in plasma, and some is carried by haemoglobin. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s4.1.4] Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors, for clotting; antibodies, for defence against pathogens; and albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
[bio29-s4.1.5] Plasma distributes heat from active organs, such as working muscles, around the body. Its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers (chemicals that resist changes in acidity) keep the blood pH near 7.4.
[bio29-s4.2.b] Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body. Almost every feature of their structure helps them do that one job well.
[bio29-s4.2.0] Red blood cells, or erythrocytes, are biconcave discs (round and thinner in the centre than at the edge), about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
[bio29-s4.2.1] The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface.
[bio29-s4.2.2] There is no nucleus, and no mitochondria, in the mature cell, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. The cell is also packed with haemoglobin, so each cell can carry a lot of oxygen.
[bio29-s4.2.3] The cell is small and flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. This shortens the distance oxygen must diffuse.
[bio29-s4.2.4] Because they have no mitochondria, red blood cells respire anaerobically (without oxygen), so they do not use up the oxygen they carry.
[bio29-s4.2.5] Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron, so each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is bright red.
[bio29-s4.2.6] In respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. The reaction is reversible. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio29-s4.3.b] Red blood cells wear out and must be constantly replaced. Problems with their number or with haemoglobin affect how much oxygen reaches the tissues.
[bio29-s4.3.0] Red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days. With no nucleus they cannot repair themselves, so they wear out.
[bio29-s4.3.1] Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen. The iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments, which are excreted in bile.
[bio29-s4.3.2] The kidney hormone EPO stimulates the production of red blood cells. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio29-s4.3.3] Anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin. A common cause is iron deficiency, because iron is needed to make haem.
[bio29-s4.3.4] Carbon monoxide, in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances, combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried and it can kill.
[bio29-s4.3.5] Sickle-cell anaemia is a further disorder of red blood cells, named here only.
[bio29-s4.4.b] White blood cells are the body's defenders against infection. They differ from red cells in several ways, which makes a good comparison to learn.
[bio29-s4.4.0] White blood cells, or leucocytes, defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, are larger and colourless (they have no haemoglobin), and are far fewer in number.
[bio29-s4.4.1] They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues, where infections often are.
[bio29-s4.4.2] There are two broad groups: phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells.
[bio29-s4.4.3] The number of white cells rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections. Leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells.
[bio29-s4.5.b] Platelets are not whole cells but fragments, and they are the first responders when a blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.5.0] Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus. They break off from large cells in the red bone marrow.
[bio29-s4.5.1] Platelets live about 7 to 10 days and start the clotting of blood.
[bio29-s4.5.2] Clotting matters because it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so that pathogens cannot enter. It is part of the first line of defence against infection.
[bio29-s4.6.b] Clotting is a chain of events in which one change triggers the next, ending in a mesh of protein threads that plugs the wound. Learn the stages in order.
[bio29-s4.6.0] 1. A blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.6.1] 2. Platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug.
[bio29-s4.6.2] 3. The platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors.
[bio29-s4.6.3] 4. A series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver with the help of vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin.
[bio29-s4.6.4] 5. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin.
[bio29-s4.6.5] 6. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot.
[bio29-s4.6.6] 7. At the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs. Calcium and vitamin K are both needed for clotting. Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing.
[bio29-s4.6.7] An unwanted clot inside a vessel, a thrombosis, can block an artery and cause a heart attack or stroke. Diagram: a flow chart of damage, platelets, clotting factors (with Ca²⁺ and vitamin K), prothrombin, thrombin, fibrinogen, fibrin mesh and clot. Add fibrin threads trapping red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.7.b] Your ABO blood group depends on molecules on the surface of your red blood cells. The plasma then carries antibodies matched to whichever molecules your own cells lack.
[bio29-s4.7.0] A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person's red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response. In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
[bio29-s4.7.1] The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen or antigens that a person's own red cells lack. This stops the body attacking its own cells.
[bio29-s4.7.2] Group A has antigen A on the red cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.3] Group AB has both antigens and no antibodies. Group O has no antigens and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.4] Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody, for example A cells meeting anti-A, the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps.
[bio29-s4.7.5] In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed, or burst. This can cause kidney failure and death.
[bio29-s4.7.6] Diagram: four red blood cells, one for each group, showing the antigens on the membrane (A, B, both, none), with the antibodies in the plasma written beside each. Add a sketch of agglutination, with antibodies linking red cells into a clump.
[bio29-s4.8.b] Transfusion works only if the donor's red cells do not meet an antibody in the recipient's plasma that matches their antigens. The rule follows from the ABO antigens and antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.0] What matters is the donor's red-cell antigens and the recipient's antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen against which their own plasma has antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.1] Group O red cells carry no antigens, so they can be given to anyone, and group O is called the universal donor. This applies to red cells: group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B.
[bio29-s4.8.2] Group AB people have no antibodies, so they can receive red cells of any ABO group, and group AB is the universal recipient. In practice, hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
[bio29-s4.8.3] Compatibility: group O can receive O only; group A can receive A or O; group B can receive B or O; group AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
[bio29-s4.8.4] Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and with anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only means group A; clumping with anti-B only means group B; clumping with both means AB; clumping with neither means O.
[bio29-s4.8.5] A third test, with anti-D, tests the Rhesus group.
[bio29-s4.9.b] The Rhesus factor is a second red-cell antigen system, on top of ABO. Unlike the ABO antibodies, the Rhesus antibody has to be learned by the immune system first.
[bio29-s4.9.0] The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+), and those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−).
[bio29-s4.9.1] A person's full blood group combines both systems, for example O negative or A positive.
[bio29-s4.9.2] Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present. A Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood, for example through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy.
[bio29-s4.9.3] A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
[bio29-s4.10.b] A Rhesus negative mother carrying a Rhesus positive baby can become sensitised to her baby's blood, and that may endanger a later baby. The problem is called haemolytic disease of the newborn, and it can be prevented.
[bio29-s4.10.0] 1. A Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby, inherited from a Rh+ father.
[bio29-s4.10.1] 2. Normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth, or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury, some fetal red cells enter the mother's blood.
[bio29-s4.10.2] 3. Her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells, and she is now sensitised. The first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made.
[bio29-s4.10.3] 4. In a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.10.4] 5. The baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage or death.
[bio29-s4.10.5] Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies, anti-D immunoglobulin, during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby, or after a miscarriage or injury.
[bio29-s4.10.6] These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells. The injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks.
[bio29-s4.10.7] This is artificial passive immunity (antibodies given ready-made, not made by the body). Every pregnant woman's blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason.
[bio29-s4.11.b] Knowing a person's blood group saves lives in several ways, from transfusions to pregnancy care. Each use below comes from knowing the groups.
[bio29-s4.11.0] Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient's ABO and Rhesus groups are tested, and the donor blood is cross-matched, meaning it is mixed with the patient's plasma to check that no clumping occurs.
[bio29-s4.11.1] This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and allows transfusions in surgery, after accidents, in childbirth, for anaemia and in cancer treatment.
[bio29-s4.11.2] Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
[bio29-s4.11.3] Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
[bio29-s4.11.4] Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks. Donors of all groups are needed, because blood can be stored only for a limited time.
[bio29-s4.11.5] In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service, and donated blood is screened for infections.
[bio29-s4.11.6] Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group and tissue type to reduce the chance of rejection.
[bio29-s4.11.7] Nature of Science: before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, which made safe transfusion possible.
[bio29-s4.11.8] In 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after the rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.

CANDIDATES bio7-s2: Microscopy
[bio7-s2.0.b] Microscopes do two different jobs: they make things look bigger, and they let you see fine detail. Students often mix these up, but only the second one limits what a light microscope can show.
[bio7-s2.0.0] Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the real object, so a ×100 image is 100 times the real size.
[bio7-s2.0.1] Resolution (also called resolving power) is the ability to show two points that are close together as separate points. It decides how much detail can be seen.
[bio7-s2.0.2] Magnifying an image beyond the resolution limit only makes it bigger and blurrier, not more detailed, because the two close points have already merged into one blur.
[bio7-s2.0.3] Total magnification of a light microscope = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification. For example, a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.
[bio7-s2.1.b] The light (optical) microscope passes light through a thin specimen and uses glass lenses to enlarge the image. These parts form the image and bring it into focus.
[bio7-s2.1.0] Eyepiece (ocular) lens: the lens you look through. It magnifies the image from the objective, usually ×10.
[bio7-s2.1.1] Objective lenses: mounted on a revolving nosepiece so they can be swapped. They are usually ×4 (scanning), ×10 (low power) and ×40 (high power), sometimes ×100 (oil immersion). They produce the first magnified image.
[bio7-s2.1.2] Stage with clips: the platform that holds the slide in place over the hole through which light passes.
[bio7-s2.1.3] Coarse focus knob: moves the stage (or tube) a long way quickly, so it is used for focusing at low power only.
[bio7-s2.1.4] Fine focus knob: moves the stage or tube a small distance to give a sharp image. It is the only focus used at high power.
[bio7-s2.2.b] Light has to reach the specimen in the right amount and direction, and the instrument needs a stable frame. These parts do that.
[bio7-s2.2.0] Light source: a built-in lamp, or a mirror that reflects light up through the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.1] Condenser: a lens under the stage that focuses light onto the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.2] Diaphragm (iris): adjusts the amount of light reaching the specimen. Less light often improves contrast for pale, unstained cells, which are nearly transparent and look washed out in bright light.
[bio7-s2.2.3] Arm and base: support the microscope. The arm is used for carrying it.
[bio7-s2.2.4] Diagram: a light microscope labelled eyepiece, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source (or mirror), coarse focus knob, fine focus knob, arm and base.
[bio7-s2.3.b] The method is built to protect the slide and the lenses. You always start on the lowest power, which has the widest view and the most room between lens and slide.
[bio7-s2.3.0] 1. Carry the microscope with one hand on the arm and the other under the base, so its weight is supported.
[bio7-s2.3.1] 2. Switch on the light and turn the lowest-power objective into place.
[bio7-s2.3.2] 3. Clip the slide on the stage with the specimen over the hole, so light can pass through it.
[bio7-s2.3.3] 4. Looking from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide without touching it. Watching from the side stops you grinding the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.3.4] 5. Looking through the eyepiece, focus by moving the objective and slide apart until the image is clear.
[bio7-s2.3.5] 6. Use the fine focus to sharpen the image, and the diaphragm to adjust the light.
[bio7-s2.3.6] 7. Move the part to be studied to the centre of the field of view (the circle of specimen you can see), so it stays in view when you increase magnification.
[bio7-s2.3.7] 8. Turn the next objective into place and use only the fine focus.
[bio7-s2.3.8] 9. Record the total magnification with every drawing.
[bio7-s2.4.b] The image behaves in predictable ways as you change objective, and one safety rule matters for your eyes.
[bio7-s2.4.0] The image is inverted, meaning upside down and reversed left to right. This means moving the slide to the left moves the image to the right.
[bio7-s2.4.1] At higher magnification the field of view is smaller and the image is dimmer, so more light is needed.
[bio7-s2.4.2] At high power the objective is very close to the slide, which is why the coarse focus must not be used: it could crash the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.4.3] Never use a mirror to reflect direct sunlight into the microscope, as it can damage the eye.
[bio7-s2.5.b] To view a specimen with light passing through it, you mount it on a glass slide. The technique below is the general method that any slide uses.
[bio7-s2.5.0] A wet mount: the specimen is placed in a drop of water or stain on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip (a thin square of glass).
[bio7-s2.5.1] The specimen must be very thin, ideally one layer of cells thick, so that light can pass through it and the cells do not overlap.
[bio7-s2.5.2] Stains add contrast to transparent structures by colouring some parts more than others. Iodine solution is used for plant cells: it stains the nucleus yellow-brown and turns starch blue-black. Methylene blue is used for animal cells: it stains the nucleus dark blue.
[bio7-s2.5.3] Lowering the coverslip: touch one edge of the coverslip to the liquid at an angle of about 45° and lower it slowly with a mounted needle. This pushes air out ahead of it and avoids air bubbles, which appear as black-edged circles and can hide cells.
[bio7-s2.5.4] The coverslip keeps the specimen flat, stops it drying out, and protects the objective lens from the liquid. Excess liquid is removed by touching filter paper or a paper towel to the edge.
[bio7-s2.5.5] Stain can also be added after the coverslip is on. Put a drop at one edge and draw it under the coverslip with filter paper held at the opposite edge (irrigation). There is more on the full procedures in Investigation: plant and animal cells.
[bio7-s2.6.b] A light microscope cannot resolve anything much smaller than 0.2 µm. Electron microscopes get round this by using electrons instead of light, so they show far finer detail.
[bio7-s2.6.0] An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by electromagnets instead of glass lenses. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, so resolution is far better.
[bio7-s2.6.1] The specimen is in a vacuum, because air would scatter the electrons, so it must be dead. The image is viewed on a screen and recorded digitally. It is black and white, and colour is added afterwards (false colour).
[bio7-s2.6.2] Transmission electron microscope (TEM): electrons pass through an extremely thin, stained section of a specimen. It gives a flat (2D) image of internal structure at the highest magnifications, e.g. mitochondria, ribosomes, nuclear pores and viruses.
[bio7-s2.6.3] Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a beam of electrons scans across the surface of a specimen coated with a very thin layer of metal, e.g. gold. Electrons bouncing off it form an image. It gives a 3D image of surfaces, e.g. the surface of cells, pollen grains and insects.
[bio7-s2.6.4] The SEM has lower resolution than the TEM and shows no internal structure unless the specimen is broken open.
[bio7-s2.6.5] The first electron microscope was built in Germany in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska.
[bio7-s2.7.b] The two types differ in how they work, what they can resolve and what they demand of the specimen. Compare them point by point.
[bio7-s2.7.0] Radiation: light for the light microscope, electrons for the electron microscope. Lenses: glass for the light microscope, electromagnets for the electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.1] Maximum useful magnification: about ×1000–1500 for the light microscope, compared with hundreds of thousands of times or more for electron microscopes.
[bio7-s2.7.2] Resolution: about 0.2 µm (200 nm) for the light microscope, compared with less than 1 nm for the TEM, roughly a thousand times better.
[bio7-s2.7.3] So the light microscope shows the nucleus, cell wall, vacuole and chloroplasts. Ribosomes, nuclear pores, the inner structure of mitochondria and viruses are too small and need an electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.4] Specimens: the light microscope can view living or dead specimens, with colour visible. The electron microscope needs dead specimens in a vacuum, and its images are black and white.
[bio7-s2.7.5] Preparation and cost: light microscopes are simple, quick, cheap and portable. Electron microscopes need complex preparation (thin sectioning, metal coating) that can create artefacts, structures that are not really in the living cell. They are very expensive, large, and need trained operators.
[bio7-s2.8.b] Microscopy has a long history and is still developing. Newer techniques tag, scan or freeze specimens to reveal what older methods could not.
[bio7-s2.8.0] Robert Hooke described and named “cells” from thin slices of cork in his book Micrographia (1665).
[bio7-s2.8.1] Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using his own single-lens microscopes, was the first to see and describe bacteria and other single-celled organisms, in the 1670s.
[bio7-s2.8.2] Fluorescence microscopy: specific molecules are tagged with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins that glow when lit with a particular colour of light. This shows exactly where a protein is in a living cell.
[bio7-s2.8.3] Green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a jellyfish, earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.
[bio7-s2.8.4] Confocal laser scanning microscopy: a laser scans one thin plane of the specimen at a time, giving sharp images that a computer combines into a 3D model.
[bio7-s2.8.5] Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy overcomes the light microscope’s normal resolution limit. It earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William Moerner.
[bio7-s2.8.6] Cryo-electron microscopy: samples are flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice instead of being dried and stained, so molecules keep their natural shape. It shows the structure of proteins and viruses in near-atomic detail.
[bio7-s2.8.7] Cryo-electron microscopy earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson.
[bio7-s2.8.8] Cryo-electron microscopy was used to show the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which helped vaccine design.
[bio7-s2.9.b] Microscopes are everyday tools in medicine, research and forensics, and modern imaging adds automation.
[bio7-s2.9.0] Uses in health and technology: examining blood smears and biopsies to diagnose disease, including spotting cancer cells; identifying pathogens; research into how cells and viruses work; and forensic science, e.g. comparing hairs and fibres.
[bio7-s2.9.1] Digital cameras and computer image analysis now allow images to be measured, shared and analysed automatically.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.
