CANDIDATES bio18-s3: The appendicular skeleton
[bio18-s3.0.b] The appendicular skeleton is the skeleton of the limbs and of the two girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton (the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum). It is the part of the skeleton that lets you reach, walk and run.
[bio18-s3.0.0] A girdle is a ring or half-ring of bones that anchors a pair of limbs to the trunk. The pectoral (shoulder) girdle attaches the arms, and the pelvic (hip) girdle attaches the legs.
[bio18-s3.0.1] Its main job is movement, because the limb bones act as levers for the muscles that pull on them. The legs and pelvic girdle also bear the body's weight, so they are built for strength as well as movement.
[bio18-s3.1.b] Most of the limb bones are long bones, and their structure shows how a bone can be both strong and light. Knowing the layers of a long bone also lets you label a section through one.
[bio18-s3.1.0] The limb bones named in the specification, except the patella, are long bones: a long shaft with a wider end at each joint. The patella is a small flat bone, so it is the exception.
[bio18-s3.1.1] The shaft is a hollow tube of dense compact bone. A tube is nearly as strong as a solid rod of the same width but much lighter, so the limb is strong but light enough to move quickly.
[bio18-s3.1.2] The wider ends are made of spongy bone, a lattice of thin bony struts that is light but strong along the lines of force, and that contains red bone marrow.
[bio18-s3.1.3] The central cavity of the shaft (the marrow cavity) contains yellow marrow.
[bio18-s3.1.4] The ends are covered with smooth articular cartilage (cartilage covering the joint surface) where they form joints. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.1.5] Apart from the joint surfaces, the whole bone is covered by a tough membrane, the periosteum, to which tendons (which join muscle to bone) and ligaments (which join bone to bone) attach.
[bio18-s3.1.6] Diagram: a long bone (the femur) in longitudinal section, labelling compact bone, spongy bone, marrow cavity with yellow marrow, red marrow in the spongy bone, articular cartilage, periosteum and shaft.
[bio18-s3.2.b] The pectoral girdle is the shoulder girdle that hangs the arm from the trunk. Its design favours freedom of movement over strength, which is the opposite of the pelvic girdle.
[bio18-s3.2.0] The pectoral girdle is formed on each side by a clavicle and a scapula. It attaches the arm to the axial skeleton and is built for a wide range of movement rather than for bearing weight.
[bio18-s3.2.1] The clavicle (collarbone) is a slender, S-shaped bone running across the top of the chest from the sternum (breastbone) to the scapula.
[bio18-s3.2.2] The clavicle acts as a strut, holding the shoulder out to the side so the arm can swing freely, and passes forces from the arm to the axial skeleton.
[bio18-s3.2.3] Its joint with the sternum is the only bony connection between the arm and the axial skeleton, which is why the arm is so mobile.
[bio18-s3.2.4] The scapula (shoulder blade) is a large, flat, triangular bone lying on the back of the rib cage.
[bio18-s3.2.5] The scapula is not joined to the ribs or vertebrae but is held in place by muscles, so it can slide and rotate, increasing the range of movement of the arm.
[bio18-s3.2.6] Its large flat surface and ridges give a large area for attachment of shoulder and arm muscles, including the biceps and triceps. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.2.7] The scapula has a shallow socket into which the rounded head of the humerus (the upper arm bone) fits, forming the shoulder joint, a ball-and-socket joint. The shallow socket allows the arm to move in almost every direction, including rotation, but makes the joint less stable than the hip.
[bio18-s3.3.b] The pelvic girdle is the hip girdle that joins the legs to the trunk. Because the legs carry the whole body, it is built for stability and strength rather than range of movement.
[bio18-s3.3.0] The pelvic girdle (pelvis) is a strong, bowl-shaped ring formed by the two hip bones, joined firmly to the sacrum (the base of the vertebral column) at the back and to each other at the front.
[bio18-s3.3.1] Each hip bone is made of several bones fused together; the names of these bones are not required.
[bio18-s3.3.2] The pelvic girdle is joined firmly to the vertebral column through the sacrum, so it is rigid. It carries the weight of the upper body and passes it to the legs, which makes it much stronger and less movable than the pectoral girdle.
[bio18-s3.3.3] It has a deep, cup-shaped socket on each side into which the head of the femur (the thigh bone) fits, forming the hip joint, a ball-and-socket joint.
[bio18-s3.3.4] The deep socket makes the hip very stable and suited to bearing weight, but it gives a smaller range of movement than the shoulder.
[bio18-s3.3.5] The pelvic girdle protects the organs of the lower abdomen: the bladder, lower intestine and reproductive organs, including the uterus.
[bio18-s3.3.6] Its broad surfaces anchor the large muscles of the legs and trunk.
[bio18-s3.3.7] The female pelvis is wider and shallower, with a larger, rounder central opening than the male pelvis, so that a baby's head can pass through during birth.
[bio18-s3.4.b] The two girdles do different jobs, and each difference in structure explains a difference in function. Set them side by side to answer comparison questions.
[bio18-s3.4.0] Attachment: the pectoral girdle is loosely attached to the axial skeleton by muscles, so the arm is free to move; the pelvic girdle is fused to the sacrum, so it is rigid.
[bio18-s3.4.1] Socket: the pectoral girdle has a shallow socket, which allows a wide range of movement; the pelvic girdle has a deep socket, which holds the femur firmly.
[bio18-s3.4.2] Function: the pectoral girdle gives mobility; the pelvic girdle gives stability and carries weight.
[bio18-s3.5.b] The arm has one bone in the upper arm and two in the forearm. Each joint between them is a different type, which gives the arm both stability and mobility.
[bio18-s3.5.0] The humerus is the long bone of the upper arm. Its rounded head fits into the socket of the scapula at the shoulder, a ball-and-socket joint.
[bio18-s3.5.1] The lower end of the humerus meets the radius and ulna at the elbow, a hinge joint (a joint that bends in one direction only). Its long shaft gives attachment to the muscles that move the shoulder and elbow.
[bio18-s3.5.2] The ulna is the longer forearm bone, on the little-finger side.
[bio18-s3.5.3] The upper end of the ulna hooks around the end of the humerus, forming the main part of the elbow hinge and the point of the elbow, which stops the arm bending backwards beyond straight.
[bio18-s3.5.4] The triceps (the muscle at the back of the upper arm) is attached to the ulna. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.5.5] The radius is the shorter forearm bone, on the thumb side. It forms most of the joint with the wrist.
[bio18-s3.5.6] At the elbow, the rounded head of the radius turns against the ulna in a pivot joint, so the radius can rotate around the ulna to turn the palm up or down. The biceps (the muscle at the front of the upper arm) is attached to the radius.
[bio18-s3.5.7] The two forearm bones make the hand both strong and highly mobile: the ulna gives a stable hinge at the elbow, and the radius allows rotation of the hand.
[bio18-s3.6.b] The leg has one bone in the thigh and two in the lower leg, plus the patella at the knee. These bones carry the body's weight and drive walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s3.6.0] The femur (thigh bone) is the longest and strongest bone in the body.
[bio18-s3.6.1] The rounded head of the femur fits into the deep socket of the pelvic girdle at the hip. Its lower end forms the knee joint, a hinge joint, with the tibia.
[bio18-s3.6.2] The thick shaft of compact bone carries the body's weight, and it gives attachment to the large thigh muscles used in walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s3.6.3] The patella (kneecap) is a small, flat, roughly triangular bone at the front of the knee, embedded in the tendon of the quadriceps, the large muscle at the front of the thigh.
[bio18-s3.6.4] The patella protects the front of the knee joint and holds the tendon away from the joint, improving the leverage of the quadriceps when it straightens the leg.
[bio18-s3.6.5] The tibia (shinbone) is the larger, inner bone of the lower leg, on the big-toe side. It forms the knee joint with the femur and the ankle joint below, and carries almost all the weight passed down from the femur.
[bio18-s3.6.6] The fibula is the thin outer bone of the lower leg. It is not part of the knee joint and carries little weight, but it provides attachment for muscles of the lower leg and helps form and stabilise the outer side of the ankle.
[bio18-s3.6.7] The wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones are not named in the specification; label them on diagrams as wrist bones, hand bones, ankle bones and foot bones. Many small bones give the hands their fine, flexible movements and give the feet a flexible, weight-spreading base.
[bio18-s3.7.b] The arm and the leg look different, but they follow the same basic plan. This similarity is a useful pattern to spot on a skeleton diagram.
[bio18-s3.7.0] The arm and the leg are built to the same plan: one bone in the upper part (humerus; femur), two in the lower part (radius and ulna; tibia and fibula), then many small bones in the wrist or ankle and five digits.
[bio18-s3.7.1] The same plan in the forelimbs of other vertebrates is evidence of common ancestry, because it suggests the animals inherited the pattern from a shared ancestor. There is more on this in Explaining biological phenomena.
[bio18-s3.8.b] Putting the axial and appendicular parts together on one diagram shows how the girdles and limbs attach to the central skeleton.
[bio18-s3.8.0] Diagram: front view of the whole human skeleton, labelling the axial parts (skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum) and the appendicular parts named in the specification (clavicle, scapula, pelvic girdle, humerus, radius, ulna, femur, patella, tibia and fibula), with the wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones labelled as groups.

CANDIDATES bio18-s4: Muscles and joints
[bio18-s4.0.b] Bones only help the body move where they meet other bones. These meeting places are called joints, and they are grouped by how much movement they allow.
[bio18-s4.0.0] A joint is a place where two or more bones meet. Joints are grouped by how much movement they allow, from none at all to a wide range.
[bio18-s4.0.1] Fixed (immovable) joints join bones with fibrous tissue and allow no movement, so they protect and hold structures rigid, for example the sutures of the cranium (the joins between the bones of the skull).
[bio18-s4.0.2] Slightly movable (cartilaginous) joints join bones with cartilage (a firm, flexible tissue) and allow a little movement, for example between vertebrae, which are joined by intervertebral discs. There is more on this in The axial skeleton.
[bio18-s4.0.3] Freely movable (synovial) joints have bones separated by a fluid-filled cavity and allow a wide range of movement, for example the shoulder, elbow, hip and knee.
[bio18-s4.1.b] Not all freely movable joints move in the same way. Their types are named after the shape of the surfaces and the movement this allows.
[bio18-s4.1.0] Ball-and-socket joints (shoulder, hip): a rounded head fits into a cup, so movement is possible in all directions and the limb can also rotate.
[bio18-s4.1.1] Hinge joints (elbow, knee): movement is in one plane only, bending and straightening, like a door opening and closing on its hinge.
[bio18-s4.1.2] Pivot joints: one bone rotates around another, for example the atlas (the top vertebra) turning on the axis (the second vertebra) to turn the head, and the radius turning against the ulna to rotate the forearm.
[bio18-s4.1.3] Gliding joints: flat surfaces slide over each other, for example between the small wrist and ankle bones, so only small sliding movements are possible.
[bio18-s4.2.b] A synovial joint has to move smoothly thousands of times without the bone ends wearing away. Three parts working together make this possible: the cartilage on the bone ends, the membrane that makes the fluid, and the fluid itself.
[bio18-s4.2.0] Articular cartilage (hyaline cartilage) is a smooth, slippery, slightly compressible layer covering the ends of the bones inside the joint.
[bio18-s4.2.1] Articular cartilage reduces friction so the bones glide over each other, absorbs shock to cushion the bones against impact, and prevents the bone ends from wearing each other away.
[bio18-s4.2.2] Cartilage has no blood vessels, so it gets its nutrients from the synovial fluid and heals slowly if it is damaged.
[bio18-s4.2.3] The synovial membrane is a thin membrane lining the inside of the joint capsule, but it does not cover the cartilage. It secretes synovial fluid into the joint cavity.
[bio18-s4.2.4] Synovial fluid is a thick, slippery fluid filling the joint cavity. It lubricates the joint, reducing friction and wear, and helps absorb shock.
[bio18-s4.2.5] Synovial fluid also supplies oxygen and nutrients to the cartilage and removes its wastes, which is how cartilage stays alive without a blood supply of its own.
[bio18-s4.3.b] Smooth surfaces are not enough: the joint must also be held together and be moved by muscles. The capsule and ligaments hold the bones in place, tendons connect the muscles that move them, and in the knee fibrocartilage pads add extra cushioning.
[bio18-s4.3.0] The joint capsule is a tough, fibrous sleeve that encloses the joint, holds the synovial fluid in and helps hold the bones together. It is strengthened by ligaments.
[bio18-s4.3.1] Ligaments are tough, strong bands of fibrous connective tissue, mainly collagen with some elastic fibres, that join bone to bone across the joint.
[bio18-s4.3.2] Ligaments hold the bones of the joint together and keep them in line. Being slightly elastic, they allow the joint to move in its normal directions while limiting movement in others, which prevents dislocation (the bones being forced out of position).
[bio18-s4.3.3] Some ligaments lie outside the capsule and some inside the joint, for example inside the knee.
[bio18-s4.3.4] Tendons are tough, strong, inelastic (non-stretchy) cords of fibrous connective tissue, mainly collagen, that join muscle to bone. The Achilles tendon, for example, joins the calf muscle to the heel bone.
[bio18-s4.3.5] A tendon transmits the pull of a contracting muscle to the bone, so the bone moves at the joint. Because it does not stretch, all of the muscle's pull goes into moving the bone.
[bio18-s4.3.6] Pads of fibrocartilage (cartilage with extra tough fibres) inside some joints, the menisci in the knee, deepen the joint surfaces, spread the load and absorb shock.
[bio18-s4.3.7] Diagram: a generalised synovial joint (or the knee or hip) in section, labelling the two bones, articular cartilage, joint cavity with synovial fluid, synovial membrane, joint capsule, ligament, and a tendon attaching a muscle to one bone.
[bio18-s4.4.b] The knee shows how every part of a synovial joint has its own job and how they combine to let the leg move without the joint falling apart.
[bio18-s4.4.0] At the knee, articular cartilage on the femur (thigh bone), tibia (shin bone) and the back of the patella (kneecap), together with the synovial fluid, lets the bones glide over each other.
[bio18-s4.4.1] Ligaments inside and outside the joint keep the femur and tibia aligned and stop the knee bending sideways or backwards.
[bio18-s4.4.2] The quadriceps tendon, which contains the patella, passes the pull of the thigh muscle to the tibia to straighten the leg.
[bio18-s4.5.b] Ligaments, tendons and cartilage are all tough tissues found at joints, so they are easily confused. They differ in what they join, how much they stretch and the job they do.
[bio18-s4.5.0] What each joins: a ligament joins bone to bone; a tendon joins muscle to bone; cartilage either covers the ends of bones (articular cartilage) or joins bones (discs).
[bio18-s4.5.1] Elasticity: a ligament is slightly elastic, allowing some movement; a tendon is inelastic, so it transmits force without wasting it; cartilage is firm but compressible, so it absorbs shock.
[bio18-s4.5.2] Main function: a ligament holds the joint together and prevents dislocation; a tendon moves the bone when the muscle contracts; cartilage reduces friction and absorbs shock.
[bio18-s4.6.b] Muscles can only do one thing: shorten. That limitation is why the body arranges muscles in opposing pairs around each joint.
[bio18-s4.6.0] A muscle can only contract and pull, never push, so each movement at a joint needs a second muscle to pull the bone back.
[bio18-s4.6.1] An antagonistic pair is two muscles that produce opposite movements at the same joint: when one contracts, the other relaxes and is stretched.
[bio18-s4.6.2] The flexor bends a joint (flexion); the extensor straightens it (extension).
[bio18-s4.6.3] Each muscle is attached by tendons at both ends. The origin is the attachment to the bone that stays still; the insertion is the attachment to the bone that moves.
[bio18-s4.6.4] When the muscle contracts it shortens (and becomes fatter), pulling the insertion towards the origin and so moving the bone.
[bio18-s4.7.b] The biceps and triceps in the upper arm are the standard example of an antagonistic pair, and the thigh muscles at the knee are a second one.
[bio18-s4.7.0] At the elbow, the biceps lies at the front of the upper arm, with its origin on the scapula (shoulder blade) and its insertion on the radius.
[bio18-s4.7.1] The triceps lies at the back of the upper arm, with its origin on the scapula and humerus and its insertion on the ulna.
[bio18-s4.7.2] Bending the arm (flexion): the biceps contracts and shortens, pulling the radius and forearm up, while the triceps relaxes and is stretched. The biceps is the flexor.
[bio18-s4.7.3] Straightening the arm (extension): the triceps contracts, pulling the ulna and forearm down, while the biceps relaxes and is stretched. The triceps is the extensor.
[bio18-s4.7.4] A second example is the knee: the quadriceps (front of the thigh) contracts to straighten the leg, as the extensor, and the hamstrings (back of the thigh) contract to bend it, as the flexor.
[bio18-s4.8.b] The arm works as a lever, and the nervous system controls the pair so that movement is smooth or the joint is held still.
[bio18-s4.8.0] Lever action: the forearm acts as a lever with the elbow joint as the pivot (the fixed point it turns around).
[bio18-s4.8.1] The biceps is inserted close to the elbow, so a small shortening of the muscle moves the hand through a large distance quickly, at the cost of needing a large muscle force.
[bio18-s4.8.2] Coordination: the nervous system sends impulses to one muscle of the pair while the other is allowed to relax, so movements are smooth.
[bio18-s4.8.3] Both muscles of a pair contracting slightly together hold a joint steady in one position, for example when holding a weight still.
[bio18-s4.8.4] Diagram: two drawings of the arm, bent and straight, showing scapula, humerus, radius, ulna, elbow joint, biceps and triceps with their tendons, each muscle labelled contracted or relaxed in each position, with arrows showing the direction of pull.
[bio18-s4.9.b] A simple model of the arm lets you see antagonistic muscles work. Each part of the model stands for a real part of the arm.
[bio18-s4.9.0] A simple physical model uses two strips of stiff card or wood to represent the upper arm (humerus) and the forearm (radius and ulna), joined at one end by a split pin, which represents the elbow hinge joint.
[bio18-s4.9.1] Two pieces of string, elastic bands or long balloons represent the biceps and triceps. Each is fixed to the upper arm at one end (origin) and to the forearm at the other (insertion), one on the front and one on the back, with their ends standing for tendons.
[bio18-s4.9.2] Pulling (shortening) the front string raises the forearm while the back string slackens. This shows the biceps contracting and the triceps relaxing in flexion.
[bio18-s4.9.3] Pulling the back string lowers the forearm, showing extension.
[bio18-s4.9.4] Pushing on a string does nothing, which shows that a muscle can only pull.
[bio18-s4.9.5] The model shows that muscles work in pairs with opposite effects, that a muscle must be attached on both sides of a joint to move it, and that an insertion close to the joint gives a large movement of the forearm end (the lever action).
[bio18-s4.10.b] No model is perfect. Judging a model means saying what it leaves out or gets wrong compared with the real arm.
[bio18-s4.10.0] Real muscles shorten actively by contracting and get fatter, while the model's strings are pulled by hand.
[bio18-s4.10.1] The model has no articular cartilage, synovial fluid or ligaments, so it does not show how the joint surfaces glide or how the bones are held together.
[bio18-s4.10.2] A split pin allows movement in only one plane and does not show the rotation of the radius.
[bio18-s4.10.3] The model has no nervous control and no energy supply (ATP), whereas real muscles need impulses to contract and ATP as their energy source.
[bio18-s4.10.4] Real tendons are inelastic, while elastic bands stretch.

CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio20-s1.10.5] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; oestrogen; female secondary sexual characteristics and thickening of the endometrium.
[bio20-s1.10.6] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; progesterone; maintains the endometrium and pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio23-s1: The male reproductive system
[bio23-s1.0.b] The male reproductive system has two jobs: making sperm and a hormone, and getting the sperm to the female. Knowing the route sperm take makes each organ's role easy to place.
[bio23-s1.0.0] The male reproductive system produces sperm, the male gametes (sex cells), and the hormone testosterone, and delivers sperm into the female's vagina during sexual intercourse, so that a sperm can fertilise an egg.
[bio23-s1.0.1] Sperm pass in order: testis → epididymis → vas deferens → urethra (where fluids from the seminal vesicles and prostate are added) → out through the penis. Sperm are made in the testis, so that is the starting point.
[bio23-s1.0.2] Semen is sperm suspended in seminal fluid. Seminal fluid, made by the seminal vesicles and the prostate, gives the sperm a liquid to swim in and supplies an energy source.
[bio23-s1.0.3] Seminal fluid is slightly alkaline overall, which neutralises the acidity of the vagina and of any urine left in the urethra, because acid damages sperm.
[bio23-s1.0.4] Sperm make up only a small part of the volume of semen, because most of it is seminal fluid.
[bio23-s1.1.b] Sperm cannot be made at normal body temperature, so the testes hang outside the body. The scrotum is the sac that holds them and keeps them at the right temperature.
[bio23-s1.1.0] Scrotum: a sac of skin and muscle hanging outside the abdomen that holds the two testes. Muscles in it pull the testes closer to the body when it is cold, to keep them warm, and relax to let them hang lower when it is warm.
[bio23-s1.1.1] The scrotum keeps the testes about 2–3 °C below core body temperature (37 °C), which sperm production needs; this is why the testes are outside the body.
[bio23-s1.2.b] The testes are where sperm and testosterone are made. Inside, two different tissues do these two jobs.
[bio23-s1.2.0] Testes (singular testis): the two oval male gonads, which are the primary sex organs (the organs that make the gametes).
[bio23-s1.2.1] Each testis is packed with many long, tightly coiled seminiferous tubules, whose walls produce sperm by meiosis (cell division that halves the chromosome number), continuously from puberty.
[bio23-s1.2.2] The coiling packs a very long length of tubule, and so a large sperm-producing surface, into a small organ, because a long tube can fit in little space when coiled.
[bio23-s1.2.3] Between the seminiferous tubules are interstitial cells, which secrete testosterone into the blood; the testes are therefore also endocrine glands (glands that release hormones into the blood, so the hormone reaches the whole body).
[bio23-s1.2.4] Testosterone has roles in puberty, sperm production and the secondary sexual characteristics (the body changes that develop at puberty). There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s1.2.5] The testes form inside the abdomen of the foetus and normally descend into the scrotum before birth, which puts them in the cooler position sperm production needs.
[bio23-s1.2.6] A testis that has not descended cannot produce sperm normally, which is a known cause of male infertility.
[bio23-s1.3.b] Once made, sperm travel through a series of tubes. Each one has a different job: storage, transport and exit from the body.
[bio23-s1.3.0] Epididymis: a single, very long, tightly coiled tube lying against the back of each testis.
[bio23-s1.3.1] Sperm from the seminiferous tubules pass into the epididymis, where they are stored and mature, gaining the ability to swim. The coiling gives a long tube for storage in a small space.
[bio23-s1.3.2] Vas deferens (sperm duct; plural vasa deferentia): a muscular tube carrying sperm from the epididymis up into the abdomen and around the bladder, to join the urethra.
[bio23-s1.3.3] During ejaculation the smooth muscle of the vas deferens contracts in waves (peristalsis) to push sperm along, which is why the tube has a thick muscular wall.
[bio23-s1.3.4] The vas deferens is the tube that is cut and tied in a vasectomy, the male sterilisation operation, so that sperm can no longer reach the urethra.
[bio23-s1.3.5] Urethra: the tube running from the bladder through the length of the penis to the outside, so it is the final exit for both urine and semen.
[bio23-s1.3.6] In males the urethra carries both urine and semen, but never at the same time: during ejaculation a ring of muscle (sphincter) at the base of the bladder closes, so urine cannot mix with semen.
[bio23-s1.3.7] In females the urethra carries only urine and opens separately from the vagina. There is more on this in The female reproductive system.
[bio23-s1.4.b] Sperm need a liquid to swim in and an energy source. Two glands add fluids that supply these, so that sperm can swim and reach the egg.
[bio23-s1.4.0] Seminal vesicles: two glands behind the bladder whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
[bio23-s1.4.1] The seminal vesicles secrete a thick, alkaline fluid rich in the sugar fructose; the sperm's mitochondria respire fructose to release energy for swimming.
[bio23-s1.4.2] This fluid makes up most of the volume of semen, so the seminal vesicles supply the bulk of the liquid sperm swim in.
[bio23-s1.4.3] Prostate gland: a single gland, about the size of a walnut, just below the bladder, surrounding the start of the urethra.
[bio23-s1.4.4] The prostate secretes a thin, milky fluid containing enzymes that keep the semen liquid after ejaculation, so the sperm can swim free.
[bio23-s1.4.5] The secretions of the seminal vesicles and the prostate together form seminal fluid, which is why seminal fluid as a whole is slightly alkaline.
[bio23-s1.4.6] Because the prostate surrounds the urethra, an enlarged prostate, common in older men, can press on the urethra and make urination difficult.
[bio23-s1.5.b] The penis delivers semen into the vagina and also lets urine out. Its tip and the skin covering it have their own roles.
[bio23-s1.5.0] Penis: the male organ that places semen in the vagina during intercourse, and through which urine and semen leave the body via the urethra.
[bio23-s1.5.1] The penis contains spongy erectile tissue. During sexual arousal its blood vessels widen and the spaces fill with blood under pressure, so the penis becomes larger, firm and erect, which allows it to enter the vagina.
[bio23-s1.5.2] Glans: the rounded, sensitive tip of the penis, with many nerve endings; stimulation of these leads to ejaculation.
[bio23-s1.5.3] The urethra opens at the tip of the glans, so semen and urine leave the body there.
[bio23-s1.5.4] Foreskin: a fold of loose skin that covers and protects the glans when the penis is not erect, and pulls back (retracts) during an erection.
[bio23-s1.5.5] The foreskin is removed in circumcision, an operation that leaves the glans uncovered.
[bio23-s1.5.6] Ejaculation: at the peak of sexual stimulation, waves of muscle contraction in the epididymis, vasa deferentia, glands and urethra expel semen from the penis.
[bio23-s1.5.7] The semen from one ejaculation (a few millilitres) contains many millions of sperm, so many sperm are available to reach the egg.
[bio23-s1.6.b] Exams ask you to draw and label this system, so it helps to know where each part sits in relation to the bladder.
[bio23-s1.6.0] Diagram: side view (longitudinal section) with the bladder for orientation. Label testis, scrotum, epididymis, vas deferens (sperm duct), seminal vesicle, prostate gland, urethra, penis, glans, foreskin, bladder. Draw the vas deferens running up from the epididymis and looping up and over the ureter behind the bladder to join the urethra just below the bladder.
[bio23-s1.7.b] Each point gives the part, where it is, what it does and how its structure suits that job. They run in the order of the sperm pathway.
[bio23-s1.7.0] Scrotum – holds the testes outside the abdomen – keeps them 2–3 °C below body temperature – muscles pull the testes closer to the body in cold conditions.
[bio23-s1.7.1] Testis – the sperm-producing organ, inside the scrotum – makes sperm in the coiled seminiferous tubules and testosterone in the interstitial cells – very long coiled tubules give a large surface in a small organ.
[bio23-s1.7.2] Epididymis – on the back of each testis – stores and matures sperm so they can swim – a single very long, coiled tube that stores a lot in a small space.
[bio23-s1.7.3] Vas deferens – from the epididymis, up into the abdomen and around the bladder to the urethra – carries sperm along the route – a muscular tube whose walls contract in peristalsis.
[bio23-s1.8.b] The pathway continues with the glands that add fluid, then the urethra and the penis, which delivers the semen.
[bio23-s1.8.0] Seminal vesicle – behind the bladder – adds a thick, alkaline, fructose-rich fluid that gives energy and makes up most of the semen – a gland whose ducts join the vasa deferentia.
[bio23-s1.8.1] Prostate – below the bladder, around the urethra – adds an enzyme-containing fluid that keeps semen liquid – a gland that surrounds the start of the urethra.
[bio23-s1.8.2] Urethra – from the bladder through the penis – carries semen and urine, never together – a sphincter closes the bladder during ejaculation.
[bio23-s1.8.3] Penis – outside the body – places semen in the vagina – erectile tissue fills with blood so it becomes firm enough to enter the vagina.
[bio23-s1.8.4] Glans – tip of the penis – detects stimulation and leads to ejaculation – many nerve endings in its sensitive tip, and the urethra opens there.
[bio23-s1.8.5] Foreskin – fold of skin over the glans – protects the glans and pulls back during an erection – loose skin that retracts.

CANDIDATES bio23-s3: Gametes and fertilisation
[bio23-s3.0.b] Gametes are the cells that carry genes from one generation to the next, and they are made by a special kind of cell division. Knowing where this division happens, and which cells do it, is the starting point for sperm and egg production.
[bio23-s3.0.0] A gamete is a haploid sex cell, meaning it has only one chromosome of each pair. In humans the haploid number is 23 (n = 23).
[bio23-s3.0.1] Sperm and eggs are made by meiosis (the cell division that halves the chromosome number), so each gamete carries one chromosome of each pair, and each gamete is genetically different from the others. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio23-s3.0.2] At fertilisation two haploid gametes fuse to give a diploid zygote (the first cell of a new individual, with both chromosomes of each pair) with 46 chromosomes (2n = 46).
[bio23-s3.0.3] Meiosis happens only in the gonads (the organs that make gametes): in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes) for sperm, and in the ovaries for eggs.
[bio23-s3.0.4] The cells that undergo meiosis are diploid. Every other cell in the body divides by mitosis, which keeps the chromosome number the same.
[bio23-s3.1.b] Unlike eggs, sperm are made continuously, in huge numbers, from puberty onwards. The process happens in the testes and finishes in a storage tube beside them.
[bio23-s3.1.0] Sperm production begins at puberty and then continues every day throughout a man's life, though it declines in old age.
[bio23-s3.1.1] Many millions of sperm are made each day in the walls of the seminiferous tubules, from diploid cells that first multiply by mitosis (which keeps a supply of cells available) and then divide by meiosis.
[bio23-s3.1.2] Each cell that completes meiosis gives four sperm, all of equal size.
[bio23-s3.1.3] Half the sperm carry an X chromosome and half carry a Y, because the dividing cell has one X and one Y. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio23-s3.1.4] The new sperm move into the epididymis (a coiled tube on the back of each testis), where they are stored and mature, becoming able to swim.
[bio23-s3.1.5] It takes about two to three months for a sperm to be produced and matured, so a change in lifestyle or health affects sperm only after some weeks.
[bio23-s3.1.6] Production needs the lower temperature of the scrotum (the sac that holds the testes outside the body) and the hormones FSH and testosterone.
[bio23-s3.2.b] Egg production runs on a very different timetable from sperm production. A girl's eggs begin forming before she is born, and she never makes more.
[bio23-s3.2.0] Egg production starts before birth: while a girl is a foetus (an unborn baby), her ovaries form all the immature eggs she will ever have, each inside a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac of cells that nourishes the egg).
[bio23-s3.2.1] Each of these eggs begins meiosis but stops part-way.
[bio23-s3.2.2] A baby girl is born with about one to two million immature eggs. Most degenerate (break down), and only about 400–500 are ever released in her life.
[bio23-s3.2.3] No new eggs are made after birth, which is why the supply is fixed.
[bio23-s3.2.4] From puberty, usually one follicle each month develops fully and releases its egg at ovulation (the release of an egg from the ovary). The monthly cycle is dealt with in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s3.2.5] Meiosis continues around ovulation but is completed only if a sperm enters the egg, so an egg that is never fertilised never finishes meiosis.
[bio23-s3.3.b] Meiosis in the ovary does not split the cell evenly, and the eggs it makes are as old as the woman herself. Both facts matter for the early embryo and for fertility.
[bio23-s3.3.0] Division in egg production is unequal: almost all the cytoplasm goes to one cell, the egg, and the other products are tiny cells called polar bodies, which degenerate.
[bio23-s3.3.1] So one cell undergoing meiosis gives one egg, not four.
[bio23-s3.3.2] This unequal split gives the egg a large store of cytoplasm, food reserves and organelles (the small working structures inside a cell) to support the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.3.3] Because the eggs are as old as the woman, they age with her. In older women more eggs have errors in chromosome separation, which is why the risk of conditions such as Down syndrome rises with the mother's age. There is more on this in Mutations.
[bio23-s3.3.4] Fertility also falls with age because of these egg changes.
[bio23-s3.4.b] A sperm has one job, to carry the father's haploid nucleus to the egg. Every part of its structure helps it do that, so it is small, streamlined and powered for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.0] A sperm is a very small, mobile cell, about 0.05 mm long.
[bio23-s3.4.1] The head contains the haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes) and, at its tip, the acrosome, a cap containing enzymes that digest a path through the layers around the egg.
[bio23-s3.4.2] The middle piece is packed with mitochondria that release energy (ATP) by aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen) to power the tail.
[bio23-s3.4.3] The tail is a flagellum (a whip-like tail) that beats to make the sperm swim.
[bio23-s3.4.4] A sperm has very little cytoplasm, which keeps it small and streamlined for swimming.
[bio23-s3.4.5] Diagram: a sperm cell. Label the head, acrosome, nucleus, middle piece, mitochondria and tail.
[bio23-s3.5.b] The egg has a different job from the sperm. It cannot move, so its structure is built to supply and protect the new life once a sperm arrives.
[bio23-s3.5.0] The egg (ovum) is one of the largest cells in the body, about 0.1 mm across, just visible to the naked eye. It cannot move by itself.
[bio23-s3.5.1] It contains a haploid nucleus (23 chromosomes, always including an X), a cell membrane, and a large volume of cytoplasm holding food reserves and many mitochondria, which feed and power the early embryo.
[bio23-s3.5.2] Around the egg is a thick, clear, jelly-like coat called the zona pellucida, and outside that an outer layer of follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.5.3] Diagram: an egg, drawn much larger than the sperm. Label the nucleus, cytoplasm, cell membrane, zona pellucida (jelly coat) and follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.6.b] Sperm and eggs are both haploid gametes, but their differences follow from their jobs: the sperm travels and delivers, and the egg stores food and waits. Compare them in pairs, one feature at a time.
[bio23-s3.6.0] Size: sperm are tiny, about 0.05 mm long, while the egg is far larger, about 0.1 mm across, with a far greater volume.
[bio23-s3.6.1] Movement: the sperm swims with its tail, but the egg cannot swim and is moved along the fallopian tube by cilia (tiny hair-like structures lining the tube).
[bio23-s3.6.2] Numbers: there are millions of sperm per ejaculation, but usually one egg a month.
[bio23-s3.6.3] Cytoplasm and food: sperm have almost none, but the egg is a large store.
[bio23-s3.6.4] Sex chromosome: a sperm carries an X or a Y, but the egg always carries an X.
[bio23-s3.6.5] Production: sperm are made continuously from puberty, four per meiosis, while eggs begin to form before birth, one per meiosis.
[bio23-s3.7.b] Fertilisation needs sperm and egg to meet in the right place at the right time. Only a tiny fraction of sperm survive the journey, and the timing is tight because both gametes live for a limited time.
[bio23-s3.7.0] During intercourse semen (the fluid containing the sperm) is ejaculated into the top of the vagina, near the cervix (the neck of the uterus).
[bio23-s3.7.1] Sperm swim through the cervical mucus, which is easiest to pass around ovulation when it is thin, then through the uterus and into the fallopian tubes, helped by contractions of the uterus.
[bio23-s3.7.2] Only a few hundred of the millions of sperm reach the egg. Most die in the acidic vagina, are blocked by mucus, or go into the tube with no egg.
[bio23-s3.7.3] Sperm can survive up to about five days in the female tract.
[bio23-s3.7.4] An egg survives only about 12–24 hours after ovulation.
[bio23-s3.7.5] So intercourse in the five days before ovulation, or on the day of ovulation, can lead to fertilisation, because live sperm and a live egg are then present together.
[bio23-s3.8.b] Fertilisation is more than a sperm touching an egg. It is the fusion of two nuclei, and it happens in a set order that stops more than one sperm getting in.
[bio23-s3.8.0] Fertilisation is the fusion of the haploid nucleus of a sperm with the haploid nucleus of an egg to form a diploid zygote.
[bio23-s3.8.1] In humans it normally takes place in the upper part of a fallopian tube, within a day of ovulation.
[bio23-s3.8.2] 1. Sperm reach the egg and push between the follicle cells.
[bio23-s3.8.3] 2. The acrosome of a sperm releases its enzymes, which digest a path through the zona pellucida. This is called the acrosome reaction.
[bio23-s3.8.4] 3. The membrane of one sperm fuses with the egg's membrane, and the sperm's nucleus enters the egg. The tail is left outside, so the sperm's mitochondria stay out.
[bio23-s3.8.5] 4. At once the egg's outer layer changes and the zona pellucida hardens, so no other sperm can enter. This ensures the zygote receives only one set of chromosomes from the father.
[bio23-s3.8.6] 5. The egg completes its meiosis.
[bio23-s3.8.7] 6. The sperm nucleus and egg nucleus fuse, giving a zygote with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs, one of each pair from each parent.
[bio23-s3.9.b] Fertilisation settles several things at once: the child's sex, where its mitochondria come from, and the start of a new genetically unique individual.
[bio23-s3.9.0] At fertilisation the sex of the child is decided by whether the sperm carries an X or a Y, because the egg always carries an X. The zygote receives its mitochondria from the egg only. There is more on this in Sex determination and in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio23-s3.9.1] Fertilisation restores the diploid number, which would otherwise halve in every generation.
[bio23-s3.9.2] It combines genetic material from two parents, so the offspring is genetically unique. Together with meiosis, this is a source of variation.
[bio23-s3.9.3] Fertilisation starts the development of the embryo. The zygote begins to divide by mitosis as it moves down the tube. There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s3.9.4] Diagram: a sequence showing a sperm entering an egg (the acrosome digesting the zona pellucida), the sperm nucleus inside the egg, and the two nuclei fusing to form the zygote. Label the sperm nucleus, egg nucleus, zona pellucida, zygote, n and 2n.
[bio23-s3.10.b] Twins can arise in two different ways, and the difference explains why some twins are genetically identical and others are not.
[bio23-s3.10.0] Identical twins come from one zygote that splits into two embryos early in development, so they have the same genes.
[bio23-s3.10.1] Non-identical (fraternal) twins come from two eggs released at the same time and fertilised by two different sperm, so they are no more alike genetically than other brothers and sisters.

CANDIDATES bio23-s8: Fertility control and infertility
[bio23-s8.0.b] Fertility is the ability to produce offspring, but it is not available all the time in everyone. Males and females differ because sperm production runs continuously while egg release happens once per cycle.
[bio23-s8.0.0] A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.0.1] Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life, declining slowly in old age, and every ejaculation (release of semen) contains millions of sperm. This is why a man is fertile on any day.
[bio23-s8.0.2] Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle (ovulation), so fertility comes in a short window once each cycle rather than every day.
[bio23-s8.0.3] The egg survives only about 12–24 hours after release, while sperm can survive up to about five days in the female body.
[bio23-s8.0.4] So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation, when sperm already present can wait for the egg, and the day of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.0.5] In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because the day of ovulation varies from woman to woman and cycle to cycle, the window cannot be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
[bio23-s8.1.b] A woman's fertility is not the same at every age. It follows the state of her eggs, which she is born with and which do not renew.
[bio23-s8.1.0] Female fertility is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline.
[bio23-s8.1.1] Female fertility ends at the menopause, the time of life when ovulation and periods stop for good. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s8.2.b] Contraception means stopping a pregnancy from starting. Every method blocks one step on the route from egg release to implantation, and methods differ in how reliable they are and in what else they protect against.
[bio23-s8.2.0] Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.2.1] Methods work in one of three ways: by preventing ovulation, by preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or by preventing implantation, the embedding of the embryo in the lining of the uterus.
[bio23-s8.2.2] Methods are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
[bio23-s8.2.3] Effectiveness with perfect use: the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective.
[bio23-s8.2.4] With typical, real-life use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time, namely natural methods, condoms and the pill, fail more often, because people forget or make mistakes.
[bio23-s8.2.5] Long-acting methods, namely the implant, coils and sterilisation, stay above 99% effective with typical use, because there is nothing to remember.
[bio23-s8.2.6] Only condoms, male and female, protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), because they stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.2.7] People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.b] Natural methods use no drugs or devices. They rely on behaviour: avoiding intercourse altogether, or avoiding it on the days when pregnancy is possible.
[bio23-s8.3.0] Abstinence, not having intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.1] Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, which they find by combining several signs.
[bio23-s8.3.2] Sign 1, the calendar (rhythm) method: recording cycle lengths over time to estimate when ovulation happens.
[bio23-s8.3.3] Sign 2, body temperature: measured each morning before getting up, it rises by a small amount, a few tenths of a degree Celsius, just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of the hormone progesterone.
[bio23-s8.3.4] Sign 3, cervical mucus (the fluid made by the cervix, the neck of the uterus): it becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation.
[bio23-s8.3.5] Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used, giving a warning that ovulation is near. There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.4.b] Natural methods appeal to some people but are demanding to use and fail more often than other methods in real life. Knowing both sides lets you discuss them fairly.
[bio23-s8.4.0] Advantages of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and they are acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception, for example the teaching of the Catholic Church, which accepts only natural methods.
[bio23-s8.4.1] Limits of natural methods: they need training, daily records and regular cycles, and are much less reliable with typical use.
[bio23-s8.4.2] Natural methods are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, because cycles are then irregular. They also give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.4.3] Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation.
[bio23-s8.4.4] Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception, because ovulation can return without warning.
[bio23-s8.5.b] Barrier methods put a physical block between sperm and egg. Of the methods used during intercourse, only the condoms in this group protect against STIs, because they also stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.5.0] Male condom: a thin latex, or non-latex, sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm cannot enter the vagina.
[bio23-s8.5.1] The male condom also blocks the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV.
[bio23-s8.5.2] The male condom is cheap, widely available and has no hormones; it can tear or slip, and must be put on before any contact.
[bio23-s8.5.3] Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; it also protects against STIs.
[bio23-s8.5.4] Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide (a sperm-killing chemical) and left in for several hours afterwards; it does not protect well against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.b] The copper coil is a device rather than a barrier, but it is grouped with mechanical methods because it is a physical object placed in the body. It works without hormones.
[bio23-s8.6.0] Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse.
[bio23-s8.6.1] Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and it changes the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) so an embryo is unlikely to implant.
[bio23-s8.6.2] The copper coil lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility.
[bio23-s8.6.3] The copper coil can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days.
[bio23-s8.6.4] The copper coil can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.5] It is grouped as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical methods.
[bio23-s8.7.b] Sterilisation is a permanent operation that blocks the tubes gametes travel along. In men this is vasectomy, which stops sperm joining the semen without changing anything else about the man.
[bio23-s8.7.0] Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens (the tube carrying sperm from the testis) is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes. There is more on this in The male reproductive system.
[bio23-s8.7.1] After a vasectomy the man still ejaculates semen, because the fluids come from glands that are untouched; his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected.
[bio23-s8.7.2] The sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.7.3] A vasectomy is not effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
[bio23-s8.8.b] Female sterilisation works on the same principle as vasectomy: the tubes are blocked so egg and sperm cannot meet. Unlike vasectomy, it works straight away.
[bio23-s8.8.0] Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube, the tube that carries the egg from the ovary to the uterus, is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm cannot meet.
[bio23-s8.8.1] After tubal ligation ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before, because the egg is reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.8.2] Tubal ligation is effective at once.
[bio23-s8.9.b] Sterilisation suits some people very well and others badly. The key issue is that it is meant to be permanent.
[bio23-s8.9.0] Sterilisation is very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember, but it should be treated as irreversible: reversal is difficult and often fails.
[bio23-s8.9.1] Sterilisation carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.9.2] It suits people who are sure they want no more children.
[bio23-s8.9.3] Diagram: outline of the male system with the cut in each vas deferens marked, and of the female system with the cut or clip in each fallopian tube marked; label the vas deferens, testis, fallopian tube, ovary, uterus and “cut and tied”.
[bio23-s8.10.b] Chemical methods use hormones or sperm-killing chemicals. The pill is the best known: it is taken by mouth every day and uses synthetic versions of the body's own reproductive hormones.
[bio23-s8.10.0] The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation; the detail of how is in Hormonal manipulation. It also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass.
[bio23-s8.10.1] The combined pill is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed (a bleed like a period) happens.
[bio23-s8.10.2] The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who cannot take oestrogen.
[bio23-s8.11.b] Some hormonal methods release their hormone slowly over months or years, so the user has nothing to remember daily. This is why the implant and the coils, which are long-acting, stay above 99% effective in real life.
[bio23-s8.11.0] Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously.
[bio23-s8.11.1] The implant is a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years.
[bio23-s8.11.2] The injection is given every few months.
[bio23-s8.11.3] The hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS) is placed in the uterus and lasts several years; it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.
[bio23-s8.11.4] The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
[bio23-s8.12.b] Some chemical methods are used after the event or alongside another method rather than as a regular choice.
[bio23-s8.12.0] Emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, work mainly by delaying ovulation, so there is no egg for sperm to meet; one type works within 3 days and another within 5 days.
[bio23-s8.12.1] The copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method. Emergency pills are not for regular use.
[bio23-s8.12.2] Spermicides are chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
[bio23-s8.13.b] Hormonal methods are very effective and have extra health benefits, but they act on the whole body, so they also carry side effects and risks.
[bio23-s8.13.0] Hormonal methods are very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful.
[bio23-s8.13.1] The combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb).
[bio23-s8.13.2] Hormonal methods can cause side effects, for example headaches, mood changes and irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods.
[bio23-s8.13.3] The combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it is not prescribed to everyone.
[bio23-s8.13.4] Hormonal methods give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.13.5] The pill must be taken correctly every day; vomiting or some medicines can stop it working.
[bio23-s8.14.b] Some infections spread mainly through sexual contact, and some of them damage the reproductive system. This links contraception to infertility.
[bio23-s8.14.0] An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact.
[bio23-s8.14.1] Bacterial STIs are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are treated with antibiotics.
[bio23-s8.14.2] Viral STIs are HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B, which can be controlled but is not usually cured. There is more on pathogens in Pathogens.
[bio23-s8.14.3] Chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes, causing pelvic inflammatory disease, which scars and blocks the tubes.
[bio23-s8.14.4] Blocked tubes from pelvic inflammatory disease are a major preventable cause of female infertility, because the egg and sperm can no longer meet, and they raise the risk of ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy that starts outside the uterus).
[bio23-s8.14.5] In men chlamydia and gonorrhoea can inflame the epididymis, the coiled tube on the testis where sperm mature and are stored.
[bio23-s8.15.b] Because many STIs cause no symptoms and can damage fertility, prevention combines protection during sex, testing and treatment, and vaccination.
[bio23-s8.15.0] Prevention: correct use of condoms every time, and fewer sexual partners.
[bio23-s8.15.1] Prevention: regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners breaks the chain of transmission, because many infected people have no symptoms and would otherwise pass the infection on without knowing.
[bio23-s8.15.2] Prevention: vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B. There is more on HPV in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio23-s8.15.3] For HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP) lower the risk of infection.
[bio23-s8.15.4] Treatment of people with HIV reduces their virus to levels at which it is not passed on sexually.
[bio23-s8.15.5] Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics (the drugs no longer kill it), which makes prevention more important.
[bio23-s8.16.b] Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) lets a woman store her eggs while they are still young and healthy, to be used later. It uses a series of steps similar to the start of IVF.
[bio23-s8.16.0] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once, rather than the usual one.
[bio23-s8.16.1] 2. The eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation.
[bio23-s8.16.2] 3. The eggs are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, do not form, and they are stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
[bio23-s8.16.3] 4. Later the eggs are thawed and fertilised by IVF (fertilisation in a dish) or by ICSI (one sperm injected into an egg).
[bio23-s8.17.b] Egg freezing preserves fertility but cannot promise a baby. Who uses it, and why it is not a guarantee, are the points to know.
[bio23-s8.17.0] Uses: before cancer treatment, because chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries, or by women who wish to delay having children.
[bio23-s8.17.1] Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.17.2] Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen, since younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better.
[bio23-s8.17.3] Freezing gives no guarantee of a baby, and not every egg survives thawing.
[bio23-s8.17.4] The process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.
[bio23-s8.18.b] Infertility is common, and its causes can lie with the man, the woman, both, or neither. Finding the cause decides which treatment is suitable.
[bio23-s8.18.0] Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
[bio23-s8.18.1] The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives.
[bio23-s8.18.2] Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither, which is called unexplained infertility.
[bio23-s8.19.b] Male infertility usually comes down to too few sperm, sperm that cannot do their job, or sperm that cannot get out. Lifestyle can also reduce sperm production.
[bio23-s8.19.0] Low sperm count (too few sperm in the semen), and sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped, for example with a faulty tail, reduce the chance that one reaches the egg.
[bio23-s8.19.1] A blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens, for example after infection or vasectomy, stops sperm leaving the testes.
[bio23-s8.19.2] Undescended testes (testes that have not moved down into the scrotum) and low testosterone or low pituitary hormones are also male causes.
[bio23-s8.19.3] Lifestyle and environmental factors reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids and overheating of the testes. There is more on steroids in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s8.19.4] Male infertility is diagnosed by semen analysis, which tests sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope.
[bio23-s8.20.b] Female infertility arises when no egg is released, when egg and sperm cannot meet, or when the uterus cannot support an embryo.
[bio23-s8.20.0] Failure to ovulate, caused by a hormone imbalance, for example polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight, means no egg is released.
[bio23-s8.20.1] Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, for example after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI, stop egg and sperm meeting.
[bio23-s8.20.2] Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries.
[bio23-s8.20.3] Female causes also include problems of the uterus or cervical mucus, and age, because egg number and quality fall.
[bio23-s8.20.4] Female infertility is diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.
[bio23-s8.21.b] If the problem is that a woman does not release eggs regularly, the treatment is to make her ovaries release them. This is a first step before more complex techniques.
[bio23-s8.21.0] Stimulating ovulation is for women who do not ovulate regularly, for example because of PCOS.
[bio23-s8.21.1] Fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs.
[bio23-s8.21.2] The woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles (the fluid-filled sacs in the ovary that each contain an egg) develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation.
[bio23-s8.21.3] Risk: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely.
[bio23-s8.21.4] Risk: the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious.
[bio23-s8.21.5] Lifestyle change, for example weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.
[bio23-s8.22.b] Fertility drugs work by acting on the same hormone system that controls the menstrual cycle, either by boosting the brain's signals to the ovaries or by supplying them directly.
[bio23-s8.22.0] Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation. There is more on feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.22.1] Injected fertility drugs contain FSH, often with LH, and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop.
[bio23-s8.22.2] A final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge.
[bio23-s8.22.3] The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.
[bio23-s8.23.b] Artificial insemination helps when the sperm have trouble reaching the egg. It puts sperm into the uterus by hand, so more of them start closer to the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.0] Artificial insemination, or intrauterine insemination (IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.23.1] This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes, where fertilisation happens.
[bio23-s8.23.2] It is used for a mildly low sperm count or poor motility (swimming), cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm.
[bio23-s8.23.3] The woman's fallopian tubes must be open, because the sperm still have to travel along them to meet the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.4] The same technique is widely used in farming, for example to breed cattle from selected bulls.
[bio23-s8.24.b] IVF takes fertilisation out of the body. Eggs and sperm are brought together in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is then placed in the uterus.
[bio23-s8.24.0] IVF means “in glass”, that is, fertilisation outside the body.
[bio23-s8.24.1] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs.
[bio23-s8.24.2] 2. The eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound.
[bio23-s8.24.3] 3. The eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place.
[bio23-s8.24.4] 4. The embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days, up to the blastocyst stage (a ball of cells ready to implant). There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.24.5] 5. Usually one, sometimes two, embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube.
[bio23-s8.24.6] 6. A pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted.
[bio23-s8.24.7] Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts.
[bio23-s8.25.b] IVF is used when simpler treatments will not work or are unsuitable. It was developed in the twentieth century and is now widely used.
[bio23-s8.25.0] IVF is used for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (checking embryos for a genetic condition before transfer). There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s8.25.1] History: the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe.
[bio23-s8.25.2] Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010.
[bio23-s8.25.3] Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.
[bio23-s8.26.b] ICSI is a version of IVF designed for severe male infertility. Instead of leaving sperm to fertilise the egg in the dish, the laboratory places one sperm inside the egg.
[bio23-s8.26.0] ICSI is the same as IVF except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope.
[bio23-s8.26.1] Because the sperm does not have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility.
[bio23-s8.26.2] ICSI is used for very low sperm count, sperm that cannot swim or cannot penetrate the egg, and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF.
[bio23-s8.26.3] ICSI is also used when sperm are taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle, for example after a vasectomy or a blockage.
[bio23-s8.26.4] ICSI is also used with frozen eggs, which are thawed before the sperm is injected.
[bio23-s8.27.b] A flow chart shows IVF and ICSI together, because they share every step except how the egg is fertilised.
[bio23-s8.27.0] Diagram: a flow chart of IVF with ICSI as a branch: stimulate ovaries → collect eggs → (IVF: mix eggs and sperm in a dish | ICSI: inject one sperm into each egg) → grow embryos for 2–5 days → transfer an embryo to the uterus → pregnancy test.
[bio23-s8.28.b] Discussing contraception means weighing benefits against drawbacks. The benefits range from the individual to the whole planet.
[bio23-s8.28.0] Contraception lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and to space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies.
[bio23-s8.28.1] It prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers.
[bio23-s8.28.2] Condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit.
[bio23-s8.28.3] Hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods, and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer.
[bio23-s8.28.4] At a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats. This is a sustainability link; there is more on this in The cross-cutting themes.
[bio23-s8.29.b] Contraception also has drawbacks, some medical, some personal, and one environmental.
[bio23-s8.29.0] Hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots.
[bio23-s8.29.1] No method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one.
[bio23-s8.29.2] User-dependent methods often fail in real life, because they must be used correctly every time.
[bio23-s8.29.3] Sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted.
[bio23-s8.29.4] Some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds.
[bio23-s8.29.5] Synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.
[bio23-s8.30.b] Treatments for infertility bring benefits that are both personal and medical. Each argument rests on the biology of the technique.
[bio23-s8.30.0] It allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress.
[bio23-s8.30.1] ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers.
[bio23-s8.30.2] Egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.30.3] Preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on.
[bio23-s8.30.4] Donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children.
[bio23-s8.30.5] Research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
[bio23-s8.31.b] Infertility treatments also raise health, cost and ethical concerns. Some are risks to mother and baby, others are questions of fairness and identity.
[bio23-s8.31.0] Stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother; this is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured.
[bio23-s8.31.1] Ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous.
[bio23-s8.31.2] Success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who cannot pay.
[bio23-s8.31.3] ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility, for example some changes on the Y chromosome, can be passed to a son.
[bio23-s8.31.4] Spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s8.31.5] Children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity.
[bio23-s8.31.6] Treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk, for example a higher chance of chromosome conditions.
[bio23-s8.32.b] Egg freezing gives women more control over timing but is not a guarantee. Weigh the benefits against the limits.
[bio23-s8.32.0] For: it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.32.1] For: it gives women more choice over when to have children.
[bio23-s8.32.2] Against: success is uncertain, and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages.
[bio23-s8.32.3] Against: it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs.
[bio23-s8.32.4] Against: it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio26-s2: The nephron
[bio26-s2.0.b] Urine is made by tiny tubes called nephrons, so you need to know the parts of one in the order the fluid flows through them. Each part has its own job, and its position in the kidney is part of the answer.
[bio26-s2.0.0] A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney. It is the kidney's functional unit (the smallest part that does the kidney's job), where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one cell thick throughout.
[bio26-s2.0.1] Bowman's capsule is the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex (the outer layer of the kidney), wrapped around the glomerulus (a capillary knot). Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the capsule's inner space.
[bio26-s2.0.2] The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the first, long, coiled (convoluted) section after the capsule, in the cortex. Proximal means nearer the capsule.
[bio26-s2.0.3] The loop of Henle is a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla (the inner part of the kidney), with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
[bio26-s2.0.4] The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) is the second coiled section, back in the cortex. Distal means further from the capsule. It empties into a collecting duct.
[bio26-s2.0.5] The collecting duct is a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla, through a pyramid, and opens into the renal pelvis (the funnel that collects urine).
[bio26-s2.0.6] The syllabus does not list the collecting duct, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH. Some textbooks do not count it as part of the nephron.
[bio26-s2.1.b] A nephron works because blood is brought right up against it. The blood vessels are arranged so that pressure builds up for filtering, and so that the tubule is wrapped in a second set of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) to take back what it reabsorbs.
[bio26-s2.1.0] A branch of the renal artery (the artery that brings blood to the kidney) divides into arterioles (small arteries). The afferent arteriole (afferent means carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
[bio26-s2.1.1] The capillaries of the glomerulus rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent means carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. The afferent is the wider one, so do not say the efferent is wider.
[bio26-s2.1.2] Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus, like water backing up behind a narrowed nozzle. This high pressure drives filtration.
[bio26-s2.1.3] The glomerulus is unusual because it is a capillary network lying between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule (a small vein) as capillaries normally do.
[bio26-s2.1.4] The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries, so they must be close to the tubule.
[bio26-s2.1.5] These capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration, because the cells need ATP for active transport, and they carry away the cells' carbon dioxide.
[bio26-s2.1.6] The capillaries join into venules, then into a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
[bio26-s2.1.7] Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below; mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).
[bio26-s2.2.b] The first step in making urine is filtration. It happens in the glomerulus and capsule, where pressure forces fluid out of the blood without checking whether the body needs it.
[bio26-s2.2.0] Filtration, also called ultrafiltration or filtration under pressure, is the forcing of small molecules out of the blood by the high pressure in the glomerulus. They pass through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
[bio26-s2.2.1] Substances that pass into the filtrate (the fluid that enters the capsule) are water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea (the waste made from breaking down amino acids), vitamins and some hormones.
[bio26-s2.2.2] Substances that are not filtered and stay in the blood are red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
[bio26-s2.2.3] Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances such as glucose and amino acids are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
[bio26-s2.2.4] Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood); this is why reabsorption is essential.
[bio26-s2.2.5] Structure suits filtration: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arteriole raise the pressure, and the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous, so fluid passes through easily.
[bio26-s2.2.6] The many capillaries in each glomerulus, with about a million glomeruli in the kidney, give a large filtering surface, so a large volume of fluid can be filtered every day.
[bio26-s2.3.b] Filtration lets everything small through, so the body has to take back what it wants. Most of this reabsorption happens in the PCT, whose structure is built for the job.
[bio26-s2.3.0] Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
[bio26-s2.3.1] The PCT is the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here.
[bio26-s2.3.2] Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, which uses ATP (the cell's energy currency) to move them into the cells. Water follows by osmosis, and some urea diffuses back into the blood.
[bio26-s2.3.3] Water is always reabsorbed passively by osmosis; it is never actively pumped. Only salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.3.4] The PCT is long and coiled, which gives time and surface area for reabsorption. Its cells have microvilli (tiny finger-like projections) on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area.
[bio26-s2.3.5] The PCT cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. The tubule is closely surrounded by capillaries, and its wall is one cell thick, so the distance to the blood is short.
[bio26-s2.3.6] The carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.
[bio26-s2.4.b] After the PCT, the rest of the nephron fine-tunes the filtrate. The loop of Henle makes the medulla salty, and this lets the collecting duct take back more water.
[bio26-s2.4.0] The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty, with the highest solute concentration deep in the medulla.
[bio26-s2.4.1] The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
[bio26-s2.4.2] In the DCT more salts and water are reabsorbed, with the amounts adjusted to the body's needs.
[bio26-s2.4.3] The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption.
[bio26-s2.4.4] In the collecting duct, as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood, so the urine becomes more concentrated. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH, a hormone. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation.
[bio26-s2.4.5] What remains in the collecting duct is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s2.5.b] Comparing the composition of blood plasma, glomerular filtrate and urine shows what each part of the nephron has done. Each difference between them has one explanation.
[bio26-s2.5.0] Protein is present in plasma but absent from the filtrate and urine, because it is too large to be filtered.
[bio26-s2.5.1] Glucose and amino acids are present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration, because filtration lets them through freely. They are absent from urine, because all of them are reabsorbed in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.5.2] Urea is at the same concentration in plasma and filtrate, because it is freely filtered. It is much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
[bio26-s2.5.3] Salts are similar in plasma and filtrate. In urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
[bio26-s2.5.4] Skill: given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference. Absent from the filtrate means not filtered (too large); absent from the urine means reabsorbed; more concentrated in the urine means water was reabsorbed and the substance was not.
[bio26-s2.6.b] Following the fluid from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, each part of the nephron does one main job.
[bio26-s2.6.0] Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule: filtration under pressure.
[bio26-s2.6.1] Proximal convoluted tubule: reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and most water.
[bio26-s2.6.2] Loop of Henle: makes the medulla salty; water is reabsorbed from the descending limb.
[bio26-s2.6.3] Distal convoluted tubule: adjusts salts and water, and secretes hydrogen ions, potassium ions and some drugs.
[bio26-s2.6.4] Collecting duct: water is reabsorbed under ADH control.
[bio26-s2.6.5] Renal pelvis: receives the urine that is left.

CANDIDATES bio26-s3: ADH and osmoregulation
[bio26-s3.0.b] The water content of the body is never constant on its own: we take water in and lose it all day. Osmoregulation (keeping the water and solute balance of the body fluids steady) stops these swings from harming our cells.
[bio26-s3.0.0] Osmoregulation in humans is mainly done by the kidneys, which change how much water is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood) from the collecting ducts, the final tubes that carry urine out of the kidney. The variable being controlled is the concentration of the blood plasma.
[bio26-s3.0.1] Water is gained from drink, food and respiration, and lost in urine, sweat, breath (as water vapour) and faeces. Gains and losses change from hour to hour, so the kidney must adjust the urine to match.
[bio26-s3.0.2] If the blood becomes too concentrated, body cells lose water by osmosis (the movement of water from a dilute region to a more concentrated one) and shrink. If it becomes too dilute, cells gain water and swell. Either upsets cell function, especially in the brain.
[bio26-s3.1.b] The kidney needs a message telling it how much water to save. That message is a hormone, ADH, which changes how leaky the collecting duct wall is to water.
[bio26-s3.1.0] ADH stands for antidiuretic hormone. Diuresis is the production of a large volume of dilute urine, so ADH works against it and reduces urine volume. ADH is released from the pituitary gland.
[bio26-s3.1.1] The target cells (the cells that respond to the hormone) line the collecting ducts and the last part of the distal convoluted tubule.
[bio26-s3.1.2] ADH binds to receptors on these cells, which causes water channel proteins, called aquaporins, to be inserted into their membranes. This makes the duct wall more permeable to water.
[bio26-s3.1.3] The collecting duct passes through the salty medulla (the inner region of the kidney), so water moves out of the more permeable duct by osmosis into the medulla and is carried away in the blood capillaries.
[bio26-s3.1.4] More ADH means more water reabsorbed, less water left in the duct, and a small volume of concentrated urine.
[bio26-s3.1.5] Less ADH means fewer water channels, so the duct wall is less permeable and less water is reabsorbed. A large volume of dilute urine is the result.
[bio26-s3.1.6] ADH changes water reabsorption only. It does not change the amount of salt or urea (the waste made from the breakdown of proteins) excreted. There is more on channel proteins in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio26-s3.2.b] When the blood becomes too concentrated, the body responds in a fixed sequence that saves water and brings the concentration back to normal. Follow the steps in order.
[bio26-s3.2.0] 1. Stimulus: the water concentration of the blood falls (its solute concentration rises), for example after heavy sweating, not drinking enough, eating salty food, or diarrhoea.
[bio26-s3.2.1] 2. Receptors: osmoreceptors, special cells in the hypothalamus of the brain that detect the concentration of the blood, notice the change.
[bio26-s3.2.2] 3. The hypothalamus causes the pituitary gland to release more ADH into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.3] 4. ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys.
[bio26-s3.2.4] 5. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.5] 6. A small volume of concentrated, dark-yellow urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.2.6] 7. The water concentration of the blood rises back towards normal, and ADH release falls again. This is negative feedback, because the response reverses the original change.
[bio26-s3.2.7] At the same time the hypothalamus triggers thirst, so the person drinks more. Drinking is the only way to replace lost water, because the kidneys can only reduce further loss.
[bio26-s3.3.b] The same system works in reverse when the blood becomes too dilute. The kidney then lets more water go, so the concentration returns to normal.
[bio26-s3.3.0] 1. Stimulus: after drinking a large volume of water, the water concentration of the blood rises (it becomes more dilute).
[bio26-s3.3.1] 2. The hypothalamus detects the change.
[bio26-s3.3.2] 3. The pituitary releases less ADH.
[bio26-s3.3.3] 4. The collecting ducts become less permeable to water, because fewer water channels are present in their walls.
[bio26-s3.3.4] 5. Less water is reabsorbed, so more stays in the duct.
[bio26-s3.3.5] 6. A large volume of dilute, pale urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.3.6] 7. The blood's water concentration falls back to normal and ADH release returns to its usual level.
[bio26-s3.4.b] Everyday situations change the concentration of the blood, and ADH release changes with them. Each example below follows the same chain from blood concentration to urine volume.
[bio26-s3.4.0] On a hot day or during exercise, much water is lost in sweat, so more ADH is released and the urine is small in volume and dark.
[bio26-s3.4.1] A high salt intake raises the solute concentration of the blood, so more ADH is released, more water is reabsorbed and a smaller volume of urine is produced. The extra salt is excreted in this smaller volume, and the person also feels thirsty.
[bio26-s3.4.2] Alcohol reduces the release of ADH, so the collecting ducts reabsorb less water and a large volume of dilute urine is produced. This causes dehydration (loss of body water), which contributes to the headache and thirst of a hangover.
[bio26-s3.5.b] The two feedback responses can be drawn as one negative-feedback loop around the normal blood water concentration. Drawing it shows that every change is pushed back towards normal.
[bio26-s3.5.0] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop for water balance, with normal blood water concentration at the centre. One branch shows the water concentration falling, the hypothalamus detecting it, the pituitary releasing more ADH, the collecting ducts becoming more permeable, more water being reabsorbed, a small volume of concentrated urine being produced, and the concentration returning to normal. The other branch shows the water concentration rising, less ADH, less permeable collecting ducts, less water reabsorbed, a large volume of dilute urine, and a return to normal. Optionally add a small sketch of a collecting duct passing through the salty medulla with arrows for water leaving by osmosis.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio29-s1: Investigation: the heart
[bio29-s1.0.b] The heart is a muscular pump that never stops, and its structure is built around one job: moving blood. It is divided into four chambers, two upper and two lower, split into a left and a right side by a central wall.
[bio29-s1.0.0] Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax (the chest) between the two lungs and behind the sternum (breastbone), with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left.
[bio29-s1.0.1] The heart is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers. The fluid reduces friction as the heart beats, and the membrane prevents the heart from over-stretching.
[bio29-s1.0.2] Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
[bio29-s1.0.3] Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries (the vessels that carry blood away from the heart), so their walls are thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.0.4] The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance. The right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels).
[bio29-s1.0.5] Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.
[bio29-s1.0.6] In other words, the heart is two separate pumps side by side: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.b] Blood has to arrive at the heart and leave it by separate routes. Veins bring blood in to the atria, and arteries carry it out from the ventricles.
[bio29-s1.1.0] Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it towards it; this is not about oxygen, which is why one artery and one set of veins break the usual pattern below.
[bio29-s1.1.1] The venae cavae bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium: the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms, and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body.
[bio29-s1.1.2] The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
[bio29-s1.1.3] Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs.
[bio29-s1.1.4] The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.5] The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself with blood. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.2.b] Blood pressure inside the heart changes sharply as the muscle squeezes, and blood would simply flow back where it came from without help. Valves are flaps of tissue that keep blood flowing in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.2.0] Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. A reminder is tRIcuspid on the RIght.
[bio29-s1.2.1] AV valves let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
[bio29-s1.2.2] The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (heart strings), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
[bio29-s1.2.3] Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets.
[bio29-s1.2.4] Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
[bio29-s1.3.b] The heart wall is made of a special muscle found nowhere else in the body. Its features suit it to beating without rest for a whole lifetime.
[bio29-s1.3.0] Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped) and branched, and are joined end to end by intercalated discs.
[bio29-s1.3.1] Intercalated discs let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell, so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit.
[bio29-s1.3.2] Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue).
[bio29-s1.3.3] Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. There is more on the pacemaker that starts each beat in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.3.4] Diagram: a vertical section through the heart viewed from the front, so that the heart's left side is on the right of the page. Label the right and left atria, the right and left ventricles (left wall thicker), the septum, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves, the chordae tendineae, the papillary muscles, the semilunar valves, the superior and inferior venae cavae, the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary veins and the aorta. Add arrows for the direction of blood flow, and shade or label the oxygenated (left) and deoxygenated (right) sides.
[bio29-s1.3.5] Diagram: cardiac muscle as seen under the microscope, from a prepared slide or micrograph, showing branched, striated cells with a central nucleus and intercalated discs. Label the striations, nucleus, intercalated disc and branch.
[bio29-s1.4.b] A dissection is an investigation, so it needs an aim, hypotheses to test, controlled variables and both kinds of data. The heart you cut open is a model for the human heart.
[bio29-s1.4.0] Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection, and to relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs and images of the human heart).
[bio29-s1.4.1] Hypothesis to test: the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body.
[bio29-s1.4.2] Hypothesis to test: arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it.
[bio29-s1.4.3] Hypothesis to test: the valves allow blood to flow in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.4.4] Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): the independent variable (the one you change) is the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle or atrium); the dependent variable (the one you measure) is the wall thickness in mm.
[bio29-s1.4.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): measure at the same level, about halfway between the base and the apex, with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall.
[bio29-s1.4.6] Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean. Pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
[bio29-s1.4.7] Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle; published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart, because mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
[bio29-s1.4.8] Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep's, pig's or ox's heart from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long; dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; a camera or phone to record observations.
[bio29-s1.5.b] Start with protection and with the outside of the heart. Identifying the front surface and the vessels first tells you which side is which before you cut anything.
[bio29-s1.5.0] 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
[bio29-s1.5.1] 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it, and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart's left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
[bio29-s1.5.2] 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top), the ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top.
[bio29-s1.5.3] Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae and pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see, and photograph it.
[bio29-s1.5.4] 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters.
[bio29-s1.5.5] The vena cava leads to the right atrium; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the pulmonary veins lead to the left atrium; the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
[bio29-s1.5.6] Butchers often trim the vessels short, so use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
[bio29-s1.6.b] Now cut the heart open to look at the chambers, valves and wall thickness. Work on the right side first, then the left, then compare the walls in cross-section.
[bio29-s1.6.0] 5. Open the right side. Using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers.
[bio29-s1.6.1] Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles. Pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap. 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
[bio29-s1.6.2] 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve. Look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
[bio29-s1.6.3] 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing that the valves allow one-way flow.
[bio29-s1.6.4] Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
[bio29-s1.6.5] 9. Cut a transverse section (straight across) across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them.
[bio29-s1.6.6] Measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler, following the variables and repeats above. Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
[bio29-s1.6.7] 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope, or a micrograph, and draw it. 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings. Dispose of the heart and clean up as described under Safety and ethics.
[bio29-s1.7.b] A dissection uses sharp blades and raw animal tissue, so each hazard has a specific precaution. There is also an ethical side to using animal material.
[bio29-s1.7.0] Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person. Hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade, carry sharp instruments pointing down, and report any cut at once.
[bio29-s1.7.1] Raw meat can carry bacteria, such as Salmonella, that cause food poisoning. Wear gloves, do not touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
[bio29-s1.7.2] Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection. Wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher; it is not to be left in an open bin.
[bio29-s1.7.3] Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or when pouring water through the vessels.
[bio29-s1.7.4] Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food, a by-product of the food industry, so that no animal is killed for the dissection, and treat the tissue with respect.
[bio29-s1.7.5] A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos as secondary data, in line with the 3Rs of animal use. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio29-s1.8.b] Your results come from two sources: primary data, which you collect yourself from the dissected heart, and secondary data, which comes from diagrams, models and micrographs. Together they support the conclusions.
[bio29-s1.8.0] Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right, typically several times thicker in measurements; the septum thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.8.1] Valves: the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s1.8.2] Vessels: the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface, with their openings just above the aortic valve.
[bio29-s1.8.3] Results table to record: the structure; where it is found; its description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); the measured wall thickness in mm, with three readings and the mean; and its function.
[bio29-s1.8.4] Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels. Differences seen are due to the butcher's trimming, which may remove the atria or vessels, or to species size.
[bio29-s1.8.5] Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.
[bio29-s1.9.b] The conclusion links each structure you found to its function, and states whether each hypothesis was supported by the evidence.
[bio29-s1.9.0] The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
[bio29-s1.9.1] Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
[bio29-s1.9.2] The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
[bio29-s1.9.3] The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
[bio29-s1.9.4] Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls. There is more on this in Blood vessels.
[bio29-s1.9.5] For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.
[bio29-s1.10.b] A dissected animal heart is not a living human heart, and the measurements are rough. Knowing the limits shows how far the conclusions can be trusted and how to improve them.
[bio29-s1.10.0] Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
[bio29-s1.10.1] Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
[bio29-s1.10.2] Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
[bio29-s1.10.3] A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
[bio29-s1.10.4] One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio29-s2: Circulation
[bio29-s2.0.b] Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to get rid of wastes. The circulatory system is the transport network that does this for all of them.
[bio29-s2.0.0] The human circulatory system has three parts: the heart, a pump; the blood vessels, a closed network of tubes made up of arteries, capillaries and veins; and the blood, the transport medium that carries the materials.
[bio29-s2.0.1] It is a closed system: blood stays inside the vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across the thin walls of the capillaries (the tiniest vessels, which pass right alongside the cells).
[bio29-s2.0.2] In a large, many-celled animal, diffusion (the slow, random spreading of molecules) alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut and kidneys) and cells deep in the body.
[bio29-s2.0.3] The circulatory system solves this by carrying materials quickly by mass flow (the whole liquid moving together), so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
[bio29-s2.0.4] Materials transported: oxygen (lungs to all cells); carbon dioxide (cells to lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine to liver to cells); urea (liver to kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands to target organs); water and salts.
[bio29-s2.0.5] Also transported: antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets (which seal wounds); and heat, carried from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin.
[bio29-s2.1.b] Humans have two circuits joined at the heart rather than one loop. Blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and is then sent out to the rest of the body.
[bio29-s2.1.0] Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. This does not mean two hearts; it is one heart with two sides.
[bio29-s2.1.1] The pulmonary circuit (pulmonary means 'of the lungs') runs heart to lungs to heart. The systemic circuit runs heart to body organs to heart.
[bio29-s2.1.2] The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit. There is more on the chambers in Investigation: the heart.
[bio29-s2.1.3] Full route of a red blood cell: vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, semilunar valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valve, aorta, artery, arteriole, capillaries of an organ, venule, vein, vena cava.
[bio29-s2.1.4] Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation. Heart as four boxes, lungs above, body organs below. Add arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae.
[bio29-s2.1.5] Diagram labels: right and left atrium and ventricle, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta, venae cavae, pulmonary circuit, systemic circuit. Shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue.
[bio29-s2.2.b] The pulmonary circuit has one job: to send blood that has given up its oxygen to the lungs to collect more. It starts at the right side of the heart.
[bio29-s2.2.0] 1. Deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) is pumped by the right ventricle (the lower right chamber, which pumps blood out of the heart) into the pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s2.2.1] 2. The pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung, so both lungs receive blood.
[bio29-s2.2.2] 3. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂, because oxygen diffuses in from the air in the alveoli (air sacs) and CO₂ diffuses out. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio29-s2.2.3] 4. Oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium (the upper left chamber, which receives blood coming into the heart).
[bio29-s2.3.b] The systemic circuit takes oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to every organ and brings it back. Its steps follow on from the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.3.0] 1. Oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve (the one-way valve between the left atrium and left ventricle) to the left ventricle.
[bio29-s2.3.1] 2. The left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, the body's largest artery.
[bio29-s2.3.2] 3. Arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ, where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes.
[bio29-s2.3.3] 4. Deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae (the large veins bringing blood from the upper and lower body) and into the right atrium.
[bio29-s2.3.4] 5. It passes through the tricuspid valve (the one-way valve between the right atrium and right ventricle) to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
[bio29-s2.4.b] A double circulation is better than a single loop for an active, warm-blooded animal. Passing the blood through the heart twice solves two problems: lost pressure and the risk of damage to the lungs.
[bio29-s2.4.0] (a) Blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all the body organs quickly.
[bio29-s2.4.1] (b) The two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit, to drive blood through the many organs, and lower in the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.4.2] The lower pulmonary pressure matters because the lung capillaries are thin-walled, so they are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs).
[bio29-s2.4.3] (c) The septum (the wall dividing the two sides of the heart) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated.
[bio29-s2.4.4] Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
[bio29-s2.5.b] Blood reaches each organ along its own named artery and leaves along its own named vein. Knowing these names lets you describe the route to any organ.
[bio29-s2.5.0] A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and is drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and each is usually named after the organ.
[bio29-s2.5.1] Lungs (pulmonary pathway): the pulmonary artery, from the right ventricle, carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins, to the left atrium, carry oxygenated blood. In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s2.5.2] Head and brain: the carotid arteries, in the neck, supply it, and the jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava. Arms: the subclavian arteries and veins. Legs: the iliac arteries and veins.
[bio29-s2.5.3] Kidneys: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered, and the renal veins return filtered blood, low in urea, to the inferior vena cava. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio29-s2.5.4] Heart muscle: the coronary arteries and cardiac veins, which supply the heart's own muscle with blood.
[bio29-s2.5.5] Liver and gut: the liver receives blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut). Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
[bio29-s2.5.6] Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels showing the heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
[bio29-s2.6.b] Most veins take blood straight back towards the heart. The vein from the gut is unusual because it detours through the liver first, so the liver gets the first chance to deal with what has just been absorbed.
[bio29-s2.6.0] A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. Blood therefore passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
[bio29-s2.6.1] The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the capillaries in the villi (the tiny finger-like folds of the small intestine wall), and from the walls of the stomach and large intestine.
[bio29-s2.6.2] It carries this blood to a second set of capillaries, the sinusoids, in the liver. After a meal this blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
[bio29-s2.6.3] Absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio29-s2.6.4] The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood, because it has already passed through the gut capillaries, so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery.
[bio29-s2.7.b] The circulatory system works as the delivery service for every other system. Each system either puts materials into the blood or takes materials out of it, and you can model these exchanges.
[bio29-s2.7.0] Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂. The circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and carries CO₂ back, so without circulation the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
[bio29-s2.7.1] Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals, small vessels in the villi) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells. The blood also supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
[bio29-s2.7.2] Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it. The kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume.
[bio29-s2.7.3] Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs, for example insulin from the pancreas to the liver and ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys. Adrenaline, carried in the blood, increases heart rate.
[bio29-s2.7.4] Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds.
[bio29-s2.7.5] Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells. Active muscles receive extra blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, and contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping to return blood to the heart.
[bio29-s2.7.6] Nervous system: the medulla oblongata (the part of the brain that controls automatic functions) adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles (small arteries), so blood is sent where it is needed. The brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
[bio29-s2.7.7] Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs, and widening or narrowing of the skin arterioles controls heat loss.
[bio29-s2.8.b] A model is a simplified picture that shows how the systems connect. A flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre makes it clear which materials travel where.
[bio29-s2.8.0] Diagram: a labelled flow diagram modelling these interactions, with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain. Label each arrow with the materials transferred: O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones and heat.
[bio29-s2.8.1] One limitation of the model is that it shows what moves but not the structure of the vessels or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
[bio29-s2.8.2] Worked example: during exercise, muscles respire faster, so breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s2.8.3] In the same example, heart rate rises and the arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them, while the arterioles to the gut narrow. Heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

CANDIDATES bio29-s3: Heartbeat
[bio29-s3.0.b] The heart does not wait for instructions to beat. A small patch of muscle in its own wall sets the pace and sends a wave of electrical signals that makes the chambers contract in the right order.
[bio29-s3.0.0] A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles (the two lower chambers) into the arteries.
[bio29-s3.0.1] An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
[bio29-s3.0.2] Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid.
[bio29-s3.0.3] The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium (an upper chamber), near where the superior vena cava (the large vein from the upper body) enters.
[bio29-s3.0.4] The SA node produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
[bio29-s3.1.b] The impulse from the SA node does not reach every part at once. It travels in a set route with a built-in pause, which makes the atria contract first and the ventricles second.
[bio29-s3.1.0] 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.1] 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles, so the ventricles cannot contract too early.
[bio29-s3.1.2] 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum (the wall between the two sides of the heart). The AV node delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.3] 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex (the pointed lower tip of the heart), and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
[bio29-s3.1.4] 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery, the two arteries leaving the heart. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.
[bio29-s3.1.5] Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.
[bio29-s3.2.b] The SA node sets a basic rhythm, but the body's needs change from moment to moment. Nerves and a hormone adjust the rate the SA node sets, so the heart can speed up or slow down.
[bio29-s3.2.0] The rate is changed to match the body's needs. It rises during exercise, because the muscles then need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂, which the faster-flowing blood delivers and carries away.
[bio29-s3.2.1] Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata (part of the brainstem) sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down.
[bio29-s3.2.2] The medulla responds to information from receptors, which detect changes in the body. For example, a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate, and a rise in blood pressure slows it.
[bio29-s3.2.3] Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction.
[bio29-s3.2.4] Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
[bio29-s3.2.5] Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart.
[bio29-s3.2.6] The artificial pacemaker detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.
[bio29-s3.3.b] Each time the ventricles contract, they force a surge of blood into the arteries. You can feel this surge as the pulse, which gives an easy way to measure heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.0] The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.1] The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe.
[bio29-s3.3.2] Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries (the tiny vessels between arteries and veins) before the blood reaches them.
[bio29-s3.3.3] Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes, so the reading is a resting one.
[bio29-s3.3.4] 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
[bio29-s3.3.5] 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
[bio29-s3.3.6] 4. Repeat three times and take the mean, which reduces the effect of any one miscount.
[bio29-s3.3.7] If asked what the pulse is, say it is a wave in the artery wall, not blood flowing past your finger.
[bio29-s3.4.b] Exercise makes the muscles need more oxygen, so the heart speeds up. Measuring the pulse before and after exercise shows this, and how quickly it recovers shows how fit someone is.
[bio29-s3.4.0] This is a standard class investigation with primary data (data you collect yourself). Measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value.
[bio29-s3.4.1] The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people.
[bio29-s3.4.2] Fitter people also tend to have a lower resting pulse, because each beat pumps more blood.
[bio29-s3.4.3] Independent variable (the one you change): activity level (rest, or after exercise). Dependent variable (the one you measure): pulse rate in beats per minute.
[bio29-s3.4.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.
[bio29-s3.5.b] Blood only flows round the body because the ventricles push it with force. That force, pressing on the walls of the vessels, is blood pressure, and when it stays too high it harms the body.
[bio29-s3.5.0] Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.5.1] Pressure is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles (small arteries) and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae.
[bio29-s3.5.2] Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
[bio29-s3.5.3] Pressure is given as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.4] Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.5] Hypertension usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
[bio29-s3.5.6] Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet, being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.5.7] High blood pressure is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.
[bio29-s3.6.b] The heart works non-stop, so its muscle needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose. It cannot get these from the blood inside its chambers, so it has a separate circulation of its own.
[bio29-s3.6.0] The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
[bio29-s3.6.1] The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve (the valve at the exit of the left ventricle). They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle.
[bio29-s3.6.2] These small arteries supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration (energy release using oxygen) that keeps it working continuously.
[bio29-s3.6.3] Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.
[bio29-s3.6.4] Diagram: a front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins. Label each.
[bio29-s3.7.b] The coronary arteries are narrow, so damage to them has serious effects. When they narrow or block, the heart muscle is starved of oxygen.
[bio29-s3.7.0] Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
[bio29-s3.7.1] If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction.
[bio29-s3.7.2] Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after a heart attack can break down the clot and restore blood flow to the starved muscle, so they limit the damage (see 'Industrial enzymes').
[bio29-s3.7.3] Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol, high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.7.4] Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight, which each remove a risk factor.
[bio29-s3.7.5] Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter (a thin tube) is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open.
[bio29-s3.7.6] Alternatively, the narrowed artery can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.

CANDIDATES bio29-s5: Blood vessels
[bio29-s5.0.b] Human blood never leaves a set of tubes called blood vessels, which is why the system is described as closed. The vessels differ in structure, and the differences make sense once you know the circuit and the three-layered wall that most of them share.
[bio29-s5.0.0] Blood flows in a closed circuit: heart, arteries, arterioles (the smallest arteries), capillaries, venules (the smallest veins), veins, and back to the heart.
[bio29-s5.0.1] Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood towards the heart. Capillaries link them inside the organs, and it is across capillary walls that materials are exchanged with the cells.
[bio29-s5.0.2] The walls of arteries and veins have three layers. The inner layer is the endothelium, one cell thick and smooth, which reduces friction as blood slides past. The middle layer is smooth muscle and elastic fibres.
[bio29-s5.0.3] The outer layer is tough connective tissue containing collagen fibres, which protects the vessel and stops it over-stretching. The space in the centre, through which blood flows, is the lumen.
[bio29-s5.0.4] Capillaries have only the endothelium, with no muscle, elastic or connective layers. This is why they can act as exchange surfaces, as the capillary block below shows.
[bio29-s5.1.b] Blood leaves the heart in surges at high pressure, and arteries have to carry it to the organs without bursting. Each feature of an artery's structure helps it cope with that pressure and keep the blood moving.
[bio29-s5.1.0] Role: arteries carry blood away from the heart to the organs, at high pressure. All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery, which takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
[bio29-s5.1.1] Thick wall, with a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic fibres, to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the ventricles (the heart's lower pumping chambers) without bursting.
[bio29-s5.1.2] Elastic fibres let the wall stretch as each surge of blood enters, then recoil between beats. This pushes the blood on, keeps the pressure up between beats and smooths the flow. The stretching is felt as the pulse.
[bio29-s5.1.3] Narrow lumen relative to the thickness of the wall, which helps keep the pressure high. In section, an artery keeps a round shape, because its thick wall holds it open.
[bio29-s5.1.4] No valves, except the semilunar valves (pocket-shaped valves) where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart. The high pressure keeps blood moving forwards, so valves are not needed along the length.
[bio29-s5.1.5] Blood flows fast and in spurts, and a cut artery spurts bright red blood. Bright red is the colour of oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s5.1.6] Arterioles are the smallest arteries, leading into the capillary networks. Their walls have a high proportion of smooth muscle, which contracts to narrow them (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to widen them (vasodilation).
[bio29-s5.1.7] By narrowing or widening, arterioles control how much blood flows to each organ, for example more to the muscles in exercise and more to the skin when hot.
[bio29-s5.2.b] By the time blood has passed through the capillaries its pressure is low, so veins must return it to the heart without a strong push behind it. Their structure reduces resistance, and valves and the squeezing of nearby muscles keep blood moving one way.
[bio29-s5.2.0] Role: veins carry blood from the organs back towards the heart, at low pressure. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary veins, which bring oxygenated blood from the lungs.
[bio29-s5.2.1] Thin wall, with a thin middle layer and few elastic fibres, because the pressure is low and there is no surge to withstand. The wall is easily squashed, so in section a vein often looks flattened or irregular.
[bio29-s5.2.2] Wide lumen, which reduces resistance to the slow-moving blood, so it can flow with little pressure behind it.
[bio29-s5.2.3] Valves: pocket-shaped, or semilunar, valves are found especially in the veins of the limbs and let blood flow only towards the heart. When blood starts to flow backwards, it fills the pockets, which close the vein.
[bio29-s5.2.4] Skeletal muscle pump: contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins that run between them, pushing blood along, and the valves prevent backflow. This is why standing still for a long time makes blood pool in the legs.
[bio29-s5.2.5] Breathing movements lower the pressure in the thorax (the chest cavity) during inhalation, which draws blood towards the heart. Veins next to arteries are also squeezed by the arterial pulse.
[bio29-s5.2.6] Venules are small veins that collect blood from capillary networks and join to form veins.
[bio29-s5.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a vein showing an open valve, with blood flowing towards the heart, and a closed valve, with its pockets filled and blocking backflow. Add arrows, and label the wall, lumen and valve.
[bio29-s5.3.b] Capillaries are where the circulatory system does its real work, because this is where materials pass between the blood and the cells. Everything about their structure makes that exchange quick and gives it time to happen.
[bio29-s5.3.0] Role: the exchange of materials between the blood and the body cells. Capillaries form dense networks, called capillary beds, in every tissue, so that no cell is far from one.
[bio29-s5.3.1] The wall is only one cell thick, consisting of endothelium only, with no muscle or elastic layers, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio29-s5.3.2] The lumen is very narrow, only wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, which brings them close to the wall.
[bio29-s5.3.3] Capillaries are so numerous that their total cross-section is large, so blood flows slowly through them, giving time for exchange.
[bio29-s5.3.4] Leaky: there are small gaps between the endothelial cells, so plasma fluid and small dissolved molecules can pass out, and white blood cells can squeeze through. Red blood cells and large plasma proteins cannot.
[bio29-s5.3.5] Pressure falls as blood flows through the capillaries, so blood leaving them enters the venules at low pressure. That is why veins have no pulse.
[bio29-s5.4.b] Cells do not touch the blood directly. Instead, part of the plasma leaks out of the capillaries and bathes the cells, and exchange takes place through this fluid.
[bio29-s5.4.0] At the arterial end of a capillary bed (the end nearer the arteriole), the blood pressure is higher and forces plasma out through the capillary wall into the spaces between the cells. Plasma is water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts and oxygen.
[bio29-s5.4.1] This fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes every cell and forms the cells' internal environment. Red blood cells and plasma proteins stay in the capillary because they are too large to pass through the gaps.
[bio29-s5.4.2] Substances pass between tissue fluid and cells by diffusion and active transport. Oxygen and nutrients enter the cells; CO₂, urea and other wastes leave them.
[bio29-s5.4.3] At the venous end the pressure is lower, and the plasma proteins left in the blood draw water back in by osmosis. So most of the tissue fluid, now carrying CO₂ and wastes, returns to the capillary.
[bio29-s5.4.4] The small amount of excess fluid is drained away by lymph vessels and returned to the blood.
[bio29-s5.4.5] Diagram: a capillary bed between an arteriole and a venule, surrounded by body cells. Add arrows for fluid leaving at the arterial end and returning at the venous end, and for O₂ and nutrients entering the cells and CO₂ and wastes leaving them.
[bio29-s5.5.b] Each vessel's structure matches its job, so the clearest way to revise is to compare the three feature by feature. Learn the pairing of each feature with its reason, because exam questions ask you to relate structure to role.
[bio29-s5.5.0] Direction of flow: arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it towards the heart; capillaries link arterioles to venules within an organ.
[bio29-s5.5.1] Pressure: high and pulsing in arteries; falling through the capillaries; low and steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.2] Wall: thick, muscular and elastic in arteries to withstand pressure; thin, with little muscle and elastic tissue, in veins; one cell thick, endothelium only, in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.3] Lumen: narrow relative to the wall in arteries; wide in veins; just wide enough for one red blood cell in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.4] Valves: none in arteries, apart from at the heart; present in veins; none in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.5] Speed of flow: fast in arteries; slow in capillaries; slow but steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.6] Oxygen content: arteries usually carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood, except in the pulmonary circuit, and capillaries are where exchange happens. This is not how the vessels are defined; they are defined by direction of flow.
[bio29-s5.5.7] Exchange: no exchange with the tissues through the thick walls of arteries and veins; all exchange happens in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.8] Diagram: transverse sections of an artery (round, thick wall, narrow lumen), a vein (thin wall, same three layers, wide lumen, often flattened) and a capillary (ring of one or two endothelial cells round one red blood cell). Label each layer and the lumen.
