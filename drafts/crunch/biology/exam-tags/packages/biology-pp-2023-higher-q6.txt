CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio26-s3: ADH and osmoregulation
[bio26-s3.0.b] The water content of the body is never constant on its own: we take water in and lose it all day. Osmoregulation (keeping the water and solute balance of the body fluids steady) stops these swings from harming our cells.
[bio26-s3.0.0] Osmoregulation in humans is mainly done by the kidneys, which change how much water is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood) from the collecting ducts, the final tubes that carry urine out of the kidney. The variable being controlled is the concentration of the blood plasma.
[bio26-s3.0.1] Water is gained from drink, food and respiration, and lost in urine, sweat, breath (as water vapour) and faeces. Gains and losses change from hour to hour, so the kidney must adjust the urine to match.
[bio26-s3.0.2] If the blood becomes too concentrated, body cells lose water by osmosis (the movement of water from a dilute region to a more concentrated one) and shrink. If it becomes too dilute, cells gain water and swell. Either upsets cell function, especially in the brain.
[bio26-s3.1.b] The kidney needs a message telling it how much water to save. That message is a hormone, ADH, which changes how leaky the collecting duct wall is to water.
[bio26-s3.1.0] ADH stands for antidiuretic hormone. Diuresis is the production of a large volume of dilute urine, so ADH works against it and reduces urine volume. ADH is released from the pituitary gland.
[bio26-s3.1.1] The target cells (the cells that respond to the hormone) line the collecting ducts and the last part of the distal convoluted tubule.
[bio26-s3.1.2] ADH binds to receptors on these cells, which causes water channel proteins, called aquaporins, to be inserted into their membranes. This makes the duct wall more permeable to water.
[bio26-s3.1.3] The collecting duct passes through the salty medulla (the inner region of the kidney), so water moves out of the more permeable duct by osmosis into the medulla and is carried away in the blood capillaries.
[bio26-s3.1.4] More ADH means more water reabsorbed, less water left in the duct, and a small volume of concentrated urine.
[bio26-s3.1.5] Less ADH means fewer water channels, so the duct wall is less permeable and less water is reabsorbed. A large volume of dilute urine is the result.
[bio26-s3.1.6] ADH changes water reabsorption only. It does not change the amount of salt or urea (the waste made from the breakdown of proteins) excreted. There is more on channel proteins in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio26-s3.2.b] When the blood becomes too concentrated, the body responds in a fixed sequence that saves water and brings the concentration back to normal. Follow the steps in order.
[bio26-s3.2.0] 1. Stimulus: the water concentration of the blood falls (its solute concentration rises), for example after heavy sweating, not drinking enough, eating salty food, or diarrhoea.
[bio26-s3.2.1] 2. Receptors: osmoreceptors, special cells in the hypothalamus of the brain that detect the concentration of the blood, notice the change.
[bio26-s3.2.2] 3. The hypothalamus causes the pituitary gland to release more ADH into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.3] 4. ADH travels in the blood to the kidneys.
[bio26-s3.2.4] 5. The collecting ducts become more permeable to water, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood.
[bio26-s3.2.5] 6. A small volume of concentrated, dark-yellow urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.2.6] 7. The water concentration of the blood rises back towards normal, and ADH release falls again. This is negative feedback, because the response reverses the original change.
[bio26-s3.2.7] At the same time the hypothalamus triggers thirst, so the person drinks more. Drinking is the only way to replace lost water, because the kidneys can only reduce further loss.
[bio26-s3.3.b] The same system works in reverse when the blood becomes too dilute. The kidney then lets more water go, so the concentration returns to normal.
[bio26-s3.3.0] 1. Stimulus: after drinking a large volume of water, the water concentration of the blood rises (it becomes more dilute).
[bio26-s3.3.1] 2. The hypothalamus detects the change.
[bio26-s3.3.2] 3. The pituitary releases less ADH.
[bio26-s3.3.3] 4. The collecting ducts become less permeable to water, because fewer water channels are present in their walls.
[bio26-s3.3.4] 5. Less water is reabsorbed, so more stays in the duct.
[bio26-s3.3.5] 6. A large volume of dilute, pale urine is produced.
[bio26-s3.3.6] 7. The blood's water concentration falls back to normal and ADH release returns to its usual level.
[bio26-s3.4.b] Everyday situations change the concentration of the blood, and ADH release changes with them. Each example below follows the same chain from blood concentration to urine volume.
[bio26-s3.4.0] On a hot day or during exercise, much water is lost in sweat, so more ADH is released and the urine is small in volume and dark.
[bio26-s3.4.1] A high salt intake raises the solute concentration of the blood, so more ADH is released, more water is reabsorbed and a smaller volume of urine is produced. The extra salt is excreted in this smaller volume, and the person also feels thirsty.
[bio26-s3.4.2] Alcohol reduces the release of ADH, so the collecting ducts reabsorb less water and a large volume of dilute urine is produced. This causes dehydration (loss of body water), which contributes to the headache and thirst of a hangover.
[bio26-s3.5.b] The two feedback responses can be drawn as one negative-feedback loop around the normal blood water concentration. Drawing it shows that every change is pushed back towards normal.
[bio26-s3.5.0] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop for water balance, with normal blood water concentration at the centre. One branch shows the water concentration falling, the hypothalamus detecting it, the pituitary releasing more ADH, the collecting ducts becoming more permeable, more water being reabsorbed, a small volume of concentrated urine being produced, and the concentration returning to normal. The other branch shows the water concentration rising, less ADH, less permeable collecting ducts, less water reabsorbed, a large volume of dilute urine, and a return to normal. Optionally add a small sketch of a collecting duct passing through the salty medulla with arrows for water leaving by osmosis.

CANDIDATES bio29-s1: Investigation: the heart
[bio29-s1.0.b] The heart is a muscular pump that never stops, and its structure is built around one job: moving blood. It is divided into four chambers, two upper and two lower, split into a left and a right side by a central wall.
[bio29-s1.0.0] Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax (the chest) between the two lungs and behind the sternum (breastbone), with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left.
[bio29-s1.0.1] The heart is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers. The fluid reduces friction as the heart beats, and the membrane prevents the heart from over-stretching.
[bio29-s1.0.2] Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
[bio29-s1.0.3] Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries (the vessels that carry blood away from the heart), so their walls are thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.0.4] The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance. The right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels).
[bio29-s1.0.5] Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.
[bio29-s1.0.6] In other words, the heart is two separate pumps side by side: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.b] Blood has to arrive at the heart and leave it by separate routes. Veins bring blood in to the atria, and arteries carry it out from the ventricles.
[bio29-s1.1.0] Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it towards it; this is not about oxygen, which is why one artery and one set of veins break the usual pattern below.
[bio29-s1.1.1] The venae cavae bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium: the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms, and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body.
[bio29-s1.1.2] The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
[bio29-s1.1.3] Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs.
[bio29-s1.1.4] The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.5] The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself with blood. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.2.b] Blood pressure inside the heart changes sharply as the muscle squeezes, and blood would simply flow back where it came from without help. Valves are flaps of tissue that keep blood flowing in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.2.0] Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. A reminder is tRIcuspid on the RIght.
[bio29-s1.2.1] AV valves let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
[bio29-s1.2.2] The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (heart strings), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
[bio29-s1.2.3] Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets.
[bio29-s1.2.4] Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
[bio29-s1.3.b] The heart wall is made of a special muscle found nowhere else in the body. Its features suit it to beating without rest for a whole lifetime.
[bio29-s1.3.0] Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped) and branched, and are joined end to end by intercalated discs.
[bio29-s1.3.1] Intercalated discs let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell, so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit.
[bio29-s1.3.2] Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue).
[bio29-s1.3.3] Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. There is more on the pacemaker that starts each beat in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.3.4] Diagram: a vertical section through the heart viewed from the front, so that the heart's left side is on the right of the page. Label the right and left atria, the right and left ventricles (left wall thicker), the septum, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves, the chordae tendineae, the papillary muscles, the semilunar valves, the superior and inferior venae cavae, the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary veins and the aorta. Add arrows for the direction of blood flow, and shade or label the oxygenated (left) and deoxygenated (right) sides.
[bio29-s1.3.5] Diagram: cardiac muscle as seen under the microscope, from a prepared slide or micrograph, showing branched, striated cells with a central nucleus and intercalated discs. Label the striations, nucleus, intercalated disc and branch.
[bio29-s1.4.b] A dissection is an investigation, so it needs an aim, hypotheses to test, controlled variables and both kinds of data. The heart you cut open is a model for the human heart.
[bio29-s1.4.0] Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection, and to relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs and images of the human heart).
[bio29-s1.4.1] Hypothesis to test: the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body.
[bio29-s1.4.2] Hypothesis to test: arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it.
[bio29-s1.4.3] Hypothesis to test: the valves allow blood to flow in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.4.4] Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): the independent variable (the one you change) is the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle or atrium); the dependent variable (the one you measure) is the wall thickness in mm.
[bio29-s1.4.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): measure at the same level, about halfway between the base and the apex, with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall.
[bio29-s1.4.6] Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean. Pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
[bio29-s1.4.7] Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle; published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart, because mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
[bio29-s1.4.8] Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep's, pig's or ox's heart from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long; dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; a camera or phone to record observations.
[bio29-s1.5.b] Start with protection and with the outside of the heart. Identifying the front surface and the vessels first tells you which side is which before you cut anything.
[bio29-s1.5.0] 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
[bio29-s1.5.1] 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it, and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart's left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
[bio29-s1.5.2] 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top), the ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top.
[bio29-s1.5.3] Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae and pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see, and photograph it.
[bio29-s1.5.4] 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters.
[bio29-s1.5.5] The vena cava leads to the right atrium; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the pulmonary veins lead to the left atrium; the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
[bio29-s1.5.6] Butchers often trim the vessels short, so use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
[bio29-s1.6.b] Now cut the heart open to look at the chambers, valves and wall thickness. Work on the right side first, then the left, then compare the walls in cross-section.
[bio29-s1.6.0] 5. Open the right side. Using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers.
[bio29-s1.6.1] Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles. Pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap. 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
[bio29-s1.6.2] 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve. Look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
[bio29-s1.6.3] 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing that the valves allow one-way flow.
[bio29-s1.6.4] Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
[bio29-s1.6.5] 9. Cut a transverse section (straight across) across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them.
[bio29-s1.6.6] Measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler, following the variables and repeats above. Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
[bio29-s1.6.7] 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope, or a micrograph, and draw it. 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings. Dispose of the heart and clean up as described under Safety and ethics.
[bio29-s1.7.b] A dissection uses sharp blades and raw animal tissue, so each hazard has a specific precaution. There is also an ethical side to using animal material.
[bio29-s1.7.0] Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person. Hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade, carry sharp instruments pointing down, and report any cut at once.
[bio29-s1.7.1] Raw meat can carry bacteria, such as Salmonella, that cause food poisoning. Wear gloves, do not touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
[bio29-s1.7.2] Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection. Wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher; it is not to be left in an open bin.
[bio29-s1.7.3] Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or when pouring water through the vessels.
[bio29-s1.7.4] Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food, a by-product of the food industry, so that no animal is killed for the dissection, and treat the tissue with respect.
[bio29-s1.7.5] A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos as secondary data, in line with the 3Rs of animal use. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio29-s1.8.b] Your results come from two sources: primary data, which you collect yourself from the dissected heart, and secondary data, which comes from diagrams, models and micrographs. Together they support the conclusions.
[bio29-s1.8.0] Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right, typically several times thicker in measurements; the septum thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.8.1] Valves: the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s1.8.2] Vessels: the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface, with their openings just above the aortic valve.
[bio29-s1.8.3] Results table to record: the structure; where it is found; its description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); the measured wall thickness in mm, with three readings and the mean; and its function.
[bio29-s1.8.4] Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels. Differences seen are due to the butcher's trimming, which may remove the atria or vessels, or to species size.
[bio29-s1.8.5] Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.
[bio29-s1.9.b] The conclusion links each structure you found to its function, and states whether each hypothesis was supported by the evidence.
[bio29-s1.9.0] The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
[bio29-s1.9.1] Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
[bio29-s1.9.2] The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
[bio29-s1.9.3] The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
[bio29-s1.9.4] Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls. There is more on this in Blood vessels.
[bio29-s1.9.5] For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.
[bio29-s1.10.b] A dissected animal heart is not a living human heart, and the measurements are rough. Knowing the limits shows how far the conclusions can be trusted and how to improve them.
[bio29-s1.10.0] Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
[bio29-s1.10.1] Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
[bio29-s1.10.2] Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
[bio29-s1.10.3] A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
[bio29-s1.10.4] One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio29-s2: Circulation
[bio29-s2.0.b] Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to get rid of wastes. The circulatory system is the transport network that does this for all of them.
[bio29-s2.0.0] The human circulatory system has three parts: the heart, a pump; the blood vessels, a closed network of tubes made up of arteries, capillaries and veins; and the blood, the transport medium that carries the materials.
[bio29-s2.0.1] It is a closed system: blood stays inside the vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across the thin walls of the capillaries (the tiniest vessels, which pass right alongside the cells).
[bio29-s2.0.2] In a large, many-celled animal, diffusion (the slow, random spreading of molecules) alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut and kidneys) and cells deep in the body.
[bio29-s2.0.3] The circulatory system solves this by carrying materials quickly by mass flow (the whole liquid moving together), so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
[bio29-s2.0.4] Materials transported: oxygen (lungs to all cells); carbon dioxide (cells to lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine to liver to cells); urea (liver to kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands to target organs); water and salts.
[bio29-s2.0.5] Also transported: antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets (which seal wounds); and heat, carried from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin.
[bio29-s2.1.b] Humans have two circuits joined at the heart rather than one loop. Blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and is then sent out to the rest of the body.
[bio29-s2.1.0] Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. This does not mean two hearts; it is one heart with two sides.
[bio29-s2.1.1] The pulmonary circuit (pulmonary means 'of the lungs') runs heart to lungs to heart. The systemic circuit runs heart to body organs to heart.
[bio29-s2.1.2] The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit. There is more on the chambers in Investigation: the heart.
[bio29-s2.1.3] Full route of a red blood cell: vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, semilunar valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valve, aorta, artery, arteriole, capillaries of an organ, venule, vein, vena cava.
[bio29-s2.1.4] Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation. Heart as four boxes, lungs above, body organs below. Add arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae.
[bio29-s2.1.5] Diagram labels: right and left atrium and ventricle, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta, venae cavae, pulmonary circuit, systemic circuit. Shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue.
[bio29-s2.2.b] The pulmonary circuit has one job: to send blood that has given up its oxygen to the lungs to collect more. It starts at the right side of the heart.
[bio29-s2.2.0] 1. Deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) is pumped by the right ventricle (the lower right chamber, which pumps blood out of the heart) into the pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s2.2.1] 2. The pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung, so both lungs receive blood.
[bio29-s2.2.2] 3. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂, because oxygen diffuses in from the air in the alveoli (air sacs) and CO₂ diffuses out. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio29-s2.2.3] 4. Oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium (the upper left chamber, which receives blood coming into the heart).
[bio29-s2.3.b] The systemic circuit takes oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to every organ and brings it back. Its steps follow on from the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.3.0] 1. Oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve (the one-way valve between the left atrium and left ventricle) to the left ventricle.
[bio29-s2.3.1] 2. The left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, the body's largest artery.
[bio29-s2.3.2] 3. Arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ, where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes.
[bio29-s2.3.3] 4. Deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae (the large veins bringing blood from the upper and lower body) and into the right atrium.
[bio29-s2.3.4] 5. It passes through the tricuspid valve (the one-way valve between the right atrium and right ventricle) to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
[bio29-s2.4.b] A double circulation is better than a single loop for an active, warm-blooded animal. Passing the blood through the heart twice solves two problems: lost pressure and the risk of damage to the lungs.
[bio29-s2.4.0] (a) Blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all the body organs quickly.
[bio29-s2.4.1] (b) The two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit, to drive blood through the many organs, and lower in the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.4.2] The lower pulmonary pressure matters because the lung capillaries are thin-walled, so they are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs).
[bio29-s2.4.3] (c) The septum (the wall dividing the two sides of the heart) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated.
[bio29-s2.4.4] Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
[bio29-s2.5.b] Blood reaches each organ along its own named artery and leaves along its own named vein. Knowing these names lets you describe the route to any organ.
[bio29-s2.5.0] A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and is drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and each is usually named after the organ.
[bio29-s2.5.1] Lungs (pulmonary pathway): the pulmonary artery, from the right ventricle, carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins, to the left atrium, carry oxygenated blood. In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s2.5.2] Head and brain: the carotid arteries, in the neck, supply it, and the jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava. Arms: the subclavian arteries and veins. Legs: the iliac arteries and veins.
[bio29-s2.5.3] Kidneys: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered, and the renal veins return filtered blood, low in urea, to the inferior vena cava. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio29-s2.5.4] Heart muscle: the coronary arteries and cardiac veins, which supply the heart's own muscle with blood.
[bio29-s2.5.5] Liver and gut: the liver receives blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut). Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
[bio29-s2.5.6] Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels showing the heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
[bio29-s2.6.b] Most veins take blood straight back towards the heart. The vein from the gut is unusual because it detours through the liver first, so the liver gets the first chance to deal with what has just been absorbed.
[bio29-s2.6.0] A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. Blood therefore passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
[bio29-s2.6.1] The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the capillaries in the villi (the tiny finger-like folds of the small intestine wall), and from the walls of the stomach and large intestine.
[bio29-s2.6.2] It carries this blood to a second set of capillaries, the sinusoids, in the liver. After a meal this blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
[bio29-s2.6.3] Absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio29-s2.6.4] The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood, because it has already passed through the gut capillaries, so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery.
[bio29-s2.7.b] The circulatory system works as the delivery service for every other system. Each system either puts materials into the blood or takes materials out of it, and you can model these exchanges.
[bio29-s2.7.0] Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂. The circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and carries CO₂ back, so without circulation the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
[bio29-s2.7.1] Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals, small vessels in the villi) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells. The blood also supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
[bio29-s2.7.2] Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it. The kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume.
[bio29-s2.7.3] Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs, for example insulin from the pancreas to the liver and ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys. Adrenaline, carried in the blood, increases heart rate.
[bio29-s2.7.4] Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds.
[bio29-s2.7.5] Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells. Active muscles receive extra blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, and contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping to return blood to the heart.
[bio29-s2.7.6] Nervous system: the medulla oblongata (the part of the brain that controls automatic functions) adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles (small arteries), so blood is sent where it is needed. The brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
[bio29-s2.7.7] Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs, and widening or narrowing of the skin arterioles controls heat loss.
[bio29-s2.8.b] A model is a simplified picture that shows how the systems connect. A flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre makes it clear which materials travel where.
[bio29-s2.8.0] Diagram: a labelled flow diagram modelling these interactions, with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain. Label each arrow with the materials transferred: O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones and heat.
[bio29-s2.8.1] One limitation of the model is that it shows what moves but not the structure of the vessels or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
[bio29-s2.8.2] Worked example: during exercise, muscles respire faster, so breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s2.8.3] In the same example, heart rate rises and the arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them, while the arterioles to the gut narrow. Heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

CANDIDATES bio31-s7: Energy and nutrient transfer
[bio31-s7.0.b] Every ecosystem sorts its organisms by how they get energy and nutrients. Producers bring energy in, consumers pass it along, and decomposers return the nutrients to the soil or water.
[bio31-s7.0.0] Producers are autotrophs: organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly by photosynthesis, using light energy. They include green plants, algae, phytoplankton (microscopic floating algae) and photosynthetic bacteria.
[bio31-s7.0.1] Producers are the entry point of energy and matter into every food chain, because no other group can turn light energy and inorganic substances into organic food. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio31-s7.0.2] Consumers are heterotrophs, meaning they cannot make their own food and obtain energy and nutrients by eating other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers, e.g. rabbits, slugs, caterpillars and limpets.
[bio31-s7.0.3] Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, e.g. thrushes, frogs and crabs. Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers, e.g. sparrowhawks, foxes and herons.
[bio31-s7.0.4] A top (apex) consumer is at the end of a chain and is not normally eaten. Carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat plants and animals, e.g. badgers, foxes and humans.
[bio31-s7.0.5] Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, feed on dead organisms and waste. They secrete enzymes onto the dead matter and absorb the products (saprophytic nutrition), and so release minerals and CO₂ for producers to reuse.
[bio31-s7.0.6] Detritivores are animals that eat dead organic matter (detritus), e.g. earthworms and woodlice. They break it into smaller pieces, which gives decomposers a larger surface to act on, so decomposition is faster. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio31-s7.1.b] A food chain shows who eats whom, and each step is a trophic (feeding) level. Following the chain shows how energy and nutrients are passed on from one organism to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.0] A trophic (feeding) level is the position an organism occupies in a food chain, according to how it obtains its energy. Producers are trophic level 1, primary consumers level 2, secondary consumers level 3 and tertiary consumers level 4.
[bio31-s7.1.1] A food chain is a sequence of organisms in which each is eaten by the next, showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one trophic level to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.2] Arrows point from the organism that is eaten towards the organism that eats it, which is the direction of energy flow ("is eaten by").
[bio31-s7.1.3] Irish examples, one per ecosystem. Grassland: grass → rabbit → fox. Hedgerow or woodland: oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk. Rocky shore: seaweed → periwinkle → crab → herring gull.
[bio31-s7.1.4] Two more Irish examples. Freshwater: phytoplankton → water flea → minnow → heron (or pike). Sea: phytoplankton → zooplankton → herring → seal.
[bio31-s7.1.5] Grazing food chains start with living producers. Detritus (decomposer) food chains start with dead organic matter, e.g. dead leaves → earthworm → blackbird. Much of the energy in a woodland passes through detritus chains.
[bio31-s7.1.6] Food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels, because so much energy is lost at each level that too little is left to support a further level.
[bio31-s7.2.b] Real organisms rarely eat just one thing or get eaten by just one thing. A food web joins the separate food chains of an ecosystem into one network, which is why it is a more realistic picture.
[bio31-s7.2.0] A food web is a set of interlinked food chains in an ecosystem. It is more realistic than a single chain because most organisms eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator.
[bio31-s7.2.1] An organism may also feed at more than one trophic level, as an omnivore does, or as a fox does when it eats rabbits and also beetles.
[bio31-s7.2.2] Example grassland and hedgerow web to draw: grass → rabbit, grass → slug and grass → caterpillar; grass seeds → wood mouse; slug → thrush and caterpillar → thrush; rabbit → fox; wood mouse → fox and wood mouse → barn owl; thrush → sparrowhawk.
[bio31-s7.2.3] Decomposers (bacteria, fungi and earthworms) feed on dead material from every level of this web, because every organism eventually dies or produces waste.
[bio31-s7.2.4] Food webs show interdependence: a change in one population affects many others, directly through feeding and indirectly through competition for the same food. For example, the fox and the barn owl both eat wood mice, so they compete.
[bio31-s7.2.5] Diagram: the food web drawn with arrows pointing towards the consumer, organisms placed in rows by trophic level, and decomposers shown at the side receiving arrows from all levels.
[bio31-s7.3.b] Energy enters an ecosystem once, as sunlight, and then dwindles as it is passed along a food chain. Most of it is lost at every step, and this explains both the shape of food chains and how we should feed people.
[bio31-s7.3.0] Energy enters ecosystems as light. Producers capture only a small fraction of the light that reaches them, around 1–2%, and store it as chemical energy in organic molecules. The rest is reflected, passes through leaves, or is lost as heat.
[bio31-s7.3.1] On average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to, and stored in, the next. The figure varies, roughly from 5% to 20%, so about 90% is lost at each transfer.
[bio31-s7.3.2] Respiration releases energy for movement, growth, active transport and keeping warm, and this ends up as heat lost to the surroundings. Birds and mammals lose the most, because they keep a constant body temperature.
[bio31-s7.3.3] Roots, bark, wood, bones and fur are left uneaten. Undigested food is egested as faeces, e.g. cellulose eaten by herbivores, and some energy is lost in excretory products such as urea. The energy in uneaten parts, faeces and dead bodies passes to decomposers, not to the next level.
[bio31-s7.3.4] Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuous input of energy from the Sun.
[bio31-s7.3.5] Worked example (illustrative, using the 10% figure, not measured data): grass in a field stores 10 000 kJ, the rabbits that eat it store about 1 000 kJ, and the foxes about 100 kJ. A fourth level would store about 10 kJ, too little to support a population.
[bio31-s7.3.6] So there are fewer top predators, they often need large territories, and they are especially vulnerable to habitat loss.
[bio31-s7.3.7] Consequence for food production: eating plants directly, a shorter chain, uses the energy captured by crops more efficiently than feeding crops to animals and then eating the animals. A given area of land can therefore feed more people on plant-based food.
[bio31-s7.4.b] Matter, unlike energy, passes along a food chain in the form of the food organisms eat. Some is lost at each level, but the ecosystem as a whole does not lose it, because decomposers send it back to producers.
[bio31-s7.4.0] Nutrients (matter) pass between trophic levels when one organism eats another. The carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids of the food are digested, absorbed and built into the consumer's own body (assimilated).
[bio31-s7.4.1] The elements involved are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (in all biomolecules), nitrogen (in proteins and nucleic acids), phosphorus (in DNA, ATP and phospholipids), and minerals such as calcium and iron.
[bio31-s7.4.2] The amount of matter passed on also decreases at each level. Much of the food eaten is respired (carbon leaves as CO₂, hydrogen as water), egested in faeces, or excreted (nitrogen as urea or ammonia). So biomass (the mass of living material) also decreases up a food chain.
[bio31-s7.4.3] Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the ecosystem. Decomposers break down dead bodies, faeces and urine, releasing CO₂ and minerals such as nitrate, phosphate and ammonium that producers absorb and reuse, so nutrients cycle.
[bio31-s7.4.4] Contrast: energy flows one way through the ecosystem and is lost as heat, whereas nutrients cycle and are reused again and again.
[bio31-s7.4.5] Persistent substances that are not broken down or excreted, such as the insecticide DDT and mercury, pass up the food chain with the food and build up in the bodies of organisms at each level. They reach the highest concentrations in top consumers, which is called biomagnification.
[bio31-s7.4.6] Biomagnification is how DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey, which sit at the top of food chains and so carry the highest doses.
[bio31-s7.4.7] Diagram: a flow diagram of one food chain, with energy arrows getting narrower at each level, heat-loss arrows from every level, and a separate loop of nutrient arrows from all levels to decomposers and back to producers.
[bio31-s7.5.b] A food chain or web is a model: a simplified picture that lets you work out what happens when one population goes up or down. Following a set method keeps the prediction logical.
[bio31-s7.5.0] The method has five steps. 1. Find the organisms that eat the changed species (its predators).
[bio31-s7.5.1] 2. Find what the changed species eats (its food).
[bio31-s7.5.2] 3. Find its competitors, the species that share its food.
[bio31-s7.5.3] 4. Predict the direction of each change, giving reasons.
[bio31-s7.5.4] 5. Note other effects, e.g. on plants and habitat.
[bio31-s7.5.5] Worked prediction using the food web above, if a disease such as myxomatosis (a viral disease of rabbits) kills most of the rabbits: (a) grass is grazed less and grows taller, providing more food and cover for slugs, caterpillars and wood mice.
[bio31-s7.5.6] Continued: (b) foxes lose their main prey, so they eat more wood mice and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds, and fox numbers may fall. (c) Wood mice decline from the extra fox predation, so barn owls, which compete with foxes for wood mice, have less food.
[bio31-s7.5.7] Real outcomes depend on how flexible each predator's diet is, because a predator that can switch to other food will be affected less.
[bio31-s7.6.b] Changes spread through a web both when a species is removed and when one is added. Because the model is simplified, its predictions have limits.
[bio31-s7.6.0] Removing a top predator can cause a trophic cascade, a chain of effects passing down the food web. In Ireland, with no wolves, deer numbers increase, they browse young trees, and the woodland cannot regenerate, which affects every species that depends on the woodland.
[bio31-s7.6.1] Adding a species also spreads through a web. An invasive filter-feeder such as the zebra mussel removes phytoplankton at the base of a lake food web, reducing food for zooplankton and for the fish that eat them. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio31-s7.6.2] Food chains and webs simplify real feeding, because many links are left out and diets change with the season and with age.
[bio31-s7.6.3] They usually show no numbers or amounts of energy, which pyramids do, and decomposers are often left out. There is more on models in general in Models.

CANDIDATES bio32-s4: Biotic factors and relationships
[bio32-s4.0.b] Biotic factors are the effects that living organisms have on each other. In a grassland they include food supply, predation, grazing, competition, parasites and disease, pollination and decomposition, and human activities such as grazing livestock, mowing and fertilising. Grazing and mowing show how one biotic factor shapes which plants can live in a habitat.
[bio32-s4.0.0] Grazing by rabbits, cattle or sheep and mowing keep grass short. This lets low rosette plants (plants whose leaves lie flat close to the ground) such as daisy, dandelion and plantain get light, and stops shrubs and trees from invading.
[bio32-s4.0.1] If grazing or mowing stops, taller grasses shade out the rosette plants because they grow above them and take the light. In time scrub (a tangle of woody shrubs, e.g. brambles, hawthorn) invades.
[bio32-s4.1.b] Other living things work in the grassland soil and on its flowers. Some recycle dead material back into the ground, and others carry pollen between flowers.
[bio32-s4.1.0] Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) and detritivores (animals that eat dead organic matter, e.g. earthworms, woodlice, and slugs in part) break down dead plants, dung and dead animals, returning minerals to the soil so that plants can take them up again.
[bio32-s4.1.1] Decomposition recycles carbon and nitrogen, and there is more on this in The carbon cycle, The nitrogen cycle and Bacteria and fungi.
[bio32-s4.1.2] Earthworms also drag leaves underground, mix and aerate the soil (let air into it), and improve drainage.
[bio32-s4.1.3] Pollinators such as bumblebees, hoverflies and butterflies feed on the nectar and pollen of clover, dandelion and buttercup flowers, and carry pollen between them, which is how these plants are pollinated.
[bio32-s4.2.b] To work out who eats whom in a grassland, you gather primary data, meaning information you collect yourself in the field. You then check it against what other people have already recorded.
[bio32-s4.2.0] Direct observation of animals feeding shows a feeding relationship at the moment it happens.
[bio32-s4.2.1] Feeding signs are evidence left behind: nibbled grass, holes in leaves, and broken snail shells at a thrush’s stone.
[bio32-s4.2.2] Droppings and pellets show what an animal ate, e.g. undigested fur and bones in the pellets of birds of prey.
[bio32-s4.2.3] The habitat where each animal was caught is a clue, e.g. aphids found on the stems they feed on.
[bio32-s4.2.4] Your findings are then checked against secondary sources (information collected by others), such as field guides, for what each species eats.
[bio32-s4.3.b] Every feeding relationship starts with a green plant that makes food. Animals then feed on the plants, and on each other, in layers called trophic levels (feeding levels).
[bio32-s4.3.0] Producers in the grassland are grasses, clover, daisy, dandelion, buttercup and other green plants, which make food by photosynthesis.
[bio32-s4.3.1] Primary consumers (herbivores, which eat plants) in the grassland: rabbits and hares graze grass and clover; slugs and snails eat leaves; grasshoppers and caterpillars chew leaves; aphids suck sap from stems; bumblebees take nectar and pollen.
[bio32-s4.3.2] Secondary and higher consumers (carnivores, which eat animals, and omnivores, which eat both): ladybirds eat aphids; spiders catch flies, grasshoppers and other insects; ground beetles eat slugs and other invertebrates.
[bio32-s4.3.3] Song thrushes eat snails (smashing their shells on a stone) and earthworms; blackbirds eat earthworms and insects; hedgehogs eat slugs, beetles and earthworms.
[bio32-s4.3.4] Badgers eat mainly earthworms; foxes eat rabbits, mice and birds; kestrels eat wood mice, pygmy shrews and large insects.
[bio32-s4.3.5] Pygmy shrews eat small invertebrates such as beetles, spiders and woodlice.
[bio32-s4.4.b] A food chain follows one line of feeding, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder. Because most animals eat more than one thing, the chains join up into a food web.
[bio32-s4.4.0] Worked example of grassland food chains: grass → rabbit → fox; clover leaves → slug → song thrush; plant sap → aphid → ladybird → (insect-eating bird); grass seeds and invertebrates → wood mouse → kestrel.
[bio32-s4.4.1] These chains connect into a food web. For example, the fox eats rabbits, wood mice and birds, so it is on several chains.
[bio32-s4.4.2] The earthworm feeds on dead leaves and is eaten by thrushes, blackbirds, badgers and hedgehogs, so it links the dead material in the soil to several animals above it.
[bio32-s4.4.3] Diagram: a grassland food web of 8–12 of the species listed above, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder.
[bio32-s4.5.b] A food web shows that organisms depend on each other, so a change in one population spreads through the whole community.
[bio32-s4.5.0] The web shows interdependence: a change in one population affects others.
[bio32-s4.5.1] For example, if disease kills many rabbits, foxes eat more wood mice and birds, so those populations fall, and the grass grows taller because fewer rabbits graze it, shading out daisies.
[bio32-s4.5.2] An omnivore or a predator with several prey is less affected than one with a single food source, because it can switch to another food when one becomes scarce. There is more on predicting changes from webs in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio32-s4.6.b] Predation is the best-known feeding relationship between animals. Predators have features that help them find, catch and kill their prey.
[bio32-s4.6.0] Predation is a feeding relationship in which one animal, the predator, kills and eats another, the prey.
[bio32-s4.6.1] Grassland examples of predation: fox (predator) and rabbit (prey); kestrel and wood mouse; ladybird and aphid; song thrush and snail.
[bio32-s4.6.2] The fox has acute hearing and smell to find prey, forward-facing eyes for judging distance, speed and sharp teeth, and hunts mostly at night, when rabbits feed.
[bio32-s4.6.3] The kestrel hovers into the wind while scanning the ground with very keen eyesight, then drops onto its prey, gripping it with sharp talons and tearing it with a hooked beak.
[bio32-s4.6.4] Spiders build webs to trap flying insects.
[bio32-s4.7.b] Prey animals need to spot danger early, escape, or avoid being noticed or eaten in the first place.
[bio32-s4.7.0] Rabbits have eyes on the sides of the head, giving an almost all-round field of view, and large mobile ears, so they can detect a predator coming from any direction.
[bio32-s4.7.1] Rabbits feed in groups so that some can watch for danger, thump the ground with a hind foot and flash the white underside of the tail as a warning, run in zigzags, and shelter in burrows.
[bio32-s4.7.2] Snails withdraw into their shells for protection.
[bio32-s4.7.3] Ladybirds have bright warning colours and a bad taste, so predators learn to avoid them.
[bio32-s4.7.4] Many caterpillars and grasshoppers are green, camouflaged against the grass, so predators find them hard to see.
[bio32-s4.8.b] Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together in a repeating cycle, because each population depends on the other.
[bio32-s4.8.0] 1. When prey are plentiful, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise.
[bio32-s4.8.1] 2. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.2] 3. With less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.3] 4. With fewer predators, prey numbers recover, and the cycle repeats.
[bio32-s4.8.4] Predator numbers peak after prey numbers, because predators need time to breed once food is plentiful. There are usually fewer predators than prey.
[bio32-s4.8.5] The classic data are the fur-trading records of the snowshoe hare and the lynx in Canada, which rose and fell in cycles of about ten years.
[bio32-s4.8.6] Diagram: a graph of predator and prey numbers against time: two out-of-step waves, the predator curve lower and peaking after the prey curve.
[bio32-s4.9.b] Predators do more than eat prey. They also help to set how many prey a habitat holds.
[bio32-s4.9.0] Predators usually take the old, sick, young or weak prey, which are the easiest to catch.
[bio32-s4.9.1] The effect of predation increases as prey become more numerous, because prey are easier to find. This helps keep the prey population near the habitat’s carrying capacity (the largest population the habitat can support). There is more on this in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio32-s4.9.2] Removing a predator can let its prey increase until it overgrazes its food.
[bio32-s4.10.b] Organisms compete when they need the same resource and there is not enough for all. Competition can be sorted by who is competing and by how the resource is shared out.
[bio32-s4.10.0] Competition is the struggle between organisms for a resource that is in short supply. Plants compete for light, water, minerals and space; animals compete for food, water, space or territory, shelter and mates.
[bio32-s4.10.1] If the resource is plentiful, there is no competition.
[bio32-s4.10.2] Intraspecific competition is between members of the same species. It is the most intense kind, because they have exactly the same needs, e.g. daisy rosettes crowding each other for space and light, or male blackbirds or robins defending territories that contain food and nest sites.
[bio32-s4.10.3] Interspecific competition is between different species that need the same resource, e.g. grasses and daisies competing for light, or rabbits and sheep grazing the same grass.
[bio32-s4.10.4] Contest competition: there is an active physical contest for the resource and the winner takes it, e.g. birds fighting over territory, or stags fighting over mates.
[bio32-s4.10.5] Scramble competition: each competitor gets as much as it can of a shared resource, so when it is scarce all get too little, e.g. seedlings crowded together all grow weak and thin, or aphids crowded on a stem.
[bio32-s4.11.b] Competition has a cost for the organisms that lose, and this affects the whole population. Organisms also have features that help them win.
[bio32-s4.11.0] Competition reduces the growth, survival and breeding of the losers, and so limits population size. Its effect grows as the population becomes more crowded.
[bio32-s4.11.1] Between species, one may exclude the other from the habitat, or each may survive by using the resources in a slightly different way (different niches, meaning different roles or ways of living in the habitat). There is more on this in The niche.
[bio32-s4.11.2] Adaptations that help organisms compete: fast growth, and tall stems or climbing (e.g. ivy, brambles) in plants competing for light; deep roots for water; rapid breeding; aggressive defence of territory in animals.
[bio32-s4.12.b] Two examples show interspecific competition at work: one in the grassland and one from Ireland.
[bio32-s4.12.0] In short, grazed or mown grass, daisies and other rosette plants get enough light. Where grass is left long, it outgrows and shades them, and they disappear (interspecific competition for light).
[bio32-s4.12.1] Irish example: grey squirrels, introduced to Ireland at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford, in 1911, compete with the native red squirrel for food, especially seeds and nuts.
[bio32-s4.12.2] Grey squirrels are larger, can digest acorns (which red squirrels digest poorly), eat hazelnuts before they ripen, and carry squirrelpox virus, which kills red squirrels but rarely harms grey squirrels. Red squirrels have disappeared from much of the area greys occupy.
[bio32-s4.12.3] Where pine marten numbers have recovered in Ireland, grey squirrels have declined and red squirrels have returned.
[bio32-s4.13.b] Some organisms do not just compete or eat each other but live together. This close partnership is called symbiosis, and it is sorted into three types by who benefits.
[bio32-s4.13.0] Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship in which two organisms of different species live together.
[bio32-s4.13.1] There are three types, named by who benefits: mutualism (both benefit, +/+), commensalism (one benefits and the other is unaffected, +/0) and parasitism (one benefits and the other is harmed, +/−).
[bio32-s4.13.2] Pollinators and flowers benefit each other (food for the insect, pollination for the plant), so the relationship is mutualistic, but the two don’t live together, so many biologists don’t call it symbiosis.
[bio32-s4.14.b] In mutualism both partners gain. The first example is a bacterium that gives clover a supply of nitrogen, which is why clover can enrich grassland.
[bio32-s4.14.0] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) live in root nodules (small swellings) of clover and other legumes. The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonium, which the plant uses to make amino acids and proteins.
[bio32-s4.14.1] The plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (from photosynthesis) and a protected home.
[bio32-s4.14.2] Active nodules are pink inside, from a pigment (leghaemoglobin) that controls oxygen levels for the bacteria.
[bio32-s4.14.3] This is why clover grows well in soils poor in nitrogen and enriches grassland. There is more on this in The nitrogen cycle.
[bio32-s4.14.4] To observe: dig up a clover plant, wash the roots and examine the nodules with a hand lens; cut one to see the pink colour.
[bio32-s4.15.b] Mutualism is common wherever one partner can do something the other cannot. Here are three more examples, and in each the partners swap food or shelter for something they lack.
[bio32-s4.15.0] Cellulose-digesting microorganisms (bacteria, protists and fungi) live in the rumen (first stomach) of cattle and sheep, and in the caecum (a pouch of the gut) of rabbits. They digest the cellulose of grass, which the animal has no enzyme to digest.
[bio32-s4.15.1] The microorganisms get a warm, food-rich home, and the animal gets the products (fatty acids) as food. Rabbits also eat some of their own soft droppings to absorb nutrients released by these microorganisms.
[bio32-s4.15.2] Mycorrhizal fungi grow around and into the roots of most grassland plants and trees. The fungal hyphae (thread-like filaments) spread far through the soil, absorbing water and minerals (especially phosphate) and passing them to the plant; the plant supplies the fungus with sugars.
[bio32-s4.15.3] Lichens (e.g. on walls, rocks and tree bark around the grassland) are a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) living together. The alga photosynthesises and supplies sugars; the fungus gives shelter, holds water and absorbs minerals.
[bio32-s4.15.4] Bacteria in the human gut are another example of mutualism. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.b] In commensalism one partner gains and the other is neither helped nor harmed. It is easy to confuse with mutualism, so check whether the second partner really gets nothing.
[bio32-s4.16.0] Commensalism: one partner benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
[bio32-s4.16.1] Mosses and lichens growing on the bark of trees get a place in the light without harming the tree.
[bio32-s4.16.2] Many bacteria living on human skin feed on dead skin cells and sweat without harming the host. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.3] Seeds with hooks (e.g. burdock) are carried in an animal’s fur, and robins follow a gardener or grazing cattle to catch the invertebrates they disturb; both are commensal relationships, since the animal or gardener is unaffected.
[bio32-s4.17.b] A parasite lives on or in another organism, the host, and feeds on it. Unlike a predator, it usually keeps its host alive.
[bio32-s4.17.0] Parasitism: the parasite lives in or on the host and feeds on it, harming it. A parasite usually weakens its host without killing it quickly, since it depends on the host.
[bio32-s4.17.1] Grassland ectoparasites (parasites living on the outside of the host): fleas on rabbits and hedgehogs; ticks on sheep, deer and humans. Ticks are also vectors of Lyme disease, meaning they carry it from host to host.
[bio32-s4.17.2] The rabbit flea carries the myxoma virus that causes myxomatosis, which has killed very large numbers of rabbits.
[bio32-s4.17.3] Grassland endoparasites (parasites living inside the host): liver fluke in sheep and cattle on wet pasture; roundworms and tapeworms in the gut of grazing animals. There is more on liver fluke and tapeworms in Pathogens.
[bio32-s4.17.4] Plant parasites: rust fungi on grasses and cereals.
[bio32-s4.17.5] Yellow rattle, a meadow plant, is a partial parasite: it photosynthesises but taps the roots of grasses for water and minerals, weakening the grasses so that other wild flowers can grow. For this reason it is sown when restoring wildflower meadows.
[bio32-s4.17.6] Ivy is not a parasite: it climbs trees but makes its own food and takes nothing from them.
[bio32-s4.18.b] A parasite usually weakens its host rather than killing it quickly, but parasites and disease can still change how many hosts there are, because weakened hosts breed less and are caught more easily.
[bio32-s4.18.0] Parasites and disease weaken hosts, making them easier prey and less able to breed.
[bio32-s4.18.1] Parasites spread fastest when hosts are crowded, so their effect increases with population density, like predation.
[bio32-s4.19.b] The signs show the effect on each partner: + for benefits, − for harmed, and 0 for unaffected. Use them to compare the five relationships.
[bio32-s4.19.0] Predation (+/−): a predator kills and eats its prey, e.g. fox and rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.1] Competition (−/−): both organisms lose some of a scarce resource, e.g. grass and daisy for light, or grey and red squirrel.
[bio32-s4.19.2] Mutualism (+/+): both benefit, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules.
[bio32-s4.19.3] Commensalism (+/0): one benefits and the other is unaffected, e.g. mosses on tree bark.
[bio32-s4.19.4] Parasitism (+/−): the host is harmed but usually not killed quickly, e.g. a flea on a rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.5] Predation and parasitism are both +/−. The difference is that a predator kills its prey at once and eats it, while a parasite lives on a living host over a long time.
