PART (c)(iii) (9 marks) subsections: bio32-s4
MODEL ANSWER: When prey are plentiful, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall. With less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall. With fewer predators, prey numbers recover and the cycle repeats. There is a time lag, so predator numbers peak after prey numbers.

CANDIDATES bio31-s1: Biosphere, ecosystems, habitats and biodiversity
[bio31-s1.0.b] Ecology looks at how living things fit into the world around them. To talk about it clearly, ecologists sort life into levels, each one bigger than the last, and each has its own precise name.
[bio31-s1.0.0] Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment. The word comes from the Greek oikos, meaning house or home, because it is about the place where organisms live and how they get on there.
[bio31-s1.0.1] A population is all the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time, e.g. all the daisies in a field, or all the red squirrels in a woodland. Members can interbreed because they are one species.
[bio31-s1.0.2] A community is all the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time, e.g. all the plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms of a woodland. It is the living (biotic) part of an ecosystem only, with no soil, water or air.
[bio31-s1.0.3] The ecological levels, smallest to largest, are: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere. Each level includes the one before it, and from the ecosystem upward the non-living environment is included too.
[bio31-s1.0.4] These levels continue the levels of organisation inside a body (cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism): the organism is where the two sets of levels meet, and ecology then builds upward from it.
[bio31-s1.0.5] Diagram: nested boxes or a stepped sequence. Show one rabbit (organism), a group of rabbits (population), rabbits with grass, foxes and other species (community), the community with soil, water, air and light (ecosystem), and the Earth's living layer (biosphere). Label each level.
[bio31-s1.1.b] The biosphere is the largest ecological level: the whole of the Earth's life taken together. Its limits and the way matter and energy move through it explain why life depends on the Sun.
[bio31-s1.1.0] The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists: the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water (fresh water and oceans). It is the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.1.1] The biosphere is a thin layer compared with the size of the Earth. Life is found from the deep ocean floor to a few kilometres up in the air, in birds, spores and bacteria carried on the wind.
[bio31-s1.1.2] Most life is concentrated in a narrow zone near the surface, because that is where light, liquid water and suitable temperatures are all present.
[bio31-s1.1.3] The biosphere is a single, closed system for matter, because atoms are recycled and not added. It is an open system for energy, because energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer, The carbon cycle and The nitrogen cycle.
[bio31-s1.2.b] An ecosystem is a community together with the non-living world it lives in. The living and non-living parts affect each other, which is why you have to study both.
[bio31-s1.2.0] An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment in a particular area. It has living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts that affect each other.
[bio31-s1.2.1] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment and the effects organisms have on each other: food supply, predation (one animal hunting and eating another), competition, disease, parasites, pollinators and decomposers.
[bio31-s1.2.2] Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions of the environment: light, temperature, water, oxygen, soil type and pH, minerals, wind, slope and aspect (the direction a slope faces). There is more on this in Abiotic factors within Investigating an Ecosystem, and in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio31-s1.2.3] Ecosystems have no fixed size: a rock pool, a hedgerow, a lake, a raised bog or an ocean can each be treated as an ecosystem. Their boundaries are often gradual, e.g. a woodland edge merging into grassland.
[bio31-s1.2.4] Every ecosystem needs a continual input of energy, almost always sunlight captured by producers (such as plants) in photosynthesis. Its matter is recycled by decomposers (such as fungi and bacteria), so the ecosystem keeps running.
[bio31-s1.2.5] Producers, consumers and decomposers each have a different role in an ecosystem. Consumers are the animals that get their energy by eating other organisms. The roles of all three are taught in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio31-s1.2.6] Irish land ecosystems include grassland, the most widespread land cover in Ireland, mostly farmed pasture and meadow. Woodland includes the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park. Peatlands have raised bogs in the midlands and blanket bogs in the uplands and the west.
[bio31-s1.2.7] Irish water and coastal ecosystems include freshwater, such as lakes like Lough Derg, a lake on the River Shannon, and rivers. Coastal and marine ecosystems include rocky shores, sandy shores and sand dunes, salt marshes and the open sea.
[bio31-s1.3.b] An ecosystem is too big to describe where one particular animal lives. Ecologists therefore talk about habitats, and smaller microhabitats, to say exactly where an organism is found.
[bio31-s1.3.0] A habitat is the place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs. A woodlouse's habitat is damp leaf litter, a limpet's is the rocky shore, and a frog's is a pond and the damp vegetation around it.
[bio31-s1.3.1] A microhabitat is a small area within a habitat with its own conditions, e.g. the underside of a stone on a rocky shore, a rock pool, a rotting log, or the bark of a tree. Shade, moisture and temperature there can differ greatly from the surroundings.
[bio31-s1.3.2] One ecosystem contains many habitats: a woodland contains the canopy, the trunk, the leaf litter and the soil, each with different species. A habitat is the organism's 'address'; its role there is its niche, which is dealt with in The niche.
[bio31-s1.4.b] Biodiversity is more than a count of species. It describes how much variety there is in life, and it can be measured at three different levels, from genes up to whole habitats.
[bio31-s1.4.0] Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels. It is usually described at three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem (habitat) diversity.
[bio31-s1.4.1] Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (different versions of a gene) within a species or population, e.g. the different breeds of cattle, or the variation in disease resistance among wild ash trees. It allows a population to adapt by natural selection when conditions change.
[bio31-s1.4.2] Low genetic diversity left the Irish potato crop of the 1840s vulnerable, because it was largely one variety, so when blight (a disease) arrived it destroyed it.
[bio31-s1.4.3] Species diversity is the variety of species in an area. It has two parts: species richness, the number of different species present, and species evenness, how evenly the individuals are spread among those species.
[bio31-s1.4.4] Two areas with the same species can differ in diversity if one is dominated by a single species. The Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness into one number, is Higher Level only. There is more on this in Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio31-s1.4.5] Ecosystem (habitat) diversity is the variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region, e.g. Ireland's bogs, woodlands, grasslands, lakes, rivers and coasts. A region with many habitats supports more species, because each habitat suits different organisms.
[bio31-s1.5.b] Life is not spread evenly across the Earth. Some places hold far more species than others, and Ireland is a good local example of a place with fewer.
[bio31-s1.5.0] Biodiversity is generally higher in warm, wet, stable environments and lower in cold or extreme ones. Tropical rainforests and coral reefs are the most species-rich ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.5.1] Ireland has fewer species than Britain or mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age, before many species had recolonised it from the south.
[bio31-s1.5.2] In brief, biodiversity provides food, medicines and materials, supports processes such as pollination and nutrient cycling, and makes ecosystems more stable. There is more on this, and on its loss and conservation, in Biodiversity loss and conservation.

CANDIDATES bio31-s2: Biodiversity loss and conservation
[bio31-s2.0.b] Biodiversity (the variety of living things in an ecosystem) is not just nice to have. It supplies services people depend on, keeps ecosystems stable, and many people argue it has a value of its own.
[bio31-s2.0.0] Ecosystem services are the benefits people get from ecosystems. Provisioning services are the products we take: food (crops, fish and game), timber, fibre, clean water and medicines.
[bio31-s2.0.1] Many medicines come from organisms, for example penicillin from the mould Penicillium, and aspirin's forerunner from willow bark (salicin). Losing a species can mean losing a drug nobody has found yet.
[bio31-s2.0.2] Regulating services control natural processes: pollination of crops by insects, purification of water by wetlands and soil, and pest control by predators. Bogs, forests and the sea also store carbon.
[bio31-s2.0.3] Wetlands and bogs also give flood control, because they soak up rain and release it slowly instead of letting it rush into rivers.
[bio31-s2.0.4] Supporting services keep the system running, such as soil formation and nutrient cycling by decomposers (organisms that break down dead matter). Cultural services include recreation, tourism, education, heritage and wellbeing.
[bio31-s2.0.5] Resilience (ability to withstand change) is higher in an ecosystem with many species and much genetic diversity. If one species declines, others with similar roles can take its place, and some individuals are likely to survive a new disease or a change in climate.
[bio31-s2.0.6] Low-diversity systems, such as a single crop variety, can collapse from one disease, because every plant is equally vulnerable.
[bio31-s2.0.7] The ethical argument: many people argue that species have a right to exist regardless of their usefulness, and that this generation has a duty to pass on a living world to future generations. Extinction is permanent, so the loss can never be undone.
[bio31-s2.1.b] Species are lost mainly because of human activity. There are seven main causes, and each one has Irish examples worth knowing.
[bio31-s2.1.0] Habitat loss and fragmentation is the biggest cause. Land is drained, built on, cut, or converted to intensive farming or plantation forestry. Irish examples: drainage and cutting of raised bogs, drainage of wet grassland, and removal of hedgerows.
[bio31-s2.1.1] Fragmentation (breaking habitat into pieces) leaves small, isolated patches that support only small populations, which are more easily wiped out by predators, disease or chance.
[bio31-s2.1.2] Agricultural intensification means earlier and more frequent mowing for silage (grass cut and stored as animal feed), reseeding with a few grass species, and more fertiliser. These reduce the variety of wild plants and destroy the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew and the corncrake.
[bio31-s2.1.3] Pollution: excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser, slurry and sewage cause eutrophication (nutrient overload) of rivers and lakes. Silt from drainage, forestry and land works smothers river beds, and pesticides harm insects and their predators.
[bio31-s2.1.4] Invasive species are non-native species introduced by people that spread and harm native species and ecosystems, often because they have no natural predators or diseases in their new home. Irish examples: grey squirrel, zebra mussel and Rhododendron ponticum.
[bio31-s2.1.5] Overexploitation is harvesting faster than a population can replace itself, for example overfishing, peat extraction and over-collection.
[bio31-s2.1.6] Climate change forces species to shift their range or the timing of their life cycles. Cold-adapted species, and those in fragmented habitats, cannot always move far enough.
[bio31-s2.1.7] Disease: new pathogens (disease-causing organisms) spread by trade. Ash dieback, a fungal disease recorded in Ireland in 2012, is killing ash trees in hedgerows and woodland.
[bio31-s2.2.b] Raised bogs are one of Ireland's most distinctive habitats and one of its most damaged. They show clearly how one habitat can matter environmentally, economically, socially and culturally.
[bio31-s2.2.0] Raised bogs are dome-shaped peatlands, mainly in the Irish midlands, formed over thousands of years as Sphagnum moss grew over waterlogged ground. They are wet, acidic and low in nutrients.
[bio31-s2.2.1] Because conditions are so harsh, raised bogs support specialised species: Sphagnum mosses, sundews (insect-eating plants), bog cotton and heather, and animals such as the curlew, the red grouse and the Irish hare.
[bio31-s2.2.2] Loss came from centuries of hand turf cutting, then large-scale industrial peat extraction for fuel and horticulture in the twentieth century, plus drainage for farming and forestry.
[bio31-s2.2.3] As a result, less than about 1% of the original raised bog area is still active raised bog, meaning bog that is still forming peat.
[bio31-s2.2.4] Environmental impacts: loss of a rare habitat and its specialised species; drained peat releases stored carbon; and loss of flood control and water purification.
[bio31-s2.2.5] Economic impacts: peat fuelled power stations and supported jobs in the midlands, so ending extraction cost jobs, while restoration can bring tourism and “green” jobs.
[bio31-s2.2.6] Social and cultural impacts: turf cutting for home fuel is a centuries-old rural tradition, and many families hold turbary (turf-cutting) rights. Bogs also preserve archaeology and are part of Irish heritage and literature.
[bio31-s2.3.b] Once the scale of bog loss was clear, the law and public bodies stepped in. The result protects species but affects people who rely on the bog, so it is a good example of a conservation trade-off.
[bio31-s2.3.0] Many raised bogs are protected as Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) under the EU Habitats Directive. Turf cutting on them is banned, and compensation is offered to turf cutters, which has been controversial.
[bio31-s2.3.1] Bord na Móna ended industrial peat harvesting in 2021. Drained bogs are being rewetted by blocking drains to raise the water table, so that Sphagnum can regrow.
[bio31-s2.3.2] For restoration: it protects species and stores carbon.
[bio31-s2.3.3] Against restoration: it removes a traditional right and a cheap fuel, so it needs compensation and local support to succeed.
[bio31-s2.4.b] The curlew shows how several pressures acting together can push a once-common bird towards disappearing from Ireland. It also shows how targeted conservation work tries to reverse that.
[bio31-s2.4.0] The curlew is a large wading bird with a long, down-curved bill that breeds on bogs and wet grassland. It is now one of Ireland's most threatened breeding birds.
[bio31-s2.4.1] A national survey in 2015–2017 found only 138 breeding pairs in the Republic of Ireland, a decline of at least 96% since the 1980s. A 2021 survey found about 105 pairs, so the fall has continued.
[bio31-s2.4.2] Causes of the decline: loss and drainage of bogs and wet grassland; plantation forestry on breeding sites; intensive farming with early mowing; and predation of eggs and chicks by foxes and crows.
[bio31-s2.4.3] Predation is worse because small breeding areas are surrounded by forest and farmland that support many predators.
[bio31-s2.4.4] Conservation: the Curlew Conservation Programme, run by the National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) since 2017, employs local nest-finders and works with farmers to protect nests, manage habitat and control predators. Electric fencing against foxes is one example.
[bio31-s2.5.b] The corncrake is a second bird lost mainly to changes in how farmland is managed. Its story shows how timing and direction of mowing can decide whether chicks survive.
[bio31-s2.5.0] The corncrake is a secretive grassland bird with a rasping call that was once heard across rural Ireland in summer. Its population has fallen by more than 96% since the 1970s.
[bio31-s2.5.1] It now breeds mainly in Co. Donegal (including offshore islands) and on the western coast of Mayo and Galway. It has not disappeared from Ireland.
[bio31-s2.5.2] Main cause: mechanised mowing of hay and silage during the nesting season destroys nests. Mowing from the edge of a field inwards traps the chicks in the centre, because they have nowhere to escape.
[bio31-s2.5.3] Conservation: farmers are paid to delay mowing until the chicks can fly, to mow from the centre of the field outwards so chicks can escape to the edges, and to leave tall vegetation as cover.
[bio31-s2.5.4] The Corncrake LIFE project, EU-funded and launched in 2022, uses payments based on results, so farmers are rewarded for the outcome for the birds.
[bio31-s2.5.5] Cultural impact of both birds: the sounds of the curlew and the corncrake are part of rural Irish life, folklore, music and place names, so their loss is felt as a cultural loss as well as an ecological one.
[bio31-s2.6.b] An invasive species is one that, once introduced, spreads and harms native species. The grey squirrel is Ireland's best-known example, and the pine marten shows how a native predator can help reverse the damage.
[bio31-s2.6.0] The grey squirrel was introduced from North America. The Irish population began with a few animals released in 1911 at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford. It spread across the eastern half of the island, and red squirrels declined where it arrived.
[bio31-s2.6.1] The grey squirrel is larger and competes with the red squirrel for food, because their niches (their roles and resource needs) overlap.
[bio31-s2.6.2] It carries squirrelpox virus, which does not harm grey squirrels but is usually fatal to red squirrels. It also damages trees by stripping their bark.
[bio31-s2.6.3] The native pine marten, protected under the Wildlife Act 1976, has recovered. Where pine martens are common, for example in the Irish midlands, grey squirrels have declined or disappeared and red squirrels have returned.
[bio31-s2.6.4] The heavier grey squirrel is more easily caught by the pine marten.
[bio31-s2.6.5] This shows how restoring a native predator can help a native species.
[bio31-s2.7.b] Invasive species are not only land animals. In Irish lakes and rivers the zebra mussel removes the phytoplankton that other life depends on, and it has itself now been displaced by a newer invader.
[bio31-s2.7.0] The zebra mussel is a small freshwater mussel native to the Ponto-Caspian region, around the Black and Caspian Seas. It was first recorded in Ireland in Lough Derg on the Shannon in 1997, probably brought on boats.
[bio31-s2.7.1] It spreads attached to boat hulls and in bilge water (the water that collects in the bottom of a boat), which is how it moves between lakes.
[bio31-s2.7.2] The zebra mussel filters huge volumes of water, removing phytoplankton (microscopic algae), the base of the food chain. The water becomes clearer, but food for zooplankton (tiny animals) and young fish falls.
[bio31-s2.7.3] The zebra mussel smothers native mussels and blocks water-intake pipes, an economic cost.
[bio31-s2.7.4] The zebra mussel has itself been displaced in Lough Derg by a related invader, the quagga mussel, first recognised there in 2021. The quagga mussel is a separate species.
[bio31-s2.8.b] A plant can be invasive too. Rhododendron ponticum was brought in as a garden shrub, but in Killarney it now threatens the native woodland.
[bio31-s2.8.0] Rhododendron ponticum is a shrub introduced to Killarney for ornamental planting in the nineteenth century. It forms dense thickets in the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park.
[bio31-s2.8.1] Its evergreen leaves let very little light reach the woodland floor, so oak seedlings and ground plants cannot grow and the wood cannot regenerate (replace its old trees).
[bio31-s2.8.2] Clearing it by cutting and treating regrowth is slow and costly, and the work must be repeated.
[bio31-s2.9.b] Ireland has other invasive species too, and because removal is so costly, the best approach is to stop them spreading in the first place.
[bio31-s2.9.0] Elodea canadensis (Canadian pondweed) is an invasive plant in fresh water.
[bio31-s2.9.1] Japanese knotweed spreads from small root fragments, which makes it hard to remove, and damages buildings and riverbanks.
[bio31-s2.9.2] Prevention is through biosecurity (measures that stop species being moved). An example is the “check, clean, dry” routine for boats and angling gear moved between waters.
[bio31-s2.9.3] Never release aquarium plants or pets into the wild, because they may establish and spread where they have no natural predators.
[bio31-s2.10.b] Clean water is vital to freshwater life. Two cases show what happens when silt and excess nutrients get into Irish rivers and lakes.
[bio31-s2.10.0] The freshwater pearl mussel is a long-lived mussel of clean, fast-flowing rivers. Its larvae (glochidia) must attach to the gills of young native salmon or trout to develop, and the juveniles need clean, well-oxygenated gravel.
[bio31-s2.10.1] Silt and nutrient enrichment from drainage, forestry and farming clog the gravel. In many Irish rivers few or no young mussels survive, and populations consist only of old adults.
[bio31-s2.10.2] Its decline is also linked to the decline of salmon and trout, its hosts, because without them the larvae cannot develop.
[bio31-s2.10.3] Lough Neagh, in Northern Ireland and the largest lake on the island of Ireland, had a massive bloom of blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) in summer 2023.
[bio31-s2.10.4] The bloom was linked mainly to excess phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater built up over decades, along with warm, still weather and zebra mussels.
[bio31-s2.10.5] Impacts of the Lough Neagh bloom: toxic water unsafe for people, pets and livestock; harm to fish and birds; damage to the eel fishery and to tourism; and anger in local communities.
[bio31-s2.11.b] Biodiversity loss damages ecosystems and costs money. Each point below is paired with an example from the Irish case studies.
[bio31-s2.11.0] Environmental: food webs are disrupted when a species is lost, affecting its predators and prey. Ecosystem services such as pollination, water purification and flood control are lost, ecosystems become less resilient, and extinction is irreversible.
[bio31-s2.11.1] Environmental example: about one third of Ireland's wild bee species are threatened with extinction, mainly through loss of flowers and nesting sites. This threatens the pollination of wild plants and crops.
[bio31-s2.11.2] Economic: farming depends on pollinators and healthy soil, and fisheries and angling depend on clean rivers and lakes.
[bio31-s2.11.3] Tourism, for example at Killarney National Park, the Burren and the bogs, depends on landscapes and wildlife.
[bio31-s2.11.4] Invasive species cost money to control, for example clearing rhododendron or unblocking pipes fouled by zebra mussels. Polluted water also costs more to treat.
[bio31-s2.11.5] Conservation also has costs, including compensation to farmers and turf cutters and lost income from land use.
[bio31-s2.12.b] Biodiversity loss also affects people's health, communities and identity. It can cause conflict, because both loss and the rules to prevent it can hit livelihoods.
[bio31-s2.12.0] Social: health and wellbeing benefit from access to nature. Polluted water threatens drinking supplies and recreation, for example on Lough Neagh.
[bio31-s2.12.1] Social conflict: communities that depend on fishing, farming or peat can lose their livelihoods either from biodiversity loss or from conservation rules, which causes conflict.
[bio31-s2.12.2] Cultural: species and landscapes are part of identity, heritage, folklore, music, literature and place names, for example the curlew, the corncrake and the bogs.
[bio31-s2.12.3] Traditional practices such as turf cutting are themselves cultural heritage, which is why some conservation measures are resisted.
[bio31-s2.13.b] Conservation means acting to protect nature. There are two broad approaches, depending on whether the species is protected where it lives or somewhere else.
[bio31-s2.13.0] Conservation is the protection, management and restoration of the natural environment to maintain biodiversity and prevent extinction, while allowing sustainable use.
[bio31-s2.13.1] In situ conservation (in the natural habitat) uses protected areas such as national parks. Killarney National Park was Ireland's first, in 1932.
[bio31-s2.13.2] Natura 2000 sites across the EU include Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) under the EU Habitats Directive (1992) and Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for birds under the EU Birds Directive (1979).
[bio31-s2.13.3] Species protection under the Wildlife Act 1976 made it illegal to kill or disturb protected species.
[bio31-s2.13.4] Habitat management and restoration: rewetting bogs; planting native trees and hedgerows; and controlling invasive species.
[bio31-s2.13.5] Farming for nature uses agri-environment schemes that pay farmers for results, such as species-rich meadows, for example in the Burren.
[bio31-s2.13.6] Species recovery programmes include the Curlew Conservation Programme and the Corncrake LIFE project.
[bio31-s2.13.7] Ex situ conservation (outside the natural habitat) uses seed banks, captive breeding and reintroduction, for example captive rearing of freshwater pearl mussels to release young mussels into rivers.
[bio31-s2.14.b] Laws and national plans set the direction for conservation, but individuals and communities also take part.
[bio31-s2.14.0] The All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, launched in 2015, asks councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide flowers and nesting sites for bees, for example by mowing road verges less often.
[bio31-s2.14.1] In May 2019 the Dáil declared a climate and biodiversity emergency, making Ireland the second country to do so.
[bio31-s2.14.2] The Citizens' Assembly on Biodiversity Loss reported in April 2023 with 159 recommendations.
[bio31-s2.14.3] Ireland's fourth National Biodiversity Action Plan covers 2023–2030.
[bio31-s2.14.4] The EU Nature Restoration Law, adopted in 2024, requires member states to restore damaged ecosystems.
[bio31-s2.14.5] Individuals can record species for citizen-science surveys (research that members of the public contribute to), for example through the National Biodiversity Data Centre. They can also garden for pollinators and avoid spreading invasive species.
[bio31-s2.15.b] A discuss question wants both sides: where conservation has worked, and where it has fallen short. A balanced answer then reaches a conclusion.
[bio31-s2.15.0] Arguments for its effectiveness: protection has brought recoveries, for example the pine marten after 1976, with the return of red squirrels that followed.
[bio31-s2.15.1] Rewetting can restore bog habitat, and targeted payments have raised the breeding success of curlew and corncrake in some areas.
[bio31-s2.15.2] Protected areas keep the last strongholds of rare species.
[bio31-s2.15.3] Limits: declines continue, as curlew numbers fell further between 2015–17 and 2021. Designating a site is not enough without enforcement and management.
[bio31-s2.15.4] Schemes cost money and can conflict with the incomes and traditions of farmers, turf cutters and landowners.
[bio31-s2.15.5] Small, isolated populations may still be lost to predators, disease or chance. Restoration is slow: bogs take centuries to re-form.
[bio31-s2.15.6] Conclusion to model: conservation limits biodiversity loss when it has funding, enforcement and the support of the local people who manage the land.
[bio31-s2.16.b] The syllabus asks you to link what you learn to real, current stories about local ecosystems. Follow these steps to analyse a story.
[bio31-s2.16.0] 1. Find a current local story about a local ecosystem or species. Sources include local newspapers and radio, national news, the websites of the NPWS and the EPA (water quality), BirdWatch Ireland, the National Biodiversity Data Centre, and the county council's heritage or biodiversity officer.
[bio31-s2.16.1] 2. Identify the ecosystem, the species affected, the cause, and the evidence, such as survey data, photographs or expert opinion.
[bio31-s2.16.2] 3. Evaluate the source: who wrote it, whether it is based on data, whether it shows bias (for example from a campaign group or an industry body), and whether other sources agree.
[bio31-s2.16.3] 4. Describe the impacts under the four headings: environmental, economic, social and cultural.
[bio31-s2.16.4] 5. Assess what conservation is being done and whether it is likely to work.
[bio31-s2.16.5] 6. Where possible, visit or survey the ecosystem to gather primary data (data you collect yourself).

CANDIDATES bio31-s5: Carrying capacity and limiting factors
[bio31-s5.0.b] A population is all the individuals of one species living in one area. Its size rises and falls, and in every habitat there is an upper limit that the environment can support. That limit is the carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.0.0] A population's size changes through four processes. Births (natality) and immigration (individuals arriving) increase it; deaths (mortality) and emigration (individuals leaving) decrease it.
[bio31-s5.0.1] A population grows when births plus immigration exceed deaths plus emigration. If the two sides are equal, the size stays steady.
[bio31-s5.0.2] Every population could grow very fast if nothing stopped it, because organisms produce more offspring than are needed to replace themselves. In reality, resources run short, so growth slows and stops.
[bio31-s5.0.3] The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum population size of a species that it can support indefinitely, given the food, water, space and other resources available. It is often given the symbol K.
[bio31-s5.0.4] A limiting factor is any factor that restricts the size, growth or distribution of a population. The factor in shortest supply relative to need sets the limit, even if all the others are plentiful, which is the same principle as limiting factors in photosynthesis.
[bio31-s5.0.5] Carrying capacity is set by the limiting factors acting together, not by any one of them alone.
[bio31-s5.0.6] Carrying capacity is not fixed. It changes when conditions change, such as in a wet or dry year, after a new predator or disease arrives, or with habitat loss or restoration. It also differs between species in the same habitat.
[bio31-s5.0.7] If a population rises above carrying capacity, it damages its resources, for example by overgrazing (eating plants faster than they regrow), and then its numbers fall.
[bio31-s5.1.b] Abiotic factors are the non-living parts of the environment. Each of them can cap the number of plants and animals a habitat supports, and the Irish examples below show how.
[bio31-s5.1.0] Water: all organisms need water. On the dry, free-draining sand of coastal sand dunes, water shortage limits which plants can grow and how many.
[bio31-s5.1.1] Marram grass grows on sand dunes because its rolled leaves and deep roots reduce water loss and reach water, so it survives where most plants cannot. In a dry summer, grass growth slows and fewer grazing animals can be supported.
[bio31-s5.1.2] Oxygen: aquatic animals depend on oxygen dissolved in water, which is scarce compared with air. Salmon, trout and stonefly nymphs need well-oxygenated, cool, fast-flowing water, so oxygen limits their numbers in slow, warm or polluted rivers.
[bio31-s5.1.3] Decomposing organic pollution uses up oxygen in a river, which is why polluted water supports fewer oxygen-demanding animals. In waterlogged bog soil, low oxygen limits roots and decomposers, so few plant species can grow there.
[bio31-s5.1.4] Space: organisms need space to attach, shelter, nest or hold a territory (an area an animal defends). On a rocky shore, bare rock is limited, so barnacles, mussels and seaweeds compete for space to attach.
[bio31-s5.1.5] Seabirds nest only on cliffs and islands with suitable ledges. Many songbirds, such as robins, defend territories, so the number of territories in a woodland limits the breeding population.
[bio31-s5.1.6] Temperature affects enzyme activity, growth and survival. Cold winters kill many small birds such as wrens, and low temperatures in the uplands shorten the growing season, limiting plant growth. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, so temperature also limits fish.
[bio31-s5.1.7] Soil type, pH, minerals, water content and air content limit plants, and so the animals that feed on them. Acidic, nitrogen-poor bog peat supports only specialised plants such as Sphagnum, heather and sundews.
[bio31-s5.2.b] Soil is one of the factors that limit which plants can grow in a place, and plants are the food base for animals. Light is not on the specification's list of abiotic factors, but it is a key limiting factor for plants.
[bio31-s5.2.0] The thin limestone soils of the Burren, Co. Clare, support lime-loving plants, because only plants suited to thin, lime-rich soil can grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.1] Fertile, well-drained lowland soils support productive grassland, because they are fertile and drain well.
[bio31-s5.2.2] Under a closed woodland canopy (the roof of leaves formed by the treetops) in summer, little light reaches the floor, so few plants grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.3] Woodland plants such as bluebells grow and flower in spring, before the leaves of the trees open, which means they get the light they need while the canopy is still open.
[bio31-s5.3.b] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment: other organisms and the diseases they carry. Food, disease, competition and predation all limit populations, and some of them also limit one another.
[bio31-s5.3.0] Food: the supply of food usually sets the carrying capacity for animals. For grazers it is the amount of grass or browse (leaves and shoots of shrubs and trees).
[bio31-s5.3.1] Seed-eaters such as squirrels and wood mice do better in years when trees produce large seed crops. Birds of prey such as barn owls are limited by the number of small mammals they can catch.
[bio31-s5.3.2] Disease: disease spreads faster in dense populations, so it limits large populations most. Squirrelpox virus, carried by grey squirrels, kills red squirrels.
[bio31-s5.3.3] Myxomatosis, a viral disease, killed most of Ireland's rabbits after it arrived in the 1950s. Ash dieback is killing ash trees.
[bio31-s5.3.4] Competition: organisms competing for the same limited resource, such as food, light, water, space or mates, reduce each other's share, so fewer survive or breed. Competition between members of the same species (intraspecific) and between different species (interspecific) both limit populations.
[bio31-s5.3.5] Grey squirrels outcompete red squirrels for food in broadleaf woods, and on a woodland floor taller plants shade out shorter ones. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio31-s5.3.6] Predation: predators kill and eat prey, keeping prey numbers below what the food supply alone would allow. Foxes take rabbits and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew.
[bio31-s5.3.7] Pine martens prey on grey squirrels, reducing their numbers where martens are common. Predator numbers are in turn limited by the number of prey, so the two populations affect each other.
[bio31-s5.4.b] Predation normally keeps herbivore numbers down. Where a predator is missing, food becomes the main limit, and the habitat can pay the price.
[bio31-s5.4.0] Ireland's deer, the native red deer and the sika deer introduced to Powerscourt, Co. Wicklow, in 1860, have had no natural predator since wolves died out in Ireland more than two centuries ago.
[bio31-s5.4.1] Their numbers are limited mainly by food, so in parts of Co. Wicklow they have risen high enough to browse away tree seedlings and stop woodland regenerating.
[bio31-s5.4.2] Culling (killing selected animals) by people is used to keep deer below the level that damages habitats, so people take over the role the predator once had.
[bio31-s5.5.b] Limiting factors fall into two groups, depending on whether their effect changes with how crowded the population is. This explains why some factors steady a population while others crash it without warning.
[bio31-s5.5.0] Density-dependent factors have a greater effect as the population becomes more crowded. They are food shortage, competition, disease and predation, and are mostly biotic.
[bio31-s5.5.1] They slow growth as a population approaches carrying capacity, so they regulate population size, pulling it back towards K.
[bio31-s5.5.2] Density-independent factors affect a population regardless of its size, for example a severe frost, a flood, a storm, a drought or a fire, and are mostly abiotic.
[bio31-s5.5.3] Density-independent factors can cause sudden crashes at any population size, because a frost kills the same fraction whether the population is small or large.
[bio31-s5.5.4] Human activity changes carrying capacity. Draining wet grassland and bogs removes curlew breeding habitat, lowering its carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.5.5] Rewetting a bog or planting hedgerows raises carrying capacity for the species that depend on them, and garden feeders increase the food available for some birds.
[bio31-s5.6.b] A grassland field shows how several limiting factors act on one species at once. For rabbits, the field's carrying capacity is the result of all of them together.
[bio31-s5.6.0] Food: grass is the main density-dependent limit. As rabbit numbers rise, the grass is grazed shorter and competition increases.
[bio31-s5.6.1] Predation by foxes, stoats and birds of prey removes many young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.2] Disease: myxomatosis causes crashes when the population is dense, because the virus spreads fastest between crowded animals.
[bio31-s5.6.3] Space: burrows need well-drained soil, for example on banks and slopes, which limits where warrens (burrow systems) can be dug.
[bio31-s5.6.4] Wet winters, an abiotic and density-independent factor, can flood burrows and kill young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.5] The carrying capacity of the field for rabbits is the population that these factors together allow it to support over time.
[bio31-s5.6.6] Diagram: a simple graph of a population rising and levelling off at a horizontal dashed line labelled “carrying capacity (K)”, with arrows labelled “limiting factors (environmental resistance)” pressing down on the curve.

CANDIDATES bio32-s4: Biotic factors and relationships
[bio32-s4.0.b] Biotic factors are the effects that living organisms have on each other. In a grassland they include food supply, predation, grazing, competition, parasites and disease, pollination and decomposition, and human activities such as grazing livestock, mowing and fertilising. Grazing and mowing show how one biotic factor shapes which plants can live in a habitat.
[bio32-s4.0.0] Grazing by rabbits, cattle or sheep and mowing keep grass short. This lets low rosette plants (plants whose leaves lie flat close to the ground) such as daisy, dandelion and plantain get light, and stops shrubs and trees from invading.
[bio32-s4.0.1] If grazing or mowing stops, taller grasses shade out the rosette plants because they grow above them and take the light. In time scrub (a tangle of woody shrubs, e.g. brambles, hawthorn) invades.
[bio32-s4.1.b] Other living things work in the grassland soil and on its flowers. Some recycle dead material back into the ground, and others carry pollen between flowers.
[bio32-s4.1.0] Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) and detritivores (animals that eat dead organic matter, e.g. earthworms, woodlice, and slugs in part) break down dead plants, dung and dead animals, returning minerals to the soil so that plants can take them up again.
[bio32-s4.1.1] Decomposition recycles carbon and nitrogen, and there is more on this in The carbon cycle, The nitrogen cycle and Bacteria and fungi.
[bio32-s4.1.2] Earthworms also drag leaves underground, mix and aerate the soil (let air into it), and improve drainage.
[bio32-s4.1.3] Pollinators such as bumblebees, hoverflies and butterflies feed on the nectar and pollen of clover, dandelion and buttercup flowers, and carry pollen between them, which is how these plants are pollinated.
[bio32-s4.2.b] To work out who eats whom in a grassland, you gather primary data, meaning information you collect yourself in the field. You then check it against what other people have already recorded.
[bio32-s4.2.0] Direct observation of animals feeding shows a feeding relationship at the moment it happens.
[bio32-s4.2.1] Feeding signs are evidence left behind: nibbled grass, holes in leaves, and broken snail shells at a thrush’s stone.
[bio32-s4.2.2] Droppings and pellets show what an animal ate, e.g. undigested fur and bones in the pellets of birds of prey.
[bio32-s4.2.3] The habitat where each animal was caught is a clue, e.g. aphids found on the stems they feed on.
[bio32-s4.2.4] Your findings are then checked against secondary sources (information collected by others), such as field guides, for what each species eats.
[bio32-s4.3.b] Every feeding relationship starts with a green plant that makes food. Animals then feed on the plants, and on each other, in layers called trophic levels (feeding levels).
[bio32-s4.3.0] Producers in the grassland are grasses, clover, daisy, dandelion, buttercup and other green plants, which make food by photosynthesis.
[bio32-s4.3.1] Primary consumers (herbivores, which eat plants) in the grassland: rabbits and hares graze grass and clover; slugs and snails eat leaves; grasshoppers and caterpillars chew leaves; aphids suck sap from stems; bumblebees take nectar and pollen.
[bio32-s4.3.2] Secondary and higher consumers (carnivores, which eat animals, and omnivores, which eat both): ladybirds eat aphids; spiders catch flies, grasshoppers and other insects; ground beetles eat slugs and other invertebrates.
[bio32-s4.3.3] Song thrushes eat snails (smashing their shells on a stone) and earthworms; blackbirds eat earthworms and insects; hedgehogs eat slugs, beetles and earthworms.
[bio32-s4.3.4] Badgers eat mainly earthworms; foxes eat rabbits, mice and birds; kestrels eat wood mice, pygmy shrews and large insects.
[bio32-s4.3.5] Pygmy shrews eat small invertebrates such as beetles, spiders and woodlice.
[bio32-s4.4.b] A food chain follows one line of feeding, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder. Because most animals eat more than one thing, the chains join up into a food web.
[bio32-s4.4.0] Worked example of grassland food chains: grass → rabbit → fox; clover leaves → slug → song thrush; plant sap → aphid → ladybird → (insect-eating bird); grass seeds and invertebrates → wood mouse → kestrel.
[bio32-s4.4.1] These chains connect into a food web. For example, the fox eats rabbits, wood mice and birds, so it is on several chains.
[bio32-s4.4.2] The earthworm feeds on dead leaves and is eaten by thrushes, blackbirds, badgers and hedgehogs, so it links the dead material in the soil to several animals above it.
[bio32-s4.4.3] Diagram: a grassland food web of 8–12 of the species listed above, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder.
[bio32-s4.5.b] A food web shows that organisms depend on each other, so a change in one population spreads through the whole community.
[bio32-s4.5.0] The web shows interdependence: a change in one population affects others.
[bio32-s4.5.1] For example, if disease kills many rabbits, foxes eat more wood mice and birds, so those populations fall, and the grass grows taller because fewer rabbits graze it, shading out daisies.
[bio32-s4.5.2] An omnivore or a predator with several prey is less affected than one with a single food source, because it can switch to another food when one becomes scarce. There is more on predicting changes from webs in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio32-s4.6.b] Predation is the best-known feeding relationship between animals. Predators have features that help them find, catch and kill their prey.
[bio32-s4.6.0] Predation is a feeding relationship in which one animal, the predator, kills and eats another, the prey.
[bio32-s4.6.1] Grassland examples of predation: fox (predator) and rabbit (prey); kestrel and wood mouse; ladybird and aphid; song thrush and snail.
[bio32-s4.6.2] The fox has acute hearing and smell to find prey, forward-facing eyes for judging distance, speed and sharp teeth, and hunts mostly at night, when rabbits feed.
[bio32-s4.6.3] The kestrel hovers into the wind while scanning the ground with very keen eyesight, then drops onto its prey, gripping it with sharp talons and tearing it with a hooked beak.
[bio32-s4.6.4] Spiders build webs to trap flying insects.
[bio32-s4.7.b] Prey animals need to spot danger early, escape, or avoid being noticed or eaten in the first place.
[bio32-s4.7.0] Rabbits have eyes on the sides of the head, giving an almost all-round field of view, and large mobile ears, so they can detect a predator coming from any direction.
[bio32-s4.7.1] Rabbits feed in groups so that some can watch for danger, thump the ground with a hind foot and flash the white underside of the tail as a warning, run in zigzags, and shelter in burrows.
[bio32-s4.7.2] Snails withdraw into their shells for protection.
[bio32-s4.7.3] Ladybirds have bright warning colours and a bad taste, so predators learn to avoid them.
[bio32-s4.7.4] Many caterpillars and grasshoppers are green, camouflaged against the grass, so predators find them hard to see.
[bio32-s4.8.b] Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together in a repeating cycle, because each population depends on the other.
[bio32-s4.8.0] 1. When prey are plentiful, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise.
[bio32-s4.8.1] 2. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.2] 3. With less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.3] 4. With fewer predators, prey numbers recover, and the cycle repeats.
[bio32-s4.8.4] Predator numbers peak after prey numbers, because predators need time to breed once food is plentiful. There are usually fewer predators than prey.
[bio32-s4.8.5] The classic data are the fur-trading records of the snowshoe hare and the lynx in Canada, which rose and fell in cycles of about ten years.
[bio32-s4.8.6] Diagram: a graph of predator and prey numbers against time: two out-of-step waves, the predator curve lower and peaking after the prey curve.
[bio32-s4.9.b] Predators do more than eat prey. They also help to set how many prey a habitat holds.
[bio32-s4.9.0] Predators usually take the old, sick, young or weak prey, which are the easiest to catch.
[bio32-s4.9.1] The effect of predation increases as prey become more numerous, because prey are easier to find. This helps keep the prey population near the habitat’s carrying capacity (the largest population the habitat can support). There is more on this in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio32-s4.9.2] Removing a predator can let its prey increase until it overgrazes its food.
[bio32-s4.10.b] Organisms compete when they need the same resource and there is not enough for all. Competition can be sorted by who is competing and by how the resource is shared out.
[bio32-s4.10.0] Competition is the struggle between organisms for a resource that is in short supply. Plants compete for light, water, minerals and space; animals compete for food, water, space or territory, shelter and mates.
[bio32-s4.10.1] If the resource is plentiful, there is no competition.
[bio32-s4.10.2] Intraspecific competition is between members of the same species. It is the most intense kind, because they have exactly the same needs, e.g. daisy rosettes crowding each other for space and light, or male blackbirds or robins defending territories that contain food and nest sites.
[bio32-s4.10.3] Interspecific competition is between different species that need the same resource, e.g. grasses and daisies competing for light, or rabbits and sheep grazing the same grass.
[bio32-s4.10.4] Contest competition: there is an active physical contest for the resource and the winner takes it, e.g. birds fighting over territory, or stags fighting over mates.
[bio32-s4.10.5] Scramble competition: each competitor gets as much as it can of a shared resource, so when it is scarce all get too little, e.g. seedlings crowded together all grow weak and thin, or aphids crowded on a stem.
[bio32-s4.11.b] Competition has a cost for the organisms that lose, and this affects the whole population. Organisms also have features that help them win.
[bio32-s4.11.0] Competition reduces the growth, survival and breeding of the losers, and so limits population size. Its effect grows as the population becomes more crowded.
[bio32-s4.11.1] Between species, one may exclude the other from the habitat, or each may survive by using the resources in a slightly different way (different niches, meaning different roles or ways of living in the habitat). There is more on this in The niche.
[bio32-s4.11.2] Adaptations that help organisms compete: fast growth, and tall stems or climbing (e.g. ivy, brambles) in plants competing for light; deep roots for water; rapid breeding; aggressive defence of territory in animals.
[bio32-s4.12.b] Two examples show interspecific competition at work: one in the grassland and one from Ireland.
[bio32-s4.12.0] In short, grazed or mown grass, daisies and other rosette plants get enough light. Where grass is left long, it outgrows and shades them, and they disappear (interspecific competition for light).
[bio32-s4.12.1] Irish example: grey squirrels, introduced to Ireland at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford, in 1911, compete with the native red squirrel for food, especially seeds and nuts.
[bio32-s4.12.2] Grey squirrels are larger, can digest acorns (which red squirrels digest poorly), eat hazelnuts before they ripen, and carry squirrelpox virus, which kills red squirrels but rarely harms grey squirrels. Red squirrels have disappeared from much of the area greys occupy.
[bio32-s4.12.3] Where pine marten numbers have recovered in Ireland, grey squirrels have declined and red squirrels have returned.
[bio32-s4.13.b] Some organisms do not just compete or eat each other but live together. This close partnership is called symbiosis, and it is sorted into three types by who benefits.
[bio32-s4.13.0] Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship in which two organisms of different species live together.
[bio32-s4.13.1] There are three types, named by who benefits: mutualism (both benefit, +/+), commensalism (one benefits and the other is unaffected, +/0) and parasitism (one benefits and the other is harmed, +/−).
[bio32-s4.13.2] Pollinators and flowers benefit each other (food for the insect, pollination for the plant), so the relationship is mutualistic, but the two don’t live together, so many biologists don’t call it symbiosis.
[bio32-s4.14.b] In mutualism both partners gain. The first example is a bacterium that gives clover a supply of nitrogen, which is why clover can enrich grassland.
[bio32-s4.14.0] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) live in root nodules (small swellings) of clover and other legumes. The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonium, which the plant uses to make amino acids and proteins.
[bio32-s4.14.1] The plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (from photosynthesis) and a protected home.
[bio32-s4.14.2] Active nodules are pink inside, from a pigment (leghaemoglobin) that controls oxygen levels for the bacteria.
[bio32-s4.14.3] This is why clover grows well in soils poor in nitrogen and enriches grassland. There is more on this in The nitrogen cycle.
[bio32-s4.14.4] To observe: dig up a clover plant, wash the roots and examine the nodules with a hand lens; cut one to see the pink colour.
[bio32-s4.15.b] Mutualism is common wherever one partner can do something the other cannot. Here are three more examples, and in each the partners swap food or shelter for something they lack.
[bio32-s4.15.0] Cellulose-digesting microorganisms (bacteria, protists and fungi) live in the rumen (first stomach) of cattle and sheep, and in the caecum (a pouch of the gut) of rabbits. They digest the cellulose of grass, which the animal has no enzyme to digest.
[bio32-s4.15.1] The microorganisms get a warm, food-rich home, and the animal gets the products (fatty acids) as food. Rabbits also eat some of their own soft droppings to absorb nutrients released by these microorganisms.
[bio32-s4.15.2] Mycorrhizal fungi grow around and into the roots of most grassland plants and trees. The fungal hyphae (thread-like filaments) spread far through the soil, absorbing water and minerals (especially phosphate) and passing them to the plant; the plant supplies the fungus with sugars.
[bio32-s4.15.3] Lichens (e.g. on walls, rocks and tree bark around the grassland) are a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) living together. The alga photosynthesises and supplies sugars; the fungus gives shelter, holds water and absorbs minerals.
[bio32-s4.15.4] Bacteria in the human gut are another example of mutualism. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.b] In commensalism one partner gains and the other is neither helped nor harmed. It is easy to confuse with mutualism, so check whether the second partner really gets nothing.
[bio32-s4.16.0] Commensalism: one partner benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
[bio32-s4.16.1] Mosses and lichens growing on the bark of trees get a place in the light without harming the tree.
[bio32-s4.16.2] Many bacteria living on human skin feed on dead skin cells and sweat without harming the host. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.3] Seeds with hooks (e.g. burdock) are carried in an animal’s fur, and robins follow a gardener or grazing cattle to catch the invertebrates they disturb; both are commensal relationships, since the animal or gardener is unaffected.
[bio32-s4.17.b] A parasite lives on or in another organism, the host, and feeds on it. Unlike a predator, it usually keeps its host alive.
[bio32-s4.17.0] Parasitism: the parasite lives in or on the host and feeds on it, harming it. A parasite usually weakens its host without killing it quickly, since it depends on the host.
[bio32-s4.17.1] Grassland ectoparasites (parasites living on the outside of the host): fleas on rabbits and hedgehogs; ticks on sheep, deer and humans. Ticks are also vectors of Lyme disease, meaning they carry it from host to host.
[bio32-s4.17.2] The rabbit flea carries the myxoma virus that causes myxomatosis, which has killed very large numbers of rabbits.
[bio32-s4.17.3] Grassland endoparasites (parasites living inside the host): liver fluke in sheep and cattle on wet pasture; roundworms and tapeworms in the gut of grazing animals. There is more on liver fluke and tapeworms in Pathogens.
[bio32-s4.17.4] Plant parasites: rust fungi on grasses and cereals.
[bio32-s4.17.5] Yellow rattle, a meadow plant, is a partial parasite: it photosynthesises but taps the roots of grasses for water and minerals, weakening the grasses so that other wild flowers can grow. For this reason it is sown when restoring wildflower meadows.
[bio32-s4.17.6] Ivy is not a parasite: it climbs trees but makes its own food and takes nothing from them.
[bio32-s4.18.b] A parasite usually weakens its host rather than killing it quickly, but parasites and disease can still change how many hosts there are, because weakened hosts breed less and are caught more easily.
[bio32-s4.18.0] Parasites and disease weaken hosts, making them easier prey and less able to breed.
[bio32-s4.18.1] Parasites spread fastest when hosts are crowded, so their effect increases with population density, like predation.
[bio32-s4.19.b] The signs show the effect on each partner: + for benefits, − for harmed, and 0 for unaffected. Use them to compare the five relationships.
[bio32-s4.19.0] Predation (+/−): a predator kills and eats its prey, e.g. fox and rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.1] Competition (−/−): both organisms lose some of a scarce resource, e.g. grass and daisy for light, or grey and red squirrel.
[bio32-s4.19.2] Mutualism (+/+): both benefit, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules.
[bio32-s4.19.3] Commensalism (+/0): one benefits and the other is unaffected, e.g. mosses on tree bark.
[bio32-s4.19.4] Parasitism (+/−): the host is harmed but usually not killed quickly, e.g. a flea on a rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.5] Predation and parasitism are both +/−. The difference is that a predator kills its prey at once and eats it, while a parasite lives on a living host over a long time.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio34-s3: Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling
[bio34-s3.0.b] Nutrient cycles run naturally, but people now speed them up and shift material between stores. That raises two kinds of question: will the environment cope in future, and is it fair who gains and who pays?
[bio34-s3.0.0] Humans now move huge amounts of carbon and nitrogen between stores. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests and bogs moves carbon into the air, and making and spreading fertiliser, and keeping large numbers of livestock, adds fixed nitrogen (nitrogen in usable compounds, not nitrogen gas) to soils and water.
[bio34-s3.0.1] A sustainability issue arises when a practice damages the environment's ability to support future generations. An ethical issue arises when people disagree about what is right, especially who gets the benefits and who bears the costs.
[bio34-s3.0.2] Ethical questions to ask of every issue: who benefits and who is harmed, now, elsewhere and in the future; who should pay to prevent or repair the harm, under the polluter pays principle (the polluter meets the cost), which is part of EU environmental law.
[bio34-s3.0.3] Also ask: whether the harm is fair (justice); whether we should act before harm is proven (the precautionary principle); and whether the people affected have a say.
[bio34-s3.1.b] Eutrophication is the enrichment of rivers, lakes or coastal water with nutrients, mainly nitrate and phosphate, leading to excessive growth of algae and plants and a loss of oxygen. It is the main way nutrient pollution harms water, and the later issues keep returning to it.
[bio34-s3.1.0] Nitrate is leached from fertilised fields after rain. Leached means washed down through the soil into streams and groundwater, and nitrate is very soluble, so it is lost easily.
[bio34-s3.1.1] Slurry (a liquid mix of animal dung and urine) and farmyard manure are washed off the land, especially if spread before heavy rain or on wet or frozen ground, because the soil cannot absorb them.
[bio34-s3.1.2] Untreated or poorly treated sewage and septic tanks release nutrients, as do phosphate-containing detergents and runoff from forestry and peat extraction.
[bio34-s3.1.3] Phosphate is as important as nitrate: it is a major cause of eutrophication and is often the main one in lakes, so nitrogen is not the only culprit.
[bio34-s3.2.b] The harm to fish is not caused by the nutrients directly. It follows a chain, and the key link is that decomposers (organisms that break down dead matter) use up the oxygen.
[bio34-s3.2.0] 1. Extra nitrate and phosphate enter the water.
[bio34-s3.2.1] 2. Algae grow very rapidly, forming an algal bloom that turns the water green and covers the surface.
[bio34-s3.2.2] 3. The bloom blocks light, so rooted plants and the algae below it die.
[bio34-s3.2.3] 4. Algae have short lives, so huge numbers die.
[bio34-s3.2.4] 5. Aerobic decomposers (bacteria) feed on the dead algae and plants, multiply and respire, using up dissolved oxygen, so the water's biochemical oxygen demand (the oxygen it needs for decomposition) rises.
[bio34-s3.2.5] 6. Dissolved oxygen falls, so fish, especially salmon and trout, which need high oxygen, and invertebrates die.
[bio34-s3.2.6] 7. The decomposition of the dead fish and plants uses still more oxygen, and anaerobic decomposition (without oxygen) may release foul-smelling gases.
[bio34-s3.2.7] Diagram: flow chart of the sequence. Labels: nutrients in, algal bloom, plants below die, algae die, decomposers increase, oxygen falls, fish die.
[bio34-s3.3.b] Some blooms do more than starve the water of oxygen. They produce poisons, which turns a wildlife problem into a human one.
[bio34-s3.3.0] Some blooms are of blue-green algae, which are bacteria, not true algae. They make toxins harmful to animals, pets and people, so lakes may be closed for swimming, and the cost of treating drinking water rises.
[bio34-s3.3.1] Example: in summer 2023, Lough Neagh in Northern Ireland, the largest freshwater lake in Ireland and Britain, had widespread blue-green algal blooms. They were linked to excess nutrients from farming and wastewater, and to an unusually warm summer.
[bio34-s3.4.b] To evaluate nitrogen fertiliser you weigh its benefits against its costs to water, air and climate, and against fairness between those who gain and those who pay. First, the case for it.
[bio34-s3.4.0] Nitrogen is usually the nutrient that limits crop and grass growth (the one in shortest supply, so plants grow only as fast as it allows), so fertiliser greatly raises yields.
[bio34-s3.4.1] Food grown with fertiliser made by the Haber process, the industrial process that makes ammonia from nitrogen in the air, feeds about half of the world's people.
[bio34-s3.4.2] Higher yields mean less land must be cleared for farming, which spares forests and wild habitats.
[bio34-s3.4.3] In Ireland, grass-based livestock farming depends on nitrogen for grass growth, and farming supports rural incomes and exports.
[bio34-s3.5.b] Nitrogen that crops do not take up does not disappear. It escapes into water, where it causes the problems of eutrophication and a health risk in drinking water.
[bio34-s3.5.0] Leached nitrate and washed-off slurry cause eutrophication, harming fisheries, angling, tourism and wildlife.
[bio34-s3.5.1] Nitrate in drinking water above the EU limit of 50 mg per litre is a health risk, especially to bottle-fed babies, because it reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen. This is called blue baby syndrome.
[bio34-s3.5.2] Water above the limit must then be treated or the source abandoned, which is a cost to everyone who relies on it.
[bio34-s3.6.b] Fertiliser also causes harm away from water. Making it, using it and keeping livestock all add gases to the air, and some of the nitrogen falls back onto wild land.
[bio34-s3.6.0] Making fertiliser by the Haber process uses a lot of energy, mainly from natural gas, and releases CO₂.
[bio34-s3.6.1] Extra soil nitrate increases denitrification (bacteria converting nitrate to nitrogen gases), which releases nitrous oxide (N₂O), a powerful greenhouse gas.
[bio34-s3.6.2] Ammonia escapes from slurry and urine. In Ireland agriculture produces about 99% of ammonia emissions.
[bio34-s3.6.3] Ammonia falling on bogs, heaths and woods acts as a fertiliser, letting a few fast-growing plants crowd out species adapted to low nutrients, which reduces biodiversity.
[bio34-s3.6.4] Ammonia also forms fine particles in the air that harm human health.
[bio34-s3.7.b] Fertiliser pollution is an ethical issue because the people who gain are not always the people who pay. Both sides have a fair point.
[bio34-s3.7.0] The farmer gains the yield, but others pay the cost: downstream communities, anglers, people drinking the water and future generations.
[bio34-s3.7.1] The polluter pays principle therefore argues that farmers should bear the cost of prevention.
[bio34-s3.7.2] Against this, farm incomes are often low and food must stay affordable, and consumers who want cheap food share responsibility.
[bio34-s3.7.3] Rules must be fair to small farmers as well as large ones, who would find the same cost harder to meet.
[bio34-s3.7.4] Justice: globally, many poor farmers use too little fertiliser and suffer low yields and hunger, while richer regions use too much. The same chemical is a benefit in one place and a pollutant in another.
[bio34-s3.8.b] Because nitrate pollution crosses farm boundaries, the EU sets limits that Ireland must apply. These are the rules to know.
[bio34-s3.8.0] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) requires member states to protect water from farm nitrate.
[bio34-s3.8.1] Under it, livestock manure may not add more than 170 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year. Ireland has had a derogation (special permission to go above this) allowing some intensive farms more, under stricter conditions.
[bio34-s3.8.2] Slurry and fertiliser may not be spread in a winter closed period or near watercourses, and farms must have enough slurry storage so that slurry is not spread at the wrong time.
[bio34-s3.8.3] Ireland applies these rules through its Nitrates Action Programme.
[bio34-s3.9.b] Farmers can cut nitrogen losses without giving up fertiliser. Each response works for a biological reason, and together they lead to a judgement.
[bio34-s3.9.0] Test soil and apply only the nitrogen the crop needs, when it is growing, and not before heavy rain, because excess nitrogen is lost to water.
[bio34-s3.9.1] Grow clover or other legumes with grass, and legumes in crop rotation, so that nitrogen fixation (bacteria turning nitrogen gas into usable nitrogen) replaces some fertiliser.
[bio34-s3.9.2] Leave uncultivated buffer strips of vegetation beside rivers, which absorb nutrients before they reach the water.
[bio34-s3.9.3] Spread slurry close to the ground using low-emission methods, such as a trailing shoe, to cut ammonia loss.
[bio34-s3.9.4] Protect wetlands, where denitrification removes nitrate, and treat sewage fully.
[bio34-s3.9.5] Judgement: nitrogen fertiliser is needed to feed the world, so banning it would be unethical. But applying more than crops can use wastes money and transfers the costs to others and to the future.
[bio34-s3.9.6] The sustainable and fair position is to use less fertiliser, use it more precisely, and recycle nitrogen in manure and legumes.
[bio34-s3.10.b] Instead of losing nutrients in waste, we can return them to the soil. This is the core idea of sustainable nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s3.10.0] Sustainable nutrient cycling means returning nutrients from wastes to the soil instead of losing them. Methods include slurry and manure used as fertiliser at the right time and rate, and composting of garden and food waste.
[bio34-s3.10.1] In composting, decomposers (bacteria and fungi) turn organic matter into a nutrient-rich soil improver.
[bio34-s3.10.2] Other methods include treated sewage sludge spread on land, and anaerobic digestion (breakdown without oxygen) of slurry and food waste.
[bio34-s3.10.3] Anaerobic digestion produces biogas (methane) for fuel and a digestate (the solid left over) that is used as fertiliser.
[bio34-s3.11.b] Recycling looks like an easy win, but it has limits. A good evaluation gives both sides and then a judgement.
[bio34-s3.11.0] For: it cuts the need for manufactured fertiliser and the fossil energy used to make it.
[bio34-s3.11.1] For: it keeps wastes out of landfill, where they would decompose anaerobically and release methane.
[bio34-s3.11.2] For: it improves soil structure and soil organic matter, which is a carbon store.
[bio34-s3.11.3] Against: manure and sludge can carry pathogens, and sludge may contain heavy metals and other pollutants, so it must be treated and tested.
[bio34-s3.11.4] Against: spreading at the wrong time causes the same pollution as fertiliser does.
[bio34-s3.11.5] Against: anaerobic digesters are costly to build, and leaks of biogas release methane.
[bio34-s3.11.6] Against: many people are uneasy about food grown on land treated with sewage sludge.
[bio34-s3.11.7] Judgement: recycling is more sustainable than manufactured fertiliser alone when it is treated, tested and spread at the right time and rate.
[bio34-s3.12.b] Forests hold carbon in wood and soil and take it in from the air. Clearing them reverses this and raises ethical questions about who has the right to use the land.
[bio34-s3.12.0] Tropical forests are cleared, often by burning, mainly for cattle ranching (for example in the Amazon in Brazil), for soya grown largely as animal feed, and for oil-palm plantations (for example in Indonesia and Malaysia).
[bio34-s3.12.1] Deforestation turns a carbon sink (a store that takes in more carbon than it gives out) into a source, removes nutrients with the crop, exposes soil to erosion, and destroys habitats of very high biodiversity.
[bio34-s3.12.2] For clearing: poorer countries argue that they have the same right to develop their land as richer countries had when they cleared their own forests, and that farming gives jobs and food.
[bio34-s3.12.3] Against clearing: the losses (carbon released, species extinct, rainfall changed) affect the whole world and future generations, and indigenous people who live in the forests lose their homes and way of life.
[bio34-s3.12.4] Consumers in richer countries share responsibility, because they buy the beef, soya-fed meat and palm oil.
[bio34-s3.12.5] Responses: protected areas; payments to countries for keeping forests; and EU rules that ban imports of products linked to recent deforestation.
[bio34-s3.13.b] Bogs are a store of carbon, and their habitats cannot be replaced on any human timescale. In Ireland, cutting and draining them is a debate about tradition, jobs and climate.
[bio34-s3.13.0] Peatlands cover about a fifth of Ireland's land area. For centuries turf was cut by hand for household fuel. In the twentieth century bogs were drained and milled by Bord na Móna for electricity and horticultural compost.
[bio34-s3.13.1] Drained peat decomposes aerobically (with oxygen) and releases CO₂, so it is lost as a carbon store. Raised bogs are also rare habitats for specialised species such as sphagnum moss.
[bio34-s3.13.2] For continuing to cut peat: families have traditional rights to cut turf on their bog (turbary rights). Turf is a cheap local fuel and part of rural culture, and peat extraction gave jobs to Midlands communities.
[bio34-s3.13.3] Against cutting peat: burning peat releases more CO₂ per unit of energy than coal. Bog habitats cannot be replaced on any human timescale, and the climate cost falls on everyone and on the future.
[bio34-s3.14.b] Ireland has begun to end peat extraction, but doing so is not free of conflict. The same fairness questions apply on a global scale to fossil fuels.
[bio34-s3.14.0] Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021.
[bio34-s3.14.1] Cutting on protected raised bogs (Special Areas of Conservation) is restricted, which has caused conflict with turf-cutting families.
[bio34-s3.14.2] Bogs are being rewetted, and a Just Transition fund supports workers and communities that depended on peat.
[bio34-s3.14.3] Judgement on peat: fairness to the people who lose income is part of the judgement, and it must be weighed against the carbon and habitat losses.
[bio34-s3.14.4] Fossil fuels as an ethical issue: the countries that industrialised first have released most of the CO₂ in the atmosphere, while many of the people most harmed by the resulting climate change, in low-lying and poor countries, have contributed least.
[bio34-s3.14.5] Future generations will bear costs they did not choose, which is intergenerational justice (fairness between generations). There is more on the biological strategies to respond in Climate change and biological strategies.
