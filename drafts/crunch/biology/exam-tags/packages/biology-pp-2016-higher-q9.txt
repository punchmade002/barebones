CANDIDATES bio13-s1: Enzymes as catalysts
[bio13-s1.0.b] Cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions, and left alone most of them would be far too slow to keep a cell alive. Catalysts speed reactions up, and enzymes are the catalysts that living things make for themselves.
[bio13-s1.0.0] A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being used up or permanently changed by it, so it can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.0.1] A catalyst does not change which products form or how much product is made in the end; it only makes the reaction reach that point faster.
[bio13-s1.0.2] An enzyme is a biological catalyst: a catalyst made by a living cell.
[bio13-s1.0.3] Almost all enzymes are globular proteins (proteins folded into a compact, rounded ball), folded into a precise three-dimensional shape. That shape decides what the enzyme can do. There is more on protein structure in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio13-s1.0.4] Because enzymes are proteins, they are made at ribosomes (the tiny structures in a cell where proteins are built), following the instructions in a gene (the length of DNA that codes for the protein).
[bio13-s1.1.b] Because an enzyme is a protein that is reused rather than consumed, it has a set of properties that follow from that.
[bio13-s1.1.0] Enzymes are needed only in small amounts, because each molecule is reused many times; catalase, among the fastest, breaks down millions of hydrogen peroxide molecules per second.
[bio13-s1.1.1] Enzymes are unchanged at the end of the reaction, which is why the same molecule can start work on another substrate straight away.
[bio13-s1.1.2] Enzymes are specific: each acts on one reaction or one type of reaction, because only a substrate of the right shape fits the enzyme's active site (the region where the substrate binds).
[bio13-s1.1.3] Enzyme activity is affected by temperature and pH, and enzymes are denatured (their shape is changed so that they stop working) by heat and by extremes of pH. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio13-s1.2.b] Enzyme names usually give away the job the enzyme does. Enzymes also work in different places, depending on whether their job is inside or outside the cell.
[bio13-s1.2.0] Most enzyme names end in -ase, and many are named after their substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on), so the name tells you the job: maltase acts on maltose, lipase on lipids, protease on proteins, sucrase on sucrose.
[bio13-s1.2.1] Some older names, such as pepsin and trypsin, don't follow this rule, so don't assume that every enzyme name ends in -ase.
[bio13-s1.2.2] Most enzymes work inside the cell that made them (intracellular), for example the enzymes of respiration (releasing energy from food) in the cytoplasm and mitochondria and of photosynthesis (making food using light) in chloroplasts.
[bio13-s1.2.3] Some enzymes are secreted to work outside the cell (extracellular), for example digestive enzymes released into the gut and the enzymes that fungi and bacteria release onto their food. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio13-s1.3.b] Even a reaction that releases energy overall needs a push to get started. An enzyme works by making that push smaller, not by adding energy.
[bio13-s1.3.0] Activation energy is the minimum energy that reacting molecules need before a reaction can start: the energy needed to break or strain existing bonds.
[bio13-s1.3.1] Think of activation energy as an energy hill that must be climbed even when the reaction releases energy overall.
[bio13-s1.3.2] Without a catalyst, most reactions of metabolism (the chemical reactions of life) have such a high activation energy that, at body temperature (37 °C), they would happen far too slowly to keep a cell alive.
[bio13-s1.3.3] Heating would supply the energy, but high temperatures would damage cells and their proteins, so heating is not an option for living things.
[bio13-s1.3.4] An enzyme provides an alternative route for the reaction with a lower activation energy, so many more molecules have enough energy to react at the temperature of the cell, and the reaction is many times faster.
[bio13-s1.3.5] The overall energy change of the reaction (energy released or taken in) stays the same; only the hill to be climbed gets lower.
[bio13-s1.3.6] Diagram: energy-profile graph, energy on the y-axis, progress of reaction on the x-axis. Reactants high on the left, products lower on the right.
[bio13-s1.3.7] Draw two humps, a tall one labelled without enzyme and a lower one labelled with enzyme. Mark each activation energy with a vertical arrow from the reactants to the top of its hump.
[bio13-s1.4.b] Every enzyme reaction has two partners: the enzyme and the molecule it works on. One small region of the enzyme does the work, and its shape is what makes the enzyme selective.
[bio13-s1.4.0] The substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on; the products are the molecules made.
[bio13-s1.4.1] Example: catalase acts on its substrate hydrogen peroxide: 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂.
[bio13-s1.4.2] The active site is a small region of the enzyme, a pocket or groove on its surface, where the substrate binds and the reaction takes place.
[bio13-s1.4.3] The active site's shape, and the chemical charges in it, are complementary to the substrate, meaning the two fit together like matching pieces.
[bio13-s1.4.4] The active site is a part of the enzyme, not of the substrate. The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site, not the same as it.
[bio13-s1.5.b] Once the substrate meets the active site, the reaction follows three steps. Together they make the active-site (lock-and-key) model of enzyme action.
[bio13-s1.5.0] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex (the enzyme and substrate joined together).
[bio13-s1.5.1] Enzyme and substrate molecules meet by random movement, so anything that increases collisions (more substrate, more enzyme, higher temperature) increases the rate, up to a point.
[bio13-s1.5.2] 2. While the substrate is held in the active site, the enzyme lowers the activation energy, for example by straining the bonds that must break, or by holding two substrate molecules close together in the right position to react.
[bio13-s1.5.3] The substrate is then converted to products, forming an enzyme–product complex.
[bio13-s1.5.4] 3. The products have a different shape, no longer fit the active site, and are released.
[bio13-s1.5.5] The active site is then free and unchanged, ready to bind another substrate molecule, which is why one enzyme molecule can be used again and again.
[bio13-s1.5.6] In summary: enzyme + substrate → enzyme–substrate complex → enzyme + products.
[bio13-s1.5.7] Diagram: three stages in a row: enzyme with active site and complementary substrate approaching; enzyme–substrate complex; unchanged enzyme with two products leaving. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, enzyme–substrate complex and products. A matching diagram for a joining reaction is optional.
[bio13-s1.6.b] The same active-site mechanism works whichever way a reaction runs. Metabolism has reactions that break molecules down and reactions that build them up, and enzymes catalyse both.
[bio13-s1.6.0] In catabolic reactions one substrate is split into smaller products, for example amylase splits starch into maltose.
[bio13-s1.6.1] In anabolic reactions two or more substrates are joined into a larger product. For example, DNA polymerase joins nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA) into a new DNA strand when DNA is copied.
[bio13-s1.6.2] Another anabolic example is ATP synthase, which joins ADP and phosphate into ATP, the molecule cells use to carry energy.
[bio13-s1.7.b] A cell contains thousands of different molecules but does not react them all at random. Enzymes are selective: each one picks out its own substrate, and that selectivity comes from the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s1.7.0] Specificity means that each enzyme catalyses only one reaction, or one type of reaction, because only a substrate with the complementary shape fits its active site.
[bio13-s1.7.1] This is what selective catalysts means: amylase digests starch but not protein or fat; pepsin digests protein but not starch.
[bio13-s1.7.2] The lock-and-key model is the simplest explanation: the substrate (the key) fits only the active site (the lock) of the right shape.
[bio13-s1.7.3] The lock-and-key model was later refined to the induced-fit model, in which the active site changes shape slightly as the substrate binds. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio13-s1.7.4] Because enzymes are specific, a cell needs thousands of different enzymes, one for each of its reactions, and it can control a reaction by making, or not making, the one enzyme that catalyses it.
[bio13-s1.7.5] Anything that changes the shape of the active site stops the substrate fitting, so the enzyme stops working: for example high temperature or extreme pH (denaturation).
[bio13-s1.7.6] A change in the enzyme's amino-acid sequence (the order of the amino acids that make up the protein), caused by a mutation in its gene, also changes the active site and stops the substrate fitting.
[bio13-s1.8.b] Metabolism is the sum of all the chemical reactions in a living thing. Enzymes make these reactions possible, and they usually work in sequences called pathways.
[bio13-s1.8.0] Every reaction of metabolism is catalysed by its own specific enzyme.
[bio13-s1.8.1] Without enzymes, the reactions that keep cells alive (respiration, photosynthesis, protein synthesis, DNA copying, digestion) would be far too slow at the temperatures and pH inside living things.
[bio13-s1.8.2] Most processes are metabolic pathways: a sequence of reactions, each catalysed by a different enzyme, in which the product of one reaction is the substrate of the next.
[bio13-s1.8.3] Respiration and photosynthesis are such pathways. There is more on this in Respiration and Photosynthesis.
[bio13-s1.8.4] Diagram: a pathway written as A → B → C → D, with enzyme 1, enzyme 2 and enzyme 3 written over the arrows.
[bio13-s1.9.b] Because every step in a pathway needs its own enzyme, a cell controls its chemistry by controlling its enzymes.
[bio13-s1.9.0] A cell controls which reactions happen, and how fast, by switching on or off the genes for particular enzymes and by activating or inhibiting enzymes already made.
[bio13-s1.9.1] If one enzyme in a pathway is missing, the pathway stops at that step: its substrate builds up and the later products are not made.
[bio13-s1.9.2] Many enzymes need a non-protein helper to work: a mineral ion (for example magnesium for many enzymes that use ATP) or a coenzyme made from a vitamin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio13-s1.10.b] Because each reaction depends on one enzyme, blocking or losing that enzyme has serious effects. Some poisons act this way, and some inherited or common conditions come from a missing enzyme.
[bio13-s1.10.0] Some drugs and poisons work by blocking an enzyme: for example some insecticides and nerve agents inhibit the enzyme that breaks down a neurotransmitter (a chemical messenger between nerve cells). There is more on this in Conduction across the synaptic cleft and its disruption.
[bio13-s1.10.1] Lactose intolerance: many adults make little lactase, the enzyme that digests lactose (milk sugar), so undigested lactose passes to the large intestine, where bacteria ferment it, causing bloating, wind, cramps and diarrhoea.
[bio13-s1.10.2] Lactose-free milk is made using lactase, which digests the lactose in the milk before it is drunk.
[bio13-s1.10.3] Phenylketonuria (PKU): an inherited lack of the enzyme that converts the amino acid phenylalanine into another amino acid (tyrosine), so phenylalanine builds up in the blood and damages the developing brain.
[bio13-s1.10.4] PKU is detected by the heel-prick test given to newborn babies in Ireland, and is controlled by a diet low in phenylalanine.

CANDIDATES bio13-s3: Investigation: rate of enzyme activity
[bio13-s3.0.b] An enzyme is a biological catalyst, so what we want to know in an investigation is how fast it works. That speed is called the rate, and it is always measured against time.
[bio13-s3.0.0] The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction is the amount of substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on) converted into product per unit time, e.g. cm³ of oxygen produced per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.1] The rate can be measured either by how fast a product appears or by how fast the substrate disappears, whichever is easier to detect for that reaction.
[bio13-s3.0.2] Catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂); the rate is measured by the oxygen released, as foam or as a volume of gas.
[bio13-s3.0.3] Amylase digests starch to maltose; the rate is measured by the time taken for the starch to disappear, tested with iodine.
[bio13-s3.0.4] Rate = amount of product ÷ time: e.g. 30 cm³ of foam in 2 minutes gives a rate of 15 cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.0.5] Where the time taken to reach an end-point (such as the starch disappearing) is measured, rate = 1 ÷ time: starch gone in 120 s gives a rate of 1/120 = 0.0083 s⁻¹, often written as 1000/time to give whole numbers.
[bio13-s3.0.6] A shorter time means a faster rate, because the same change has been completed more quickly.
[bio13-s3.0.7] Measure the initial rate, at the start of the reaction (e.g. in the first one or two minutes), because the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
[bio13-s3.1.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. Presenting them clearly in a table and a graph is how you show the pattern in your results.
[bio13-s3.1.0] A results table has the independent variable (the factor you change) in the first column, then the repeat readings, the mean and the rate, each column headed with its unit.
[bio13-s3.1.1] A graph has the independent variable on the x-axis and the rate on the y-axis, with points plotted accurately and joined by a smooth curve or line of best fit.
[bio13-s3.2.b] All five investigations use the same small set of enzymes, substrates and equipment. Knowing what each is for makes the methods easy to follow.
[bio13-s3.2.0] Catalase is found in almost all cells that use oxygen, where it breaks down hydrogen peroxide, a toxic by-product of metabolism. Good sources are celery, potato, liver and yeast.
[bio13-s3.2.1] Catalase extract: blend a fixed mass of chopped celery (e.g. 20 g) with distilled water (e.g. 100 cm³), then filter through muslin or filter paper. Make one batch and use it for every test, so the enzyme is the same each time.
[bio13-s3.2.2] Amylase: a commercial (e.g. fungal) amylase solution made up at a fixed concentration; starch solution (e.g. 1%) is its substrate. Saliva contains amylase but is avoided for hygiene reasons.
[bio13-s3.2.3] Equipment for measuring: 100 cm³ graduated cylinders, syringes or graduated pipettes for measuring volumes, thermometer to check temperatures, stopwatch to time each run, electronic balance.
[bio13-s3.2.4] Other equipment: test tubes and boiling tubes, blender or mortar and pestle, knife and chopping board, filter funnel, spotting tile and droppers, conical flask with bung and delivery tube, trough, labels.
[bio13-s3.2.5] Water baths can be thermostatically controlled electric baths, or beakers of water warmed with kettle water or cooled with ice, checked with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.2.6] Reagents: hydrogen peroxide solution (one stock of fixed concentration), buffer solutions or buffer tablets of known pH, washing-up liquid, distilled water, starch solution, amylase solution and iodine solution.
[bio13-s3.3.b] Four ideas recur across the investigations: a buffer to hold pH steady, washing-up liquid to make the oxygen measurable in the foam methods, a waiting time so the mixture reaches the right temperature, and a control to prove the enzyme is responsible.
[bio13-s3.3.0] A buffer is a solution that keeps the pH constant even when acids or alkalis are produced or added.
[bio13-s3.3.1] A buffer at pH 7 (or at the enzyme's optimum) is added in every investigation except the pH investigation, so that pH is a controlled variable (kept the same each time).
[bio13-s3.3.2] In the foam methods, a drop of washing-up liquid traps the oxygen released by catalase as a foam, so its volume can be read in a graduated cylinder; the same amount is added every time.
[bio13-s3.3.3] Reaching temperature (equilibration): the enzyme and the substrate are placed in the same water bath, in separate containers, for about 5 minutes before they are mixed, so the reaction starts at the chosen temperature.
[bio13-s3.3.4] A control in each investigation uses enzyme that has been boiled (denatured), or distilled water in place of the enzyme, with everything else the same.
[bio13-s3.3.5] The control shows that the oxygen (or the loss of starch) is caused by the enzyme and not by the substrate breaking down on its own.
[bio13-s3.4.b] The chemicals and equipment here are mostly mild, but several can harm skin, eyes or clothes if handled carelessly.
[bio13-s3.4.0] Hydrogen peroxide is an oxidising agent that irritates or burns the eyes and skin; wear safety glasses (and gloves), measure it with a syringe, and wash any splash off with plenty of water.
[bio13-s3.4.1] Buffers of very low or very high pH are irritants, so wear safety glasses.
[bio13-s3.4.2] Use tongs or a test-tube holder and take care with boiling water and hot water baths; keep electrical water baths and blenders away from spilt water.
[bio13-s3.4.3] Take care with knives and blender blades. If liver is used, treat it as raw meat: wear gloves, keep it off benches used for other work and wash hands afterwards.
[bio13-s3.4.4] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes, so handle it with care. Never eat or drink in the laboratory, because chemicals could be swallowed.
[bio13-s3.5.b] Here we vary how much hydrogen peroxide (the substrate) the same amount of catalase has to work on, and see how the rate of oxygen production changes.
[bio13-s3.5.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of substrate (hydrogen peroxide) concentration on the rate of catalase activity, by changing only the substrate so any change in rate must be due to it.
[bio13-s3.5.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of hydrogen peroxide increases the rate of oxygen production, because more substrate molecules collide with active sites per second, until all the active sites are occupied and the rate levels off.”
[bio13-s3.5.2] Independent variable: hydrogen peroxide concentration, made by diluting the stock with distilled water to 0%, 20%, 40%, 60%, 80% and 100% of the stock (e.g. 2 cm³ stock + 8 cm³ water = 20%), always to the same total volume (e.g. 10 cm³).
[bio13-s3.5.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): volume of oxygen collected in the first minute (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.5.4] Controlled variables: volume of the same celery extract (so enzyme concentration is constant), temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), total volume of solution, timing and apparatus.
[bio13-s3.5.5] Control: the 100% hydrogen peroxide with boiled extract (or with water in place of the extract), which gives little or no oxygen; the 0% tube also shows that no oxygen forms without substrate.
[bio13-s3.6.b] The oxygen given off is collected in an upturned cylinder of water, so its volume can be read directly.
[bio13-s3.6.0] 1. Fill a graduated cylinder with water and turn it upside down in a trough of water, with no air at the top.
[bio13-s3.6.1] 2. Put 10 cm³ of the first hydrogen peroxide dilution and 2 cm³ of pH 7 buffer in a conical flask (or boiling tube) in the water bath, with 2 cm³ of celery extract in a separate tube beside it, for about 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.6.2] 3. Place the end of the delivery tube under the mouth of the upturned cylinder.
[bio13-s3.6.3] 4. Add the extract to the flask, immediately put in the bung with the delivery tube, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.6.4] 5. Read the volume of oxygen collected in the cylinder after 1 minute (or every 30 seconds for 3 minutes).
[bio13-s3.6.5] 6. Rinse the flask and repeat for each concentration. Do each concentration three times and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.6.6] Alternatives: a gas syringe connected to the flask measures the oxygen volume more accurately than collection over water; or the foam method used in the pH investigation can be used.
[bio13-s3.6.7] Another alternative: filter-paper discs soaked in catalase extract are dropped into each concentration and the time for each disc to rise to the surface (buoyed by oxygen bubbles) is timed, with rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.6.8] Diagram: a conical flask in a water bath, with a bung and delivery tube leading under an upturned graduated cylinder full of water in a trough. Label hydrogen peroxide + buffer + catalase extract, water bath, thermometer, delivery tube, oxygen collecting, trough of water.
[bio13-s3.7.b] The results follow a typical curve: a steep rise, then a plateau. Each part has its own explanation, and the plateau is caused by saturation of the active sites, not by the enzyme being denatured or used up.
[bio13-s3.7.0] Expected results: no oxygen at 0%; at low concentrations the rate rises steeply, roughly in proportion to concentration; at high concentrations it rises less and levels off into a plateau. The boiled-enzyme control gives little or no oxygen.
[bio13-s3.7.1] The rise is explained by collisions: more substrate molecules in the same volume means more collisions with active sites (the part of the enzyme where the substrate binds) per second, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form and more product is made per minute.
[bio13-s3.7.2] The plateau is explained by saturation: at high substrate concentration every active site is occupied at any moment, so the enzyme (saturated) works at its maximum rate, and adding more substrate cannot increase the rate.
[bio13-s3.7.3] At the plateau the amount of enzyme is the limiting factor (the thing holding the rate back); adding more enzyme would raise the rate again.
[bio13-s3.7.4] Within the range used in a school investigation the plateau may not be reached if the enzyme is in excess.
[bio13-s3.7.5] Conclusion: state the trend shown by the mean rates, whether it supports the hypothesis, and the concentration above which the rate stopped increasing, if one was found.
[bio13-s3.7.6] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against substrate concentration (x-axis); a curve rising from the origin, steep at first and then levelling off, with the plateau labelled all active sites occupied (saturated).
[bio13-s3.8.b] Now the substrate stays the same and the amount of enzyme changes. Amylase digests starch, and the end-point is found with iodine, which turns blue-black with starch and stays yellow-brown when starch is absent.
[bio13-s3.8.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of enzyme (amylase) concentration on the rate at which starch is digested.
[bio13-s3.8.1] Hypothesis: “Increasing the concentration of amylase increases the rate of starch digestion, because more active sites are available to bind starch molecules, as long as starch is in excess.”
[bio13-s3.8.2] Independent variable: amylase concentration, made by diluting the stock amylase solution with distilled water to 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of the stock, always to the same volume.
[bio13-s3.8.3] Dependent variable: time taken for the starch to disappear (the iodine test no longer turns blue-black), converted to rate = 1 ÷ time.
[bio13-s3.8.4] Controlled variables: volume and concentration of starch solution, volume of amylase solution, temperature (water bath at e.g. 37 °C), pH (buffer at pH 7), volume of iodine in each well, and the sampling interval.
[bio13-s3.8.5] Control: starch solution with distilled water (0% amylase) or with boiled amylase in place of the amylase; it stays blue-black with iodine for the whole investigation, showing that the starch does not disappear without active enzyme.
[bio13-s3.9.b] Drops of the reacting mixture are tested with iodine at regular intervals until the blue-black colour no longer appears.
[bio13-s3.9.0] 1. Put a drop of iodine solution into each well of a spotting tile (a white tile with small hollows), so each sample can be tested as soon as it is taken.
[bio13-s3.9.1] 2. Put 5 cm³ of starch solution and 1 cm³ of pH 7 buffer into a test tube, and 2 cm³ of the first amylase dilution into another, then stand both in the water bath for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.9.2] 3. Pour the amylase into the starch, mix, and start the stopwatch.
[bio13-s3.9.3] 4. Every 30 seconds, take one drop of the mixture with a clean dropper and add it to the next well of iodine.
[bio13-s3.9.4] 5. Note the time at which a drop first leaves the iodine yellow-brown (no blue-black): this is the end-point, when all the starch has been digested.
[bio13-s3.9.5] 6. Repeat for each amylase concentration, three times each, so that a mean can be calculated and a one-off error does not mislead you; then calculate the mean time and the rate.
[bio13-s3.9.6] Alternative: with catalase, vary the volume of celery extract (e.g. 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2 and 2.5 cm³, each made up to the same total volume with distilled water) or the number of equal-sized celery discs, and measure the foam or oxygen volume in a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.9.7] Diagram: a spotting tile with a row of wells, the first wells blue-black and the later wells yellow-brown, labelled with sampling times; and the starch–amylase tube in a water bath with a thermometer.
[bio13-s3.10.b] With more enzyme, the starch disappears sooner. Unlike the substrate investigation, the graph is a straight line, as long as there is plenty of substrate.
[bio13-s3.10.0] Expected results: the higher the amylase concentration, the shorter the time for the starch to disappear, so the faster the rate; the 0% and boiled-enzyme controls never reach the end-point.
[bio13-s3.10.1] A graph of rate against enzyme concentration is a straight line through the origin: doubling the enzyme roughly doubles the rate.
[bio13-s3.10.2] Explanation: more enzyme molecules means more active sites, so more substrate molecules can be bound and converted at the same time, and more product is made per unit time.
[bio13-s3.10.3] The straight line continues only while substrate is in excess (more than the enzyme can use). If the enzyme concentration becomes very high, substrate becomes the limiting factor and the line levels off.
[bio13-s3.10.4] Diagram: a graph of rate (y-axis) against enzyme concentration (x-axis); a straight line rising from the origin, labelled substrate in excess.
[bio13-s3.11.b] Each enzyme works best at a particular pH. Here, buffers set the pH, and the volume of foam produced by catalase shows how active it is at each one.
[bio13-s3.11.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of pH on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.11.1] Hypothesis: “Catalase has an optimum pH at which its rate is highest, and the rate decreases at pH values above and below it, because the shape of the active site is changed.”
[bio13-s3.11.2] Independent variable: pH, set with buffer solutions (e.g. pH 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 and 10), which hold each pH steady so it does not drift as the reaction proceeds.
[bio13-s3.11.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam produced in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.11.4] Controlled variables: temperature (water bath at e.g. 25 °C), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract (or mass of celery), volume of buffer, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.11.5] Control: at each pH (or at pH 7), boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.12.b] The foam is built up in a graduated cylinder, so its height can be read at the end of a fixed time.
[bio13-s3.12.0] 1. Into a 100 cm³ graduated cylinder put 10 cm³ of pH 4 buffer, 2 cm³ of celery extract (or a fixed mass of chopped celery) and one drop of washing-up liquid.
[bio13-s3.12.1] 2. Stand the cylinder in the 25 °C water bath, with a boiling tube of hydrogen peroxide beside it, for 5 minutes.
[bio13-s3.12.2] 3. Note the starting volume of liquid in the cylinder, so the foam volume can be found by subtraction.

[bio13-s3.13.b] The rate rises to a peak at one pH and falls on both sides. The explanation lies in how pH affects the shape of the active site.
[bio13-s3.13.0] Expected results: the most foam forms at around pH 7 (the optimum for celery and liver catalase), with less in more acidic and more alkaline buffers and very little at the extremes. The control gives no foam.
[bio13-s3.13.1] The optimum pH is the pH at which the active site has the shape and charges that best fit the substrate.
[bio13-s3.13.2] A higher or lower hydrogen ion concentration, in more acidic or more alkaline conditions, changes the charges in the active site and the bonds holding the enzyme's shape, so the substrate fits less well and fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
[bio13-s3.13.3] Small changes from the optimum are reversible; extreme pH denatures the enzyme permanently.
[bio13-s3.13.4] Each enzyme's optimum suits the place where it works: pepsin in the acid stomach has an optimum of about pH 2; salivary amylase in the mouth about pH 7; trypsin in the alkaline small intestine about pH 8.

[bio13-s3.14.b] Temperature has two opposite effects on an enzyme: it speeds up collisions, but too much heat destroys the enzyme. This investigation measures catalase activity across a range of water-bath temperatures.
[bio13-s3.14.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of temperature on the rate of catalase activity.
[bio13-s3.14.1] Hypothesis: “As temperature increases, the rate of catalase activity increases up to an optimum temperature, because molecules move faster and collide more often; above the optimum the rate falls sharply because the enzyme is denatured.”
[bio13-s3.14.2] Independent variable: temperature, set with water baths at e.g. 0 °C (ice water), 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60 and 70 °C, each checked with a thermometer because baths drift from their set temperature.
[bio13-s3.14.3] Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³), giving the rate in cm³ per minute.
[bio13-s3.14.4] Controlled variables: pH (same volume of pH 7 buffer), volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, volume of the same celery extract, one drop of washing-up liquid, time and size of cylinder.
[bio13-s3.14.5] Control: boiled celery extract (or water) in place of the extract at each temperature, giving no foam.
[bio13-s3.15.b] The temperature method reuses the foam method from the pH investigation, with the buffer fixed and the water bath changed.
[bio13-s3.15.0] Method: follow the pH investigation method, but with pH 7 buffer in every cylinder, and with each cylinder (buffer, extract and washing-up liquid) and its tube of hydrogen peroxide left in a water bath at the chosen temperature for 5 minutes before the peroxide is added.
[bio13-s3.15.1] Check the bath temperature with the thermometer during the 2 minutes, because it can drift; repeat three times at each temperature and calculate the mean rate.
[bio13-s3.15.2] The same method works with amylase and starch, timing the starch's disappearance at each temperature, as in the enzyme concentration investigation.

[bio13-s3.16.b] The curve rises slowly to a peak and then drops steeply. The rise and the fall have different causes.
[bio13-s3.16.0] Expected results: little foam at 0 °C; the rate increases as temperature rises, to a maximum at the optimum temperature; above the optimum the rate falls steeply, with little or no activity at the highest temperatures tested.
[bio13-s3.16.1] The graph is not symmetrical: a gradual rise and a steep fall. For human enzymes the optimum is about 37 °C, body temperature.
[bio13-s3.16.2] Explanation of the rise: as temperature increases, enzyme and substrate molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), move faster and collide more often, and more collisions have enough energy to react, so more enzyme–substrate complexes form per second.
[bio13-s3.16.3] Over the lower part of the range, the rate roughly doubles for each 10 °C rise (an approximate pattern, not an exact law).
[bio13-s3.16.4] Explanation of the fall: above the optimum, the enzyme molecule vibrates so much that the bonds holding its shape break, the active site changes shape and the substrate no longer fits: the enzyme is denatured.
[bio13-s3.16.5] As temperature rises further, more enzyme molecules are denatured, so the rate falls even though collisions are more frequent.
[bio13-s3.16.6] At low temperature the enzyme is inactive, not denatured: the molecules move too slowly for many collisions, but the active site keeps its shape, so activity returns when the enzyme is warmed (shown in the heat denaturation investigation).

[bio13-s3.17.b] Heat can do more than slow an enzyme: it can destroy its shape for good. This investigation shows that boiled catalase stays inactive even after it is cooled.
[bio13-s3.17.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of heat denaturation on the activity of catalase.
[bio13-s3.17.1] Denaturation is the permanent loss of an enzyme's shape, and so of its active site and its activity, caused by high temperature or extreme pH.
[bio13-s3.17.2] Hypothesis: “Catalase that has been boiled will not break down hydrogen peroxide, even when cooled, because its active site has been permanently changed.”
[bio13-s3.17.3] Independent variable: whether the extract has been boiled (heat-treated) or not. Dependent variable: volume of foam in 2 minutes (cm³).
[bio13-s3.17.4] Controlled variables: both extracts tested at the same temperature (e.g. 25 °C) and pH (pH 7 buffer), with the same volumes of extract, buffer and hydrogen peroxide, the same drop of washing-up liquid and the same time.
[bio13-s3.17.5] Control: the unboiled extract, which shows how much foam active catalase produces under the same conditions, so the boiled result has something to be compared with.
[bio13-s3.18.b] Half the extract is boiled and half is not; both are then tested at the same temperature, so any difference is due to boiling alone.
[bio13-s3.18.0] 1. Divide the celery extract between two labelled boiling tubes, A and B.
[bio13-s3.18.1] 2. Place tube A in a boiling water bath for 10 minutes; leave tube B at room temperature.
[bio13-s3.18.2] 3. Cool tube A, then stand both tubes in the 25 °C water bath for 5 minutes, so the boiled extract is tested at the same temperature as the unboiled.
[bio13-s3.18.3] 4. Set up two graduated cylinders, each with 10 cm³ of pH 7 buffer, a drop of washing-up liquid and 2 cm³ of extract A or B.
[bio13-s3.18.4] 5. Add 2 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide to each and read the foam after 2 minutes. Repeat three times.

[bio13-s3.18.7] Extension: extract kept in ice water for 10 minutes and then warmed to 25 °C gives as much foam as unchilled extract, showing that cooling makes an enzyme inactive without denaturing it, whereas heating denatures it.
[bio13-s3.18.8] The same investigation works with amylase: boiled amylase added to starch leaves the iodine test blue-black at every sampling time, while unboiled amylase digests the starch so the iodine stays yellow-brown.
[bio13-s3.19.b] Secondary data are results that someone else collected. They let you check and extend your own conclusions, and the specification requires conclusions supported by both primary and secondary data.
[bio13-s3.19.0] Secondary data are data collected by someone else: results pooled from other groups in the class, published tables, textbooks or research papers, or online databases.
[bio13-s3.19.1] Uses: compare your optimum with published optima (the optimum pH of digestive enzymes and the optimum temperature of human enzymes given in the pH and temperature investigations).
[bio13-s3.19.2] Uses: check whether your trend agrees with other groups' (reproducibility), and extend a conclusion beyond the range you could test.
[bio13-s3.19.3] Secondary data on enzymes from extreme environments support the link between structure and conditions: Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, works best at about 72 °C and is not denatured at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s3.19.4] When using secondary data, record its source, check that it was measured under comparable conditions (same enzyme source, substrate, pH or temperature), and judge whether the source is reliable. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio13-s3.20.b] Every method has weaknesses. Knowing where the error comes from tells you how to reduce it.
[bio13-s3.20.0] Foam volume is imprecise: bubbles burst, the top of the foam is uneven, and foam may stick to the cylinder walls. Read at eye level at exactly the same time each run; collecting the oxygen in a gas syringe is more accurate.
[bio13-s3.20.1] Gas is lost in the time between adding the enzyme and inserting the bung, or through leaks; some oxygen dissolves in the water when it is collected over water.
[bio13-s3.20.2] Water-bath temperatures drift, especially in beakers of hot water or ice; check with a thermometer throughout or use a thermostatically controlled bath.
[bio13-s3.20.3] The reaction mixture may not reach the bath temperature if equilibration is too short, so allow the full time.
[bio13-s3.21.b] Some errors come from the enzyme and reagents themselves, or from judging a colour change. Repeating and averaging makes results more reliable.
[bio13-s3.21.0] Enzyme activity varies between celery samples and falls as an extract stands, so make one batch, keep it cool and use it quickly for all the tests; a fresh batch for a repeat can give different values.
[bio13-s3.21.1] Hydrogen peroxide slowly breaks down on standing, especially in light, so its concentration may fall during the session: use one fresh stock kept in a dark bottle.
[bio13-s3.21.2] The iodine end-point is a judgement of colour, and sampling every 30 seconds means the time is only known to within 30 seconds; sample more often near the end-point, compare against a control well, or use a colorimeter.
[bio13-s3.21.3] Measure volumes with syringes or graduated pipettes rather than by eye, and use a clean syringe or dropper for each solution to avoid contamination.
[bio13-s3.21.4] Repeat each value three times, calculate the mean, and identify and investigate any anomalous result; pool class data to check reproducibility.
[bio13-s3.21.5] Use at least five values of the independent variable so the shape of the graph (peak or plateau) can be seen.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio27-s1: The digestive system
[bio27-s1.0.b] Humans can't make their own food, so every meal has to be taken in, broken down and moved into the body before cells can use it. This happens in four stages: ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.
[bio27-s1.0.0] Humans are heterotrophs, meaning they obtain food from other organisms rather than making it. They are also omnivores: they eat both plant and animal material.
[bio27-s1.0.1] Ingestion is taking food into the body through the mouth, which is where every meal enters the gut.
[bio27-s1.0.2] Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. It is both mechanical (physical breakdown into smaller pieces) and chemical (hydrolysis, the splitting of large molecules by enzymes).
[bio27-s1.0.3] Digestion is necessary because large molecules such as starch, proteins and fats cannot pass through the wall of the gut or through cell membranes. It converts them to small soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.
[bio27-s1.0.4] Cells then use the absorbed molecules to build the body's own molecules or for respiration. This use of absorbed molecules is called assimilation: for example, amino acids are built into proteins and glucose is respired to release energy.
[bio27-s1.0.5] Absorption is the passage of the small soluble products of digestion, together with water, vitamins and minerals, through the wall of the gut into the blood or lymph. It happens mainly in the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.0.6] Egestion is the removal of undigested and unabsorbed material, mainly dietary fibre, as faeces through the anus. This material is left over because the body cannot digest or absorb it.
[bio27-s1.0.7] Egestion is not excretion. Faeces were never part of the body's metabolism (the chemical reactions inside cells), except for bile pigments, whereas excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells.
[bio27-s1.1.b] The alimentary canal, or gut, is the pathway food follows through the body. Its muscular wall and lining are built to move food along and to protect the gut itself.
[bio27-s1.1.0] The alimentary canal, or gut, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. Food passes in order through the mouth, pharynx (throat), oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus.
[bio27-s1.1.1] The gut wall contains two layers of smooth muscle, circular and longitudinal, which move food along by peristalsis (waves of muscle contraction that push food onwards).
[bio27-s1.1.2] The inner lining of the gut makes mucus. Mucus lubricates the food so it slides along, and protects the lining from digestive enzymes and acid, which would otherwise damage the gut's own cells.
[bio27-s1.1.3] Sphincters are rings of muscle that control movement between parts of the gut, for example at the entrance and exit of the stomach (the exit one is the pyloric sphincter) and at the anus.
[bio27-s1.2.b] The first three organs prepare food and carry it onwards. The mouth breaks it up and starts starch digestion, the oesophagus transports it, and the stomach stores and churns it while starting protein digestion.
[bio27-s1.2.0] The mouth is where food is ingested and chewed by the teeth, then mixed with saliva from three pairs of salivary glands. The tongue shapes it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. Starch digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.1] The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries food by peristalsis from the pharynx, down through the chest behind the trachea (windpipe) and through the diaphragm (the sheet of muscle below the lungs), to the stomach. No digestion or absorption happens in it.
[bio27-s1.2.2] During swallowing the epiglottis (a flap) covers the entrance to the trachea, so food goes down the oesophagus and not into the lungs.
[bio27-s1.2.3] The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the upper left of the abdomen, just below the diaphragm. It stores food for a few hours, which means a meal can be eaten quickly and released to the intestine gradually.
[bio27-s1.2.4] The stomach churns food with gastric juice (acid and a protease, an enzyme that digests protein) into a thick liquid called chyme. The acid kills most bacteria in food, and protein digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.5] The pyloric sphincter at the stomach's exit releases chyme a little at a time into the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.3.b] After the stomach, chyme enters the small intestine, where digestion is finished and most absorption happens. What is left passes to the large intestine, which recovers water and stores the waste until it leaves the body.
[bio27-s1.3.0] The small intestine is the longest part of the gut, coiled in the abdomen. Its first part, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas, and most chemical digestion happens there.
[bio27-s1.3.1] The later parts, the jejunum and the ileum, complete digestion and absorb most digested food and water through millions of villi (tiny finger-like projections of the lining that give a large surface for absorption).
[bio27-s1.3.2] The large intestine is wider but shorter than the small intestine; "large" refers to its width, not its length. Its parts are the caecum, with the appendix attached, the colon and the rectum.
[bio27-s1.3.3] The appendix is a small dead-end tube with no digestive role in humans, so food is not digested in it.
[bio27-s1.3.4] The colon absorbs water and some minerals from the remaining material, and is home to many bacteria. It is not an excretory organ, because what leaves is egested, not excreted.
[bio27-s1.3.5] The rectum is the last part of the large intestine, which stores faeces until egestion, so waste does not have to leave the body continuously.
[bio27-s1.3.6] The anus is the opening at the end of the gut, closed by sphincter muscles. The outer sphincter is under voluntary control, so egestion can be delayed until it is convenient.
[bio27-s1.4.b] Some organs help digestion without food ever entering them. They make secretions that reach the gut through ducts (narrow tubes), so they are called associated organs.
[bio27-s1.4.0] The salivary glands are three pairs, around the mouth and below the jaw, releasing saliva into the mouth through ducts. Saliva contains water, mucus and the enzyme salivary amylase.
[bio27-s1.4.1] The liver is the largest internal organ, in the upper right of the abdomen just below the diaphragm. Its digestive role is to make bile.
[bio27-s1.4.2] The liver also receives the absorbed products of digestion through the hepatic portal vein (the vein carrying blood from the gut to the liver), stores glycogen and deaminates amino acids (removes their nitrogen-containing part).
[bio27-s1.4.3] The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile. It squeezes bile through the bile duct into the duodenum when fatty food arrives there. It makes no bile itself.
[bio27-s1.4.4] The pancreas is a soft, pinkish gland behind the stomach, lying in the curve of the duodenum. It makes pancreatic juice, which contains enzymes for starch, protein and fat and sodium hydrogencarbonate, and this flows through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum.
[bio27-s1.4.5] The pancreas is also an endocrine gland (a ductless gland that releases hormones into the blood) making insulin, so it has both ducted (exocrine) and ductless (endocrine) roles.
[bio27-s1.4.6] The liver, gallbladder and pancreas are associated organs: food never passes through them, and their secretions reach the gut through ducts. Food does pass through the mouth, stomach and intestines.
[bio27-s1.5.b] A labelled diagram shows how the gut and its associated organs sit in relation to each other. Getting the positions right matters as much as the labels.
[bio27-s1.5.0] Diagram: alimentary canal and associated glands, front view, as one continuous tube. Labels: mouth, salivary glands, tongue, oesophagus, diaphragm, liver (above stomach, body's right), gallbladder (under liver), bile duct, stomach, pyloric sphincter, pancreas (in duodenum's loop), pancreatic duct, duodenum, ileum, caecum, appendix, colon, rectum, anus.
[bio27-s1.6.b] Following one mouthful from start to finish pulls the stages of nutrition together. Each part of the gut has its own main job.
[bio27-s1.6.0] Mouth: ingestion; mechanical and chemical digestion begin.
[bio27-s1.6.1] Oesophagus: transport of food to the stomach, because the mouth and stomach are too far apart for food to move unaided.
[bio27-s1.6.2] Stomach: storage and churning; protein digestion begins.
[bio27-s1.6.3] Duodenum: most chemical digestion, with bile and pancreatic juice.
[bio27-s1.6.4] Ileum: digestion is completed and absorption takes place.
[bio27-s1.6.5] Colon: absorption of water, so the remaining waste becomes more solid.
[bio27-s1.6.6] Rectum: storage of faeces.
[bio27-s1.6.7] Anus: egestion, the removal of faeces from the body.

CANDIDATES bio33-s2: Investigation: growth of microorganisms
[bio33-s2.0.b] Before testing what helps or harms microorganisms, you need to know what counts as growth and how to put a number on it. Most investigations in this subsection measure growth in one of the ways below; the antibacterial test measures the width of a clear zone instead.
[bio33-s2.0.0] Growth of a microbial population means an increase in the number of cells (bacteria and yeasts dividing) or in the size of the mycelium (the mass of thread-like filaments that makes up a mould), not the growth of one cell.
[bio33-s2.0.1] A colony is a visible mass of cells on a solid medium (a jelly-like surface the cells grow on), grown by repeated division from a single cell or spore. Counting colonies therefore estimates how many living cells were on the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.2] Bacterial colonies are usually small, round, shiny or matt spots; mould colonies are fuzzy and spread outwards. This difference in appearance helps you tell which organism has grown.
[bio33-s2.0.3] On plates, count the colonies on each plate if they are separate, or measure the diameter of a mould colony with a ruler under the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.4] Alternatively, estimate the percentage of the plate's surface covered, using a grid of squares drawn on the base. This works when colonies have merged and cannot be counted.
[bio33-s2.0.5] In liquid broth cultures, measure the cloudiness (turbidity) with a colorimeter, an instrument that measures how much light passes through. Cloudier broth has more cells and absorbs more light.
[bio33-s2.0.6] For yeast, count cells with a microscope and a counting chamber (a slide marked with a grid of known volume), or measure the CO₂ produced.
[bio33-s2.0.7] Microorganisms need a source of nutrients (for energy and raw materials), water, a suitable temperature and pH and, for most, oxygen. Each of these is one factor tested later in this subsection.
[bio33-s2.0.8] Harmful chemicals and high external solute concentrations (a lot of dissolved salt or sugar outside the cells) inhibit growth, so these are tested too.
[bio33-s2.1.b] Microorganisms are everywhere: in the air, on skin and on benches. Aseptic technique is how you grow only the organism you chose, without letting it or any contaminant spread.
[bio33-s2.1.0] Aseptic technique is the set of procedures that keep unwanted microorganisms out of cultures, so that only the intended organism grows and results are valid.
[bio33-s2.1.1] Aseptic technique also keeps the cultured microorganisms from escaping onto people and surfaces, for safety.
[bio33-s2.1.2] Any culture may contain or become contaminated with harmful microorganisms, so every culture is treated as a possible pathogen (a microorganism that causes disease).
[bio33-s2.1.3] Before starting: tie back long hair, cover cuts with a waterproof plaster, wash hands with soap and water, and wipe the bench with disinfectant. Each step reduces the microorganisms that could get into the culture or onto you.
[bio33-s2.1.4] Do not eat, drink or touch the face during the practical, because this could carry cultured microorganisms into your mouth or eyes.
[bio33-s2.1.5] Sterilise all equipment and media before use: nutrient agar, broths and glassware are heated in an autoclave (or pressure cooker) at 121 °C for 15 minutes, which kills all microorganisms, including bacterial endospores (tough resting cells that survive ordinary heat).
[bio33-s2.1.6] Plastic Petri dishes (the shallow lidded dishes that hold the agar) are bought pre-sterilised and sealed, because plastic would melt in an autoclave.
[bio33-s2.2.b] Once equipment is sterile, the risk is airborne microorganisms and anything that touches the culture. These bench habits deal with both.
[bio33-s2.2.0] Inoculating loop (a wire loop used to transfer microorganisms): heat it in the hottest part of a Bunsen flame until it glows red-hot, before and after every use, to kill any microorganisms on it.
[bio33-s2.2.1] Let the loop cool for a few seconds, without putting it down, before touching the culture, so that the heat does not kill the microorganisms being transferred.
[bio33-s2.2.2] Work close to a lit Bunsen burner: the rising hot air carries airborne microorganisms upwards and away from the open plates and bottles.
[bio33-s2.2.3] When opening a culture bottle or tube, hold the cap in the little finger and never put it down on the bench, because the bench could contaminate the inside of the cap.
[bio33-s2.2.4] Pass the neck of the bottle briefly through the flame after opening and before closing it, to kill microorganisms at the mouth and create an upward air current.
[bio33-s2.2.5] Open a Petri dish lid only slightly, tilted up like a clam shell, and for as short a time as possible, so that airborne microorganisms do not land on the agar.
[bio33-s2.3.b] After inoculating (putting the microorganism onto the plate), the plate must be closed, labelled, incubated (kept warm to let growth happen) and finally disposed of safely. Each rule here has a reason.
[bio33-s2.3.0] After inoculating, secure the lid to the base with two or four short strips of sticky tape, not sealed all the way round, so that oxygen can still enter. Sealing the lid fully would make conditions anaerobic (without oxygen), which would favour the growth of harmful anaerobic bacteria.
[bio33-s2.3.1] Label the base, not the lid (which could be swapped), around the edge with name, date and treatment.
[bio33-s2.3.2] Incubate plates upside down, lid at the bottom, so that condensation forming on the lid does not drip onto the agar and spread the colonies, and the agar does not dry out.
[bio33-s2.3.3] Incubate school cultures at about 25 °C, never at 37 °C, because body temperature favours the growth of microorganisms that are pathogenic to humans.
[bio33-s2.3.4] Never open plates after incubation: examine and count colonies through the closed lid. Never culture samples from the human body, toilets or animal waste, because these are likely to contain pathogens.
[bio33-s2.3.5] Disposal: put used plates and cultures into an autoclave bag and autoclave them (121 °C, 15 minutes) before putting them in the bin, or soak them in strong disinfectant. Disinfect the bench again and wash hands thoroughly at the end.
[bio33-s2.3.6] Spills: cover with paper towels soaked in disinfectant, leave for the time stated on the disinfectant, then clear up wearing gloves, and tell the teacher.
[bio33-s2.3.7] Ethanol, used to sterilise a glass spreader, is highly flammable: keep the beaker of ethanol away from the Bunsen flame. Pass the spreader through the flame only briefly, to burn off the ethanol, holding it pointing down.
[bio33-s2.4.b] A culture medium is the food and water the microorganisms grow on or in. Choosing the right one, and a safe organism, is the first step of every investigation.
[bio33-s2.4.0] A culture medium supplies microorganisms with nutrients and water. Nutrient agar contains agar, a jelly extracted from seaweed, which sets solid and which most microorganisms cannot digest, so the surface stays firm while they grow.
[bio33-s2.4.1] Nutrient agar also contains nutrients such as meat or yeast extract and peptone (partly digested protein), giving amino acids, sugars, vitamins and minerals.
[bio33-s2.4.2] Nutrient broth is the same as nutrient agar without the agar, so it is liquid.
[bio33-s2.4.3] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds grow better on malt agar or potato dextrose agar, which are sugar-rich and slightly acidic.
[bio33-s2.4.4] Suitable microorganisms are baker's yeast suspended in sterile water or sugar solution, moulds such as Rhizopus from bread, or non-pathogenic bacterial cultures supplied for schools.
[bio33-s2.4.5] The source of the microorganism must be known, so the risk is low.
[bio33-s2.5.b] A poured plate is a layer of sterile agar in a Petri dish. How you then put the microorganisms on it depends on what you want to see: separate colonies or an even covering.
[bio33-s2.5.0] Pouring a plate, step 1: melt the sterile agar in its bottle in a boiling water bath.
[bio33-s2.5.1] Step 2: cool the agar to about 50 °C, which is still liquid and cool enough not to kill an organism added to it (agar sets as it cools below about 40 °C).
[bio33-s2.5.2] Step 3: flame the neck of the bottle, to kill microorganisms at the mouth.
[bio33-s2.5.3] Step 4: lift the Petri dish lid slightly and pour in enough agar to cover the base (about 15–20 cm³).
[bio33-s2.5.4] Step 5: replace the lid, swirl gently and leave on a level surface to set.
[bio33-s2.5.5] Streaking: draw the flamed, cooled loop, dipped in the culture, across the agar in a zigzag. This thins the cells out along the line, so they form separate colonies.
[bio33-s2.5.6] Spreading: spread a set volume (e.g. 0.1 cm³) of a culture or diluted suspension evenly with a sterile glass spreader to make a lawn (growth covering the whole plate). A lawn is needed for the antibacterial test. There is more on this in the antibacterial and antifungal investigation below.
[bio33-s2.5.7] Diagram: a Petri dish after inoculation, shown upside down in the incubator. Labels: base labelled with name, date and treatment, lid held with two short strips of tape. Beside it, a flamed inoculating loop held in a Bunsen flame.
[bio33-s2.6.b] All six factor investigations below follow one plan: change one thing, keep everything else the same, and compare against controls. Learn this plan once and you can design any of them.
[bio33-s2.6.0] Aim: to investigate how a named factor affects the growth of a named microorganism. Each investigation changes one factor, the independent variable, and measures growth after a fixed time; the dependent variable is colony count, colony diameter, percentage cover or turbidity.
[bio33-s2.6.1] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the same microorganism, culture and volume of inoculum (the amount of culture added) on every plate; the same medium and volume of agar; and the same incubation time.
[bio33-s2.6.2] Also controlled, except when it is the factor tested: the same temperature, pH, nutrient level and water availability, and the same method of measuring.
[bio33-s2.6.3] Uninoculated control: an uninoculated plate (sterile medium only) is incubated with the others. No growth on it shows that the medium and technique were sterile, so any growth on the test plates came from the inoculum.
[bio33-s2.6.4] A reference plate with normal conditions (e.g. normal pH, no added salt, or a water disc in the antibacterial test) is compared with the tested conditions, so you can tell what the factor changed.
[bio33-s2.6.5] Replicates: set up at least three plates for each value of the factor and calculate the mean, so that one contaminated or unusual plate does not mislead. Worked example: colony counts of 42, 38 and 46 at 25 °C give a mean of 126 ÷ 3 = 42 colonies.
[bio33-s2.6.6] Record results in a table: value of the factor, growth on each replicate, and the mean. Plot a bar chart for categories or a line graph for a continuous factor, with mean growth on the y-axis.
[bio33-s2.6.7] Conclusion: describe the trend in the mean results and say whether they support the hypothesis. Explain the results using the biology given for each factor, and compare them with secondary data.
[bio33-s2.7.b] Temperature changes how fast the chemical reactions inside cells run. This investigation compares growth at several temperatures, and tests high temperatures by heating the culture before plating.
[bio33-s2.7.0] Hypothesis: “Growth increases with temperature up to an optimum, then decreases.” The optimum is the temperature at which growth is fastest.
[bio33-s2.7.1] Independent variable: temperature, i.e. about 4 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C and 25 °C, plus 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C for the heated cultures.
[bio33-s2.7.2] Step 1: inoculate identical plates with the same volume of the same culture, e.g. yeast.
[bio33-s2.7.3] Step 2: incubate sets of three at about 4 °C (refrigerator), 15 °C, 20 °C (room) and 25 °C (incubator) for the same time, e.g. 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.7.4] Step 3: count the colonies or measure colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.7.5] Step 4: to test high temperatures safely, heat tubes of the culture in water baths at 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C (or boil one) for 10 minutes before plating them. Heating the culture first avoids incubating dangerously warm plates.
[bio33-s2.7.6] Step 5: incubate all the plates at 25 °C, so that the only difference between them is the heat treatment.
[bio33-s2.8.b] The results fit a pattern: slow growth when cold, faster when warmer, and death when too hot. The explanation comes from enzymes, the proteins that control the reactions in cells.
[bio33-s2.8.0] Expected result: little or no growth at 4 °C. Growth increases with temperature over the range tested. Cultures heated to about 60 °C or more grow poorly or not at all.
[bio33-s2.8.1] Explanation: growth depends on enzyme-controlled reactions, and at low temperatures molecules move slowly, so reactions and growth are slow.
[bio33-s2.8.2] Cold does not kill the cells: growth resumes when they are warmed, which is why refrigerated food still goes off slowly and frozen food starts to go off when thawed.
[bio33-s2.8.3] Above the optimum, enzymes and other proteins are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working), so cells die. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio33-s2.8.4] Bacterial endospores survive higher temperatures than active cells, so heating that kills the active cells may leave endospores alive.
[bio33-s2.9.b] pH measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is (7 is neutral). This investigation grows bacteria on agar made up at different pH values to find the range they prefer.
[bio33-s2.9.0] Hypothesis: “Bacteria grow best at about neutral pH and poorly in strongly acidic or alkaline conditions.”
[bio33-s2.9.1] Independent variable: the pH of the agar or broth, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11.
[bio33-s2.9.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar (or broth) at different pH values, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11, by adding buffer solutions (which hold the pH steady) or dilute acid or alkali, checked with a pH meter, before pouring.
[bio33-s2.9.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of culture.
[bio33-s2.9.4] Step 3: incubate all at the same temperature for the same time.
[bio33-s2.9.5] Step 4: measure growth.
[bio33-s2.10.b] Bacteria and fungi prefer different pH ranges, and the reason lies in the proteins that run the cell. The same effect is used to preserve food.
[bio33-s2.10.0] Expected result: most bacteria grow best near neutral pH (about pH 6–8) and little in strongly acidic conditions.
[bio33-s2.10.1] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds tolerate, and often grow best in, slightly acidic conditions (about pH 5–6). Repeating the investigation with a fungus and a bacterium shows this difference.
[bio33-s2.10.2] Explanation: pH outside the optimum changes the charges on enzymes and other proteins, so their shape changes and they work slowly or are denatured.
[bio33-s2.10.3] Acid conditions are used to preserve food, e.g. pickling in vinegar, because few microorganisms grow well at low pH.
[bio33-s2.10.4] Yogurt keeps longer because the lactic acid made by its bacteria lowers the pH.
[bio33-s2.11.b] Nutrients are the food a microorganism uses for energy and to build new cells. This investigation compares media with and without nutrients, or with increasing amounts of sugar.
[bio33-s2.11.0] Hypothesis: “Microorganisms grow more when more nutrients are available, until another factor limits growth.”
[bio33-s2.11.1] Independent variable: the nutrient content of the medium, e.g. plain agar compared with nutrient agar, or glucose at 0%, 1%, 2% and 5% for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.2] Step 1: compare plain agar (agar and water only, no nutrients) with nutrient agar; or use broths (or agar) with increasing concentrations of glucose, e.g. 0%, 1%, 2%, 5%, for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of yeast culture.
[bio33-s2.11.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together at the same temperature.
[bio33-s2.11.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or percentage cover.
[bio33-s2.12.b] More food means more growth, but only up to a point. The results also show a link forward to the solute investigation, because very high sugar is harmful.
[bio33-s2.12.0] Expected result: little or no growth on plain agar, because there is nothing for the cells to use.
[bio33-s2.12.1] Growth increases with nutrient concentration and then levels off, as another factor, such as space or the build-up of wastes, becomes limiting (the one that stops growth rising further).
[bio33-s2.12.2] At very high sugar concentrations, growth falls again. There is more on this in Investigation: effect of external solute concentration.
[bio33-s2.12.3] Explanation: nutrients supply the energy, released by respiration of sugars, and the raw materials for new cells, i.e. sugars, amino acids, minerals such as nitrate and phosphate, and vitamins.
[bio33-s2.12.4] Variation: starch agar shows that only microorganisms that secrete amylase (the enzyme that digests starch) can use starch.
[bio33-s2.12.5] Flooding the plate with iodine after incubation leaves a clear zone around such colonies, where the starch has been digested. The rest of the plate turns dark because iodine stains starch.
[bio33-s2.13.b] This investigation uses bread and mould instead of agar plates. It tests whether mould needs moisture by comparing dried bread with moistened bread.
[bio33-s2.13.0] Hypothesis: “Moulds grow only on food that is moist.”
[bio33-s2.13.1] Independent variable: the water content of the bread, dried compared with moistened.
[bio33-s2.13.2] Step 1: take slices of the same bread (without preservatives, which would inhibit growth); dry some fully in an oven at a low temperature and moisten others with the same volume of sterile water.
[bio33-s2.13.3] Step 2: expose each slice to the air of the same room for the same time, e.g. 30 minutes, so that mould spores land on it.
[bio33-s2.13.4] Step 3: seal each in a clear plastic bag or sealed dish and label it.
[bio33-s2.13.5] Step 4: keep all in the same warm, dark place, about 25 °C, for 5–7 days.
[bio33-s2.13.6] Step 5: estimate the percentage cover of mould on each, using a transparent grid of squares held over the bag, e.g. 37 of 100 squares covered = 37%.
[bio33-s2.13.7] Do not open the bags, because the spores released could be breathed in.
[bio33-s2.14.b] Water is essential for life, and microorganisms are no exception. The result explains why drying is an old way of keeping food.
[bio33-s2.14.0] Expected result: mould grows on the moist bread and not, or very little, on the dried bread.
[bio33-s2.14.1] Explanation: microorganisms need water because their enzymes work only in solution, nutrients are absorbed in solution after extracellular digestion (digestion outside the cell), and water is needed for cells to grow and divide.
[bio33-s2.14.2] Dried foods, e.g. dried fruit, pasta, powdered milk and cereals, keep for a long time for this reason.
[bio33-s2.14.3] Safety: mould spores can trigger allergies and asthma, so keep the bags sealed throughout and dispose of them unopened.
[bio33-s2.15.b] A solute is something dissolved in water, such as salt or sugar. This investigation adds increasing amounts of solute to the agar to see what happens to growth.
[bio33-s2.15.0] Hypothesis: “Growth decreases as the concentration of salt (or sugar) outside the cells increases.”
[bio33-s2.15.1] Independent variable: the concentration of sodium chloride (salt), e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10% or 20%, or of sucrose (sugar).
[bio33-s2.15.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar containing increasing concentrations of sodium chloride (e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%) or of sucrose.
[bio33-s2.15.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of bacterial culture.
[bio33-s2.15.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together for the same time.
[bio33-s2.15.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.16.b] Salt and sugar slow growth because of what they do to water movement across the cell surface. This is the basis of salting and sugaring food.
[bio33-s2.16.0] Expected result: growth decreases as the solute concentration rises, with little or no growth at the highest concentrations.
[bio33-s2.16.1] Explanation: when the solution outside a cell has a higher solute concentration, and so lower water concentration, than the cytoplasm, water leaves the cell by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio33-s2.16.2] The cell then loses water, its enzymes cannot work, and it stops growing or dies.
[bio33-s2.16.3] In a very dilute solution water enters the cell, but the bacterial or fungal cell wall stops the cell bursting.
[bio33-s2.16.4] This is the basis of preserving food with salt (salted fish and bacon) and sugar (jam). There is more on this in Osmosis in food preservation and plant health.
[bio33-s2.17.b] Some chemicals stop microorganisms growing. Knowing which type acts on which group, and what the investigation sets out to show, comes before the method.
[bio33-s2.17.0] Antibacterial chemicals kill bacteria or stop them growing: antibiotics (e.g. penicillin, streptomycin), antiseptics (used on living tissue, e.g. on skin) and disinfectants (used on surfaces and objects).
[bio33-s2.17.1] Antifungal chemicals, e.g. the antifungal creams used for athlete's foot, or fungicides, act on fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.2] Antibiotics do not affect fungi, and antifungals generally do not affect bacteria, because the two groups have different structures. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.3] Hypothesis: “Different antiseptics (or antibiotics) inhibit the growth of a bacterium to different extents.”
[bio33-s2.17.4] Independent variable: the antibacterial or antifungal chemical on each disc; dependent variable: the diameter of the clear zone around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.b] In the disc diffusion method, paper discs soaked in chemicals are placed on a lawn of bacteria. Wherever the chemical stops growth, a clear zone appears around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.0] Step 1: spread a set volume of the culture evenly over a nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader, to make a lawn.
[bio33-s2.18.1] Step 2: using sterile forceps (flamed and cooled), dip paper discs of the same size (e.g. 6 mm, cut with a hole punch and sterilised) in each chemical for the same time, drain them, and place them on the lawn, well spaced.
[bio33-s2.18.2] Step 3: include a control disc dipped in sterile distilled water. Ready-made antibiotic discs, or a multi-disc with several antibiotics on one ring, can be used instead.
[bio33-s2.18.3] Step 4: tape and label the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.4] Step 5: incubate upside down at about 25 °C for 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.18.5] Step 6: measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc with a ruler on the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.6] For an antifungal chemical, make a lawn of yeast on malt agar and use discs of the antifungal, with a water control and, to show the difference in antibacterial and antifungal action, an antibiotic disc.
[bio33-s2.18.7] Diagram: a lawn plate with four discs. Labels: each disc's chemical, clear zones of inhibition of different sizes, and no zone around the water control.
[bio33-s2.19.b] The clear zones are the result. Their size tells you how well each chemical works, and the way they form explains why.
[bio33-s2.19.0] Expected result: a clear zone, the zone of inhibition, forms around discs whose chemical stops the bacterium growing.
[bio33-s2.19.1] There is no clear zone around the water control, which shows that the disc and the water alone do not stop growth.
[bio33-s2.19.2] The larger the zone, the more effective the chemical is against that organism at that concentration. In other words, the bacterium is more sensitive to it.
[bio33-s2.19.3] On the yeast lawn, the antifungal gives a clear zone and the antibiotic gives none.
[bio33-s2.19.4] The chemical diffuses outwards from the disc into the agar, so its concentration falls with distance; bacteria cannot grow where the concentration is high enough to kill or inhibit them.
[bio33-s2.19.5] A zone's size also depends on how fast the chemical diffuses, so it is a guide to strength, not an exact measure.
[bio33-s2.19.6] A bacterium that grows right up to an antibiotic disc is resistant to that antibiotic.
[bio33-s2.19.7] In hospitals the same test is used to find which antibiotic will work against the bacterium from a patient. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s2.20.b] Comparing zone areas, not just diameters, is fairer. Here is the calculation for a clear zone of diameter 18 mm around a 6 mm disc.
[bio33-s2.20.0] Radius = 9 mm; area of the zone = π r² = 3.14 × 9 × 9 ≈ 254 mm².
[bio33-s2.20.1] Subtracting the disc (radius 3 mm, area ≈ 28 mm²) gives a clear area of about 226 mm², which is the area where bacteria were actually stopped from growing.
[bio33-s2.20.2] Areas let zones be compared fairly, because doubling the diameter quadruples the area.
[bio33-s2.21.b] Your own results are primary data. A good conclusion also checks them against data collected by other people.
[bio33-s2.21.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, e.g. other class groups, textbooks, published research or food-safety guidance.
[bio33-s2.21.1] Conclusions must be supported by both primary and secondary data: compare your own results with the class's pooled results and with published findings.
[bio33-s2.21.2] Published findings include that most human pathogens grow best near body temperature and neutral pH, and that most bacteria grow little below refrigerator temperature.
[bio33-s2.22.b] Each factor you investigated is used to keep food safe. Preserving food means stopping microorganisms growing, either by killing them or by making conditions unsuitable.
[bio33-s2.22.0] Temperature: refrigeration and freezing slow or stop growth but do not kill microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.1] Heat: pasteurisation, heating milk briefly to a temperature below boiling and then cooling it quickly, kills most microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.2] Sterilisation: canning and ultra-heat treatment (UHT) heat food enough to sterilise it, and sealing keeps microorganisms out.
[bio33-s2.22.3] Water: drying removes water, which microorganisms need for their enzymes to work.
[bio33-s2.22.4] Solute: salting and sugaring use high solute concentration, which draws water out of microbial cells.
[bio33-s2.22.5] pH: pickling uses low pH.
[bio33-s2.23.b] The remaining factors give further preservation methods and wider uses in hygiene and in industry.
[bio33-s2.23.0] Chemicals: chemical preservatives inhibit growth.
[bio33-s2.23.1] Oxygen: vacuum packing removes oxygen, slowing aerobic microorganisms (those that need oxygen to grow).
[bio33-s2.23.2] Health and hygiene: antiseptics on wounds and skin, disinfectants on surfaces, and aseptic technique in surgery. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s2.23.3] Industry: bioreactors (vessels in which microorganisms are grown on a large scale) are kept at the optimum conditions found by such investigations to maximise growth.
[bio33-s2.24.b] Every fault below can make results unreliable. Each has a simple fix, so learn each error together with its fix.
[bio33-s2.24.0] Contamination by airborne or skin microorganisms gives colonies of a different appearance; check with the uninoculated control, and repeat with better aseptic technique.
[bio33-s2.24.1] Unequal inoculum, i.e. different volumes or numbers of cells, on different plates; use a measured volume from a well-mixed culture.
[bio33-s2.24.2] Colonies that grow together cannot be counted; dilute the culture so that colonies are separate, or measure percentage cover instead.
[bio33-s2.24.3] Agar or broth that is not at the intended pH or concentration; check with a pH meter and measure accurately.
[bio33-s2.24.4] Temperature not constant, e.g. the “room temperature” plates are near a radiator.
[bio33-s2.24.5] Discs not equally soaked, or chemicals not of known concentration; soak all discs for the same time.
[bio33-s2.24.6] Too few replicates; use at least three and calculate means.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

CANDIDATES bio8-s7: Investigation: food tests
[bio8-s7.0.b] Food tests are a fair, planned investigation, not just a set of recipes. Before testing anything you decide what you are trying to find out, what you predict, and what you will change, measure and keep the same.
[bio8-s7.0.0] Aim: to find out which nutrients (starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid) are present in a range of food samples. The investigation is qualitative, which means you record whether a nutrient is present or absent, from a colour change or other observation, not how much of it there is.
[bio8-s7.0.1] Hypothesis, e.g.: “Potato contains starch but not lipid; milk contains reducing sugar (lactose), protein and lipid.” Students predict the result for each food from what the food is, so the results can later support or contradict the prediction.
[bio8-s7.0.2] Independent variable (the one you change): the food sample tested. Dependent variable (the one you observe): the result of each test, the colour or observation, recorded as present or absent.
[bio8-s7.0.3] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the volume of food solution and of reagent, the concentration of the reagents, the water-bath temperature and heating time for Benedict's, and clean apparatus for every test to avoid carry-over from one sample to the next.
[bio8-s7.0.4] Every test needs two controls. A negative control uses distilled water in place of the food and shows the colour when the nutrient is absent.
[bio8-s7.0.5] A positive control uses a known pure sample, such as starch solution, glucose solution, egg-white (albumen) solution or vegetable oil, and shows the colour when the nutrient is present.
[bio8-s7.0.6] Results for the foods are judged against these controls, so you can tell a real colour change from the reagent's starting colour, and know the test itself is working.
[bio8-s7.1.b] Choose foods that you expect to be rich in each nutrient, and gather the equipment and reagents (the chemicals that react with a nutrient to give a visible result) before you start.
[bio8-s7.1.0] Example food samples: potato, bread, rice or flour (starch); glucose, honey, grapes, fruit juice and milk (reducing sugar); egg white, milk, meat or cheese (protein); butter, vegetable oil, nuts, crisps and cheese (lipid); and table sugar, which gives a negative Benedict's result.
[bio8-s7.1.1] Equipment: test tubes and rack, test-tube holder, droppers, graduated pipettes or syringes (to measure equal volumes), spotting tile, mortar and pestle or knife and chopping board, beaker of distilled water, filter funnel and filter paper.
[bio8-s7.1.2] Also needed: a hot water bath (a beaker of near-boiling water from a kettle, or an electric water bath) with a thermometer, brown paper, labels, safety glasses and gloves.
[bio8-s7.1.3] Reagents: iodine solution (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide), Benedict's solution, biuret reagent (or sodium hydroxide solution and dilute copper sulfate solution) and Sudan III stain.
[bio8-s7.2.b] Most tests work on a liquid, so solid foods must first be turned into a food solution. This preparation is common to all the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.0] 1. Crush or grind a solid food with a little distilled water using a mortar and pestle. Crushing breaks open the cells so the nutrients can mix into the water.
[bio8-s7.2.1] 2. Add more water, stir, and filter or let the solid settle, then use the liquid food solution for the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.2] 3. Test liquid foods such as milk and juice directly, or dilute them.
[bio8-s7.2.3] 4. Label each tube with the food and the test, so the results cannot be mixed up.
[bio8-s7.3.b] Starch is the carbohydrate plants store, and iodine solution is the quickest way to detect it. It needs no heating and gives a result at once.
[bio8-s7.3.0] Method, step 1: place a few drops of food solution, or a small piece of solid food, on a white spotting tile, or place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. The white tile makes the colour easy to see.
[bio8-s7.3.1] Step 2: add a few drops of iodine solution. Step 3: observe the colour at once. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.3.2] Step 4: repeat with water as the negative control and starch solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.3.3] Result: iodine solution is yellow-brown (orange-brown) at the start. It turns blue-black if starch is present and stays yellow-brown if starch is absent.
[bio8-s7.4.b] Some sugars can react chemically with Benedict's solution and others cannot. This test detects the ones that can, and it is the one food test that needs heat.
[bio8-s7.4.0] A reducing sugar is one that can reduce (give electrons to) the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution. Glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose and lactose are reducing sugars; sucrose is not.
[bio8-s7.4.1] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add an equal volume, 2 cm³, of Benedict's solution and mix; the mixture is blue.
[bio8-s7.4.2] Step 3: place the tube in a hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) for about 5 minutes. Never heat the tube directly in a Bunsen flame, because the liquid can spurt out.
[bio8-s7.4.3] Step 4: observe the colour. Step 5: run water as the negative control and glucose solution as the positive control, in the same water bath at the same time so the conditions are identical.
[bio8-s7.4.4] Result: if a reducing sugar is present, the blue solution turns green, then yellow, then orange, and finally a brick-red precipitate (solid particles) forms. It stays blue if no reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.5] The reducing sugar reduces blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, the brick-red precipitate. The colour reached gives a rough idea of how much sugar is present: green for a little, brick red for a lot. There is more on this in Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively.
[bio8-s7.4.6] Fehling's test is an alternative: equal volumes of Fehling's solutions A and B are mixed with the sample and heated in a water bath. A blue mixture turns to a brick-red precipitate if a reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.7] Extension: sucrose stays blue. To show it is made of reducing sugars, boil a sucrose solution with a few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid to hydrolyse it to glucose and fructose. Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate until fizzing stops, then do the Benedict's test: it now turns brick red.
[bio8-s7.5.b] Biuret detects the bonds that join amino acids together in a protein. Like iodine, it works at room temperature with no heating.
[bio8-s7.5.0] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add 2 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution and mix (or add biuret reagent, which already contains it).
[bio8-s7.5.1] Step 3: add a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution, one drop at a time, shaking gently. Step 4: leave for a few minutes and observe. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.5.2] Step 5: run water as the negative control and egg-white solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.5.3] Result: the blue colour turns purple (violet or lilac) if protein is present, and stays blue if protein is absent. The copper ions react with the peptide bonds (the links between amino acids) in the protein, so the test detects peptide bonds, not single amino acids.
[bio8-s7.6.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do not dissolve in water, and two simple tests use this. They are alternatives, so you can use either one.
[bio8-s7.6.0] Grease-spot (brown paper) test, method: rub a small amount of food on a piece of brown paper, or place a drop of liquid food on it. Place a drop of water on another part of the paper as a control.
[bio8-s7.6.1] Leave the paper to dry, or warm it gently away from flames, then hold it up to the light.
[bio8-s7.6.2] Grease-spot result: a translucent (see-through), greasy spot that does not disappear when the paper dries shows that lipid is present. The water spot also looks translucent at first but disappears as it dries, which is why the paper must dry before the result is judged.
[bio8-s7.6.3] Sudan III test, method: place 2 cm³ of food solution, or a little crushed food in 2 cm³ of water, in a test tube. Add a few drops of Sudan III, a red dye that dissolves in lipid but not in water.
[bio8-s7.6.4] Shake gently and leave to stand for a minute. Run water as the negative control and vegetable oil in water as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.6.5] Sudan III result: if lipid is present, a red-stained oily layer forms on top of the water, because lipid is less dense than water and does not mix with it. With no lipid, the dye spreads through the water or settles as red specks, with no red oily layer.
[bio8-s7.7.b] Several reagents here are corrosive, harmful or flammable, and the Benedict's test uses near-boiling water. Safe practice is part of any investigation.
[bio8-s7.7.0] Wear safety glasses throughout. Sodium hydroxide (biuret test) is corrosive and especially harmful to the eyes. Benedict's and copper sulfate solutions are harmful if swallowed or splashed. Wash any splash off the skin with plenty of water.
[bio8-s7.7.1] Heat Benedict's tests only in a water bath, never directly over a flame, because the liquid can boil suddenly and spurt out of the tube. Use a test-tube holder, point the tube away from people, and take care with near-boiling water.
[bio8-s7.7.2] Sudan III stain is usually dissolved in ethanol, an alcohol that is highly flammable, so keep it away from flames. Do not warm brown paper near a flame.
[bio8-s7.7.3] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes; wear gloves if possible.
[bio8-s7.7.4] Never taste or eat food in the laboratory. Be aware of food allergies (e.g. to nuts) among the class. Clean up spills and wash hands after the practical.
[bio8-s7.8.b] Your observations are the primary data (results you collected yourself). A clear table lets you compare every food with the controls and draw a conclusion from the evidence.
[bio8-s7.8.0] Record primary data in a results table: one row per food and one column per test, recording the colour observed and whether the nutrient is present or absent (or + or −). Include the control rows.
[bio8-s7.8.1] Expected results: potato shows starch; glucose, honey and grapes show reducing sugar; table sugar is Benedict's negative, as sucrose is non-reducing; egg white shows protein; vegetable oil and butter show lipid; milk shows reducing sugar, protein and lipid; distilled water is negative for all tests.
[bio8-s7.8.2] Conclusion: state which nutrients each food contains, supported by the results compared with the controls, and say whether this supports the hypothesis. The tests show presence only, not the amount.
[bio8-s7.9.b] Food tests are simple, so small mistakes easily give a wrong result. Knowing the common errors, and how to reduce them, makes the conclusion more reliable.
[bio8-s7.9.0] Colour judgements are subjective, so different people may see different shades. Compare each tube with the controls against a white background, and have two people check the result.
[bio8-s7.9.1] Coloured foods, such as dark fruit juice, can mask a colour change. Dilute or filter them, or use a control of the food without the reagent.
[bio8-s7.9.2] Contamination: reusing a dropper or test tube can carry a nutrient from one sample to another, giving a false positive.
[bio8-s7.9.3] A very small amount of nutrient may not give a visible change, which is a false negative. Some nutrients are inside cells, so the food must be well crushed.
[bio8-s7.9.4] Benedict's detects reducing sugars only, so a food rich in sucrose can give a negative result even though it contains sugar.
[bio8-s7.9.5] Repeat each test, e.g. three times, to check that the results are repeatable.
