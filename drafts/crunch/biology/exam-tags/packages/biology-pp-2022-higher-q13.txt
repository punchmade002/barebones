CANDIDATES bio10-s1: Nucleotides, DNA and RNA
[bio10-s1.0.b] Cells need a way to store instructions for building and running themselves. They use nucleic acids, long molecules made by joining many small units, and the unit that is repeated is the nucleotide. There are four kinds of nucleotide, which differ in their base.
[bio10-s1.0.0] Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are large molecules (polymers) built from many small units called nucleotides joined in a chain, much as beads are threaded on a string.
[bio10-s1.0.1] DNA is deoxyribonucleic acid; RNA is ribonucleic acid. Both contain the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus.
[bio10-s1.0.2] Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base (a nitrogen-containing molecule) attached to the sugar.
[bio10-s1.0.3] The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen atom than ribose, which is where its name comes from: deoxy means an oxygen has been removed.
[bio10-s1.0.4] Each nucleic acid has four bases. DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). RNA: adenine (A), uracil (U), cytosine (C) and guanine (G); uracil takes the place of thymine.
[bio10-s1.0.5] Adenine and guanine are larger, double-ringed bases, called purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are smaller, single-ringed bases, called pyrimidines.
[bio10-s1.0.6] A nucleotide is the building block of DNA and RNA, and its base is one letter of the genetic information. The order, or sequence, of bases along a strand carries the information, just as the order of letters makes a word.
[bio10-s1.0.7] Modified nucleotides also carry energy. There is more on this in ATP.
[bio10-s1.0.8] Diagram: one nucleotide drawn as a circle (phosphate) joined to a pentagon (sugar) joined to a rectangle (base), labelled phosphate, deoxyribose or ribose, and nitrogenous base.
[bio10-s1.1.b] One nucleotide holds one letter of information, so to store a whole message the cell must link many of them together. The way they link leaves a strong chain with the bases free to carry the code.
[bio10-s1.1.0] Nucleotides join by bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next. These bonds are made in condensation reactions, in which a molecule of water is removed for each bond.
[bio10-s1.1.1] The repeating sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate chain is the sugar–phosphate backbone. The bonds within it are strong covalent bonds, not hydrogen bonds.
[bio10-s1.1.2] The bases stick out from the backbone, one per sugar. The backbone is the same in every DNA molecule; only the sequence of bases differs, so the bases carry the information and the backbone gives strength and support.
[bio10-s1.2.b] DNA is not one chain but two, held together in a regular pattern. This shape explains how DNA can store information and be copied accurately.
[bio10-s1.2.0] DNA is double-stranded: two strands of nucleotides lie side by side, with the sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside and the bases pointing inwards, like the rails and rungs of a ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.1] The bases pair up: adenine always pairs with thymine (A–T) and cytosine always pairs with guanine (C–G). These are complementary base pairs.
[bio10-s1.2.2] Each pair is one purine with one pyrimidine, so every rung is the same width and the ladder stays an even shape.
[bio10-s1.2.3] The bases in a pair are held together by hydrogen bonds (weak attractions between molecules): two between A and T, and three between C and G.
[bio10-s1.2.4] Each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated, or unzipped, when DNA is copied or read. There are millions of hydrogen bonds, so the molecule as a whole is stable.
[bio10-s1.2.5] The two linked strands are twisted around each other into a spiral called a double helix, like a twisted ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.6] Because pairing is fixed, the two strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand decides the sequence of the other. For example, the strand ATGCCTAG pairs with TACGGATC.
[bio10-s1.2.7] Diagram: a short section of DNA drawn as a straight ladder of about four base pairs, labelled sugar (deoxyribose), phosphate, sugar–phosphate backbone, base pairs A–T and C–G, and hydrogen bonds; with a sketch of the twisted double helix.
[bio10-s1.3.b] Because A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G, the amounts of the bases in double-stranded DNA are linked. This lets you work out the percentage of every base from just one.
[bio10-s1.3.0] In double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of C equals the amount of G, because every A is paired with a T and every C with a G.
[bio10-s1.3.1] For example, if 20% of the bases in a DNA sample are adenine, then 20% are thymine. That leaves 60% shared equally between cytosine and guanine, so each is 30%.
[bio10-s1.4.b] The order of bases in DNA is the cell's set of instructions. DNA is kept in different places in different kinds of cell.
[bio10-s1.4.0] DNA stores the genetic information (genes) that controls the cell's activities by coding for proteins. There is more on genes and proteins in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio10-s1.4.1] DNA is copied before a cell divides, so each new cell receives the full set of information, which means that it is passed from parents to offspring.
[bio10-s1.4.2] In eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), DNA is found mostly in the nucleus, in the chromosomes, with small amounts in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio10-s1.4.3] In bacteria, DNA forms a loop in the cytoplasm and is also found in small rings called plasmids.
[bio10-s1.5.b] The structure of DNA was worked out by combining physical evidence with model building. It is a good example of how scientists build on each other's data.
[bio10-s1.5.0] The double-helix model was published by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953. It was based on X-ray diffraction images of DNA (patterns made when X-rays pass through DNA, which reveal its shape) made by Rosalind Franklin, working with Maurice Wilkins.
[bio10-s1.6.b] DNA stays safe in the nucleus, so the cell needs a working copy of its instructions to take out and use. That working copy is RNA, a similar molecule with a few key differences.
[bio10-s1.6.0] RNA is usually single-stranded: one chain of nucleotides with its own sugar–phosphate backbone. RNA molecules are much shorter than DNA molecules.
[bio10-s1.6.1] RNA nucleotides contain ribose instead of deoxyribose, and uracil instead of thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine (A–U) whenever RNA pairs with DNA or with another RNA.
[bio10-s1.6.2] A single strand can fold back on itself where its bases pair up, giving some RNA molecules a fixed shape.
[bio10-s1.6.3] RNA uses the information in DNA to make proteins, so it acts as the go-between for DNA and the protein-making machinery.
[bio10-s1.6.4] Three main types of RNA are named here, with no more detail. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries a copy of a gene from the nucleus to a ribosome (the structure that makes proteins).
[bio10-s1.6.5] Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids to the ribosome; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms part of the ribosome. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s1.6.6] RNA is made in the nucleus and works in the cytoplasm, so it carries information out to where proteins are made. It is found in the nucleus (including the nucleolus), the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.6.7] Diagram: a short single RNA strand of about four nucleotides, labelled ribose, phosphate, backbone and bases, including uracil.
[bio10-s1.7.b] Put side by side, DNA and RNA differ in structure, stability and job. The differences fit their roles: DNA is a long-term store and RNA is a short-lived working copy.
[bio10-s1.7.0] Strands: DNA is a double-stranded helix, which suits long-term storage; RNA is usually single-stranded.
[bio10-s1.7.1] Sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose; RNA contains ribose.
[bio10-s1.7.2] Bases: DNA has A, T, C and G; RNA has A, U, C and G, with uracil instead of thymine.
[bio10-s1.7.3] Pairing: A–T in DNA; A–U in RNA.
[bio10-s1.7.4] Size and life span: DNA is very long and stable and is kept for the life of the cell; RNA molecules are shorter and are broken down after use, so each copy is only a temporary message.
[bio10-s1.7.5] Location: DNA is mainly in the nucleus, with some in mitochondria and chloroplasts; RNA is in the nucleus, the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.7.6] Function: DNA stores the genetic information; RNA carries and uses that information to make proteins.

CANDIDATES bio11-s1: Chromosome structure and genes
[bio11-s1.0.b] A chromosome is DNA together with protein, and it carries genes in a fixed linear order. Knowing what it is made of, how it looks in a resting and a dividing cell, and how big it is explains why cells need to pack their DNA.
[bio11-s1.0.0] A chromosome is a single, very long molecule of DNA together with proteins. It is found in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell (a cell with a nucleus) and carries many genes in a fixed linear order along its length.
[bio11-s1.0.1] Chromatin is the name for the DNA and protein material of the chromosomes when the cell is not dividing. The chromosomes are then long, thin and uncoiled, spread through the nucleus, so they cannot be seen as separate threads.
[bio11-s1.0.2] Before a cell divides, the chromatin coils and folds up tightly (condenses), so each chromosome becomes short and thick and is visible under the light microscope when stained. In other words, chromatin and chromosomes are the same material in two states.
[bio11-s1.0.3] Human body cells are diploid (two sets of chromosomes), with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes (chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes) and one pair of sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio11-s1.0.4] Homologous chromosomes are the two chromosomes of a pair. They are the same length, have the centromere (the narrowed region of the chromosome) in the same position and carry the same genes at the same positions, with one chromosome of each pair coming from each parent.
[bio11-s1.0.5] The chromosome number is fixed for a species (46 in humans); a different number does not mean a more complex organism, so you cannot judge complexity by counting chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.0.6] The human genome (all the DNA in one set of 23 chromosomes) is about 3 billion base pairs. The DNA in one human body cell, stretched out, would be about 2 m long, yet it fits in a nucleus a few micrometres across, which is why DNA must be packed.
[bio11-s1.0.7] Prokaryotes (bacteria) differ: a bacterial cell has one circular chromosome in the cytoplasm, not wound around histones (the packing proteins of eukaryotes), plus plasmids (small extra rings of DNA).
[bio11-s1.1.b] Histones are the proteins that pack eukaryotic DNA into a small space. Without them, about 2 m of DNA per cell could not fit in the nucleus or be moved safely when the cell divides.
[bio11-s1.1.0] Histones are small proteins around which the DNA of a eukaryotic chromosome is wound. They are positively charged, while the phosphate groups of the DNA backbone are negatively charged, so DNA binds tightly to them.
[bio11-s1.1.1] The DNA wraps nearly twice around a core of eight histone proteins, forming a bead called a nucleosome. Nucleosomes are joined by short stretches of linker DNA, like beads on a string.
[bio11-s1.1.2] The string of nucleosomes coils and loops further, and before division it condenses into the visible chromosome.
[bio11-s1.1.3] Histones do three jobs: they pack a very long DNA molecule into a small nucleus, they protect the DNA and keep it untangled during division, and they help control which genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.4] DNA wound tightly around histones cannot be reached by the enzymes that read genes, so those genes are switched off; loosely packed regions are active, which means their genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.5] Chemical changes to histones are one of the epigenetic mechanisms (ways of switching genes on or off without changing the DNA sequence). There is more on this in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s1.1.6] Diagram: DNA double helix wound around histone cores to form nucleosomes, then the coiled chromatin fibre, then a condensed chromosome; label DNA, histone, nucleosome, chromatin and chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.b] Before a cell divides it copies its DNA, so each chromosome ends up as two joined copies. Chromatids and the centromere are the names for those copies and for the point that holds them together.
[bio11-s1.2.0] Before a cell divides, each chromosome's DNA is copied, a process called replication. The two identical copies stay joined together; each copy is a chromatid. There is more on this in DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle.
[bio11-s1.2.1] A replicated chromosome therefore consists of two identical sister chromatids, each containing one complete DNA molecule with the same genes and the same alleles (versions of a gene).
[bio11-s1.2.2] The centromere is the constricted (narrowed) region of a chromosome where the two sister chromatids are held together. Spindle fibres (protein threads that move chromosomes in division) attach at the centromere and pull the chromatids apart to opposite poles of the cell.
[bio11-s1.2.3] Once separated, each chromatid is a chromosome in its own right.
[bio11-s1.2.4] The centromere is in a fixed position for each chromosome, dividing it into two arms of equal or different length, so it is not always in the middle. Its position, together with length and banding pattern, is used to identify each chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.5] Before replication, a chromosome is one DNA molecule and one chromatid. After replication it is still one chromosome but has two chromatids, the X shape usually drawn; the two arms of the X are chromatids, not separate chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.2.6] Homologous chromosomes (a pair, one from each parent) may carry different alleles, whereas sister chromatids are identical copies of each other, so they always carry identical alleles.
[bio11-s1.2.7] Diagram: an unreplicated chromosome (single rod) beside a replicated chromosome (X shape); label chromatid, sister chromatids, centromere, arm, and a gene locus marked as a band at the same position on both chromatids. Optionally add a homologous pair of replicated chromosomes, same locus marked on each, labelled homologous chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.3.b] A gene is the unit of heredity, the piece of information that is passed on from cell to cell and parent to offspring. Genes sit at fixed places on chromosomes and, through the proteins they code for, control traits.
[bio11-s1.3.0] A gene is a section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a protein. This means a chromosome is not simply a string of genes: genes are sections of its DNA.
[bio11-s1.3.1] Each gene has a fixed position on a particular chromosome, called its locus (plural loci). The locus of a gene is the same in every member of a species.
[bio11-s1.3.2] A gene carries the information to make one protein, although some genes code instead for RNA molecules that are used directly, such as ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA. So not every gene codes for a protein.
[bio11-s1.3.3] Through the protein it codes for, a gene controls a trait (a characteristic of the organism). The gene is copied and passed to daughter cells in division and to offspring in gametes (sex cells).
[bio11-s1.3.4] Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes spread across the 23 kinds of chromosome; each chromosome carries hundreds to thousands of genes.
[bio11-s1.3.5] Because homologous chromosomes carry the same genes, a body cell has two copies of each autosomal gene. These may be the same or different versions, called alleles. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio11-s1.4.b] Most of the DNA in a human cell does not code for protein. Separating the small coding part from the large non-coding part shows that a chromosome is far more than a list of protein recipes.
[bio11-s1.4.0] Coding DNA is DNA whose base sequence is used to make a protein: the protein-coding parts of genes. Only about 1–2% of human DNA is coding DNA.
[bio11-s1.4.1] Non-coding DNA is all DNA that does not code for protein, about 98% of the human genome. It includes several kinds of sequence, listed in the next points.
[bio11-s1.4.2] Regulatory sequences beside genes act as switches, controlling when, where and how much a gene is expressed (used to make its product).
[bio11-s1.4.3] Genes for functional RNA (rRNA and tRNA) are non-coding DNA, as are introns, the non-coding sections within genes, which are copied into mRNA but removed before it is used to make protein.
[bio11-s1.4.4] Repeated sequences, such as the DNA at centromeres and at the ends of chromosomes (telomeres), protect the chromosome and help it divide correctly. Some non-coding DNA has a function that is still unknown.
[bio11-s1.4.5] Non-coding DNA was once called 'junk DNA', but much of it is now known to have functions, especially in regulating genes. Changes in regulatory DNA can change a trait without changing any protein.
[bio11-s1.4.6] Repeated non-coding sequences vary greatly in length between people, which is why they are used in DNA profiling (identifying individuals from their DNA).
[bio11-s1.4.7] Diagram: a stretch of a chromosome's DNA drawn as a line, with two genes shown as boxes (each with a regulatory region at one end and coding sections separated by an intron), and the DNA between genes labelled non-coding; label gene, coding DNA, non-coding DNA, regulatory sequence and intron.

CANDIDATES bio11-s2: Key concepts of information transfer
[bio11-s2.0.b] Inheritance starts with a few basic ideas: what counts as one kind of organism, what is passed on, and what carries it. These terms are used throughout genetics.
[bio11-s2.0.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed in natural conditions to produce fertile offspring (offspring that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, the mule, is sterile, so horses and donkeys are different species.
[bio11-s2.0.1] The species definition has limits: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually, because they never interbreed, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding. There is more on how new species form in Evidence for evolution.
[bio11-s2.0.2] Heredity is the passing of characteristics (traits), by means of genes, from parents to offspring, that is, from one generation to the next. A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for a trait.
[bio11-s2.0.3] Genetics is the study of heredity (how traits are passed on) and of variation (the differences between individuals).
[bio11-s2.0.4] A chromosome is the structure that carries genes from one generation to the next. Body cells carry chromosomes in homologous pairs (two matching chromosomes, one from each parent), and each gamete carries one chromosome from each pair.
[bio11-s2.1.b] Parents pass genes on through special sex cells. Following the chromosome number from parent to gametes to zygote shows how each generation gets its genes and why the number stays constant.
[bio11-s2.1.0] A gamete is a sex cell: a sperm or egg in animals, or in flowering plants the male gamete nucleus in the pollen grain and the egg cell in the ovule.
[bio11-s2.1.1] Gametes are haploid, with one set of chromosomes (one from each pair), so a human gamete has 23 chromosomes. They are made by meiosis. Gametes must be haploid because fertilisation adds the two sets together, so otherwise the chromosome number would double every generation.
[bio11-s2.1.2] Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of two gametes, usually from two parents. The offspring receive half their chromosomes from each parent, so they are genetically different from both parents and from each other. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio11-s2.1.3] Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no gametes, so it produces genetically identical offspring, called clones.
[bio11-s2.1.4] Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes), the first cell of the new organism. It restores the diploid number: in humans 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.5] The zygote divides by mitosis, so every body cell carries the same set of chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.6] Diagram: life-cycle flow: parent (2n = 46) → meiosis → gametes (n = 23, sperm and egg) → fertilisation → zygote (2n = 46) → mitosis → offspring; label haploid, diploid, meiosis, fertilisation and mitosis.
[bio11-s2.2.b] A gene is a section of DNA for one trait, such as seed shape. Genes come in slightly different versions, and these versions, called alleles, are what make individuals differ.
[bio11-s2.2.0] An allele is one of the alternative forms of a gene, found at the same locus (position) on homologous chromosomes. Alleles differ slightly in base sequence, as they arise by mutation, so they may produce different versions of the trait.
[bio11-s2.2.1] In peas, the gene for seed shape has an allele for round seeds (R) and one for wrinkled seeds (r).
[bio11-s2.2.2] A diploid organism has two alleles for each autosomal gene (a gene on a non-sex chromosome), one on each homologous chromosome, one inherited from each parent.
[bio11-s2.2.3] A population may have more than two alleles of a gene, but each individual carries only two, because it has only two homologous chromosomes. The human ABO blood-group gene has three alleles (A, B and O) in the population.
[bio11-s2.2.4] Homozygous means having two identical alleles of a gene (RR or rr). A homozygous organism is also called pure-breeding, because it passes on the same allele in every gamete.
[bio11-s2.2.5] Heterozygous means having two different alleles of a gene (Rr). A heterozygous organism is also called a hybrid for that gene.
[bio11-s2.2.6] Diagram: a pair of homologous chromosomes with one gene locus marked, one chromosome carrying R and the other r (heterozygous); a second pair carrying r and r (homozygous); label homologous chromosomes, locus and allele.
[bio11-s2.3.b] Two terms separate what an organism's genes are from what it looks like or does. They are not always the same, because the environment and dominance both play a part.
[bio11-s2.3.0] Genotype is the genetic make-up of an organism: the alleles it has for a particular trait, written with letters, such as RR, Rr or rr.
[bio11-s2.3.1] Phenotype is the observable (physical, chemical or behavioural) characteristics of an organism that result from its genotype, such as round or wrinkled seeds, or blood group A.
[bio11-s2.3.2] Phenotype = genotype + environment. The genotype sets the possible range, and the environment affects how it is expressed.
[bio11-s2.3.3] Identical twins have the same genotype but may differ in mass because of diet and exercise, so the same genes can give different phenotypes.
[bio11-s2.3.4] Hydrangea plants of the same variety have blue flowers in acid soil and pink flowers in alkaline soil, so soil pH changes the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.3.5] A plant with the genes for chlorophyll is pale yellow if grown in the dark, because the environment (no light) stops the pigment forming.
[bio11-s2.3.6] Different genotypes can give the same phenotype: RR and Rr are both round, because one allele is dominant. So the phenotype does not always reveal the genotype. There is more on how to find the genotype (the test cross) in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s2.4.b] When an organism is heterozygous, the two alleles do not always show equally. Dominant and recessive describe which allele is seen in the phenotype and which is hidden.
[bio11-s2.4.0] A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote, masking the effect of the other allele. It is written with a capital letter (R), and a dominant trait shows in both RR and Rr.
[bio11-s2.4.1] A recessive allele is expressed in the phenotype only when no dominant allele is present, that is, only in the homozygous condition (rr). It is written with the lower-case letter (r).
[bio11-s2.4.2] In a heterozygote, a recessive allele is present but hidden, and it can still be passed on to offspring. It does not disappear and is not weaker.
[bio11-s2.4.3] Often the dominant allele codes for a working protein and the recessive allele for a non-working one. One working copy makes enough protein, so Rr looks the same as RR.
[bio11-s2.4.4] In peas, R codes for a working starch-branching enzyme; rr seeds make less starch and wrinkle. There is more on this chain in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio11-s2.4.5] Dominant does not mean common or better. The allele causing Huntington's disease is dominant but rare, and harmful.
[bio11-s2.4.6] Cystic fibrosis and albinism are caused by recessive alleles, so two carriers (heterozygotes) can have an affected child.
[bio11-s2.4.7] A carrier is a heterozygous individual who carries a recessive allele for a condition but does not show it.
[bio11-s2.5.b] Having a gene is not the same as using it. Gene expression is how a cell turns the information in a gene into something that affects the organism.
[bio11-s2.5.0] Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make its product (a protein or a functional RNA), so that the gene affects the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.5.1] A gene that is being used is switched on (expressed); one that is not is switched off.
[bio11-s2.5.2] Every body cell has the same genes, but each cell type expresses only some of them, which is why cells become specialised.
[bio11-s2.5.3] Expression is controlled by regulatory DNA (stretches of DNA that switch genes on or off), by signals such as hormones, and by the environment: some genes are switched on only in response to a stimulus.
[bio11-s2.5.4] There is more on how the environment and behaviour change expression in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s2.6.b] Four more terms extend the basic vocabulary. Each has a short definition and, where there is one, a named example.
[bio11-s2.6.0] Epigenetic expression is a change in gene expression that is not caused by a change in the DNA base sequence, but by chemical tags on the DNA or on the histones (the proteins DNA is wound around) that switch genes on or off.
[bio11-s2.6.1] Epigenetic tags can be caused by the environment and behaviour, can be passed on when cells divide, and some are reversible.
[bio11-s2.6.2] Incomplete dominance means neither allele is dominant over the other, so the heterozygote has a phenotype intermediate between the two homozygotes. For example, red-flowered and white-flowered snapdragons give pink-flowered heterozygotes. This contrasts with complete dominance, where the heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote.
[bio11-s2.6.3] Linkage: genes located on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together, because they do not separate into different gametes. Linked genes do not assort independently.
[bio11-s2.6.4] Sex-linkage: a gene located on a sex chromosome (in humans almost always the X chromosome) is sex-linked, so the trait's inheritance pattern differs between males and females. Examples are red–green colour blindness and haemophilia, which are much more common in males.

CANDIDATES bio11-s8: Dihybrid crosses
[bio11-s8.0.b] A monohybrid cross follows one gene. A dihybrid cross follows two genes at once, which is how you predict offspring when two traits are inherited together.
[bio11-s8.0.0] A dihybrid cross follows the inheritance of two different traits (two genes) at the same time, e.g. seed shape and seed colour in peas.
[bio11-s8.0.1] “Unlinked” means the two genes are on different chromosomes, so they assort independently (are sorted into gametes without affecting each other) and every combination of their alleles is equally likely in the gametes. There is more on genes on the same chromosome in Linkage.
[bio11-s8.0.2] Key for the worked examples: R = round seed, r = wrinkled; Y = yellow seed, y = green. Capital letters are dominant alleles (the version that shows when present), lower case are recessive.
[bio11-s8.0.3] A genotype always has two letters for each gene (four letters in all), written with the two alleles of one gene together and the dominant first: RrYy, not RYry. Keeping each gene's pair together stops you mixing the genes up.
[bio11-s8.1.b] Before you can fill in a Punnett square you need the gametes (sex cells) each parent can make. This is a short method you can use on any two-gene genotype.
[bio11-s8.1.0] Each gamete gets one allele of each gene, so every gamete has two letters, one for each gene; never two of the same gene: RY, not Rr.
[bio11-s8.1.1] Method: pair each allele of the first gene with each allele of the second gene.
[bio11-s8.1.2] For RrYy: R with Y, R with y, r with Y, r with y, giving RY, Ry, rY, ry, in equal numbers. This equal mixing is the Law of Independent Assortment: the alleles of one gene are sorted into gametes independently of the other gene.
[bio11-s8.1.3] The number of gamete types depends on how many genes are heterozygous (have two different alleles): RRYY gives one type (RY); RRYy gives two (RY, Ry); Rryy gives two (Ry, ry).
[bio11-s8.1.4] RrYy, heterozygous for both genes, gives four (RY, Ry, rY, ry); rryy gives one (ry). Write each different gamete once, however many times it could be made.
[bio11-s8.2.b] The first step in Mendel's style of cross is to breed two pure lines together. The offspring, the F1 (first filial) generation, are all identical, which sets up the F2.
[bio11-s8.2.0] Parents' phenotypes (the visible traits): round yellow × wrinkled green (both true-breeding, meaning homozygous, so they always pass on the same alleles).
[bio11-s8.2.1] Parents' genotypes: RRYY × rryy, so each parent is homozygous for both genes.
[bio11-s8.2.2] Gametes: RY × ry, because each parent has only one type of gamete.
[bio11-s8.2.3] F1 genotype: all RrYy, as every offspring gets one dominant and one recessive allele of each gene.
[bio11-s8.2.4] F1 phenotype: all round yellow (both dominant traits), because the dominant alleles hide the recessive ones.
[bio11-s8.3.b] Crossing two F1 plants is the core dihybrid cross. Both parents are heterozygous for both genes, so each makes four types of gamete and the Punnett square (a grid showing every possible gamete pairing) is 4 × 4.
[bio11-s8.3.0] Parents (F1): round yellow × round yellow. Genotypes: RrYy × RrYy, so both are heterozygous for both genes.
[bio11-s8.3.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × RY, Ry, rY, ry.
[bio11-s8.3.2] Punnett square: 4 × 4 = 16 boxes, each a 1/16 chance, because every gamete pairing is equally likely.
[bio11-s8.3.3] Punnett square, row by row (gametes RY, Ry, rY, ry across the top and down the side). Row 1 (gamete RY): RRYY, RRYy, RrYY, RrYy. Row 2 (gamete Ry): RRYy, RRyy, RrYy, Rryy.
[bio11-s8.3.4] Row 3 (gamete rY): RrYY, RrYy, rrYY, rrYy. Row 4 (gamete ry): RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy.
[bio11-s8.3.5] F2 genotypes (nine kinds): 1 RRYY, 2 RRYy, 2 RrYY, 4 RrYy, 1 RRyy, 2 Rryy, 1 rrYY, 2 rrYy, 1 rryy (total 16). Count repeated genotypes from the squares to get these numbers.
[bio11-s8.4.b] The 16 boxes contain nine genotypes but only four phenotypes, because a dominant allele hides the recessive one. Counting those phenotypes gives the famous dihybrid ratio.
[bio11-s8.4.0] F2 phenotypes: 9 round yellow (at least one R and at least one Y) : 3 round green (at least one R, yy) : 3 wrinkled yellow (rr, at least one Y) : 1 wrinkled green (rryy).
[bio11-s8.4.1] The ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is the signature of a dihybrid F2 with two unlinked genes, each with complete dominance (the heterozygote looks the same as the dominant homozygote). There is more on Mendel's F2 counts, close to this ratio, in Mendel's Laws.
[bio11-s8.5.b] Look at the F2 phenotypes against the original parents. Some combinations of traits appear that neither the P nor the F1 plants showed.
[bio11-s8.5.0] Two of the four F2 phenotypes (round green and wrinkled yellow) are new combinations, found in neither the P nor the F1 generation.
[bio11-s8.5.1] They appear because the genes assort independently, so any seed shape allele can end up with any seed colour allele. They are a source of variation in offspring.
[bio11-s8.5.2] Diagram: the full 4 × 4 Punnett square of the F1 × F1 cross, with gametes labelled on both sides, and the boxes coloured or marked by phenotype (9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, 1 wrinkled green).
[bio11-s8.6.b] A 16-box square is slow and easy to miscount. Because the two genes are independent, you can work out each gene separately and multiply, which gives a quick check.
[bio11-s8.6.0] Because the genes are independent, the dihybrid result is the two monohybrid results multiplied.
[bio11-s8.6.1] Rr × Rr gives 3/4 round and 1/4 wrinkled; Yy × Yy gives 3/4 yellow and 1/4 green, so each gene is treated as its own monohybrid cross.
[bio11-s8.6.2] So round yellow = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16, round green = 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16, wrinkled yellow = 3/16, wrinkled green = 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.
[bio11-s8.6.3] Use this to check a Punnett square; show the full square in an exam answer when asked for a genetic cross.
[bio11-s8.7.b] A test cross crosses an individual showing the dominant traits with one that is homozygous recessive for both genes. The recessive parent makes only one type of gamete, so the offspring reveal exactly which gametes the other parent makes.
[bio11-s8.7.0] Round yellow (RrYy) × wrinkled green (rryy) is the dihybrid test cross.
[bio11-s8.7.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × ry, because rryy can only make ry.
[bio11-s8.7.2] Offspring genotypes: RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy (1 : 1 : 1 : 1).
[bio11-s8.7.3] Phenotypes: 1 round yellow : 1 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green (25% each), because each of the four gametes has a 1/4 chance.
[bio11-s8.7.4] A 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 result shows that the dominant-looking parent was heterozygous for both genes and that the genes assort independently. There is more on the same cross with linked genes in Linkage.
[bio11-s8.7.5] The same pattern with other traits: in peas with tall (T) dominant to dwarf (t) and yellow (Y) dominant to green (y), TtYy × ttyy gives 1 tall yellow (TtYy) : 1 tall green (Ttyy) : 1 dwarf yellow (ttYy) : 1 dwarf green (ttyy).
[bio11-s8.8.b] Not every cross has two double heterozygotes. Here one parent is heterozygous for only one gene, so it makes just two gamete types and the square is 4 × 2.
[bio11-s8.8.0] Round yellow × round green: RrYy × Rryy, so the second parent is homozygous recessive for colour and heterozygous for shape.
[bio11-s8.8.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × Ry, ry.
[bio11-s8.8.2] Punnett square: 4 × 2 = 8 boxes; column Ry: RRYy, RRyy, RrYy, Rryy; column ry: RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy.
[bio11-s8.8.3] Phenotypes: 3 round yellow : 3 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green (3 : 3 : 1 : 1).
[bio11-s8.8.4] Check: 3/4 round × 1/2 yellow = 3/8 round yellow, matching the 3 round yellow out of 8 boxes.
[bio11-s8.9.b] When one gene shows incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has a blend in between the two homozygotes. This changes the phenotype count but the method for crossing two genes stays the same.
[bio11-s8.9.0] Snapdragons: tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t); red (R) and white (W) flower colour show incomplete dominance (RW = pink).
[bio11-s8.9.1] Parents' phenotypes: homozygous dwarf red × tall pink (heterozygous for height). Parents' genotypes: ttRR × TtRW.
[bio11-s8.9.2] Gametes: tR × TR, TW, tR, tW, because the ttRR parent can only make tR while the TtRW parent makes four types.
[bio11-s8.9.3] Offspring: TtRR (tall, red), TtRW (tall, pink), ttRR (dwarf, red), ttRW (dwarf, pink), in a 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio.
[bio11-s8.9.4] So 50% of the offspring have pink flowers (two of four genotypes) and 50% are tall.
[bio11-s8.9.5] To the second generation: TTRR × ttWW gives an F1 of all TtRW (tall pink).
[bio11-s8.9.6] F1 × F1 gives 3 tall red : 6 tall pink : 3 tall white : 1 dwarf red : 2 dwarf pink : 1 dwarf white (out of 16).
[bio11-s8.9.7] These are found by multiplying 3/4 tall and 1/4 dwarf by 1/4 red, 1/2 pink and 1/4 white, because the two genes are independent.

CANDIDATES bio17-s2: Transcription and translation
[bio17-s2.0.b] A cell can't send its DNA out to the ribosomes, so it makes a working copy of the gene instead. Three molecules are involved in making and carrying that copy: DNA, the enzyme RNA polymerase and mRNA.
[bio17-s2.0.0] DNA holds the gene, the base sequence (the order of A, T, C and G) that codes for the protein.
[bio17-s2.0.1] In transcription only one of the two strands of the gene is read, the template strand. The DNA is not used up or changed, and it stays in the nucleus, so the original instructions stay safe.
[bio17-s2.0.2] RNA polymerase is the enzyme that carries out transcription. It binds to the DNA at the start of a gene, unwinds and separates the two strands there, and joins free RNA nucleotides into a strand complementary to the template.
[bio17-s2.0.3] RNA polymerase moves along the gene until it reaches the end, building the new strand as it goes.
[bio17-s2.0.4] mRNA (messenger RNA) is a single-stranded copy of one gene's code, a series of codons (groups of three bases), and it carries the code from the nucleus to a ribosome.
[bio17-s2.0.5] mRNA is short-lived and is broken down after use, so a cell can stop making a protein by no longer making its mRNA.
[bio17-s2.1.b] Translation (turning the mRNA code into a chain of amino acids) needs a delivery molecule for the amino acids and a workbench to assemble them on. These are tRNA and the ribosome, which is built from rRNA. Together with mRNA, these are the three types of RNA in protein synthesis.
[bio17-s2.1.0] tRNA (transfer RNA) is a small RNA molecule in the cytoplasm that carries one specific amino acid to the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.1.1] Each tRNA's anticodon (three bases) pairs with a matching codon on the mRNA, so tRNA is the molecule that “translates” codons into amino acids.
[bio17-s2.1.2] tRNA brings amino acids to the ribosome; it does not make them.
[bio17-s2.1.3] rRNA (ribosomal RNA) makes up the ribosome together with proteins. It holds the mRNA and tRNAs in position.
[bio17-s2.1.4] The rRNA of the large subunit also catalyses the formation of peptide bonds (the bonds that join amino acids), so it acts as an enzyme.
[bio17-s2.1.5] The ribosome is the site of translation, made of rRNA and protein in two subunits: a small subunit that binds the mRNA and a large subunit with room for two tRNAs at a time.
[bio17-s2.1.6] The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time, reading the code in order.
[bio17-s2.1.7] Ribosomes are free in the cytoplasm, making proteins used in the cell, or attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum (a membrane network studded with ribosomes), making proteins for export or for membranes. There is more on this in Endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
[bio17-s2.2.b] Transcription is the making of an mRNA copy of a gene, and it takes place in the nucleus. It works by complementary base pairing, where each base on the DNA attracts one particular partner base.
[bio17-s2.2.0] 1. RNA polymerase attaches to the DNA at the start of the gene, unwinds the double helix there and separates the two strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs.
[bio17-s2.2.1] 2. One strand, the template strand, is read.
[bio17-s2.2.2] 2. Free RNA nucleotides pair with its exposed bases by complementary base pairing: A on DNA pairs with U on RNA, T with A, C with G, and G with C. RNA has U (uracil) where DNA has T.
[bio17-s2.2.3] 3. RNA polymerase joins the RNA nucleotides into a strand of mRNA as it moves along the gene, so the mRNA grows one nucleotide at a time.
[bio17-s2.2.4] 3. Behind it, the DNA strands re-join and rewind, so the DNA is left unchanged.
[bio17-s2.2.5] 4. At the end of the gene, RNA polymerase detaches and the finished mRNA is released.
[bio17-s2.2.6] 4. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore (a gap in the nuclear envelope) and goes to a ribosome.
[bio17-s2.2.7] Diagram: a section of DNA unwound by RNA polymerase, with the template strand and the mRNA strand growing alongside it; label DNA, template strand, RNA polymerase, free RNA nucleotides, mRNA, base pair A–U, direction of movement.
[bio17-s2.3.b] A tRNA has to do two jobs: carry the right amino acid and recognise the right codon. Its folded shape has one end for each job.
[bio17-s2.3.0] A tRNA is a single strand of about 80 RNA nucleotides that folds back on itself, so some regions lie side by side and can base-pair. Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in some regions hold the folded molecule in shape.
[bio17-s2.3.1] The folded molecule is a clover-leaf in two dimensions and folds further into an L shape in three dimensions.
[bio17-s2.3.2] At one end is the amino-acid attachment site, where one specific amino acid is attached by a specific enzyme, using energy from ATP.
[bio17-s2.3.3] At the opposite end is a loop with the anticodon: three unpaired bases that pair with a complementary codon on mRNA.
[bio17-s2.3.4] The code is read in codons of three bases, so each tRNA has an anticodon of exactly three bases and matches one codon (three bases of mRNA).
[bio17-s2.3.5] Each tRNA brings one amino acid. The attachment of a specific amino acid to each tRNA with a specific anticodon is what links each codon to its amino acid.
[bio17-s2.3.6] The ribosome holds two tRNAs side by side on neighbouring codons, so the code is read three bases at a time without gaps or overlaps.
[bio17-s2.3.7] Diagram: a clover-leaf tRNA, labelled: single RNA strand, regions of base pairing (hydrogen bonds), anticodon loop with three bases, amino-acid attachment site, amino acid.
[bio17-s2.4.b] Translation is the making of a polypeptide (a chain of amino acids) from the code in mRNA, and it takes place at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. It begins by assembling the ribosome on the mRNA and joining the first two amino acids.
[bio17-s2.4.0] 1. Start: the small ribosomal subunit attaches to the mRNA and moves along it to the start codon, AUG.
[bio17-s2.4.1] 1. A tRNA with the anticodon UAC, carrying methionine, pairs with the start codon, and the large subunit then joins.
[bio17-s2.4.2] 2. A second tRNA, whose anticodon is complementary to the next codon, arrives carrying its amino acid and pairs with that codon in the second position in the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.4.3] 3. A peptide bond forms between the two amino acids, a condensation reaction that releases water. It is catalysed by the rRNA of the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.4.4] 3. The first tRNA's amino acid is now attached to the second amino acid.
[bio17-s2.5.b] Once two amino acids are joined, the same cycle repeats along the mRNA until a stop signal is reached. Cells also run many ribosomes at once to make protein quickly.
[bio17-s2.5.0] 4. The ribosome moves along the mRNA by one codon, three bases. The first tRNA, now without its amino acid, is released into the cytoplasm, where it can pick up another of the same amino acid.
[bio17-s2.5.1] 4. A new tRNA pairs with the codon now in the empty position, because the move has exposed the next codon.
[bio17-s2.5.2] 5. Steps 2 to 4 repeat, the chain growing by one amino acid each time, in the order set by the codons.
[bio17-s2.5.3] 6. End: when a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA) is reached, no tRNA matches it, and a protein release factor binds instead.
[bio17-s2.5.4] 6. The polypeptide is released, and the ribosome's subunits separate from the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.5.5] Many ribosomes can translate the same mRNA at once, one behind the other, forming a polysome, so many copies of the protein are made quickly.
[bio17-s2.5.6] Translation also needs energy, both to attach amino acids to their tRNAs (from ATP) and to move the ribosome along the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.5.7] Diagram: a ribosome on an mRNA strand, with two tRNAs paired to neighbouring codons, a growing polypeptide on one, a peptide bond forming and a released tRNA leaving; label mRNA, codon, anticodon, tRNA, amino acid, peptide bond, ribosome (large and small subunits), polypeptide, direction of movement.
[bio17-s2.6.b] A newly made polypeptide is only a chain. It becomes a working protein when it folds into a particular three-dimensional shape, and the shape is set by its amino-acid sequence.
[bio17-s2.6.0] The sequence of amino acids (the primary structure) determines how the chain folds.
[bio17-s2.6.1] Bonds form between amino acids in different parts of the chain, such as hydrogen bonds and, in some proteins, sulphur bridges, giving the protein its particular three-dimensional shape.
[bio17-s2.6.2] Some proteins are made of more than one polypeptide, for example haemoglobin, which has four.
[bio17-s2.6.3] The shape determines the function, for example the active site of an enzyme or the binding site of an antibody or receptor. There is more on this in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio17-s2.6.4] A change in the amino-acid sequence can change the shape and stop the protein working, because the bonds that fold the chain form in different places.
[bio17-s2.6.5] Proteins made on the rough endoplasmic reticulum are folded and modified there and in the Golgi apparatus, then packaged into vesicles (small membrane sacs) for export, such as digestive enzymes and insulin.
[bio17-s2.6.6] In prokaryotes, which have no nucleus, so no nuclear envelope separates the steps, transcription and translation both take place in the cytoplasm, and ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed.
[bio17-s2.7.b] Both processes copy DNA using complementary base pairing, so they are easy to confuse. They differ in what is copied, which enzymes do it, and what happens to the product.
[bio17-s2.7.0] Product: replication makes a complete copy of all the DNA, giving two DNA molecules; transcription makes an RNA copy of one gene, the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.7.1] Strands used: replication uses both strands as templates, so both new molecules are complete; transcription uses only one strand of the gene.
[bio17-s2.7.2] Enzymes: DNA helicase and DNA polymerase in replication; RNA polymerase in transcription, so the enzyme names tell the two processes apart.
[bio17-s2.7.3] Nucleotides and pairing: DNA nucleotides contain deoxyribose (a sugar) and pair A with T; RNA nucleotides contain ribose, and A on DNA pairs with U.
[bio17-s2.7.4] When: replication happens once per cycle, in S phase; transcription happens throughout the cell's life, for whichever genes are needed, many times.
[bio17-s2.7.5] Fate of the product: new DNA stays in the nucleus, but mRNA leaves for the cytoplasm and is broken down after use.
[bio17-s2.8.b] Exam questions often give a DNA sequence and ask for the mRNA, tRNA anticodons and amino acids. You can do this with a fixed five-step method.
[bio17-s2.8.0] 1. Write the mRNA complementary to the template strand (A → U, T → A, C → G, G → C).
[bio17-s2.8.1] 2. Divide the mRNA into codons from the start codon, because the ribosome reads three bases at a time.
[bio17-s2.8.2] 3. Write the tRNA anticodon complementary to each codon, because the anticodon pairs with the codon.
[bio17-s2.8.3] 4. Use the codon table given to find the amino acids.
[bio17-s2.8.4] 5. Stop at a stop codon, because no amino acid is added there.
[bio17-s2.9.b] Here is the method applied to one short template strand, showing each product in turn.
[bio17-s2.9.0] Template DNA TAC AAA CCG ATT gives mRNA AUG UUU GGC UAA, by pairing T with A, A with U and C with G.
[bio17-s2.9.1] The anticodons are UAC, AAA and CCG, with none for the stop codon UAA, because no tRNA matches a stop codon.
[bio17-s2.9.2] The polypeptide is methionine, phenylalanine and glycine, and then the chain stops.
[bio17-s2.9.3] Three codons code for amino acids, so the worked example gives three amino acids joined by two peptide bonds.
[bio17-s2.10.b] You can also model the two processes with paper and cards. A model makes the order of events clear, but it leaves out some of the real chemistry.
[bio17-s2.10.0] Paper strips for DNA, in a “nucleus” area, are copied onto mRNA strips that are carried to a “ribosome” area.
[bio17-s2.10.1] Cards for tRNA have a three-base anticodon on one side and an amino acid on the other, so each card shows what a real tRNA does.
[bio17-s2.10.2] In the role-play, students match anticodons to codons in order and link the amino acids with clips to represent peptide bonds.
[bio17-s2.10.3] Limitations of the role-play: it is slow and flat, and ignores the enzymes, energy and folding, so it shows the order of events but not how they happen chemically.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.
