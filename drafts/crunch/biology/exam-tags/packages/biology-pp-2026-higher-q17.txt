CANDIDATES bio18-s2: The axial skeleton
[bio18-s2.0.b] The skeleton has two parts, and the axial skeleton is the one that forms the central axis (the upright line) of the body. The appendicular skeleton, the limbs and the girdles that attach them, is attached to it. There is more on this in The appendicular skeleton.
[bio18-s2.0.0] The axial skeleton is made of four parts: the skull, the vertebral column (the vertebrae, which are the individual back bones, plus the discs between them), the ribs and the sternum (breastbone).
[bio18-s2.0.1] Its main jobs are protecting the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, which are soft and easily damaged, and supporting the head and trunk (the body without the head and limbs).
[bio18-s2.1.b] The skull is the bony case of the head. It protects the brain and the sense organs, and it is built from two parts with different jobs.
[bio18-s2.1.0] The skull has two parts. The cranium is a dome of curved, flat bones that encloses and protects the brain.
[bio18-s2.1.1] The facial bones form the face, hold the teeth, and form the eye sockets (orbits) and the nasal cavity (the space inside the nose).
[bio18-s2.1.2] The bones of the cranium are joined by sutures: wavy, interlocking joints filled with fibrous tissue that allow no movement, so the cranium acts as one rigid, strong box. Its domed shape also spreads the force of a blow.
[bio18-s2.1.3] The lower jaw (mandible) is the only skull bone that moves. It joins the cranium at a freely movable joint on each side, allowing chewing and speech.
[bio18-s2.1.4] The skull also protects the sense organs: the eyes sit in the bony orbits, and the inner ear is enclosed in bone.
[bio18-s2.1.5] A large opening at the base of the cranium, the foramen magnum, lets the spinal cord pass down from the brain. The skull rests on the first vertebra.
[bio18-s2.1.6] In a newborn baby the cranial bones are not yet fused (joined into one). Soft, membrane-covered gaps called fontanelles allow the skull to change shape slightly during birth and allow the brain to grow; they close in the first two years.
[bio18-s2.1.7] Diagram: side view of the skull, labelling cranium, sutures, orbit (eye socket), facial bones, upper jaw, lower jaw (mandible), teeth and foramen magnum (or opening for spinal cord).
[bio18-s2.2.b] The vertebral column is the backbone. It is made of many small bones stacked in a line, so it is strong enough to carry the body yet flexible enough to bend. It is divided into five regions, each suited to its job.
[bio18-s2.2.0] The vertebral column (backbone or spine) is a flexible column of 33 vertebrae stacked one on another, from the skull to the pelvis.
[bio18-s2.2.1] It supports the head and trunk, protects the spinal cord, provides attachment for ribs and muscles, and joins the axial skeleton to the pelvic girdle (the hip bones).
[bio18-s2.2.2] The 7 cervical vertebrae (neck) are small and light, so they can support the head and allow it to nod and turn.
[bio18-s2.2.3] The 12 thoracic vertebrae (chest) each join a pair of ribs.
[bio18-s2.2.4] The 5 lumbar vertebrae (lower back) are the largest and strongest. They carry most of the weight of the upper body and allow bending.
[bio18-s2.2.5] The 5 sacral vertebrae are fused into one bone, the sacrum, which is joined to the pelvic girdle and passes the body's weight to the legs.
[bio18-s2.2.6] About 4 coccygeal vertebrae are fused to form the coccyx (tailbone), which anchors some muscles; the number varies from 3 to 5.
[bio18-s2.2.7] Vertebrae get larger from the neck to the lower back, because each one carries the weight of everything above it.
[bio18-s2.3.b] The top two vertebrae are shaped differently from the rest so that the head can move in two ways. Seen from the side, the whole column also bends in an S shape.
[bio18-s2.3.0] The atlas (first cervical vertebra) carries the skull and allows the head to nod.
[bio18-s2.3.1] The axis (second cervical vertebra) has a peg on which the atlas turns, allowing the head to rotate from side to side.
[bio18-s2.3.2] The column has an S-shaped curve when seen from the side: forward in the neck, back in the chest and forward in the lower back.
[bio18-s2.3.3] The curves act like a spring, absorbing shock when walking and running, and keep the body's weight balanced over the legs.
[bio18-s2.3.4] Diagram: side view of the vertebral column labelling the cervical, thoracic, lumbar, sacral and coccyx regions, the S-shaped curves and the discs between vertebrae.
[bio18-s2.4.b] A typical vertebra has a heavy part at the front to take weight, and a ring of bone at the back to protect the spinal cord. Each part of its structure suits its job.
[bio18-s2.4.0] The centrum (body) is a thick cylinder of bone at the front of the vertebra. It bears the body's weight.
[bio18-s2.4.1] The neural arch at the back surrounds the neural canal (vertebral canal), through which the spinal cord runs, so the cord is enclosed in bone.
[bio18-s2.4.2] The spinous process at the back and a transverse process on each side are projections that provide attachment for muscles and ligaments (tough bands that join bone to bone), acting as levers for the back muscles.
[bio18-s2.4.3] Facets are smooth surfaces that form small joints with the vertebrae above and below, guiding and limiting movement. In thoracic vertebrae, extra facets form the joints with the ribs.
[bio18-s2.4.4] Between neighbouring vertebrae there are small gaps on each side through which the spinal nerves leave the spinal cord. There is more on this in CNS and PNS.
[bio18-s2.4.5] Diagram: top view of a single lumbar or thoracic vertebra labelling centrum, neural arch, neural canal with spinal cord, spinous process, transverse processes and facets.
[bio18-s2.5.b] Bone on bone would be painful and would wear away, and it would not bend. Between the vertebrae lie intervertebral discs (inter means between), which solve these problems.
[bio18-s2.5.0] Position: an intervertebral disc lies between the centra of each pair of neighbouring vertebrae, from below the axis down to the sacrum, joining them together.
[bio18-s2.5.1] There are no discs within the fused sacrum and coccyx, because those vertebrae are fused into single bones and do not move against each other.
[bio18-s2.5.2] Structure: each disc is a pad of cartilage with a tough outer ring of fibrocartilage (fibrous cartilage) surrounding a soft, jelly-like centre.
[bio18-s2.5.3] Shock absorption: the discs compress under load and spring back, cushioning the vertebrae and the brain against the jolts of walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s2.5.4] Preventing friction and wear: the discs stop the bones of neighbouring vertebrae rubbing against each other.
[bio18-s2.5.5] Flexibility: each disc allows a small amount of movement between two vertebrae, so the joints between vertebrae are slightly movable. Added together along the column, these small movements let the back bend forwards, backwards and sideways, and twist.
[bio18-s2.5.6] Holding the column together: the discs, with the ligaments, bind the vertebrae into one column while allowing it to bend.
[bio18-s2.5.7] The discs are squeezed slightly during a day of standing and sitting and recover while lying down, so a person is slightly taller in the morning than in the evening.
[bio18-s2.6.b] The ribs and sternum together form the rib cage, a bony cage around the chest. It must be strong enough to protect the organs inside but also able to move, because the chest changes size when we breathe.
[bio18-s2.6.0] There are 12 pairs of ribs: curved, flat bones that form the rib cage around the chest. At the back, every rib is joined to a thoracic vertebra by small joints that let it move.
[bio18-s2.6.1] The first seven pairs (true ribs) are joined directly to the sternum by strips of flexible costal cartilage.
[bio18-s2.6.2] Pairs 8 to 10 (false ribs) are joined to the cartilage of the rib above them.
[bio18-s2.6.3] Pairs 11 and 12 (floating ribs) are attached only at the back, to the vertebrae, and are free at the front.
[bio18-s2.6.4] The rib cage protects the heart and lungs, and partly the liver, stomach and kidneys.
[bio18-s2.6.5] Because the ribs are curved and joined by flexible cartilage, the cage can absorb blows and can move. The intercostal muscles between the ribs raise and lower the rib cage, changing the volume of the chest in breathing. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio18-s2.6.6] The sternum (breastbone) is a long, flat bone in the centre of the front of the chest. It protects the heart and anchors the ribs, through the costal cartilages, and the clavicles (collarbones). Like other flat bones, it contains red bone marrow.
[bio18-s2.6.7] Diagram: front view of the rib cage labelling the sternum, ribs, costal cartilage, true, false and floating ribs, thoracic vertebrae (behind), and the position of the clavicles.

CANDIDATES bio19-s1: CNS and PNS
[bio19-s1.0.b] The nervous system detects stimuli (changes in the surroundings or inside the body) and coordinates responses by carrying nerve impulses. To do this it is split into two divisions: one that makes decisions and one that carries messages.
[bio19-s1.0.0] The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord, the control centre of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.1] The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is all the nerves outside the CNS, so it reaches every part of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.2] The CNS receives information, processes it and sends out instructions, which is why it is the part that decides what to do.
[bio19-s1.0.3] The PNS carries impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body, so it is the communication line that links the CNS to receptors and effectors.
[bio19-s1.0.4] A neuron is a nerve cell; a nerve is a bundle of many neuron fibres wrapped in connective tissue, so one nerve carries many separate signals at once.
[bio19-s1.0.5] A nerve impulse is an electrochemical signal travelling along a neuron, which is how information moves around the body.
[bio19-s1.1.b] Information has to travel into the CNS and instructions have to travel out again. The PNS does both jobs using two kinds of neuron, and its fibres run through the body bundled in nerves.
[bio19-s1.1.0] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect stimuli) to the CNS, so the CNS learns what is happening.
[bio19-s1.1.1] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors, which are muscles and glands, so the CNS's instructions produce a response such as a movement or a secretion.
[bio19-s1.1.2] Nerve fibres of the PNS run through the body inside nerves, which carry them to almost every tissue.
[bio19-s1.1.3] Humans have 31 pairs of spinal nerves, which leave the spinal cord between the vertebrae (the bones of the backbone).
[bio19-s1.1.4] Each spinal nerve joins the cord by two roots, short branches that attach it to the spinal cord, so sensory and motor fibres enter and leave by separate routes.
[bio19-s1.1.5] The dorsal (back) root carries sensory fibres into the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.1.6] The ventral (front) root carries motor fibres out of the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.b] A neuron's cell body (the part containing the nucleus) can sit in different places depending on the type of neuron. Knowing where each type lies tells you which part of the nervous system it belongs to.
[bio19-s1.2.0] Sensory neuron cell bodies lie in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on the dorsal root just outside the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.1] Motor neuron cell bodies lie in the grey matter of the CNS, inside the cord, and their fibres leave through the ventral root.
[bio19-s1.2.2] Interneurons (neurons that link one neuron to another) lie entirely within the CNS, so they never leave the brain or spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.3.b] The spinal cord runs from the base of the brain down the back, inside the vertebral column, which protects it. It is the main route between the brain and the rest of the body.
[bio19-s1.3.0] The spinal cord relays impulses between the body and the brain, so sensory information travels up to the brain and instructions travel back down.
[bio19-s1.3.1] It carries out spinal reflexes, which are fast automatic responses, such as the withdrawal reflex when you touch something painful. There is more on this in The reflex action.
[bio19-s1.4.b] Cut straight across (a transverse section), the spinal cord shows a clear pattern of grey and white tissue around a central canal. The two tissues differ because they are made of different parts of neurons.
[bio19-s1.4.0] A central canal, containing cerebrospinal fluid (a watery fluid that cushions the CNS), lies at the centre of the cord.
[bio19-s1.4.1] Around the canal is an H- or butterfly-shaped core of grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.2] White matter surrounds the grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.3] Grey matter is made of cell bodies and unmyelinated interneurons (interneurons without a fatty myelin sheath), which is why it looks grey.
[bio19-s1.4.4] White matter is made of myelinated axons (long fibres coated in myelin); it is white because myelin is fatty.
[bio19-s1.4.5] In the brain the arrangement is reversed: the cerebral cortex is grey matter on the outside, with white matter beneath.
[bio19-s1.4.6] Diagram: transverse section of the spinal cord labelling central canal, grey matter, white matter, dorsal root, dorsal root ganglion, ventral root and spinal nerve.
[bio19-s1.5.b] The brain and spinal cord control the whole body, so they need strong protection. The CNS is protected by bone, membranes and fluid.
[bio19-s1.5.0] The brain is protected by the skull (cranium), a rigid bony case.
[bio19-s1.5.1] The spinal cord is protected by the vertebrae, which form a bony tunnel around it.
[bio19-s1.5.2] Three membranes, the meninges, surround the brain and spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.5.3] Cerebrospinal fluid lies between the inner meninges and fills the brain's cavities and the central canal.
[bio19-s1.5.4] Cerebrospinal fluid cushions the CNS against shock, so a knock to the head or back is absorbed, and it supplies the CNS with nutrients.
[bio19-s1.5.5] Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, usually caused by bacterial or viral infection.
[bio19-s1.6.b] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and the part that handles most of its higher functions, such as thought and voluntary movement.
[bio19-s1.6.0] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain, divided into left and right cerebral hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum.
[bio19-s1.6.1] The cerebral cortex, its outer layer, is grey matter deeply folded to increase its surface area, and so the number of neurons.
[bio19-s1.6.2] Each cerebral hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body, so the left hemisphere controls the right side and vice versa.
[bio19-s1.6.3] The cerebrum controls voluntary movement (movement you choose to make) and interprets the senses.
[bio19-s1.6.4] It also governs memory, thought, language, intelligence and personality.
[bio19-s1.7.b] Smooth, accurate movement needs a part of the brain dedicated to coordinating it. That is the cerebellum.
[bio19-s1.7.0] The cerebellum lies at the back of the brain, below the cerebrum, and also has a folded surface.
[bio19-s1.7.1] It coordinates muscular movement and controls balance and posture.
[bio19-s1.8.b] Some vital actions must carry on without you thinking about them, even during sleep. The medulla oblongata controls these.
[bio19-s1.8.0] The medulla oblongata lies at the base of the brain, where it joins the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.8.1] It controls involuntary actions (those that happen without conscious control), including breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure.
[bio19-s1.8.2] It controls reflexes such as swallowing, coughing and sneezing.
[bio19-s1.9.b] The hypothalamus is a small region that keeps conditions in the body steady and connects the nervous system to the hormone system.
[bio19-s1.9.0] The hypothalamus lies below the cerebrum and above the pituitary gland, to which it is connected.
[bio19-s1.9.1] It controls homeostasis (keeping the internal environment steady): body temperature, water balance, hunger and thirst. There is more on this in Homeostasis.
[bio19-s1.9.2] It links the nervous and hormonal systems by controlling the pituitary gland, so nerve signals can lead to hormone release.
[bio19-s1.10.b] You may be asked to label the brain, so know where each part sits relative to the others.
[bio19-s1.10.0] Diagram: vertical section of the brain labelling cerebrum, cerebral cortex, corpus callosum, cerebellum, medulla oblongata, hypothalamus and pituitary gland, with the spinal cord continuing below.

CANDIDATES bio19-s2: The reflex action
[bio19-s2.0.b] Every reflex, and every nervous response, is built from the same three parts: something that triggers it, something that detects it and something that acts. A reflex begins with a stimulus that a receptor detects and ends with a response from an effector.
[bio19-s2.0.0] A stimulus is a change in the internal or external environment that can be detected, such as heat on the skin or an object approaching the eye.
[bio19-s2.0.1] A receptor detects the stimulus. It is a cell or nerve ending that is sensitive to that particular type of change, and it starts the impulse.
[bio19-s2.0.2] An effector, a muscle or a gland, carries out the response. A muscle responds by contracting; a gland responds by secreting.
[bio19-s2.1.b] Some situations need a response so quickly that there is no time to think. A reflex action is the body's built-in solution: a response that is fixed and does not wait for a decision.
[bio19-s2.1.0] A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, happening without conscious thought. Involuntary means you do not choose it or control it.
[bio19-s2.1.1] Reflexes protect the body from harm, for example pulling a hand from a hot object or blinking when something approaches the eye, so damage is limited before you even notice.
[bio19-s2.1.2] Reflexes also maintain posture and normal body functions; coughing and sneezing clear the airways, for example.
[bio19-s2.1.3] Reflexes are inborn, so they are not learned. You are born able to do them.
[bio19-s2.1.4] The same stimulus always produces the same response, which is what makes a reflex automatic and predictable.
[bio19-s2.2.b] A reflex works because the nerve pathway involved is short and fixed. The reflex arc is the pathway an impulse (the electrical signal carried by nerve cells) follows in a reflex action.
[bio19-s2.2.0] The pathway runs receptor, sensory neuron, interneuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord, motor neuron, effector. The sensory neuron carries the impulse in, the motor neuron carries it out, and the interneuron connects them.
[bio19-s2.2.1] The sensory neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root (the back root of the spinal nerve); its cell body lies in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on that root.
[bio19-s2.2.2] The motor neuron leaves the spinal cord through the ventral root (the front root), carrying the impulse out to the effector.
[bio19-s2.2.3] Diagram: reflex arc drawn through a transverse section of the spinal cord, labelling receptor in the skin, sensory neuron, dorsal root ganglion, interneuron, grey matter, white matter, motor neuron, ventral root and effector muscle, with arrows showing the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s2.3.b] Touching a hot object shows the whole reflex arc at work. Follow the impulse from the finger to the muscle that pulls the hand away.
[bio19-s2.3.0] 1. Pain and heat receptors in the skin of the finger are stimulated by the hot object.
[bio19-s2.3.1] 2. An impulse travels along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.3.2] 3. It passes across a synapse (the tiny gap between two neurons) to an interneuron in the grey matter.
[bio19-s2.3.3] 4. It passes across a synapse to a motor neuron.
[bio19-s2.3.4] 5. The motor neuron carries the impulse to the biceps, the effector.
[bio19-s2.3.5] 6. The biceps contracts and the hand is pulled away from the object.
[bio19-s2.4.b] A reflex is faster than a voluntary action because of the route it takes. The brain is not left out, though: it is told what happened a moment later.
[bio19-s2.4.0] The impulse takes a short route through the spinal cord, crossing few synapses. Each synapse adds a small delay, so fewer synapses means a faster response.
[bio19-s2.4.1] It does not wait for a decision by the brain, which is why reflexes are rapid.
[bio19-s2.4.2] Interneurons also carry impulses up the spinal cord to the brain, so pain is felt just after the hand has moved.
[bio19-s2.4.3] The brain can sometimes override a reflex, for example when a person holds on to a hot plate to avoid dropping it.
[bio19-s2.5.b] The knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is the simplest reflex arc, because it leaves out the interneuron. It is a useful contrast with the withdrawal reflex.
[bio19-s2.5.0] Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches the thigh muscle, which is the stimulus.
[bio19-s2.5.1] The sensory neuron synapses directly with the motor neuron in the spinal cord, with no interneuron, so the pathway has only one synapse.
[bio19-s2.5.2] The leg kicks. Doctors use this reflex to test the nervous system, because a normal response shows the reflex pathway is working.
[bio19-s2.6.b] Not every reflex passes through the spinal cord. Some are controlled by the brain instead, but they are still rapid, automatic and involuntary.
[bio19-s2.6.0] Some reflexes are controlled by the brain rather than the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.6.1] Examples are blinking, coughing, sneezing and the pupil narrowing in bright light.

CANDIDATES bio19-s3: Neurons
[bio19-s3.0.b] Your body has to pass information quickly between receptors (cells that detect changes), the brain and spinal cord, and muscles and glands. Neurons are the cells that do this job, and there are three types, each with its own role.
[bio19-s3.0.0] A neuron, or nerve cell, is the basic unit of the nervous system; it carries electrochemical nerve impulses (tiny electrical-and-chemical signals that travel along the cell).
[bio19-s3.0.1] Neurons are long and thin, so a single cell can connect distant parts of the body, for example the skin of the foot and the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.0.2] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect a stimulus) to the CNS (the central nervous system: brain and spinal cord).
[bio19-s3.0.3] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands, which carry out the response).
[bio19-s3.0.4] Interneurons, also called relay or connector neurons, carry impulses between neurons within the CNS, so they link the incoming and outgoing pathways.
[bio19-s3.1.b] Every neuron is built from the same three main regions: a cell body, branched dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that sends them on. The shape of each region suits its job.
[bio19-s3.1.0] The cell body contains the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm and organelles, which keeps the cell alive.
[bio19-s3.1.1] The cell body also makes the neurotransmitter (the chemical that passes the signal to the next cell) and the proteins the neuron needs.
[bio19-s3.1.2] Dendrites are short, branched extensions that receive impulses from other neurons or receptors and carry them towards the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.3] Branching gives dendrites a large surface, so one neuron can receive signals from many others.
[bio19-s3.1.4] The axon is a long single fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.5] The axon's length lets impulses reach distant effectors, for example a muscle in the foot.
[bio19-s3.2.b] Many axons are wrapped in a fatty coat that makes impulses travel faster. Fast conduction matters because the body must respond quickly, for example when you pull your hand from something hot.
[bio19-s3.2.0] The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer around many axons, so the impulse does not leak away along the way.
[bio19-s3.2.1] It speeds up the impulse, which jumps between the small gaps in the sheath called nodes of Ranvier.
[bio19-s3.2.2] Schwann cells are cells that wrap around the axon in the PNS (the peripheral nervous system: the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord), their membrane forming the myelin sheath.
[bio19-s3.3.b] At its far end an axon has to hand the impulse on to the next cell, whether another neuron, a muscle or a gland. The axon terminals are built for this hand-over.
[bio19-s3.3.0] The axon ends in swollen synaptic knobs, also called axon terminals.
[bio19-s3.3.1] Neurotransmitter vesicles are tiny sacs in the synaptic knobs that store neurotransmitter, so a supply is ready to be released.
[bio19-s3.3.2] The neurotransmitter is released to pass the impulse to the next cell. There is more on this in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s3.4.b] A motor neuron carries the command from the CNS out to an effector. Its structure is arranged to collect many inputs inside the CNS and then send one signal a long way out.
[bio19-s3.4.0] A motor neuron has its cell body at one end, inside the CNS, with many dendrites.
[bio19-s3.4.1] Its many dendrites receive input from many interneurons, so one motor neuron can combine signals from many sources.
[bio19-s3.4.2] It has one long axon, which ends in axon terminals on a muscle or gland, so the axon reaches the effector wherever it lies in the body.
[bio19-s3.5.b] A sensory neuron brings information in from a receptor to the CNS. It has a different layout from a motor neuron, with its cell body off to one side.
[bio19-s3.5.0] A sensory neuron has a long fibre, the dendron, which carries impulses from the receptor to the cell body. A dendron is not the same as a dendrite, which is short and branched.
[bio19-s3.5.1] Its cell body sits to one side of the fibre, in a ganglion (a cluster of cell bodies) outside the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.2] A shorter axon carries the impulse on into the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.3] The long dendron reaches from a receptor, for example in the skin of the foot, to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.5.4] The cell body lies off the main pathway, so it does not slow the impulse.
[bio19-s3.6.b] Between the sensory and motor neurons sits a third type that links them. Interneurons make the CNS a network rather than a set of separate wires.
[bio19-s3.6.0] Interneurons are short, with many branched connections, so each can link with many other neurons.
[bio19-s3.6.1] They are found only in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.6.2] They connect sensory neurons to motor neurons and link many neurons together.
[bio19-s3.7.b] You are often asked to label a neuron diagram or to say which type is shown. Practise drawing both types so that you can place every part and show the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.7.0] Diagram: a motor neuron and a sensory neuron, labelling cell body, nucleus, dendrites, dendron (sensory), axon, myelin sheath, Schwann cell, node of Ranvier, axon terminals, neurotransmitter vesicles, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.8.b] The importance of myelin shows when it is damaged. Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which this happens.
[bio19-s3.8.0] Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease (the immune system attacks the body's own tissue) in which the body's immune system damages the myelin sheath in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.8.1] Damaged myelin slows or blocks impulses, because the insulation that lets them jump between the nodes of Ranvier is lost.
[bio19-s3.8.2] Symptoms include problems with movement, balance and vision.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio26-s1: The urinary system
[bio26-s1.0.b] Every cell produces waste as it carries out its chemical reactions, and some of that waste is harmful if it stays in the body. The kidneys are the main organs that clear it away, and they do a second job at the same time: keeping the body's fluids balanced.
[bio26-s1.0.0] Excretion is the removal of the waste products of metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells). Faeces are not excreted: they are undigested food that was never absorbed, so passing them out is egestion.
[bio26-s1.0.1] The kidneys excrete urea, excess water, excess salts and excess hydrogen ions in urine. Other organs help: the lungs excrete carbon dioxide, and sweat removes some water, salts and a little urea.
[bio26-s1.0.2] Urea is made in the liver from excess amino acids (the units of proteins) by deamination, the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of an amino acid. It is carried dissolved in the blood plasma to the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.0.3] Urea is toxic if it builds up, so it must be removed continuously rather than in occasional bursts.
[bio26-s1.0.4] Transport between environments: blood (the internal environment) carries wastes to the kidneys, the kidneys transfer them from the blood into urine, and urine is passed out of the body (the external environment).
[bio26-s1.0.5] At the same time the kidneys return useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most of the water and most of the salts) to the blood, so nutrients are not lost in the urine.
[bio26-s1.0.6] The kidneys have two roles: excretion (removing urea and other wastes), and osmoregulation and homeostasis, which regulate the volume, water content, salt concentration and pH of the body fluids.
[bio26-s1.0.7] Both roles use the same two processes: filtration of the blood (squeezing small molecules out of it), then reabsorption (taking back what the body needs). What is not reabsorbed is urine.
[bio26-s1.1.b] The kidneys sit well protected at the back of the body and are supplied directly by a large artery. That position and supply let them filter a large volume of blood at high pressure.
[bio26-s1.1.0] The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs at the back of the abdomen, one on each side of the vertebral column (backbone), just below the diaphragm. The lowest ribs partly protect them.
[bio26-s1.1.1] The right kidney is slightly lower than the left because the liver lies above it and takes up space.
[bio26-s1.1.2] Each kidney is embedded in a layer of fat that cushions it against physical damage and helps hold it in place, and is covered by a tough outer membrane, the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.1.3] The adrenal glands (small hormone-producing glands) sit on top of the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.1.4] Each kidney receives blood from a renal artery, a short wide branch of the aorta (the body's main artery), so blood arrives at high pressure and in large volume, which suits filtration.
[bio26-s1.1.5] Filtered blood leaves by the renal vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart).
[bio26-s1.1.6] Blood in the renal vein has less urea, less oxygen and less glucose than blood in the renal artery, and more carbon dioxide because the kidney cells respire. It is the blood vessel with the lowest urea concentration in the body.
[bio26-s1.1.7] Diagram: front view of the urinary system. Label two kidneys, renal arteries from the aorta, renal veins to the vena cava, ureters, bladder, sphincter muscles and urethra. The adrenal glands on top of the kidneys may be shown.
[bio26-s1.2.b] Once urine has formed it must be carried away, stored and released at a suitable moment. Three structures do this in turn: the ureters carry it, the bladder stores it and the urethra lets it out.
[bio26-s1.2.0] There are two ureters: narrow muscular tubes, one from the renal pelvis (the funnel-shaped collecting space inside each kidney) down to the bladder.
[bio26-s1.2.1] The ureter walls contain smooth muscle that moves urine by peristalsis (waves of contraction), so urine reaches the bladder whatever the body position, not just by gravity.
[bio26-s1.2.2] The urinary bladder is a hollow muscular bag in the lower abdomen (pelvis) that stores urine.
[bio26-s1.2.3] The bladder wall is elastic and folded, so it can stretch to hold a large volume and contract to empty.
[bio26-s1.2.4] Two rings of sphincter muscle (muscle that closes an opening) at the bladder outlet keep it closed: an inner one that works automatically (involuntary), and an outer one under conscious (voluntary) control, which young children learn to control.
[bio26-s1.2.5] The urethra is the single tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
[bio26-s1.2.6] In males the urethra is longer, runs through the penis and also carries semen. In females it is short and opens just in front of the vagina.
[bio26-s1.3.b] Cut a kidney lengthways and you can see that it is not uniform inside. The three regions each have a different role in making and collecting urine.
[bio26-s1.3.0] A kidney cut lengthways shows three regions: cortex, medulla and renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.3.1] Cortex: the dark outer layer, under the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.3.2] Medulla: the paler inner layer, divided into cone-shaped renal pyramids.
[bio26-s1.3.3] Renal pelvis: a funnel-shaped space on the inner (concave) side that collects urine and leads into the ureter. No filtration happens in the pelvis and urine is not changed there, so it is only a collecting space.
[bio26-s1.4.b] The real work of the kidney is done by microscopic tubes called nephrons. Each one first filters the blood, then takes back what the body needs, and the leftover liquid becomes urine.
[bio26-s1.4.0] Each kidney contains about one million nephrons, microscopic tubules that are the kidney's filtering units, so together they give a huge filtering surface. Each runs from the cortex down into the medulla and back.
[bio26-s1.4.1] The nephron tubules drain into collecting ducts that run through the pyramids and open into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.4.2] Filtration takes place in the cortex: blood is forced at high pressure through knots of capillaries (glomeruli) into the cup-shaped tops of the nephrons (Bowman's capsules).
[bio26-s1.4.3] Small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea) are filtered out of the blood. Blood cells and plasma proteins are too large, so they stay in the blood. The filtered liquid is called the filtrate.
[bio26-s1.4.4] Reabsorption (taking useful substances back into the blood) takes place in the cortex, in the coiled tubules, and in the medulla, in the loops of Henle and the collecting ducts.
[bio26-s1.4.5] All the glucose and amino acids, most of the salts and most of the water are taken back into the blood. The remaining liquid, carrying urea and excess salts and water, is urine.
[bio26-s1.4.6] Diagram: longitudinal section of a kidney. Label renal capsule, cortex, medulla, renal pyramid, renal pelvis, ureter, renal artery and renal vein. Draw one nephron from cortex into medulla to show where filtration and reabsorption happen.
[bio26-s1.5.b] Urine is what is left of the filtrate once the body has taken back what it needs. Its volume and concentration change from hour to hour, which is how the kidneys keep the water content of the blood steady.
[bio26-s1.5.0] Urine is mostly water, with urea, salts (mainly sodium chloride) and other wastes dissolved in it.
[bio26-s1.5.1] Healthy urine contains no protein and no glucose, because protein is not filtered out of the blood and all the glucose is reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.5.2] After drinking a lot of water, the kidneys produce a large volume of dilute (pale) urine, which gets rid of the extra water.
[bio26-s1.5.3] When water is short (little drinking, heavy sweating in exercise or hot weather, or diarrhoea), a small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine is produced, so the body saves water.
[bio26-s1.5.4] The control is by the hormone ADH. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation; here you only need the effect on urine.
[bio26-s1.6.b] Water is not the only thing the kidneys balance. They also adjust salts and acidity, and together this keeps the body's internal conditions steady.
[bio26-s1.6.0] Salt balance: after a salty meal the kidneys excrete more salt, and when salt is short they reabsorb more.
[bio26-s1.6.1] Keeping the salt and water concentration of the blood steady stops body cells from gaining or losing water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio26-s1.6.2] pH: the kidneys remove excess hydrogen ions from the blood in urine, helping to keep blood pH at about 7.4. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio26-s1.6.3] This is homeostasis: the kidneys keep the internal environment constant whatever is eaten or drunk, much as a thermostat holds a room at one temperature. This works by negative feedback, which is taught in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio26-s1.7.b] Urine is made all the time, but it is passed out only now and then. This sequence shows how it travels and why release can be delayed.
[bio26-s1.7.0] 1. Urine forms continuously in the nephrons and drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.7.1] 2. Peristalsis in the ureter carries it to the bladder in small spurts.
[bio26-s1.7.2] 3. The bladder fills and its wall stretches. Stretch receptors in the wall send nerve impulses that give the urge to urinate.
[bio26-s1.7.3] 4. To urinate, the bladder's muscular wall contracts and the sphincters relax. The outer sphincter relaxes under voluntary control, which is learned after early childhood.
[bio26-s1.7.4] 5. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.
[bio26-s1.8.b] Each part of the urinary system has features that suit its job. Examiners ask you to link structure to function, so learn these as pairs.
[bio26-s1.8.0] Fat layer around each kidney: protects it from physical damage.
[bio26-s1.8.1] Renal artery branching directly from the aorta: delivers high-pressure blood for filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.2] About one million nephrons: a huge filtering surface.
[bio26-s1.8.3] Cortex full of capillary knots: the site of filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.4] Long tubules and loops in the medulla: the site of reabsorption.
[bio26-s1.8.5] Funnel-shaped renal pelvis: collects urine.
[bio26-s1.8.6] Muscular ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristalsis.
[bio26-s1.8.7] Elastic bladder with sphincters: stores urine and allows controlled release. The urethra is the exit for urine from the body.
[bio26-s1.9.b] Because healthy urine contains no glucose or protein, finding either one in a urine sample tells a doctor that something is wrong.
[bio26-s1.9.0] Glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes, because blood glucose is so high that not all of it can be reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.9.1] Protein in urine is a sign of damage to the kidney's filters, because healthy filters do not let protein through.
[bio26-s1.9.2] Urine tests are also used to detect drugs, for example in sport.
[bio26-s1.10.b] If the kidneys stop working, the body can no longer clear its wastes or balance its fluids. There are two treatments: a machine that does the kidney's job, or a replacement kidney.
[bio26-s1.10.0] If the kidneys stop working, urea, salts and water build up in the blood and the person will die without treatment.
[bio26-s1.10.1] Treatment is by dialysis or by a kidney transplant.
[bio26-s1.10.2] A transplant gives a working kidney, but the patient must take drugs to stop the immune system rejecting it, because the body treats the new kidney as foreign. There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio26-s1.11.b] A dialysis machine copies the kidney's filtering. It uses diffusion across a membrane to clean the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.0] The patient's blood is pumped through a machine where it flows past a selectively permeable membrane (one that lets only some substances through), with dialysis fluid on the other side.
[bio26-s1.11.1] Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid, because their concentration is higher in the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.2] The fluid contains glucose and salts at normal blood concentrations, so these are not lost from the blood. Blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane.
[bio26-s1.11.3] Dialysis must be repeated several times a week, because it does not work continuously the way healthy kidneys do.

CANDIDATES bio26-s2: The nephron
[bio26-s2.0.b] Urine is made by tiny tubes called nephrons, so you need to know the parts of one in the order the fluid flows through them. Each part has its own job, and its position in the kidney is part of the answer.
[bio26-s2.0.0] A nephron is one of the about one million microscopic tubules in each kidney. It is the kidney's functional unit (the smallest part that does the kidney's job), where blood is filtered and urine is formed. Its wall is one cell thick throughout.
[bio26-s2.0.1] Bowman's capsule is the cup-shaped, double-walled closed end of the nephron, in the cortex (the outer layer of the kidney), wrapped around the glomerulus (a capillary knot). Its inner wall is made of special cells with gaps between them, so filtered fluid passes easily into the capsule's inner space.
[bio26-s2.0.2] The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) is the first, long, coiled (convoluted) section after the capsule, in the cortex. Proximal means nearer the capsule.
[bio26-s2.0.3] The loop of Henle is a long U-shaped loop that dips down into the medulla (the inner part of the kidney), with a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up to the cortex.
[bio26-s2.0.4] The distal convoluted tubule (DCT) is the second coiled section, back in the cortex. Distal means further from the capsule. It empties into a collecting duct.
[bio26-s2.0.5] The collecting duct is a tube shared by many nephrons that runs down through the medulla, through a pyramid, and opens into the renal pelvis (the funnel that collects urine).
[bio26-s2.0.6] The syllabus does not list the collecting duct, but it is needed to explain water reabsorption and ADH. Some textbooks do not count it as part of the nephron.
[bio26-s2.1.b] A nephron works because blood is brought right up against it. The blood vessels are arranged so that pressure builds up for filtering, and so that the tubule is wrapped in a second set of capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) to take back what it reabsorbs.
[bio26-s2.1.0] A branch of the renal artery (the artery that brings blood to the kidney) divides into arterioles (small arteries). The afferent arteriole (afferent means carrying towards) brings blood to each nephron and divides into the glomerulus, a knot of capillaries inside Bowman's capsule.
[bio26-s2.1.1] The capillaries of the glomerulus rejoin to form the efferent arteriole (efferent means carrying away), which is narrower than the afferent arteriole. The afferent is the wider one, so do not say the efferent is wider.
[bio26-s2.1.2] Because blood enters the glomerulus through a wider vessel than it leaves by, pressure builds up inside the glomerulus, like water backing up behind a narrowed nozzle. This high pressure drives filtration.
[bio26-s2.1.3] The glomerulus is unusual because it is a capillary network lying between two arterioles, not between an arteriole and a venule (a small vein) as capillaries normally do.
[bio26-s2.1.4] The efferent arteriole then branches into a second capillary network that surrounds the PCT, loop of Henle and DCT closely. Substances reabsorbed from the tubule pass into these capillaries, so they must be close to the tubule.
[bio26-s2.1.5] These capillaries also supply the tubule cells with oxygen and glucose for respiration, because the cells need ATP for active transport, and they carry away the cells' carbon dioxide.
[bio26-s2.1.6] The capillaries join into venules, then into a branch of the renal vein, which carries the filtered blood back to the circulation.
[bio26-s2.1.7] Diagram: one nephron with its blood supply, labelled afferent arteriole, glomerulus, efferent arteriole, Bowman's capsule, proximal convoluted tubule, descending limb, loop of Henle, ascending limb, distal convoluted tubule, collecting duct, capillary network around the tubules, branch of renal artery and branch of renal vein; draw a line marking the boundary between cortex and medulla, with the capsule, PCT and DCT above it and the loop and most of the collecting duct below; mark arrows for filtration (glomerulus into capsule) and reabsorption (tubule into capillaries).
[bio26-s2.2.b] The first step in making urine is filtration. It happens in the glomerulus and capsule, where pressure forces fluid out of the blood without checking whether the body needs it.
[bio26-s2.2.0] Filtration, also called ultrafiltration or filtration under pressure, is the forcing of small molecules out of the blood by the high pressure in the glomerulus. They pass through the pores in the capillary walls and the inner wall of Bowman's capsule, into the capsule.
[bio26-s2.2.1] Substances that pass into the filtrate (the fluid that enters the capsule) are water, glucose, amino acids, salts (e.g. sodium and chloride ions), urea (the waste made from breaking down amino acids), vitamins and some hormones.
[bio26-s2.2.2] Substances that are not filtered and stay in the blood are red and white blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins, because they are too large to pass through.
[bio26-s2.2.3] Filtration is not selective: it sorts molecules by size only, so useful substances such as glucose and amino acids are filtered out with the waste. The glomerular filtrate therefore has the same composition as blood plasma minus the proteins.
[bio26-s2.2.4] Scale: the kidneys filter about 180 litres of fluid a day but produce only about 1.5 litres of urine, so about 99% of the filtrate is reabsorbed (taken back into the blood); this is why reabsorption is essential.
[bio26-s2.2.5] Structure suits filtration: the wide afferent and narrow efferent arteriole raise the pressure, and the capillary wall and the capsule's inner wall are thin and porous, so fluid passes through easily.
[bio26-s2.2.6] The many capillaries in each glomerulus, with about a million glomeruli in the kidney, give a large filtering surface, so a large volume of fluid can be filtered every day.
[bio26-s2.3.b] Filtration lets everything small through, so the body has to take back what it wants. Most of this reabsorption happens in the PCT, whose structure is built for the job.
[bio26-s2.3.0] Reabsorption is the return of useful substances from the filtrate into the blood in the capillaries around the tubule. It is selective: the body keeps what it needs.
[bio26-s2.3.1] The PCT is the main site of reabsorption: all the glucose and all the amino acids, most of the salts (about two-thirds of the sodium) and most of the water (about two-thirds) are reabsorbed here.
[bio26-s2.3.2] Glucose, amino acids and sodium ions are taken up by active transport, which uses ATP (the cell's energy currency) to move them into the cells. Water follows by osmosis, and some urea diffuses back into the blood.
[bio26-s2.3.3] Water is always reabsorbed passively by osmosis; it is never actively pumped. Only salts, glucose and amino acids are reabsorbed actively in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.3.4] The PCT is long and coiled, which gives time and surface area for reabsorption. Its cells have microvilli (tiny finger-like projections) on the side facing the filtrate, greatly increasing the surface area.
[bio26-s2.3.5] The PCT cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP for active transport. The tubule is closely surrounded by capillaries, and its wall is one cell thick, so the distance to the blood is short.
[bio26-s2.3.6] The carriers in the PCT can reabsorb glucose only up to a limit. In untreated diabetes the blood glucose is so high that more glucose is filtered than the PCT can reabsorb, so glucose appears in the urine.
[bio26-s2.4.b] After the PCT, the rest of the nephron fine-tunes the filtrate. The loop of Henle makes the medulla salty, and this lets the collecting duct take back more water.
[bio26-s2.4.0] The ascending limb of the loop of Henle pumps salts (sodium and chloride ions) out into the tissue fluid of the medulla, and its wall does not let water through. This makes the medulla very salty, with the highest solute concentration deep in the medulla.
[bio26-s2.4.1] The descending limb lets water through, so water leaves it by osmosis into the salty medulla and is reabsorbed into the blood. The main job of the loop is to create this salty medulla, which lets the collecting duct reabsorb water later.
[bio26-s2.4.2] In the DCT more salts and water are reabsorbed, with the amounts adjusted to the body's needs.
[bio26-s2.4.3] The DCT also secretes some substances from the blood into the filtrate, such as hydrogen ions (to control blood pH), potassium ions and some drugs. Secretion is the opposite direction to reabsorption.
[bio26-s2.4.4] In the collecting duct, as it passes down through the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and is reabsorbed into the blood, so the urine becomes more concentrated. How much water is reabsorbed is controlled by ADH, a hormone. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation.
[bio26-s2.4.5] What remains in the collecting duct is urine: water, urea, excess salts and other wastes. Urine then drains into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s2.5.b] Comparing the composition of blood plasma, glomerular filtrate and urine shows what each part of the nephron has done. Each difference between them has one explanation.
[bio26-s2.5.0] Protein is present in plasma but absent from the filtrate and urine, because it is too large to be filtered.
[bio26-s2.5.1] Glucose and amino acids are present in plasma and filtrate at the same concentration, because filtration lets them through freely. They are absent from urine, because all of them are reabsorbed in the PCT.
[bio26-s2.5.2] Urea is at the same concentration in plasma and filtrate, because it is freely filtered. It is much more concentrated in urine, because most of the water is reabsorbed but most of the urea is not.
[bio26-s2.5.3] Salts are similar in plasma and filtrate. In urine the amount varies with intake, because salt reabsorption is adjusted.
[bio26-s2.5.4] Skill: given a data table of plasma, filtrate and urine, explain each difference. Absent from the filtrate means not filtered (too large); absent from the urine means reabsorbed; more concentrated in the urine means water was reabsorbed and the substance was not.
[bio26-s2.6.b] Following the fluid from the glomerulus to the renal pelvis, each part of the nephron does one main job.
[bio26-s2.6.0] Glomerulus and Bowman's capsule: filtration under pressure.
[bio26-s2.6.1] Proximal convoluted tubule: reabsorption of all glucose and amino acids, most salts and most water.
[bio26-s2.6.2] Loop of Henle: makes the medulla salty; water is reabsorbed from the descending limb.
[bio26-s2.6.3] Distal convoluted tubule: adjusts salts and water, and secretes hydrogen ions, potassium ions and some drugs.
[bio26-s2.6.4] Collecting duct: water is reabsorbed under ADH control.
[bio26-s2.6.5] Renal pelvis: receives the urine that is left.

CANDIDATES bio29-s3: Heartbeat
[bio29-s3.0.b] The heart does not wait for instructions to beat. A small patch of muscle in its own wall sets the pace and sends a wave of electrical signals that makes the chambers contract in the right order.
[bio29-s3.0.0] A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles (the two lower chambers) into the arteries.
[bio29-s3.0.1] An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
[bio29-s3.0.2] Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid.
[bio29-s3.0.3] The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium (an upper chamber), near where the superior vena cava (the large vein from the upper body) enters.
[bio29-s3.0.4] The SA node produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
[bio29-s3.1.b] The impulse from the SA node does not reach every part at once. It travels in a set route with a built-in pause, which makes the atria contract first and the ventricles second.
[bio29-s3.1.0] 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.1] 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles, so the ventricles cannot contract too early.
[bio29-s3.1.2] 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum (the wall between the two sides of the heart). The AV node delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.3] 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex (the pointed lower tip of the heart), and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
[bio29-s3.1.4] 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery, the two arteries leaving the heart. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.
[bio29-s3.1.5] Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.
[bio29-s3.2.b] The SA node sets a basic rhythm, but the body's needs change from moment to moment. Nerves and a hormone adjust the rate the SA node sets, so the heart can speed up or slow down.
[bio29-s3.2.0] The rate is changed to match the body's needs. It rises during exercise, because the muscles then need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂, which the faster-flowing blood delivers and carries away.
[bio29-s3.2.1] Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata (part of the brainstem) sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down.
[bio29-s3.2.2] The medulla responds to information from receptors, which detect changes in the body. For example, a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate, and a rise in blood pressure slows it.
[bio29-s3.2.3] Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction.
[bio29-s3.2.4] Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
[bio29-s3.2.5] Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart.
[bio29-s3.2.6] The artificial pacemaker detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.
[bio29-s3.3.b] Each time the ventricles contract, they force a surge of blood into the arteries. You can feel this surge as the pulse, which gives an easy way to measure heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.0] The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.1] The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe.
[bio29-s3.3.2] Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries (the tiny vessels between arteries and veins) before the blood reaches them.
[bio29-s3.3.3] Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes, so the reading is a resting one.
[bio29-s3.3.4] 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
[bio29-s3.3.5] 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
[bio29-s3.3.6] 4. Repeat three times and take the mean, which reduces the effect of any one miscount.
[bio29-s3.3.7] If asked what the pulse is, say it is a wave in the artery wall, not blood flowing past your finger.
[bio29-s3.4.b] Exercise makes the muscles need more oxygen, so the heart speeds up. Measuring the pulse before and after exercise shows this, and how quickly it recovers shows how fit someone is.
[bio29-s3.4.0] This is a standard class investigation with primary data (data you collect yourself). Measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value.
[bio29-s3.4.1] The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people.
[bio29-s3.4.2] Fitter people also tend to have a lower resting pulse, because each beat pumps more blood.
[bio29-s3.4.3] Independent variable (the one you change): activity level (rest, or after exercise). Dependent variable (the one you measure): pulse rate in beats per minute.
[bio29-s3.4.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.
[bio29-s3.5.b] Blood only flows round the body because the ventricles push it with force. That force, pressing on the walls of the vessels, is blood pressure, and when it stays too high it harms the body.
[bio29-s3.5.0] Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.5.1] Pressure is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles (small arteries) and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae.
[bio29-s3.5.2] Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
[bio29-s3.5.3] Pressure is given as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.4] Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.5] Hypertension usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
[bio29-s3.5.6] Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet, being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.5.7] High blood pressure is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.
[bio29-s3.6.b] The heart works non-stop, so its muscle needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose. It cannot get these from the blood inside its chambers, so it has a separate circulation of its own.
[bio29-s3.6.0] The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
[bio29-s3.6.1] The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve (the valve at the exit of the left ventricle). They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle.
[bio29-s3.6.2] These small arteries supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration (energy release using oxygen) that keeps it working continuously.
[bio29-s3.6.3] Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.
[bio29-s3.6.4] Diagram: a front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins. Label each.
[bio29-s3.7.b] The coronary arteries are narrow, so damage to them has serious effects. When they narrow or block, the heart muscle is starved of oxygen.
[bio29-s3.7.0] Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
[bio29-s3.7.1] If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction.
[bio29-s3.7.2] Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after a heart attack can break down the clot and restore blood flow to the starved muscle, so they limit the damage (see 'Industrial enzymes').
[bio29-s3.7.3] Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol, high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.7.4] Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight, which each remove a risk factor.
[bio29-s3.7.5] Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter (a thin tube) is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open.
[bio29-s3.7.6] Alternatively, the narrowed artery can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.

CANDIDATES bio29-s5: Blood vessels
[bio29-s5.0.b] Human blood never leaves a set of tubes called blood vessels, which is why the system is described as closed. The vessels differ in structure, and the differences make sense once you know the circuit and the three-layered wall that most of them share.
[bio29-s5.0.0] Blood flows in a closed circuit: heart, arteries, arterioles (the smallest arteries), capillaries, venules (the smallest veins), veins, and back to the heart.
[bio29-s5.0.1] Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood towards the heart. Capillaries link them inside the organs, and it is across capillary walls that materials are exchanged with the cells.
[bio29-s5.0.2] The walls of arteries and veins have three layers. The inner layer is the endothelium, one cell thick and smooth, which reduces friction as blood slides past. The middle layer is smooth muscle and elastic fibres.
[bio29-s5.0.3] The outer layer is tough connective tissue containing collagen fibres, which protects the vessel and stops it over-stretching. The space in the centre, through which blood flows, is the lumen.
[bio29-s5.0.4] Capillaries have only the endothelium, with no muscle, elastic or connective layers. This is why they can act as exchange surfaces, as the capillary block below shows.
[bio29-s5.1.b] Blood leaves the heart in surges at high pressure, and arteries have to carry it to the organs without bursting. Each feature of an artery's structure helps it cope with that pressure and keep the blood moving.
[bio29-s5.1.0] Role: arteries carry blood away from the heart to the organs, at high pressure. All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery, which takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
[bio29-s5.1.1] Thick wall, with a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic fibres, to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the ventricles (the heart's lower pumping chambers) without bursting.
[bio29-s5.1.2] Elastic fibres let the wall stretch as each surge of blood enters, then recoil between beats. This pushes the blood on, keeps the pressure up between beats and smooths the flow. The stretching is felt as the pulse.
[bio29-s5.1.3] Narrow lumen relative to the thickness of the wall, which helps keep the pressure high. In section, an artery keeps a round shape, because its thick wall holds it open.
[bio29-s5.1.4] No valves, except the semilunar valves (pocket-shaped valves) where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart. The high pressure keeps blood moving forwards, so valves are not needed along the length.
[bio29-s5.1.5] Blood flows fast and in spurts, and a cut artery spurts bright red blood. Bright red is the colour of oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s5.1.6] Arterioles are the smallest arteries, leading into the capillary networks. Their walls have a high proportion of smooth muscle, which contracts to narrow them (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to widen them (vasodilation).
[bio29-s5.1.7] By narrowing or widening, arterioles control how much blood flows to each organ, for example more to the muscles in exercise and more to the skin when hot.
[bio29-s5.2.b] By the time blood has passed through the capillaries its pressure is low, so veins must return it to the heart without a strong push behind it. Their structure reduces resistance, and valves and the squeezing of nearby muscles keep blood moving one way.
[bio29-s5.2.0] Role: veins carry blood from the organs back towards the heart, at low pressure. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary veins, which bring oxygenated blood from the lungs.
[bio29-s5.2.1] Thin wall, with a thin middle layer and few elastic fibres, because the pressure is low and there is no surge to withstand. The wall is easily squashed, so in section a vein often looks flattened or irregular.
[bio29-s5.2.2] Wide lumen, which reduces resistance to the slow-moving blood, so it can flow with little pressure behind it.
[bio29-s5.2.3] Valves: pocket-shaped, or semilunar, valves are found especially in the veins of the limbs and let blood flow only towards the heart. When blood starts to flow backwards, it fills the pockets, which close the vein.
[bio29-s5.2.4] Skeletal muscle pump: contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins that run between them, pushing blood along, and the valves prevent backflow. This is why standing still for a long time makes blood pool in the legs.
[bio29-s5.2.5] Breathing movements lower the pressure in the thorax (the chest cavity) during inhalation, which draws blood towards the heart. Veins next to arteries are also squeezed by the arterial pulse.
[bio29-s5.2.6] Venules are small veins that collect blood from capillary networks and join to form veins.
[bio29-s5.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a vein showing an open valve, with blood flowing towards the heart, and a closed valve, with its pockets filled and blocking backflow. Add arrows, and label the wall, lumen and valve.
[bio29-s5.3.b] Capillaries are where the circulatory system does its real work, because this is where materials pass between the blood and the cells. Everything about their structure makes that exchange quick and gives it time to happen.
[bio29-s5.3.0] Role: the exchange of materials between the blood and the body cells. Capillaries form dense networks, called capillary beds, in every tissue, so that no cell is far from one.
[bio29-s5.3.1] The wall is only one cell thick, consisting of endothelium only, with no muscle or elastic layers, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio29-s5.3.2] The lumen is very narrow, only wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, which brings them close to the wall.
[bio29-s5.3.3] Capillaries are so numerous that their total cross-section is large, so blood flows slowly through them, giving time for exchange.
[bio29-s5.3.4] Leaky: there are small gaps between the endothelial cells, so plasma fluid and small dissolved molecules can pass out, and white blood cells can squeeze through. Red blood cells and large plasma proteins cannot.
[bio29-s5.3.5] Pressure falls as blood flows through the capillaries, so blood leaving them enters the venules at low pressure. That is why veins have no pulse.
[bio29-s5.4.b] Cells do not touch the blood directly. Instead, part of the plasma leaks out of the capillaries and bathes the cells, and exchange takes place through this fluid.
[bio29-s5.4.0] At the arterial end of a capillary bed (the end nearer the arteriole), the blood pressure is higher and forces plasma out through the capillary wall into the spaces between the cells. Plasma is water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts and oxygen.
[bio29-s5.4.1] This fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes every cell and forms the cells' internal environment. Red blood cells and plasma proteins stay in the capillary because they are too large to pass through the gaps.
[bio29-s5.4.2] Substances pass between tissue fluid and cells by diffusion and active transport. Oxygen and nutrients enter the cells; CO₂, urea and other wastes leave them.
[bio29-s5.4.3] At the venous end the pressure is lower, and the plasma proteins left in the blood draw water back in by osmosis. So most of the tissue fluid, now carrying CO₂ and wastes, returns to the capillary.
[bio29-s5.4.4] The small amount of excess fluid is drained away by lymph vessels and returned to the blood.
[bio29-s5.4.5] Diagram: a capillary bed between an arteriole and a venule, surrounded by body cells. Add arrows for fluid leaving at the arterial end and returning at the venous end, and for O₂ and nutrients entering the cells and CO₂ and wastes leaving them.
[bio29-s5.5.b] Each vessel's structure matches its job, so the clearest way to revise is to compare the three feature by feature. Learn the pairing of each feature with its reason, because exam questions ask you to relate structure to role.
[bio29-s5.5.0] Direction of flow: arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it towards the heart; capillaries link arterioles to venules within an organ.
[bio29-s5.5.1] Pressure: high and pulsing in arteries; falling through the capillaries; low and steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.2] Wall: thick, muscular and elastic in arteries to withstand pressure; thin, with little muscle and elastic tissue, in veins; one cell thick, endothelium only, in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.3] Lumen: narrow relative to the wall in arteries; wide in veins; just wide enough for one red blood cell in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.4] Valves: none in arteries, apart from at the heart; present in veins; none in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.5] Speed of flow: fast in arteries; slow in capillaries; slow but steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.6] Oxygen content: arteries usually carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood, except in the pulmonary circuit, and capillaries are where exchange happens. This is not how the vessels are defined; they are defined by direction of flow.
[bio29-s5.5.7] Exchange: no exchange with the tissues through the thick walls of arteries and veins; all exchange happens in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.8] Diagram: transverse sections of an artery (round, thick wall, narrow lumen), a vein (thin wall, same three layers, wide lumen, often flattened) and a capillary (ring of one or two endothelial cells round one red blood cell). Label each layer and the lumen.
