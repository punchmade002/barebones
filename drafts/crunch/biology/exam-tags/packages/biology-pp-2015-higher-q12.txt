CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio14-s2: Investigation: rate of photosynthesis
[bio14-s2.0.b] Before measuring anything, you need to know what "rate" means and which conditions can speed photosynthesis up or hold it back. This theory is what every hypothesis in the investigation rests on.
[bio14-s2.0.0] The rate of photosynthesis is the amount of product made, or reactant used, per unit time. It is usually measured as the rate of oxygen production, in bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute, but it can also be measured as the rate of CO₂ uptake.
[bio14-s2.0.1] Oxygen is a good thing to measure because it is a product: it comes from the splitting of water in the light-dependent stage (the first stage, which needs light).
[bio14-s2.0.2] Light intensity: light supplies the energy for the light-dependent stage, so more light means more ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and hydrogen are made per minute and the rate rises, until another factor holds it back.
[bio14-s2.0.3] Carbon dioxide concentration: CO₂ is the raw material of the light-independent stage (the second stage, which builds glucose), so more CO₂ raises the rate, until another factor holds it back.
[bio14-s2.0.4] Temperature: the light-independent stage is controlled by enzymes. Raising the temperature increases the rate, because molecules move faster and collide more often with the enzymes' active sites (the part of the enzyme where the reaction happens), up to an optimum.
[bio14-s2.0.5] Above the optimum the rate falls quickly, because the enzymes are denatured (their shape is permanently changed, so they stop working). There is more on this in Enzymes as catalysts.
[bio14-s2.0.6] A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply, which holds back the rate of a process even when the other factors are increased. At any moment the rate is limited by whichever of light intensity, CO₂ concentration or temperature is furthest from its best value.
[bio14-s2.0.7] Raising a limiting factor increases the rate. Raising a factor that is not limiting has no effect, because something else is still holding the rate back.
[bio14-s2.1.b] The bubbles you count are not the whole story. A plant is also respiring, and respiration uses oxygen, so what you measure is a net result.
[bio14-s2.1.0] A plant respires all the time, using some of the oxygen it makes, so the oxygen given off is photosynthesis minus respiration. This is called the net (apparent) rate.
[bio14-s2.1.1] In very dim light the two processes are equal and no oxygen is given off, because all the oxygen made is used up. This is the compensation point.
[bio14-s2.1.2] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, so the plant takes in oxygen for respiration.
[bio14-s2.2.b] A good investigation changes one thing and measures one thing. Planning starts with a clear aim, a prediction for each factor, and a plant that lets you see the result.
[bio14-s2.2.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of light intensity (or CO₂ concentration, or temperature) on the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed. Write one investigation per factor, changing only that factor, so you know which one caused any change.
[bio14-s2.2.1] Hypothesis for light: as light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases until it levels off. This rests on the theory that light drives the light-dependent stage until something else becomes limiting.
[bio14-s2.2.2] Hypothesis for CO₂: as CO₂ concentration increases, the rate increases until it levels off. This rests on CO₂ being the raw material of the light-independent stage.
[bio14-s2.2.3] Hypothesis for temperature: the rate increases with temperature up to an optimum and then decreases, because the enzymes of the light-independent stage work faster until they are denatured.
[bio14-s2.2.4] Elodea (Canadian pondweed) is the standard school plant. It is aquatic, so the oxygen it gives off appears as visible bubbles from the cut stem, which can be counted or collected. It is also easy to obtain, grows quickly and photosynthesises rapidly.
[bio14-s2.3.b] Next you decide what to change, what to measure and what to hold steady, and gather the apparatus. The variables below are for the light-intensity investigation.
[bio14-s2.3.0] The independent variable (the one you change) is light intensity, set by the lamp's distance from the plant or measured with a light meter in lux.
[bio14-s2.3.1] The dependent variable (the one you measure) is the rate of oxygen production, as bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute.
[bio14-s2.3.2] Controlled variables (kept the same, so they cannot affect the result): temperature (water bath), CO₂ concentration (same sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration), the same pondweed (species, length, leaves), the same lamp and bulb, the adjustment time and the counting period.
[bio14-s2.3.3] For the other factors, swap the independent variable for CO₂ concentration or temperature, and keep light intensity constant.
[bio14-s2.3.4] Control: an identical set-up kept in darkness (lamp off, tube covered with foil), or a tube with no plant, gives no bubbles. This shows that the bubbles come from photosynthesis by the plant in the light, not from gas leaving the solution.
[bio14-s2.3.5] Equipment: fresh Elodea, scalpel or scissors, boiling tube or test tube, paper clip to weigh the pondweed down, beaker as a water bath (or an electric water bath), thermometer, bench lamp (an LED bulb gives out less heat), metre stick, stopwatch, and a light meter (optional).
[bio14-s2.3.6] Chemicals: sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) powder, or solutions of known concentration, which supplies the plant with CO₂, in water that has been boiled and cooled.
[bio14-s2.3.7] For measuring volume: a funnel and graduated tube, or a capillary tube attached to a syringe, called a photosynthometer. A glowing splint is optional, for testing the gas.
[bio14-s2.4.b] This basic set-up is used for all three investigations. Only the factor you change differs, so learn it once and adapt it.
[bio14-s2.4.0] 1. Fill a boiling tube with water containing a set concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate. It dissolves to supply CO₂ to the plant, so that CO₂ is not limiting (except in the carbon dioxide investigation).
[bio14-s2.4.1] 2. Cut a healthy shoot of pondweed about 5 to 8 cm long, cutting the stem cleanly and slantwise under water.
[bio14-s2.4.2] 3. Place it in the tube upside down, with the cut end at the top, weighed down with a paper clip.
[bio14-s2.4.3] 4. Stand the tube in a beaker of water at a constant temperature, for example 25 °C, checked with a thermometer, so the temperature stays steady.
[bio14-s2.4.4] 5. Carry out the investigation in a darkened room, so that the lamp is the only light source.
[bio14-s2.4.5] Counting: let the plant adjust to each new condition for about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles leaving the cut end in one minute. Repeat the count three times at each setting and calculate the mean.
[bio14-s2.4.6] Collecting (more accurate): place an inverted funnel over the pondweed in a beaker, with a water-filled graduated tube over its stem, and record the gas collected in a set time, for example 5 minutes. Alternatively, draw the gas up a capillary tube with a syringe and measure the bubble's length.
[bio14-s2.4.7] Volume per unit time is more accurate than counting, because bubbles vary in size. Optional gas test: it relights a glowing splint, so it is rich in oxygen, but not pure oxygen, since dissolved nitrogen and carbon dioxide also come out.
[bio14-s2.5.b] Here the independent variable is light intensity, which you change by moving the lamp. The challenge is changing the light without also changing the temperature.
[bio14-s2.5.0] 1. Set up as in the basic set-up.
[bio14-s2.5.1] 2. Place the lamp 10 cm from the pondweed, measured with a metre stick from the bulb to the plant.
[bio14-s2.5.2] 3. Allow 5 minutes to adjust, then count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.5.3] 4. Move the lamp to 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, and repeat.
[bio14-s2.5.4] 5. Check the water temperature at each distance and keep it constant, by adding cold or warm water to the bath.
[bio14-s2.5.5] A lamp close to the plant also heats it, which would change two variables at once. Use an LED bulb, or place a beaker or flat-sided tank of water between the lamp and the plant as a heat shield, and check the thermometer.
[bio14-s2.5.6] Moving the lamp further away lowers the light intensity as the inverse square of the distance (light intensity ∝ 1/d²): doubling the distance gives a quarter of the light intensity. It does not fall in simple proportion.
[bio14-s2.5.7] So plot the rate against 1/d² to show the relationship with light intensity, or measure the light intensity at each position with a light meter.
[bio14-s2.6.b] Here the independent variable is CO₂ concentration, which you change by using sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of different strengths. Light and temperature are held constant.
[bio14-s2.6.0] 1. Make up a series of sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of increasing concentration, for example 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1%, using water that has been boiled and cooled. Boiling removes dissolved CO₂, so the 0% tube has very little.
[bio14-s2.6.1] 2. Keep the lamp at a fixed distance close to the plant, for example 10 cm, and keep the temperature constant, so that CO₂ is the only thing that changes.
[bio14-s2.6.2] 3. Place the same pondweed in each solution in turn, allow 5 minutes to adjust, and count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.6.3] 4. Calculate the mean for each concentration.
[bio14-s2.7.b] Here the independent variable is temperature, changed with water baths. Light and CO₂ are held constant.
[bio14-s2.7.0] 1. Set up as in the basic set-up, with a fixed lamp distance and a fixed sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration.
[bio14-s2.7.1] 2. Place the tube in a water bath at a range of temperatures, for example 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C, using ice or warm water or electric water baths, and check each with a thermometer.
[bio14-s2.7.2] 3. Let the tube reach the bath temperature, about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.7.3] 4. If the first pondweed stops producing bubbles, use a fresh piece for the higher temperatures, and note that you have done so.
[bio14-s2.7.4] Warm water holds less dissolved gas, so some bubbles at high temperatures can come from gas leaving the solution. The darkness control at each temperature checks this.
[bio14-s2.8.b] This investigation mixes electricity, water, heat and sharp tools, so each hazard needs its own precaution.
[bio14-s2.8.0] Electricity and water: keep the lamp, its cable and plug away from the water bath, dry your hands before touching switches, and do not move a lamp while it is on and hot.
[bio14-s2.8.1] Lamp bulbs, other than LEDs, get very hot and can burn, so let them cool before moving them.
[bio14-s2.8.2] Take care with the scalpel: cut on a tile, away from the body. Take care with hot water baths and glassware.
[bio14-s2.8.3] Wash your hands after handling pondweed and pond water.
[bio14-s2.8.4] Elodea canadensis is a non-native invasive species (one that spreads and harms native wildlife) in Ireland. Never put leftover pondweed or its water into ponds, lakes, rivers or drains; dispose of it in compost or general waste.
[bio14-s2.9.b] The results table and graph turn counts into a relationship you can explain. For each factor the shape of the graph tells you what is limiting the rate.
[bio14-s2.9.0] Record the primary data (the results you collect yourself) in a table: the independent variable (distance in cm and 1/d², concentration in %, or temperature in °C), the three counts and the mean number of bubbles per minute.
[bio14-s2.9.1] Plot the mean rate on the y-axis against the independent variable on the x-axis, with units, and join the points with a smooth curve or straight lines.
[bio14-s2.9.2] Light intensity: as the lamp moves closer, the rate increases, so it falls as distance increases. At high intensity the curve levels off in a plateau, because another factor, CO₂ concentration or temperature, has become limiting.
[bio14-s2.9.3] CO₂ concentration: the rate increases with CO₂ concentration and then levels off, because light intensity or temperature becomes limiting. At 0% (boiled water) there are few or no bubbles.
[bio14-s2.9.4] Temperature: the rate rises up to an optimum, then falls steeply above it, as the enzymes of the light-independent stage are denatured. Bubbling may stop at the highest temperatures. The curve is not symmetrical: the fall is steeper than the rise.
[bio14-s2.9.5] Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data and whether it supports the hypothesis. On the levelling-off part of the graph, name the factor that is now limiting.
[bio14-s2.10.b] Examiners often ask you to draw the apparatus or sketch the expected graphs. Learn the labels for each.
[bio14-s2.10.0] Diagram: the set-up. Labels: lamp, metre stick, heat shield, beaker water bath, thermometer, boiling tube of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, upside-down pondweed with bubbles at the cut end (or the funnel and graduated collecting tube).
[bio14-s2.10.1] Diagram: two sketch graphs. (a) Rate against light intensity, rising then levelling off, plus a higher curve labelled higher CO₂ concentration; label the rise light limiting and the plateau CO₂ or temperature limiting. (b) Rate against temperature, rising to an optimum then falling steeply.
[bio14-s2.11.b] Your own results are only one source. You can also use other people's data to support a conclusion, and apply the idea of limiting factors to growing crops.
[bio14-s2.11.0] Secondary data are results collected by others: other groups' class results, textbook or published graphs, or data-logger results from an oxygen or CO₂ sensor. Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility (whether others get the same result).
[bio14-s2.11.1] Interpreting a graph of rate against light intensity at two CO₂ concentrations (or two temperatures): where the two curves overlap, light is the limiting factor, because raising CO₂ makes no difference.
[bio14-s2.11.2] Where the two curves separate and each levels off, CO₂ (or temperature) is limiting, because raising it raises the plateau.
[bio14-s2.11.3] Applying limiting factors: commercial glasshouse growers raise the yield of crops such as tomatoes and lettuce by supplying extra light (artificial lighting), heat and CO₂. Burning paraffin or propane gives both heat and CO₂, or CO₂ gas can be added.
[bio14-s2.11.4] Each factor is kept just high enough that it is not limiting. Raising a factor that is not limiting wastes fuel and money and adds to emissions.
[bio14-s2.12.b] No school investigation is perfect. Knowing where the errors come from tells you how to reduce them.
[bio14-s2.12.0] Bubbles vary in size and can be missed or merge when the rate is fast, so counting is imprecise. Collecting the volume of gas is more accurate.
[bio14-s2.12.1] Other light in the room, such as daylight or other groups' lamps, changes the light intensity, so work in a darkened room.
[bio14-s2.12.2] Temperature can drift, especially with a hot bulb close to the plant, so check it and adjust it.
[bio14-s2.12.3] The gas is not pure oxygen, and some oxygen dissolves in the water instead of forming bubbles, so the bubbles underestimate the true rate. The measured rate is also a net rate, after respiration.
[bio14-s2.12.4] The carbon dioxide from sodium hydrogencarbonate is used up over time, and the plant's response may change, so use fresh solution, a fresh plant for each run if needed, and repeat the whole series.
[bio14-s2.12.5] Allowing too little time to adjust after each change gives rates that still reflect the previous setting.
[bio14-s2.12.6] Repeat each reading at least three times and calculate a mean, so a single odd count has less effect. Compare your results with other groups' results.

CANDIDATES bio14-s4: The light-independent reactions
[bio14-s4.0.b] The second stage of photosynthesis is where glucose is actually built. It uses the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage as its energy and hydrogen supply, and in return sends the empty carriers back.
[bio14-s4.0.0] Location: the stroma (the fluid that fills the chloroplast), which contains the enzymes for these reactions. The reactions need no light directly, but they depend on the ATP and NADPH made in the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoids.
[bio14-s4.0.1] 1. Carbon dioxide diffuses into the stroma, from the air spaces of the leaf through the cell and the chloroplast's membranes. It is taken up, or fixed, by combining with a carbon dioxide-accepting compound already present in the stroma.
[bio14-s4.0.2] 2. NADPH and ATP from the light-dependent reactions enter the reactions, because they supply the hydrogen and the energy that CO₂ needs to become glucose.
[bio14-s4.0.3] 3. Protons and electrons, that is hydrogen, are transferred from NADPH to the carbon compounds made from CO₂. This adds hydrogen to the carbon, which is reduction, turning CO₂ into carbohydrate. NADPH becomes NADP⁺.
[bio14-s4.0.4] 4. The energy to achieve this conversion comes from ATP, which is broken down to ADP + Pi (phosphate).
[bio14-s4.0.5] 5. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the product. Six molecules of CO₂ are fixed for each molecule of glucose, because each supplies one of its six carbon atoms. The glucose is then used, or converted to sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s4.0.6] 6. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return to the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes, where they are reloaded to ATP and NADPH and can be re-used.
[bio14-s4.0.7] The reactions form a cycle, the Calvin cycle. Most of the carbon compounds made regenerate the CO₂-accepting compound, so the cycle can keep fixing CO₂, and the rest is built into glucose.
[bio14-s4.1.b] Once you know the inputs and outputs, you can explain why this stage responds to temperature and CO₂ and why it stops soon after dark, even though it uses no light directly.
[bio14-s4.1.0] Summary in words: carbon dioxide + hydrogen (from NADPH) + energy (from ATP) → glucose, with NADP⁺ and ADP + Pi released, so the carriers are free to be reloaded.
[bio14-s4.1.1] Every step is catalysed by enzymes in the stroma, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, and falls when the enzymes are denatured. This is why temperature affects the rate of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s4.1.2] CO₂ is the raw material of this stage, so its supply directly limits the rate of glucose production.
[bio14-s4.1.3] In darkness the light-dependent reactions stop, so ATP and NADPH run out within a short time. The light-independent reactions then stop too, even though they do not use light directly.
[bio14-s4.2.b] Photosynthesis is best understood as two linked stages joined by recycled carrier molecules. Modelling it means showing what goes in and out of each stage and what the carriers move between them.
[bio14-s4.2.0] Stage 1, the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes (the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast): light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll, splits water and drives electron transfer. This produces O₂, which is released, and loads the transfer molecules: ADP + Pi → ATP and NADP⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.1] Stage 2, the light-independent reactions in the stroma: the transfer molecules are unloaded, which is why this stage needs stage 1. NADPH gives its protons and electrons to CO₂, and ATP gives the energy, producing glucose. NADPH → NADP⁺ and ATP → ADP + Pi.
[bio14-s4.2.2] The transfer molecules link the two stages. They carry energy (ATP) and electrons and hydrogen (NADPH) from stage 1 to stage 2, and return empty, as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺, from stage 2 to stage 1.
[bio14-s4.2.3] Because the transfer molecules are recycled, the chloroplast needs only a small supply of each.
[bio14-s4.2.4] Tracing the atoms: the oxygen gas comes from water (stage 1). The carbon and the oxygen in glucose come from CO₂ (stage 2). The hydrogen in glucose comes from water, carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.5] Diagram: thylakoid (stage 1) beside stroma (stage 2). Stage 1: in light, H₂O; out O₂. Stage 2: in CO₂; out glucose. Two loops between them: ATP across and ADP + Pi back, NADPH across and NADP⁺ back, each arrow labelled energy, or protons and electrons.

CANDIDATES bio14-s5: The chloroplast's internal structure
[bio14-s5.0.b] Photosynthesis happens inside the chloroplast, and its two stages happen in different parts of it. Each internal part has a structure that suits its job.
[bio14-s5.0.0] Envelope: two membranes, an outer and an inner, surround the chloroplast. The inner membrane controls which substances enter and leave, such as CO₂ going in and the products of photosynthesis going out, so the stroma (the fluid inside the envelope) keeps its enzymes and conditions separate from the cytoplasm.
[bio14-s5.0.1] Thylakoids are flattened membrane sacs inside the chloroplast, separate from the envelope. Each one encloses a fluid-filled space called the thylakoid space, or lumen.
[bio14-s5.0.2] Thylakoids are stacked like piles of coins into grana (singular: granum). Grana are joined by single, unstacked thylakoid membranes called intergranal lamellae, so the whole system is one connected network of membranes.
[bio14-s5.0.3] The thylakoid membranes hold the pigments (molecules that absorb light, such as chlorophyll) in their clusters and reaction centres, the electron transport chains (chains of electron carriers) and the enzyme ATP synthase. This is why they are the site of the light-dependent reactions, the first stage of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s5.0.4] The stroma contains the enzymes of the light-independent reactions, the second stage of photosynthesis, so it is the site of those reactions. It also holds starch grains, which are a temporary store of glucose, lipid droplets, ribosomes (the small structures that make proteins) and a small loop of DNA.
[bio14-s5.0.5] The chloroplast makes some of its own proteins, including some of the proteins of the thylakoid membrane and of the light-independent reactions, using its own DNA and ribosomes. There is more on this in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio14-s5.0.6] Diagram: an electron-microscope-level drawing of a chloroplast in section, labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, stroma, granum, thylakoid, thylakoid space, intergranal lamella, starch grain, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio14-s5.1.b] The light-dependent reactions need a chain that moves electrons along and releases their energy bit by bit. In the chloroplast, this chain is built into the thylakoid membrane.
[bio14-s5.1.0] An electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers that pass electrons from one to the next, each at a slightly lower energy level. It releases energy in small steps that can be used, such as water running downhill in stages, losing height and so energy at each step.
[bio14-s5.1.1] In the thylakoid membrane, the chain is made of a number of large protein complexes (large groups of proteins that work together) embedded in, or spanning, the membrane. Smaller mobile electron carriers that move within or along the membrane link the complexes, carrying electrons from one to the next.
[bio14-s5.1.2] Some protein complexes contain the clusters of pigment molecules and the reaction-centre chlorophyll, the special chlorophyll molecule that loses electrons when light hits it. Others pass electrons on and use their energy.
[bio14-s5.1.3] NADP⁺, a carrier that takes up electrons and hydrogen, is reduced (gains electrons) to NADPH, as NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH, on the stroma side of the membrane. This means NADPH is made right where the light-independent reactions use it.
[bio14-s5.1.4] Embedding the complexes in a membrane holds them in a fixed order and close together, so electrons pass quickly and in the right sequence. The energy released can move protons across the membrane, setting up a proton gradient (a difference in proton concentration across it) that drives ATP making.
[bio14-s5.2.b] The energy released by the electron transport chain is used to move protons (H⁺) into a small enclosed space. The protons then flow back out through an enzyme, and this flow powers the making of ATP.
[bio14-s5.2.0] As electrons pass along the chain, protons (H⁺, hydrogen ions) are moved from the stroma into the thylakoid space. Protons released by the photolysis of water (splitting water using light energy) are also released there, so the small, enclosed thylakoid space builds up a high concentration of protons.
[bio14-s5.2.1] Photolysis happens on the thylakoid-space side of the membrane, and its equation is 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂, so the protons it makes go straight into the thylakoid space.
[bio14-s5.2.2] The protons flow back out of the thylakoid space into the stroma through ATP synthase in the thylakoid membrane. The enzyme uses this flow to make ATP from ADP and phosphate (Pi), as ADP + Pi → ATP, on the stroma side, where the light-independent reactions need it.
[bio14-s5.2.3] The same mechanism makes ATP in mitochondria. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio14-s5.3.b] Exams ask how the structure of the chloroplast suits it to photosynthesis. The key idea is that each feature gives the reactions more room to work, or keeps them in the right place.
[bio14-s5.3.0] Large surface area: the many stacked thylakoids give a huge area of membrane for pigment clusters, electron transport chains and ATP synthase, so more light can be absorbed and more ATP and NADPH can be made.
[bio14-s5.3.1] Organisation of pigments: grouping many pigment molecules around each reaction centre funnels the energy of photons absorbed over a wide area, and over a range of colours, to the reaction centres. This means more of the light energy reaches the reaction centres.
[bio14-s5.3.2] Separate compartments: the thylakoid space is a small, enclosed compartment, so protons can be concentrated in it to drive ATP synthase. The thylakoid membrane is not freely permeable to protons, so the gradient is kept.
[bio14-s5.3.3] Two stages side by side: the light-dependent reactions on the membranes and the light-independent reactions in the stroma are next to each other, so ATP and NADPH are released into the stroma directly where they are used. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return only a short distance.
[bio14-s5.3.4] The stroma holds all the enzymes of the light-independent reactions in one compartment, with their substrates (the substances they act on), so they meet easily. It stores its glucose temporarily as starch.
[bio14-s5.3.5] Own DNA and ribosomes let the chloroplast make some of its own proteins on the spot, such as the membrane and enzyme proteins its two stages need.
[bio14-s5.3.6] Related: the palisade mesophyll, the leaf cells that do most photosynthesis, are packed with chloroplasts, which can move within the cell towards or away from the light.
[bio14-s5.3.7] Diagram: a thylakoid membrane, stroma above and thylakoid space below. Draw pigment clusters, protein complexes and electron carriers with electron arrows, water splitting and H⁺ building up inside, and ATP synthase making ATP in the stroma. Label thylakoid membrane, thylakoid space, stroma, protein complex, mobile electron carrier, ATP synthase.

CANDIDATES bio15-s3: First-stage process
[bio15-s3.0.b] Respiration starts with glycolysis, a short pathway that partly breaks down glucose before the cell knows whether oxygen is available. It is the first stage of both aerobic respiration (with oxygen) and anaerobic respiration (without oxygen).
[bio15-s3.0.0] Glycolysis means 'sugar splitting'. It takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid part of the cell outside the organelles), is controlled by about ten enzymes working in sequence, and needs no oxygen.
[bio15-s3.0.1] 1. Activation: two ATP molecules are used to add phosphate groups to the glucose (C₆, a six-carbon sugar). This makes the glucose more reactive, so it is an investment of energy that is repaid later.
[bio15-s3.0.2] 2. Splitting: the six-carbon sugar is split into two three-carbon molecules.
[bio15-s3.0.3] 3. Oxidation: hydrogen, as electrons and protons, is removed from each three-carbon molecule by dehydrogenase enzymes and picked up by NAD⁺ (a carrier molecule that accepts hydrogen), forming NADH. Two NADH are made per glucose.
[bio15-s3.0.4] 4. ATP generation: four ATP are made as phosphate groups are transferred to ADP. Net gain: 4 made minus 2 used, so 2 ATP per glucose.
[bio15-s3.0.5] 5. Product: two molecules of pyruvate (pyruvic acid, C₃) per glucose. Pyruvate still holds most of the energy of the glucose, which is why much more energy can be released from it later.
[bio15-s3.0.6] Summary: glucose + 2 ADP + 2 Pi + 2 NAD⁺ → 2 pyruvate + 2 ATP (net) + 2 NADH. No CO₂ is released in glycolysis, because the carbons all stay in the two pyruvate molecules.
[bio15-s3.1.b] Glycolysis happens in almost every living cell, and it is not limited to glucose from food.
[bio15-s3.1.0] Glycolysis happens in almost every living cell, prokaryotic (no nucleus) and eukaryotic (with a nucleus). This suggests it evolved early in the history of life, before there was oxygen in the atmosphere, which fits with it needing no oxygen.
[bio15-s3.1.1] Other six-carbon sugars, such as fructose, and the glucose from glycogen or starch enter the same pathway.
[bio15-s3.2.b] Two transfer molecules, ATP and NAD⁺, carry energy and hydrogen through glycolysis. How each one is used explains why the cell must have a way to carry on without oxygen.
[bio15-s3.2.0] ATP: two are used to activate the glucose and four are made by transferring phosphate from the sugar molecules directly to ADP. The net 2 ATP is all the energy anaerobic respiration releases per glucose.
[bio15-s3.2.1] NAD⁺: the cell has only a small supply. Glycolysis converts NAD⁺ to NADH, so glycolysis stops if NADH is not turned back into NAD⁺, because there would be no NAD⁺ left to accept hydrogen.
[bio15-s3.2.2] The fate of NADH depends on oxygen. With oxygen, it passes its electrons to the electron transport chain in the mitochondrion (the organelle where the second stage of respiration happens). There is more on this in Second-stage process.
[bio15-s3.2.3] Without oxygen, NADH passes its hydrogen to pyruvate instead, a process called fermentation. Either way, NAD⁺ is regenerated, so glycolysis can carry on.
[bio15-s3.3.b] Without oxygen, pyruvate is not passed to the mitochondrion, and glycolysis would stop if NADH were not turned back into NAD⁺. Fermentation solves this problem, and it works in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s3.3.0] When oxygen is absent or too scarce, pyruvate is not passed to the mitochondrion. Instead, in the cytoplasm, NADH passes its hydrogen to pyruvate, or to a product made from it. This regenerates NAD⁺, so glycolysis can keep running and keep making its 2 ATP per glucose.
[bio15-s3.3.1] The purpose of fermentation is to regenerate NAD⁺, not to make more ATP. No ATP is made after glycolysis.
[bio15-s3.3.2] Lactic acid fermentation, in animal muscle cells and in lactic acid bacteria such as those used to make yogurt: pyruvate (C₃) takes hydrogen from NADH and becomes lactic acid (lactate, C₃). This is one step, and no CO₂ is released.
[bio15-s3.3.3] Overall for lactic acid fermentation: glucose → 2 lactic acid, with a net 2 ATP.
[bio15-s3.3.4] Alcoholic (ethanol) fermentation, in yeast and in plant cells short of oxygen, such as waterlogged roots: pyruvate (C₃) first loses one carbon as CO₂, forming a two-carbon compound. This then takes hydrogen from NADH and becomes ethanol (C₂).
[bio15-s3.3.5] Alcoholic fermentation is two steps, with one CO₂ released per pyruvate. Overall: glucose → 2 ethanol + 2 CO₂, with a net 2 ATP.
[bio15-s3.3.6] Lactic acid has three carbons, like pyruvate; ethanol has two, because one carbon is lost as CO₂.
[bio15-s3.4.b] Whether fermentation happens, and what it produces, depends on the organism and on the conditions. Fermentation also releases far less energy than aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s3.4.0] The product depends on the organism's enzymes. Animals and many bacteria make lactic acid, while yeast and plants make ethanol.
[bio15-s3.4.1] Whether fermentation happens at all depends on conditions. Yeast and muscle respire aerobically when oxygen is available and switch to fermentation when it is not. There is more on the conditions in Investigation: conditions for fermentation.
[bio15-s3.4.2] Fermentation releases little energy because glucose is only partly broken down. Lactic acid and ethanol still contain much of the original energy; ethanol is a fuel, for example in biofuels.
[bio15-s3.4.3] Diagram: flow chart. Glucose (C₆), 2 ATP used, 4 ATP made, 2 NAD⁺ → 2 NADH, gives 2 pyruvate (C₃). Branches: (a) oxygen: mitochondrion; (b) no oxygen, animals, some bacteria: lactic acid (C₃); (c) no oxygen, yeast, plants: CO₂, ethanol (C₂). NADH → NAD⁺ loops to glycolysis. Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion.

CANDIDATES bio15-s4: Second-stage process
[bio15-s4.0.b] Glycolysis (the first stage of respiration, in the cytoplasm) only splits glucose part of the way and ends with pyruvate. If oxygen is available, the cell passes pyruvate on to the second stage, and the first job there is a short link step that prepares it for the citric acid cycle.
[bio15-s4.0.0] When oxygen is present, each pyruvate (a 3-carbon molecule, C₃) made in glycolysis moves from the cytoplasm into the matrix of a mitochondrion. The matrix is the fluid-filled space inside the inner membrane.
[bio15-s4.0.1] In the matrix, pyruvate is broken down. One carbon atom is removed as one molecule of carbon dioxide, and hydrogen is removed and picked up by NAD⁺ (a hydrogen carrier), which turns it into NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.2] The remaining two-carbon (acetyl) group is joined to coenzyme A, a carrier molecule, forming acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA, C₂). Per pyruvate: pyruvate + coenzyme A + NAD⁺ → acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.3] Each glucose gives two pyruvate, so this link step happens twice per glucose, producing 2 CO₂ and 2 NADH.
[bio15-s4.1.b] Acetyl CoA is the fuel for the citric acid cycle, which strips the carbon from the acetyl group off as CO₂ and loads hydrogen onto carriers. Because the cycle rebuilds its starting compound every turn, it can keep running as long as acetyl CoA keeps arriving.
[bio15-s4.1.0] The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, is a cycle of enzyme-controlled reactions in the matrix of the mitochondrion, not on the cristae (the folds of the inner membrane).
[bio15-s4.1.1] 1. The acetyl CoA passes its two-carbon acetyl group into the cycle, where it joins a four-carbon compound to form citric acid (C₆), which gives the cycle its name. Coenzyme A is released to collect another acetyl group.
[bio15-s4.1.2] 2. In a series of steps, citric acid is broken down back to the four-carbon compound. This is regenerated to accept the next acetyl group, which is why it is a cycle.
[bio15-s4.1.3] 3. During each turn, two carbon atoms are removed as two molecules of CO₂, so the carbon of the acetyl group leaves as CO₂.
[bio15-s4.1.4] 4. Hydrogen, as protons and electrons, is removed at several steps and loaded onto NAD⁺, forming NADH. A small amount of ATP, one per turn, is also made directly.
[bio15-s4.1.5] The cycle turns twice for each glucose, once per acetyl CoA. With the link step, all six carbon atoms of glucose have now left as six molecules of CO₂, matching the 6CO₂ in the respiration equation. Glycolysis releases none.
[bio15-s4.1.6] The specification's products are CO₂ and H₂O. The CO₂ is released directly. The hydrogen removed is carried by NADH to the electron transport chain, where it ends up in water, so water is not made in the cycle itself.
[bio15-s4.1.7] The cycle does not need oxygen directly, but it stops without oxygen, because NADH cannot be unloaded and NAD⁺ runs out.
[bio15-s4.2.b] Most of the energy in glucose is still locked up in the hydrogen carried by NADH. The electron transport chain releases it in small steps and uses it to make ATP, which is why far more ATP per glucose is made in the second stage than the net 2 ATP of glycolysis. Aerobic respiration (respiration with oxygen) therefore releases many times more energy per glucose than fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.0] The electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers (protein complexes and mobile carriers) in the inner mitochondrial membrane, especially on the folds called cristae.
[bio15-s4.2.1] 1. NADH from glycolysis, the link step and the citric acid cycle delivers its high-energy electrons and protons to the start of the chain. NAD⁺ is released to collect more.
[bio15-s4.2.2] 2. The electrons are passed from carrier to carrier, each at a lower energy level. The energy released at each transfer is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi (phosphate).
[bio15-s4.2.3] 3. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor. At the end of the chain, low-energy electrons combine with oxygen and protons (H⁺) to form water: 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ + O₂ → 2H₂O. It is the only step of respiration using oxygen directly, and the source of the 6H₂O in the equation.
[bio15-s4.2.4] 4. If oxygen is absent, electrons cannot leave the end of the chain, so the whole chain stops. NADH cannot be unloaded, so the citric acid cycle and the link step stop too, and the cell can then only carry out glycolysis and fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.5] Cyanide is a poison because it blocks the last complex of the chain, which has the same effect as having no oxygen: the chain stops and aerobic ATP production collapses.
[bio15-s4.2.6] 5. The ATP made is exported from the mitochondrion to the cytoplasm and used for other biochemical reactions in the cell, such as synthesis of proteins, active transport and muscle contraction.
[bio15-s4.2.7] Most of the ATP from aerobic respiration is made here, via a proton gradient and ATP synthase. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.b] Respiration can be modelled as two stages joined by carrier molecules that shuttle electrons, hydrogen, acetyl groups and energy around the cell. Putting the stages together shows where each product comes from and where each reactant is used. There is more on the membrane comparison with photosynthesis in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm: glucose becomes 2 pyruvate, a net 2 ATP is made directly, and NAD⁺ is converted to NADH.
[bio15-s4.3.1] Stage 2 takes place in the mitochondrion. The link step and the citric acid cycle, in the matrix, turn pyruvate into acetyl CoA and then into CO₂, converting NAD⁺ to NADH and making a little ATP directly.
[bio15-s4.3.2] The electron transport chain, on the inner membrane (cristae), unloads NADH to NAD⁺, passes electrons to oxygen to form water, and makes most of the ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio15-s4.3.3] Transfer molecules carry energy and electrons between the stages. NAD⁺/NADH carries electrons and hydrogen from the breakdown of glucose (glycolysis, link step and cycle) to the electron transport chain, and returns empty. Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group into the cycle and returns.
[bio15-s4.3.4] ATP carries energy from the mitochondrion and cytoplasm to the cell's activities, returning as ADP + Pi to be reloaded. All the transfer molecules are recycled, so only small amounts are needed.
[bio15-s4.3.5] Tracing the atoms: the carbon of glucose leaves as CO₂ in the link step and the cycle. The hydrogen of glucose ends up in water, formed by combining with oxygen breathed in at the electron transport chain. The oxygen breathed in therefore ends up in water, not in CO₂.
[bio15-s4.3.6] Comparison with photosynthesis: in photosynthesis, NADPH and ATP are made in stage 1, using light energy, and used in stage 2 to build glucose. In respiration, NADH is made as glucose is broken down and is used at the end of stage 2 to make ATP.
[bio15-s4.3.7] Diagram: cytoplasm and a mitochondrion (outer membrane, inner membrane with cristae, matrix). Label: glucose, 2 pyruvate, NAD⁺ → NADH, acetyl CoA, CO₂, citric acid cycle (acetyl group + C₄ → citric acid (C₆) → back to C₄), electron transport chain, O₂ + H⁺ → H₂O, ATP, NAD⁺; what each carrier carries.

CANDIDATES bio35-s7: Ethics of genetic technologies
[bio35-s7.0.b] Genetic technologies raise questions that experiments cannot answer, so exam answers need a method for reasoning about them. Follow the same five steps every time and you will always cover both sides and reach a conclusion.
[bio35-s7.0.0] 1. Say what the technology does and what is new about it, so that the reader knows exactly what is being judged.
[bio35-s7.0.1] 2. Identify who is affected: the person tested or treated, relatives, future children, society, other species, and the environment.
[bio35-s7.0.2] 3. Give arguments for and against, linking each to a principle: autonomy and consent (people decide for themselves, with full information), doing good (beneficence), avoiding harm (non-maleficence), justice and fairness, privacy, and the precautionary principle.
[bio35-s7.0.3] The precautionary principle means taking care when the risks are unknown. There is more on these principles in “Science, scientists and society”.
[bio35-s7.0.4] 4. Say what rules or safeguards exist or could be added, such as laws, consent procedures or ethics committees.
[bio35-s7.0.5] 5. Reach a justified conclusion, which means a decision that follows from the arguments you have given.
[bio35-s7.0.6] Ethics is decided by reasoning about values informed by evidence: science says what can be done and with what risks, not whether it should be done. This is why two people can agree on the science and still disagree on the ethics.
[bio35-s7.1.b] Genetic data is the information in a person’s DNA, for example a genome sequence or a test result. It raises ethical problems that ordinary personal data does not, for three reasons.
[bio35-s7.1.0] Genetic data is permanent: it cannot be changed like a password, so once it is leaked the damage cannot be undone.
[bio35-s7.1.1] It identifies a person, and it predicts some future health risks, so it is both highly personal and potentially harmful if misused.
[bio35-s7.1.2] It is shared with relatives, so one person’s test reveals information about family members who did not consent.
[bio35-s7.1.3] Under the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR, applying since 2018), genetic data is a special category of personal data with extra protection.
[bio35-s7.2.b] Researchers can learn far more about disease when they can study many people’s genomes alongside their health records. This benefit has to be set against the risks to the people who donate their data.
[bio35-s7.2.0] For: large databases of genomes and health records (biobanks) speed research into the causes and treatments of disease, and allow faster diagnosis of rare diseases. There is more on this in “Bioinformatics: genome databases”.
[bio35-s7.2.1] Against: there is a risk of data leaks, or of re-identification of people from “anonymous” data, because a genome is so distinctive that removing a name does not always hide who it belongs to.
[bio35-s7.2.2] Against: people must give informed consent and know how their data will be used and shared, otherwise their autonomy is not respected.
[bio35-s7.2.3] Against: questions arise about who profits from discoveries made with donated data, because the people who donated it may not share in the benefit.
[bio35-s7.3.b] Insurers and employers would like to know about a person’s health risks. Genetic data could give them that knowledge, which is why laws now limit its use.
[bio35-s7.3.0] Risk: insurers or employers could charge more or refuse people with a high genetic risk. This would be unjust, because people don’t choose their genes, and fear of it could stop people having useful tests.
[bio35-s7.3.1] In Ireland the Disability Act 2005 prohibits the use of genetic test results in insurance, pensions, mortgages and employment.
[bio35-s7.3.2] In the USA the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act (2008) protects against discrimination in health insurance and employment.
[bio35-s7.3.3] Argument on the other side: insurers say that if customers know their risk and insurers don’t, pricing becomes unfair, because people at high risk could buy cover cheaply.
[bio35-s7.4.b] Police can store DNA profiles (the pattern of a person’s DNA used to identify them) from crime scenes and from people they arrest. This helps solve crimes but puts a great deal of personal information in state hands.
[bio35-s7.4.0] For: national DNA databases solve crimes, including old cases, and clear innocent people.
[bio35-s7.4.1] Ireland’s database was set up under the Criminal Justice (Forensic Evidence and DNA Database System) Act 2014.
[bio35-s7.4.2] Against: privacy, especially when profiles are kept of people who were arrested but never convicted.
[bio35-s7.4.3] In S and Marper v United Kingdom (2008), the European Court of Human Rights ruled that the UK’s keeping of the DNA of people not convicted of any crime breached their right to privacy.
[bio35-s7.4.4] Against: groups that are policed more heavily are over-represented on databases, so they are more likely to be matched or wrongly suspected.
[bio35-s7.4.5] Against: a match can be given too much weight in court. There is more on this in “DNA profiling”.
[bio35-s7.5.b] People send DNA to companies to learn about their ancestry. That data can be used for purposes the customer never intended, and it also exposes their relatives.
[bio35-s7.5.0] People send DNA to companies for ancestry tests, and may upload it to public genealogy websites so that they can find relatives.
[bio35-s7.5.1] In 2018 police in California identified the “Golden State Killer”, Joseph James DeAngelo, by matching crime-scene DNA with distant relatives who had uploaded their DNA to such a website.
[bio35-s7.5.2] For: serious crimes were solved. Against: the relatives never consented to police use of their DNA, which shows how one person’s upload can expose others.
[bio35-s7.5.3] Company data can be hacked or sold: in 2023 a data breach at the testing company 23andMe exposed the ancestry information of millions of customers.
[bio35-s7.6.b] Developing genetic tests and treatments is expensive, and companies protect their investment with patents (legal rights to be the only seller of an invention). The question is whether a part of the human body can be owned.
[bio35-s7.6.0] For: companies invest in genetic tests and treatments and need patents to recover the cost.
[bio35-s7.6.1] Against: patenting human genes lets one company control testing and set high prices.
[bio35-s7.6.2] In 2013 the US Supreme Court ruled, in the case against Myriad Genetics, which held patents on the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes (genes linked to breast cancer risk), that naturally occurring human DNA cannot be patented.
[bio35-s7.6.3] After this ruling, other laboratories began offering BRCA tests, so patent control had been keeping testing limited. There is more on BRCA genes in “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”.
[bio35-s7.6.4] Justice: benefits from the genetic resources of poorer countries (e.g. crop varieties, local plants) should be shared with them, rather than all the profit going to the companies that develop them.
[bio35-s7.7.b] Genetic data is also collected on crops and farm animals, and the same questions of access and ownership arise.
[bio35-s7.7.0] For: genetic data on crops and livestock speeds the breeding of higher-yielding, disease-resistant varieties, because breeders can pick out useful alleles quickly.
[bio35-s7.7.1] Against: control of seed varieties and their genetic data by a few large companies, and loss of traditional varieties, which could leave farmers dependent and food supplies less varied.
[bio35-s7.8.b] A GM (genetically modified) crop has had a gene from another organism added to its DNA. Supporters see this as a way to grow more food with less damage to the environment.
[bio35-s7.8.0] Higher yields and less crop loss, because the crop resists pests or disease or tolerates harsh conditions.
[bio35-s7.8.1] Bt crops (which make a natural insecticide, the Bt toxin) need less insecticide spraying, which protects farm workers and other insects. There is more on this in “Genetic engineering”.
[bio35-s7.8.2] Crops can be modified for drought or salt tolerance, or for added nutrition, such as Golden Rice, which is designed to help tackle vitamin A deficiency.
[bio35-s7.8.3] GM crops could help feed a growing population on less land.
[bio35-s7.8.4] Major scientific bodies have found approved GM foods to be as safe to eat as their conventional equivalents.
[bio35-s7.9.b] Opponents of GM crops raise concerns about the environment, farmers’ independence, people’s right to choose, and whether it is right to modify living things.
[bio35-s7.9.0] Genes can spread in pollen to wild relatives or to non-GM and organic crops nearby, a process called gene flow.
[bio35-s7.9.1] Weeds have evolved resistance to the herbicide glyphosate where herbicide-tolerant crops are sprayed repeatedly, and some insect pests have evolved resistance to Bt toxins. This is natural selection: resistant individuals survive and pass on their alleles. There is more on this in “The theory of evolution by natural selection”.
[bio35-s7.9.2] Large areas of a single crop reduce biodiversity, because fewer kinds of plant and animal can live there.
[bio35-s7.9.3] Patented seed must be bought each year, making farmers dependent on companies.
[bio35-s7.9.4] Consumers have a right to choose, so the EU requires food containing more than 0.9% GM material to be labelled.
[bio35-s7.9.5] Some people object that GM is unnatural (“playing God”).
[bio35-s7.10.b] Golden Rice is an example of a GM crop designed to improve health, and it shows both sides of the debate clearly.
[bio35-s7.10.0] Supporters say opposition has delayed a way to save children’s sight and lives, because vitamin A deficiency can cause blindness and death.
[bio35-s7.10.1] Opponents say vitamin A deficiency is better tackled by a more varied diet and supplements.
[bio35-s7.10.2] Its approval in the Philippines has been challenged in court.
[bio35-s7.11.b] Animals can also be genetically modified, either to produce more food or to help medical research. Animals feel pain and can escape, so the issues differ from those for crops.
[bio35-s7.11.0] For: faster-growing food animals such as AquAdvantage salmon need less feed per kilogram of food. There is more on this in “Genetic engineering”.
[bio35-s7.11.1] For: GM animals model human diseases in medical research, so treatments can be studied before they are tried in people.
[bio35-s7.11.2] Against: animal welfare, judged using the 3Rs (replace, reduce, refine animal use). There is more on this in “Science, scientists and society”.
[bio35-s7.11.3] Against: risk to wild populations if GM fish escape and breed, because their modified genes could spread.
[bio35-s7.11.4] Against: public unease about eating GM animals.
[bio35-s7.12.b] Gene therapy and gene editing change the DNA of a person’s own body cells (somatic cells, those that are not eggs or sperm) to treat disease. Because the change is not inherited, the ethical issues are fewer than for germline editing.
[bio35-s7.12.0] For: these can treat or cure serious genetic diseases, e.g. the CRISPR treatment for sickle-cell disease approved in the UK in 2023. There is more on this in “Genetic engineering”.
[bio35-s7.12.1] For: the changes are in the patient’s body cells only, so only a consenting patient is affected and nothing is passed on to children.
[bio35-s7.12.2] Against: long-term risks are not yet known, including edits at the wrong place in the genome.
[bio35-s7.12.3] Against: treatments are extremely expensive, so access is unequal (justice).
[bio35-s7.13.b] Germline editing changes eggs, sperm or embryos (the germline). Every cell of the resulting person carries the change, and so do their descendants, which makes it far more controversial than somatic therapy.
[bio35-s7.13.0] Changes to eggs, sperm or embryos would be passed to all future generations, who cannot consent.
[bio35-s7.13.1] In November 2018 the scientist He Jiankui announced in China the birth of twin girls whose embryos he had edited with CRISPR to try to make them resistant to HIV.
[bio35-s7.13.2] It was condemned worldwide as unsafe, unnecessary and done without proper consent, and he was sentenced to three years in prison in 2019.
[bio35-s7.13.3] For: it could in future remove a serious disease allele from a family permanently.
[bio35-s7.13.4] Against: unknown risks to future generations.
[bio35-s7.13.5] Against: it could lead to “designer babies” chosen for traits such as height or intelligence rather than to prevent disease, and could increase inequality.
[bio35-s7.13.6] Germline editing is not allowed in most countries.
[bio35-s7.14.b] Some serious diseases are caused by faulty mitochondrial DNA, which a child inherits from the mother’s egg. Mitochondrial donation replaces the faulty mitochondria with a donor’s healthy ones; the UK permitted it by law in 2015. There is more on the technique in “Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance”.
[bio35-s7.14.0] For: it lets a woman with a mitochondrial disease have a genetically related child free of that disease.
[bio35-s7.14.1] For: the donor contributes only the mitochondrial DNA (37 genes), not the nuclear DNA that determines the child’s characteristics.
[bio35-s7.14.2] Against: it is a germline change, passed on by daughters to their children.
[bio35-s7.14.3] Against: long-term safety is still being studied.
[bio35-s7.14.4] Against: questions arise about the donor’s relationship to the child (the “three-parent baby”).
[bio35-s7.14.5] Against: some fear it is a step towards other germline changes.
[bio35-s7.15.b] DNA testing looks for disease alleles in a person’s DNA, unlike DNA profiling, which identifies people. The ethics depend on what the test is for, so each kind is taken in turn, starting with the most straightforward.
[bio35-s7.15.0] For: diagnostic testing confirms a diagnosis so that treatment can start.
[bio35-s7.15.1] For: carrier testing (finding out whether a healthy person carries one copy of a recessive disease allele, e.g. for cystic fibrosis, common in Ireland) informs people’s choices before having children. There is more on this in “Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics”.
[bio35-s7.15.2] Against: a positive result may reveal that relatives are also carriers, raising questions about whether and how to tell them.
[bio35-s7.16.b] Predictive testing finds out whether a healthy person will develop a disease later in life. It is most controversial when the disease has no cure.
[bio35-s7.16.0] Huntington’s disease is an example of a late-onset disease (one that appears in adulthood) with no cure. It is dominant, so a child of an affected parent has a 1 in 2 risk.
[bio35-s7.16.1] For: predictive testing gives certainty, and the chance to plan life, career and family.
[bio35-s7.16.2] Against: a positive result may cause great distress, and many people at risk choose not to be tested.
[bio35-s7.16.3] Against: the right not to know (autonomy) must be respected.
[bio35-s7.16.4] Testing children is usually delayed until they can decide for themselves, because they cannot give informed consent and cannot be helped by knowing early.
[bio35-s7.16.5] Genetic counselling before and after testing is essential, so that people understand the choices and are supported with the result.
[bio35-s7.17.b] Some alleles only raise the chance of disease rather than guaranteeing it, and tests can also be done on embryos. Both create hard choices.
[bio35-s7.17.0] Testing for risk alleles (e.g. BRCA1 and BRCA2): a positive result gives a raised risk, not certainty, so people face hard decisions about preventive surgery. There is more on this in “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”.
[bio35-s7.17.1] A negative result may give false reassurance, because the person can still develop the disease for other reasons.
[bio35-s7.17.2] Preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD) tests embryos made by IVF (in vitro fertilisation, fertilising eggs outside the body) so that only embryos without a serious disease allele are implanted. Prenatal tests on the foetus and newborn screening belong to “Prenatal and postnatal care”.
[bio35-s7.17.3] For: parents avoid passing on a serious disease.
[bio35-s7.17.4] Against: unused embryos are destroyed, which some people believe is wrong because an embryo has the moral status of a person.
[bio35-s7.17.5] Against: there is worry about choosing embryos for non-medical traits (sex, appearance).
[bio35-s7.17.6] Against: concern that it devalues the lives of people living with the condition.
[bio35-s7.18.b] Some companies sell genetic tests straight to the public, online, without a doctor’s involvement.
[bio35-s7.18.0] For: tests bought online are cheap and convenient, and respect autonomy because people choose to find out for themselves.
[bio35-s7.18.1] Against: results given without counselling may be misunderstood, and accuracy varies.
[bio35-s7.18.2] Against: they may reveal unexpected family relationships, e.g. that a parent is not the biological parent.
[bio35-s7.18.3] Against: data privacy, because the company holds the customer’s permanent genetic data. The ancestry tests and police access block above gives an example.
[bio35-s7.19.b] Stem cells are the body’s raw material for growing and repairing tissue. Their ethics depend on where they come from, so first learn the types and what each can become.
[bio35-s7.19.0] A stem cell is an unspecialised cell that can divide by mitosis to make more stem cells, and can differentiate (become specialised) into specialised cell types. There is more on this in “Levels of organisation”.
[bio35-s7.19.1] Embryonic stem cells, from the inner cell mass of an embryo about five days old (a blastocyst), are pluripotent: they can become any type of body cell.
[bio35-s7.19.2] Adult (tissue) stem cells, e.g. in bone marrow, skin and the lining of the gut, are multipotent: they can become only a few related cell types. Bone-marrow stem cells give rise to the blood cells.
[bio35-s7.19.3] Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells are adult body cells (e.g. skin cells) reprogrammed in the laboratory, by switching on a few genes, to behave like embryonic stem cells.
[bio35-s7.19.4] Shinya Yamanaka first made iPS cells (in mice) in 2006, and shared the 2012 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with John Gurdon.
[bio35-s7.19.5] iPS cells avoid the use of embryos and, made from a patient’s own cells, would not be rejected by the immune system, because the cells are recognised as the patient’s own.
[bio35-s7.20.b] Because stem cells can replace lost or damaged cells, they are used or being tested as treatments for many conditions.
[bio35-s7.20.0] Bone-marrow (stem-cell) transplants already treat leukaemia and some other blood disorders. There is more on this in “Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer”.
[bio35-s7.20.1] Skin grown from stem cells treats severe burns.
[bio35-s7.20.2] Stem-cell treatments are being tested in clinical trials for conditions where cells are lost, e.g. Parkinson’s disease, type 1 diabetes, spinal cord injury, heart damage and some forms of blindness.
[bio35-s7.20.3] Stem cells grown into tissues are used to test new drugs and to study diseases.
[bio35-s7.21.b] A clone is a genetically identical copy. The same laboratory method can be used for two very different purposes, depending on whether the embryo is allowed to grow into an animal.
[bio35-s7.21.0] A clone is a genetically identical copy.
[bio35-s7.21.1] Reproductive cloning makes a whole organism by somatic cell nuclear transfer: the nucleus of a body cell is put into an egg cell whose own nucleus has been removed, the egg is stimulated to divide into an embryo, and the embryo is placed in a surrogate mother.
[bio35-s7.21.2] Dolly the sheep was born on 5 July 1996 at the Roslin Institute near Edinburgh, Scotland. She was the first mammal cloned from an adult body cell (a cell from the udder), not an embryo cell.
[bio35-s7.21.3] Dolly was one success from 277 attempts, and died in 2003 aged six.
[bio35-s7.21.4] Therapeutic cloning uses the same method to make an early embryo only to obtain embryonic stem cells that match the patient; the embryo is not implanted, so no baby is produced.
[bio35-s7.21.5] Diagram: somatic cell nuclear transfer as a flow diagram: body cell from adult A → nucleus removed; egg from female B → its nucleus removed; nucleus of A inserted into the empty egg → embryo; then two arrows: implanted in surrogate mother → clone of A (reproductive cloning), or grown in the laboratory → embryonic stem cells → specialised cells for the patient (therapeutic cloning).
[bio35-s7.22.b] Embryonic stem cells can become any type of body cell, but getting them means destroying an embryo. This is why they are ethically controversial.
[bio35-s7.22.0] For: they have the potential to treat many serious diseases and relieve great suffering (beneficence).
[bio35-s7.22.1] For: the embryos used are often spare embryos from IVF that would otherwise be discarded.
[bio35-s7.22.2] Against: obtaining the cells destroys an embryo, which those who believe life begins at fertilisation regard as killing a human being.
[bio35-s7.22.3] Against: there is concern about creating embryos for research.
[bio35-s7.23.b] Adult stem cells and iPS cells largely avoid the embryo issue, but they have their own concerns.
[bio35-s7.23.0] iPS cells may carry a risk of tumours.
[bio35-s7.23.1] Unproven “stem-cell clinics” sell untested, expensive and sometimes harmful treatments to desperate patients.
[bio35-s7.23.2] Consent is needed from donors of cells and tissues.
[bio35-s7.24.b] Reproductive cloning produces a living animal or, in principle, a person. The arguments differ a great deal between animals and humans.
[bio35-s7.24.0] In animals, for: copying valuable farm animals or conserving endangered species.
[bio35-s7.24.1] In animals, against: low success rates, many failed pregnancies and animal suffering, and clones’ health problems (animal welfare).
[bio35-s7.24.2] Reproductive cloning in humans is almost universally rejected, because of the risk of serious abnormality, the clone’s identity and individuality, and the use of people as means to an end.
[bio35-s7.24.3] It is prohibited by the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.
[bio35-s7.24.4] Research fraud has also damaged trust in this field (the Hwang Woo-suk case). There is more on this in “Science as a global enterprise”.
[bio35-s7.25.b] Therapeutic cloning aims to make stem cells genetically matched to a patient, but it raises the embryo issue again and adds a new problem about eggs.
[bio35-s7.25.0] For: it produces stem cells that exactly match the patient, so there is no rejection.
[bio35-s7.25.1] Against: embryos are created to be destroyed.
[bio35-s7.25.2] Against: it needs many donated eggs, and egg donation carries health risks and could exploit poor women if they are paid.
[bio35-s7.25.3] Against: it may make reproductive cloning easier.
[bio35-s7.26.b] The separate issues above keep returning to the same few principles, which is why they make a good checklist for any new genetic technology.
[bio35-s7.26.0] Common threads in all these issues: consent (including that of relatives and future generations, who cannot consent); privacy of permanent, shared genetic information; and fair access to expensive technologies (justice).
[bio35-s7.26.1] Two more threads: the precautionary principle where long-term effects are unknown, and the need for laws and independent ethics committees to keep up with fast-changing technology.

CANDIDATES bio8-s6: Water
[bio8-s6.0.b] Water is the most abundant substance in living things, and almost every role it plays comes from the shape and charge of its molecule. Learn the properties here first, because the later blocks all rely on them.
[bio8-s6.0.0] Water is the most abundant substance in living things. It makes up about 60% of the mass of an adult human body and most of the mass of cytoplasm (the jelly-like contents of a cell).
[bio8-s6.0.1] Many plant tissues and jellyfish are over 90% water. Organisms die quickly without water, which shows how much of life depends on it.
[bio8-s6.0.2] A water molecule, H₂O, is polar, meaning it has uneven charge: the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge (δ−) and the hydrogen ends carry slight positive charges (δ+).
[bio8-s6.0.3] Because of these opposite charges, neighbouring water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, weak attractions between the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and the δ− oxygen of another.
[bio8-s6.0.4] Because it is polar, water is an excellent solvent: ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻) and polar molecules (e.g. glucose, amino acids) dissolve in it, as the charged water molecules surround them. Lipids are non-polar and do not dissolve.
[bio8-s6.0.5] Hydrogen bonding gives water a high specific heat capacity, so it takes a lot of energy to raise its temperature, because the energy must first break hydrogen bonds. It also gives water a high latent heat of vaporisation, so a lot of energy is absorbed when it evaporates.
[bio8-s6.0.6] Hydrogen bonding makes water molecules stick to each other (cohesion) and to other surfaces (adhesion).
[bio8-s6.0.7] Diagram: two or three water molecules with δ+ on the hydrogens, δ− on the oxygen, and a dotted hydrogen bond between them.
[bio8-s6.1.b] Chemical reactions in cells need the reacting molecules to meet. Water gives them a place to do that, and it also takes part in some of the reactions itself.
[bio8-s6.1.0] Almost all metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of life) take place in solution in water, in the cytoplasm and inside organelles. This is because dissolved molecules can move, collide and meet enzymes.
[bio8-s6.1.1] Water is also a reactant, meaning it is used up in a reaction. It is split in photosynthesis, releasing oxygen, and it is used in every hydrolysis reaction (breaking a bond by adding water), including digestion.
[bio8-s6.1.2] Water is a product, meaning it is made by a reaction, in respiration and in every condensation reaction (joining molecules together with the loss of water).
[bio8-s6.2.b] Because so many substances dissolve in water, it can carry them from place to place. This works in animals and plants, and also across the surface of every cell.
[bio8-s6.2.0] In animals, blood plasma (the liquid part of blood) is mainly water. It carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, hormones, urea and carbon dioxide around the body; lymph and tissue fluid are also watery.
[bio8-s6.2.1] Urea (a waste product) and excess salts are excreted dissolved in water as urine, so water carries waste out of the body as well as supplies in.
[bio8-s6.2.2] In plants, water carries dissolved minerals up the xylem (the vessels that carry water) from the roots to the leaves. Sucrose and amino acids move through the phloem (the vessels that carry food).
[bio8-s6.2.3] Cohesion between water molecules holds the column of water together in the xylem, so it can be pulled upwards as one continuous stream. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio8-s6.2.4] Substances move into and out of cells by diffusion through the watery fluid around them, because dissolved substances can spread out through water.
[bio8-s6.3.b] Living things work best within a narrow temperature range. Water helps keep them there, thanks to its high specific heat capacity and high latent heat of vaporisation.
[bio8-s6.3.0] High specific heat capacity: water heats up and cools down slowly, so the water in an organism's body buffers it against sudden temperature changes. Lakes and seas give aquatic organisms a stable temperature.
[bio8-s6.3.1] Cooling by evaporation: because water absorbs a lot of heat when it evaporates, sweating in mammals cools the skin, panting cools dogs, and transpiration (evaporation of water from leaves) cools leaves. There is more on sweating in Homeostasis.
[bio8-s6.3.2] Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats and forms an insulating layer on ponds and lakes, letting organisms survive beneath it in winter.
[bio8-s6.4.b] Enzymes only work properly at the right pH, so cells need to keep acidity steady. Water is the medium in which this control happens.
[bio8-s6.4.0] pH depends on the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution; pure water is neutral, at pH 7. Acids and bases exert their effects only when they are dissolved in water, because that is when they release ions.
[bio8-s6.4.1] Buffers, such as hydrogencarbonate and phosphate ions, work dissolved in body water. They keep the pH of cells and blood almost constant, so enzymes keep their shape and activity.
[bio8-s6.4.2] Water is the medium by which excess H⁺ ions are removed. The kidneys excrete them dissolved in urine.
[bio8-s6.4.3] Carbon dioxide, which forms an acid in solution, is carried in the blood plasma to the lungs and breathed out, so the acid does not build up in the blood.
[bio8-s6.5.b] Water moves in and out of cells by osmosis, so cells can swell or shrink if the surrounding fluid changes. Osmoregulation is how organisms keep this under control, and animals and plants deal with it differently.
[bio8-s6.5.0] Osmoregulation is the control of the water and salt (solute) concentration of body fluids.
[bio8-s6.5.1] Water moves into or out of cells by osmosis, from a region of higher water concentration to a region of lower water concentration, through a selectively permeable membrane. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio8-s6.5.2] Animal cells placed in pure water swell and may burst; in strong salt solution they shrink. So the concentration of the blood and tissue fluid must be kept steady.
[bio8-s6.5.3] In humans the kidneys do this, adjusting the volume of water in urine under the control of ADH (a hormone).
[bio8-s6.5.4] Plant cells have a strong cell wall, so water entering by osmosis makes them turgid (swollen and firm) instead of bursting. Turgor pressure supports non-woody plants and leaves and opens stomata (the pores in leaves).
[bio8-s6.5.5] When water is lost, plant cells become flaccid (soft and limp) and the plant wilts.
[bio8-s6.5.6] Single-celled freshwater organisms such as Amoeba constantly gain water by osmosis, because their surroundings are more dilute than their cytoplasm. They expel the excess with a contractile vacuole.
[bio8-s6.6.b] Beyond reactions, transport, temperature, pH and osmoregulation, water has several further roles in living things.
[bio8-s6.6.0] Lubrication: synovial fluid lubricates joints, and mucus and saliva help swallowing.
[bio8-s6.6.1] Support: the fluid pressure of a hydrostatic skeleton supports soft-bodied animals such as earthworms, because water cannot easily be compressed.
[bio8-s6.6.2] Habitat: water is the habitat of aquatic organisms.
[bio8-s6.6.3] Fertilisation: some organisms need water for fertilisation. Moss and fern sperm swim in water to reach the egg.
