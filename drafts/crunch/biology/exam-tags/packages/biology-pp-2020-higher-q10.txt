CANDIDATES bio31-s10: The niche
[bio31-s10.0.b] Every species does a particular job in its ecosystem (a community of living things together with their non-living surroundings). That job, and the way of life that goes with it, is called its niche.
[bio31-s10.0.0] The niche of a species is its role, or functional position, in its ecosystem. It includes what it feeds on and what feeds on it, where in the habitat it lives and feeds, and when it is active.
[bio31-s10.0.1] The niche also includes the range of abiotic conditions (non-living factors such as temperature, moisture, light and pH) the species can tolerate, its interactions with other species, and its effects on its environment.
[bio31-s10.0.2] Habitat and niche differ. The habitat is where an organism lives, its address; the niche is how it lives there, its profession. Many species share one habitat, but each has its own niche.
[bio31-s10.0.3] Example: in an oak woodland, caterpillars feed on the leaves, woodlice and earthworms feed on dead leaves on the floor, blue tits eat caterpillars in the canopy, and fungi decompose fallen wood. One habitat, five different niches.
[bio31-s10.0.4] A species' niche is the result of its adaptations. Its structural, behavioural and physiological features (its body structure, its behaviour and the way its body works) suit it to a particular way of life, and they also limit it to that way of life.
[bio31-s10.1.b] Because a niche is a set of resources and conditions, two species that want the same ones are in direct competition. Whether they can live side by side depends on how much their niches overlap.
[bio31-s10.1.0] Competitive exclusion: two species cannot occupy exactly the same niche in the same place for long. They compete for the same resources, and the better-adapted species wins while the other declines or is excluded locally.
[bio31-s10.1.1] Example: the grey squirrel and the red squirrel have similar niches in Irish woodland, as tree-living seed and nut eaters. In broadleaf woodland the grey squirrel is larger and can feed efficiently on acorns that red squirrels digest poorly, so it outcompetes the red squirrel.
[bio31-s10.1.2] The red squirrel survives better in conifer woodland, where the grey squirrel's advantage with acorns counts for less.
[bio31-s10.1.3] Niche differentiation: similar species can live together if their niches differ, for example by feeding on different foods, at different heights or at different times of day. This reduces competition between them.
[bio31-s10.1.4] Example: in the same grassland, the kestrel hunts small mammals by day and the barn owl hunts them at night. Both occur in Ireland, and sharing the same prey does not put them in competition because they hunt at different times.
[bio31-s10.2.b] Niches vary in width. A narrow niche can make a species very efficient but fragile, while a broad niche makes a species flexible.
[bio31-s10.2.0] A specialist has a narrow niche, depending on one food or one habitat. This makes it efficient at using that resource but vulnerable to change, because it has nothing to fall back on.
[bio31-s10.2.1] Example: the caterpillars of the marsh fritillary butterfly feed mainly on one plant, devil's-bit scabious, so the butterfly depends on the wet grasslands and bogs where that plant grows.
[bio31-s10.2.2] A generalist has a broad niche. The red fox eats rabbits, mice, birds, insects, earthworms, fruit and scraps, and lives in countryside and towns, so it copes well with change.
[bio31-s10.2.3] An invasive species (one that spreads and thrives after arriving in a new area) is often one that finds a niche unfilled, or fills an existing niche better than the native species, in its new home.
[bio31-s10.2.4] The grey squirrel is an example of an invasive species. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio31-s10.3.b] Questions often ask you to describe the niche of a named organism. A niche is built from several parts, so cover each of them rather than only the food.
[bio31-s10.3.0] State the organism's ecosystem and habitat, then its trophic level (its feeding position in the food chain) and what it eats.
[bio31-s10.3.1] Say what eats it, and when and where it is active, so that its place in the food web and its daily pattern are both clear.
[bio31-s10.3.2] State the conditions it tolerates, then give two or three adaptations, each matched to the part of its niche it suits.
[bio31-s10.4.b] Bog peat is waterlogged, acidic and very short of nitrogen, which plants need to build proteins. The sundew solves this by photosynthesising like other plants but also catching insects to get the nitrogen it lacks.
[bio31-s10.4.0] Niche: a producer that photosynthesises in waterlogged, acidic bog where the peat is very poor in nitrogen, and that supplements its nitrogen by catching and digesting insects.
[bio31-s10.4.1] Adaptation: leaves bearing red hairs (tentacles), each tipped with a drop of sticky fluid that glistens, attracts small insects and traps them.
[bio31-s10.4.2] Adaptation: the tentacles bend over the trapped insect, holding it against the leaf.
[bio31-s10.4.3] Adaptation: glands secrete enzymes that digest the insect, and the leaf absorbs the products, including nitrogen compounds. This makes up for the nitrogen-poor peat.
[bio31-s10.4.4] Adaptation: a small rosette of leaves close to the ground, in an open, sunny habitat, which suits its photosynthesis and the insects that land on it.
[bio31-s10.4.5] Diagram: a sundew leaf with sticky tentacles curved over a trapped insect. Labels: leaf, tentacle, sticky drop, trapped insect.
[bio31-s10.5.b] On a rocky shore, the tide brings waves, predators and drying out in turn. The limpet is a grazer built to hold on, to avoid drying out and to scrape up its food.
[bio31-s10.5.0] Niche: a primary consumer (grazer) that scrapes the thin film of algae off rocks between the tides. It is eaten by birds such as oystercatchers and by crabs.
[bio31-s10.5.1] Adaptation: a low, conical shell that waves flow over without dislodging it.
[bio31-s10.5.2] Adaptation: a strong muscular foot that clamps the limpet to the rock against waves and predators.
[bio31-s10.5.3] Adaptation (behavioural): at low tide it clamps down onto its own home scar, a groove ground into the rock to match its shell. This seals in water and prevents drying out.
[bio31-s10.5.4] Adaptation: a rough, tongue-like radula that rasps algae from the rock.
[bio31-s10.5.5] Adaptation (behavioural): it feeds when covered by the tide and returns to its home scar, so it is always back in its sealed position when the tide goes out.
[bio31-s10.6.b] The barn owl hunts in the dark, so it relies on silence, hearing and night vision more than on sight in daylight. Its adaptations suit each part of that hunting niche.
[bio31-s10.6.0] Niche: a nocturnal predator (secondary or tertiary consumer) of small mammals such as mice, rats and shrews, nesting in old buildings and hollow trees.
[bio31-s10.6.1] Adaptation: soft feathers with fringed, comb-like edges on the flight feathers make its flight almost silent, so prey does not hear it coming.
[bio31-s10.6.2] Adaptation: a heart-shaped facial disc of feathers funnels sound to the ears.
[bio31-s10.6.3] Adaptation: ears at slightly different heights on each side of the head, so it can locate prey by sound alone in darkness.
[bio31-s10.6.4] Adaptation: large, forward-facing eyes give binocular vision (both eyes view the same scene, which helps judge distance) in dim light.
[bio31-s10.6.5] Adaptation: sharp, curved talons and a hooked beak grip and tear prey.
[bio31-s10.7.b] Bees feed on flowers and in doing so carry pollen between them, so their niche is tied to flowering plants. Their body structure and behaviour suit collecting food and moving pollen.
[bio31-s10.7.0] Niche: a primary consumer that feeds on nectar (energy) and pollen (protein), and a pollinator of flowering plants, transferring pollen from flower to flower. There is more on this in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio31-s10.7.1] Adaptation: a long, tube-like tongue (proboscis) to reach nectar inside flowers.
[bio31-s10.7.2] Adaptation: a hairy body to which pollen sticks, which is how pollen is carried from flower to flower.
[bio31-s10.7.3] Adaptation: pollen baskets, stiff hairs on the hind legs, carry pollen back to the nest.
[bio31-s10.7.4] Adaptation: eyes that see the ultraviolet patterns on petals, which guide the bee to nectar.
[bio31-s10.7.5] Adaptation (behavioural): honeybees communicate the location of flowers to others by a dance.
[bio31-s10.8.b] The earthworm lives in the soil, eating dead plant material, so it recycles nutrients. Its body is built for burrowing and for breathing through its skin in damp ground.
[bio31-s10.8.0] Niche: a detritivore (an animal that eats dead leaves and other dead organic matter) feeding on dead leaves and soil organic matter, recycling nutrients.
[bio31-s10.8.1] Its burrows aerate and drain the soil and it mixes the soil layers. It is also food for blackbirds, thrushes, badgers and foxes.
[bio31-s10.8.2] Adaptation: a long, thin, segmented body without legs for burrowing.
[bio31-s10.8.3] Adaptation: tiny bristles (chaetae) on each segment grip the burrow wall.
[bio31-s10.8.4] Adaptation: mucus lubricates movement and binds the burrow walls.
[bio31-s10.8.5] Adaptation: a thin, moist skin exchanges gases, so the earthworm lives in damp soil and comes to the surface mainly at night or in wet weather.
[bio31-s10.8.6] Adaptation (behavioural): it pulls leaves into its burrow, so it feeds below ground, away from predators.
[bio31-s10.9.b] Sand dunes are dry, salty and shifting, so few plants can live there. Marram grass is a pioneer, the first plant to colonise bare ground, and its adaptations suit it to these harsh conditions.
[bio31-s10.9.0] Niche: a pioneer producer that colonises loose, dry, salty sand and binds it into dunes, allowing other plants to settle later. There is more on plant water loss in Transport in Plants.
[bio31-s10.9.1] Adaptation: leaves roll into a tube in dry conditions, with stomata (the pores in the leaf) on the inner surface in grooves lined with hairs. This traps moist air and reduces water loss by transpiration.
[bio31-s10.9.2] Adaptation: long roots reach water deep in the sand.
[bio31-s10.9.3] Adaptation: underground stems (rhizomes) spread and bind the sand.
[bio31-s10.9.4] Adaptation: it grows upward when it is buried by blowing sand, so it is not smothered.

CANDIDATES bio31-s2: Biodiversity loss and conservation
[bio31-s2.0.b] Biodiversity (the variety of living things in an ecosystem) is not just nice to have. It supplies services people depend on, keeps ecosystems stable, and many people argue it has a value of its own.
[bio31-s2.0.0] Ecosystem services are the benefits people get from ecosystems. Provisioning services are the products we take: food (crops, fish and game), timber, fibre, clean water and medicines.
[bio31-s2.0.1] Many medicines come from organisms, for example penicillin from the mould Penicillium, and aspirin's forerunner from willow bark (salicin). Losing a species can mean losing a drug nobody has found yet.
[bio31-s2.0.2] Regulating services control natural processes: pollination of crops by insects, purification of water by wetlands and soil, and pest control by predators. Bogs, forests and the sea also store carbon.
[bio31-s2.0.3] Wetlands and bogs also give flood control, because they soak up rain and release it slowly instead of letting it rush into rivers.
[bio31-s2.0.4] Supporting services keep the system running, such as soil formation and nutrient cycling by decomposers (organisms that break down dead matter). Cultural services include recreation, tourism, education, heritage and wellbeing.
[bio31-s2.0.5] Resilience (ability to withstand change) is higher in an ecosystem with many species and much genetic diversity. If one species declines, others with similar roles can take its place, and some individuals are likely to survive a new disease or a change in climate.
[bio31-s2.0.6] Low-diversity systems, such as a single crop variety, can collapse from one disease, because every plant is equally vulnerable.
[bio31-s2.0.7] The ethical argument: many people argue that species have a right to exist regardless of their usefulness, and that this generation has a duty to pass on a living world to future generations. Extinction is permanent, so the loss can never be undone.
[bio31-s2.1.b] Species are lost mainly because of human activity. There are seven main causes, and each one has Irish examples worth knowing.
[bio31-s2.1.0] Habitat loss and fragmentation is the biggest cause. Land is drained, built on, cut, or converted to intensive farming or plantation forestry. Irish examples: drainage and cutting of raised bogs, drainage of wet grassland, and removal of hedgerows.
[bio31-s2.1.1] Fragmentation (breaking habitat into pieces) leaves small, isolated patches that support only small populations, which are more easily wiped out by predators, disease or chance.
[bio31-s2.1.2] Agricultural intensification means earlier and more frequent mowing for silage (grass cut and stored as animal feed), reseeding with a few grass species, and more fertiliser. These reduce the variety of wild plants and destroy the nests of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew and the corncrake.
[bio31-s2.1.3] Pollution: excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertiliser, slurry and sewage cause eutrophication (nutrient overload) of rivers and lakes. Silt from drainage, forestry and land works smothers river beds, and pesticides harm insects and their predators.
[bio31-s2.1.4] Invasive species are non-native species introduced by people that spread and harm native species and ecosystems, often because they have no natural predators or diseases in their new home. Irish examples: grey squirrel, zebra mussel and Rhododendron ponticum.
[bio31-s2.1.5] Overexploitation is harvesting faster than a population can replace itself, for example overfishing, peat extraction and over-collection.
[bio31-s2.1.6] Climate change forces species to shift their range or the timing of their life cycles. Cold-adapted species, and those in fragmented habitats, cannot always move far enough.
[bio31-s2.1.7] Disease: new pathogens (disease-causing organisms) spread by trade. Ash dieback, a fungal disease recorded in Ireland in 2012, is killing ash trees in hedgerows and woodland.
[bio31-s2.2.b] Raised bogs are one of Ireland's most distinctive habitats and one of its most damaged. They show clearly how one habitat can matter environmentally, economically, socially and culturally.
[bio31-s2.2.0] Raised bogs are dome-shaped peatlands, mainly in the Irish midlands, formed over thousands of years as Sphagnum moss grew over waterlogged ground. They are wet, acidic and low in nutrients.
[bio31-s2.2.1] Because conditions are so harsh, raised bogs support specialised species: Sphagnum mosses, sundews (insect-eating plants), bog cotton and heather, and animals such as the curlew, the red grouse and the Irish hare.
[bio31-s2.2.2] Loss came from centuries of hand turf cutting, then large-scale industrial peat extraction for fuel and horticulture in the twentieth century, plus drainage for farming and forestry.
[bio31-s2.2.3] As a result, less than about 1% of the original raised bog area is still active raised bog, meaning bog that is still forming peat.
[bio31-s2.2.4] Environmental impacts: loss of a rare habitat and its specialised species; drained peat releases stored carbon; and loss of flood control and water purification.
[bio31-s2.2.5] Economic impacts: peat fuelled power stations and supported jobs in the midlands, so ending extraction cost jobs, while restoration can bring tourism and “green” jobs.
[bio31-s2.2.6] Social and cultural impacts: turf cutting for home fuel is a centuries-old rural tradition, and many families hold turbary (turf-cutting) rights. Bogs also preserve archaeology and are part of Irish heritage and literature.
[bio31-s2.3.b] Once the scale of bog loss was clear, the law and public bodies stepped in. The result protects species but affects people who rely on the bog, so it is a good example of a conservation trade-off.
[bio31-s2.3.0] Many raised bogs are protected as Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) under the EU Habitats Directive. Turf cutting on them is banned, and compensation is offered to turf cutters, which has been controversial.
[bio31-s2.3.1] Bord na Móna ended industrial peat harvesting in 2021. Drained bogs are being rewetted by blocking drains to raise the water table, so that Sphagnum can regrow.
[bio31-s2.3.2] For restoration: it protects species and stores carbon.
[bio31-s2.3.3] Against restoration: it removes a traditional right and a cheap fuel, so it needs compensation and local support to succeed.
[bio31-s2.4.b] The curlew shows how several pressures acting together can push a once-common bird towards disappearing from Ireland. It also shows how targeted conservation work tries to reverse that.
[bio31-s2.4.0] The curlew is a large wading bird with a long, down-curved bill that breeds on bogs and wet grassland. It is now one of Ireland's most threatened breeding birds.
[bio31-s2.4.1] A national survey in 2015–2017 found only 138 breeding pairs in the Republic of Ireland, a decline of at least 96% since the 1980s. A 2021 survey found about 105 pairs, so the fall has continued.
[bio31-s2.4.2] Causes of the decline: loss and drainage of bogs and wet grassland; plantation forestry on breeding sites; intensive farming with early mowing; and predation of eggs and chicks by foxes and crows.
[bio31-s2.4.3] Predation is worse because small breeding areas are surrounded by forest and farmland that support many predators.
[bio31-s2.4.4] Conservation: the Curlew Conservation Programme, run by the National Parks and Wildlife Service (NPWS) since 2017, employs local nest-finders and works with farmers to protect nests, manage habitat and control predators. Electric fencing against foxes is one example.
[bio31-s2.5.b] The corncrake is a second bird lost mainly to changes in how farmland is managed. Its story shows how timing and direction of mowing can decide whether chicks survive.
[bio31-s2.5.0] The corncrake is a secretive grassland bird with a rasping call that was once heard across rural Ireland in summer. Its population has fallen by more than 96% since the 1970s.
[bio31-s2.5.1] It now breeds mainly in Co. Donegal (including offshore islands) and on the western coast of Mayo and Galway. It has not disappeared from Ireland.
[bio31-s2.5.2] Main cause: mechanised mowing of hay and silage during the nesting season destroys nests. Mowing from the edge of a field inwards traps the chicks in the centre, because they have nowhere to escape.
[bio31-s2.5.3] Conservation: farmers are paid to delay mowing until the chicks can fly, to mow from the centre of the field outwards so chicks can escape to the edges, and to leave tall vegetation as cover.
[bio31-s2.5.4] The Corncrake LIFE project, EU-funded and launched in 2022, uses payments based on results, so farmers are rewarded for the outcome for the birds.
[bio31-s2.5.5] Cultural impact of both birds: the sounds of the curlew and the corncrake are part of rural Irish life, folklore, music and place names, so their loss is felt as a cultural loss as well as an ecological one.
[bio31-s2.6.b] An invasive species is one that, once introduced, spreads and harms native species. The grey squirrel is Ireland's best-known example, and the pine marten shows how a native predator can help reverse the damage.
[bio31-s2.6.0] The grey squirrel was introduced from North America. The Irish population began with a few animals released in 1911 at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford. It spread across the eastern half of the island, and red squirrels declined where it arrived.
[bio31-s2.6.1] The grey squirrel is larger and competes with the red squirrel for food, because their niches (their roles and resource needs) overlap.
[bio31-s2.6.2] It carries squirrelpox virus, which does not harm grey squirrels but is usually fatal to red squirrels. It also damages trees by stripping their bark.
[bio31-s2.6.3] The native pine marten, protected under the Wildlife Act 1976, has recovered. Where pine martens are common, for example in the Irish midlands, grey squirrels have declined or disappeared and red squirrels have returned.
[bio31-s2.6.4] The heavier grey squirrel is more easily caught by the pine marten.
[bio31-s2.6.5] This shows how restoring a native predator can help a native species.
[bio31-s2.7.b] Invasive species are not only land animals. In Irish lakes and rivers the zebra mussel removes the phytoplankton that other life depends on, and it has itself now been displaced by a newer invader.
[bio31-s2.7.0] The zebra mussel is a small freshwater mussel native to the Ponto-Caspian region, around the Black and Caspian Seas. It was first recorded in Ireland in Lough Derg on the Shannon in 1997, probably brought on boats.
[bio31-s2.7.1] It spreads attached to boat hulls and in bilge water (the water that collects in the bottom of a boat), which is how it moves between lakes.
[bio31-s2.7.2] The zebra mussel filters huge volumes of water, removing phytoplankton (microscopic algae), the base of the food chain. The water becomes clearer, but food for zooplankton (tiny animals) and young fish falls.
[bio31-s2.7.3] The zebra mussel smothers native mussels and blocks water-intake pipes, an economic cost.
[bio31-s2.7.4] The zebra mussel has itself been displaced in Lough Derg by a related invader, the quagga mussel, first recognised there in 2021. The quagga mussel is a separate species.
[bio31-s2.8.b] A plant can be invasive too. Rhododendron ponticum was brought in as a garden shrub, but in Killarney it now threatens the native woodland.
[bio31-s2.8.0] Rhododendron ponticum is a shrub introduced to Killarney for ornamental planting in the nineteenth century. It forms dense thickets in the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park.
[bio31-s2.8.1] Its evergreen leaves let very little light reach the woodland floor, so oak seedlings and ground plants cannot grow and the wood cannot regenerate (replace its old trees).
[bio31-s2.8.2] Clearing it by cutting and treating regrowth is slow and costly, and the work must be repeated.
[bio31-s2.9.b] Ireland has other invasive species too, and because removal is so costly, the best approach is to stop them spreading in the first place.
[bio31-s2.9.0] Elodea canadensis (Canadian pondweed) is an invasive plant in fresh water.
[bio31-s2.9.1] Japanese knotweed spreads from small root fragments, which makes it hard to remove, and damages buildings and riverbanks.
[bio31-s2.9.2] Prevention is through biosecurity (measures that stop species being moved). An example is the “check, clean, dry” routine for boats and angling gear moved between waters.
[bio31-s2.9.3] Never release aquarium plants or pets into the wild, because they may establish and spread where they have no natural predators.
[bio31-s2.10.b] Clean water is vital to freshwater life. Two cases show what happens when silt and excess nutrients get into Irish rivers and lakes.
[bio31-s2.10.0] The freshwater pearl mussel is a long-lived mussel of clean, fast-flowing rivers. Its larvae (glochidia) must attach to the gills of young native salmon or trout to develop, and the juveniles need clean, well-oxygenated gravel.
[bio31-s2.10.1] Silt and nutrient enrichment from drainage, forestry and farming clog the gravel. In many Irish rivers few or no young mussels survive, and populations consist only of old adults.
[bio31-s2.10.2] Its decline is also linked to the decline of salmon and trout, its hosts, because without them the larvae cannot develop.
[bio31-s2.10.3] Lough Neagh, in Northern Ireland and the largest lake on the island of Ireland, had a massive bloom of blue-green algae (cyanobacteria) in summer 2023.
[bio31-s2.10.4] The bloom was linked mainly to excess phosphorus from agriculture and wastewater built up over decades, along with warm, still weather and zebra mussels.
[bio31-s2.10.5] Impacts of the Lough Neagh bloom: toxic water unsafe for people, pets and livestock; harm to fish and birds; damage to the eel fishery and to tourism; and anger in local communities.
[bio31-s2.11.b] Biodiversity loss damages ecosystems and costs money. Each point below is paired with an example from the Irish case studies.
[bio31-s2.11.0] Environmental: food webs are disrupted when a species is lost, affecting its predators and prey. Ecosystem services such as pollination, water purification and flood control are lost, ecosystems become less resilient, and extinction is irreversible.
[bio31-s2.11.1] Environmental example: about one third of Ireland's wild bee species are threatened with extinction, mainly through loss of flowers and nesting sites. This threatens the pollination of wild plants and crops.
[bio31-s2.11.2] Economic: farming depends on pollinators and healthy soil, and fisheries and angling depend on clean rivers and lakes.
[bio31-s2.11.3] Tourism, for example at Killarney National Park, the Burren and the bogs, depends on landscapes and wildlife.
[bio31-s2.11.4] Invasive species cost money to control, for example clearing rhododendron or unblocking pipes fouled by zebra mussels. Polluted water also costs more to treat.
[bio31-s2.11.5] Conservation also has costs, including compensation to farmers and turf cutters and lost income from land use.
[bio31-s2.12.b] Biodiversity loss also affects people's health, communities and identity. It can cause conflict, because both loss and the rules to prevent it can hit livelihoods.
[bio31-s2.12.0] Social: health and wellbeing benefit from access to nature. Polluted water threatens drinking supplies and recreation, for example on Lough Neagh.
[bio31-s2.12.1] Social conflict: communities that depend on fishing, farming or peat can lose their livelihoods either from biodiversity loss or from conservation rules, which causes conflict.
[bio31-s2.12.2] Cultural: species and landscapes are part of identity, heritage, folklore, music, literature and place names, for example the curlew, the corncrake and the bogs.
[bio31-s2.12.3] Traditional practices such as turf cutting are themselves cultural heritage, which is why some conservation measures are resisted.
[bio31-s2.13.b] Conservation means acting to protect nature. There are two broad approaches, depending on whether the species is protected where it lives or somewhere else.
[bio31-s2.13.0] Conservation is the protection, management and restoration of the natural environment to maintain biodiversity and prevent extinction, while allowing sustainable use.
[bio31-s2.13.1] In situ conservation (in the natural habitat) uses protected areas such as national parks. Killarney National Park was Ireland's first, in 1932.
[bio31-s2.13.2] Natura 2000 sites across the EU include Special Areas of Conservation (SACs) under the EU Habitats Directive (1992) and Special Protection Areas (SPAs) for birds under the EU Birds Directive (1979).
[bio31-s2.13.3] Species protection under the Wildlife Act 1976 made it illegal to kill or disturb protected species.
[bio31-s2.13.4] Habitat management and restoration: rewetting bogs; planting native trees and hedgerows; and controlling invasive species.
[bio31-s2.13.5] Farming for nature uses agri-environment schemes that pay farmers for results, such as species-rich meadows, for example in the Burren.
[bio31-s2.13.6] Species recovery programmes include the Curlew Conservation Programme and the Corncrake LIFE project.
[bio31-s2.13.7] Ex situ conservation (outside the natural habitat) uses seed banks, captive breeding and reintroduction, for example captive rearing of freshwater pearl mussels to release young mussels into rivers.
[bio31-s2.14.b] Laws and national plans set the direction for conservation, but individuals and communities also take part.
[bio31-s2.14.0] The All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, launched in 2015, asks councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide flowers and nesting sites for bees, for example by mowing road verges less often.
[bio31-s2.14.1] In May 2019 the Dáil declared a climate and biodiversity emergency, making Ireland the second country to do so.
[bio31-s2.14.2] The Citizens' Assembly on Biodiversity Loss reported in April 2023 with 159 recommendations.
[bio31-s2.14.3] Ireland's fourth National Biodiversity Action Plan covers 2023–2030.
[bio31-s2.14.4] The EU Nature Restoration Law, adopted in 2024, requires member states to restore damaged ecosystems.
[bio31-s2.14.5] Individuals can record species for citizen-science surveys (research that members of the public contribute to), for example through the National Biodiversity Data Centre. They can also garden for pollinators and avoid spreading invasive species.
[bio31-s2.15.b] A discuss question wants both sides: where conservation has worked, and where it has fallen short. A balanced answer then reaches a conclusion.
[bio31-s2.15.0] Arguments for its effectiveness: protection has brought recoveries, for example the pine marten after 1976, with the return of red squirrels that followed.
[bio31-s2.15.1] Rewetting can restore bog habitat, and targeted payments have raised the breeding success of curlew and corncrake in some areas.
[bio31-s2.15.2] Protected areas keep the last strongholds of rare species.
[bio31-s2.15.3] Limits: declines continue, as curlew numbers fell further between 2015–17 and 2021. Designating a site is not enough without enforcement and management.
[bio31-s2.15.4] Schemes cost money and can conflict with the incomes and traditions of farmers, turf cutters and landowners.
[bio31-s2.15.5] Small, isolated populations may still be lost to predators, disease or chance. Restoration is slow: bogs take centuries to re-form.
[bio31-s2.15.6] Conclusion to model: conservation limits biodiversity loss when it has funding, enforcement and the support of the local people who manage the land.
[bio31-s2.16.b] The syllabus asks you to link what you learn to real, current stories about local ecosystems. Follow these steps to analyse a story.
[bio31-s2.16.0] 1. Find a current local story about a local ecosystem or species. Sources include local newspapers and radio, national news, the websites of the NPWS and the EPA (water quality), BirdWatch Ireland, the National Biodiversity Data Centre, and the county council's heritage or biodiversity officer.
[bio31-s2.16.1] 2. Identify the ecosystem, the species affected, the cause, and the evidence, such as survey data, photographs or expert opinion.
[bio31-s2.16.2] 3. Evaluate the source: who wrote it, whether it is based on data, whether it shows bias (for example from a campaign group or an industry body), and whether other sources agree.
[bio31-s2.16.3] 4. Describe the impacts under the four headings: environmental, economic, social and cultural.
[bio31-s2.16.4] 5. Assess what conservation is being done and whether it is likely to work.
[bio31-s2.16.5] 6. Where possible, visit or survey the ecosystem to gather primary data (data you collect yourself).

CANDIDATES bio31-s5: Carrying capacity and limiting factors
[bio31-s5.0.b] A population is all the individuals of one species living in one area. Its size rises and falls, and in every habitat there is an upper limit that the environment can support. That limit is the carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.0.0] A population's size changes through four processes. Births (natality) and immigration (individuals arriving) increase it; deaths (mortality) and emigration (individuals leaving) decrease it.
[bio31-s5.0.1] A population grows when births plus immigration exceed deaths plus emigration. If the two sides are equal, the size stays steady.
[bio31-s5.0.2] Every population could grow very fast if nothing stopped it, because organisms produce more offspring than are needed to replace themselves. In reality, resources run short, so growth slows and stops.
[bio31-s5.0.3] The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum population size of a species that it can support indefinitely, given the food, water, space and other resources available. It is often given the symbol K.
[bio31-s5.0.4] A limiting factor is any factor that restricts the size, growth or distribution of a population. The factor in shortest supply relative to need sets the limit, even if all the others are plentiful, which is the same principle as limiting factors in photosynthesis.
[bio31-s5.0.5] Carrying capacity is set by the limiting factors acting together, not by any one of them alone.
[bio31-s5.0.6] Carrying capacity is not fixed. It changes when conditions change, such as in a wet or dry year, after a new predator or disease arrives, or with habitat loss or restoration. It also differs between species in the same habitat.
[bio31-s5.0.7] If a population rises above carrying capacity, it damages its resources, for example by overgrazing (eating plants faster than they regrow), and then its numbers fall.
[bio31-s5.1.b] Abiotic factors are the non-living parts of the environment. Each of them can cap the number of plants and animals a habitat supports, and the Irish examples below show how.
[bio31-s5.1.0] Water: all organisms need water. On the dry, free-draining sand of coastal sand dunes, water shortage limits which plants can grow and how many.
[bio31-s5.1.1] Marram grass grows on sand dunes because its rolled leaves and deep roots reduce water loss and reach water, so it survives where most plants cannot. In a dry summer, grass growth slows and fewer grazing animals can be supported.
[bio31-s5.1.2] Oxygen: aquatic animals depend on oxygen dissolved in water, which is scarce compared with air. Salmon, trout and stonefly nymphs need well-oxygenated, cool, fast-flowing water, so oxygen limits their numbers in slow, warm or polluted rivers.
[bio31-s5.1.3] Decomposing organic pollution uses up oxygen in a river, which is why polluted water supports fewer oxygen-demanding animals. In waterlogged bog soil, low oxygen limits roots and decomposers, so few plant species can grow there.
[bio31-s5.1.4] Space: organisms need space to attach, shelter, nest or hold a territory (an area an animal defends). On a rocky shore, bare rock is limited, so barnacles, mussels and seaweeds compete for space to attach.
[bio31-s5.1.5] Seabirds nest only on cliffs and islands with suitable ledges. Many songbirds, such as robins, defend territories, so the number of territories in a woodland limits the breeding population.
[bio31-s5.1.6] Temperature affects enzyme activity, growth and survival. Cold winters kill many small birds such as wrens, and low temperatures in the uplands shorten the growing season, limiting plant growth. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, so temperature also limits fish.
[bio31-s5.1.7] Soil type, pH, minerals, water content and air content limit plants, and so the animals that feed on them. Acidic, nitrogen-poor bog peat supports only specialised plants such as Sphagnum, heather and sundews.
[bio31-s5.2.b] Soil is one of the factors that limit which plants can grow in a place, and plants are the food base for animals. Light is not on the specification's list of abiotic factors, but it is a key limiting factor for plants.
[bio31-s5.2.0] The thin limestone soils of the Burren, Co. Clare, support lime-loving plants, because only plants suited to thin, lime-rich soil can grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.1] Fertile, well-drained lowland soils support productive grassland, because they are fertile and drain well.
[bio31-s5.2.2] Under a closed woodland canopy (the roof of leaves formed by the treetops) in summer, little light reaches the floor, so few plants grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.3] Woodland plants such as bluebells grow and flower in spring, before the leaves of the trees open, which means they get the light they need while the canopy is still open.
[bio31-s5.3.b] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment: other organisms and the diseases they carry. Food, disease, competition and predation all limit populations, and some of them also limit one another.
[bio31-s5.3.0] Food: the supply of food usually sets the carrying capacity for animals. For grazers it is the amount of grass or browse (leaves and shoots of shrubs and trees).
[bio31-s5.3.1] Seed-eaters such as squirrels and wood mice do better in years when trees produce large seed crops. Birds of prey such as barn owls are limited by the number of small mammals they can catch.
[bio31-s5.3.2] Disease: disease spreads faster in dense populations, so it limits large populations most. Squirrelpox virus, carried by grey squirrels, kills red squirrels.
[bio31-s5.3.3] Myxomatosis, a viral disease, killed most of Ireland's rabbits after it arrived in the 1950s. Ash dieback is killing ash trees.
[bio31-s5.3.4] Competition: organisms competing for the same limited resource, such as food, light, water, space or mates, reduce each other's share, so fewer survive or breed. Competition between members of the same species (intraspecific) and between different species (interspecific) both limit populations.
[bio31-s5.3.5] Grey squirrels outcompete red squirrels for food in broadleaf woods, and on a woodland floor taller plants shade out shorter ones. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio31-s5.3.6] Predation: predators kill and eat prey, keeping prey numbers below what the food supply alone would allow. Foxes take rabbits and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew.
[bio31-s5.3.7] Pine martens prey on grey squirrels, reducing their numbers where martens are common. Predator numbers are in turn limited by the number of prey, so the two populations affect each other.
[bio31-s5.4.b] Predation normally keeps herbivore numbers down. Where a predator is missing, food becomes the main limit, and the habitat can pay the price.
[bio31-s5.4.0] Ireland's deer, the native red deer and the sika deer introduced to Powerscourt, Co. Wicklow, in 1860, have had no natural predator since wolves died out in Ireland more than two centuries ago.
[bio31-s5.4.1] Their numbers are limited mainly by food, so in parts of Co. Wicklow they have risen high enough to browse away tree seedlings and stop woodland regenerating.
[bio31-s5.4.2] Culling (killing selected animals) by people is used to keep deer below the level that damages habitats, so people take over the role the predator once had.
[bio31-s5.5.b] Limiting factors fall into two groups, depending on whether their effect changes with how crowded the population is. This explains why some factors steady a population while others crash it without warning.
[bio31-s5.5.0] Density-dependent factors have a greater effect as the population becomes more crowded. They are food shortage, competition, disease and predation, and are mostly biotic.
[bio31-s5.5.1] They slow growth as a population approaches carrying capacity, so they regulate population size, pulling it back towards K.
[bio31-s5.5.2] Density-independent factors affect a population regardless of its size, for example a severe frost, a flood, a storm, a drought or a fire, and are mostly abiotic.
[bio31-s5.5.3] Density-independent factors can cause sudden crashes at any population size, because a frost kills the same fraction whether the population is small or large.
[bio31-s5.5.4] Human activity changes carrying capacity. Draining wet grassland and bogs removes curlew breeding habitat, lowering its carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.5.5] Rewetting a bog or planting hedgerows raises carrying capacity for the species that depend on them, and garden feeders increase the food available for some birds.
[bio31-s5.6.b] A grassland field shows how several limiting factors act on one species at once. For rabbits, the field's carrying capacity is the result of all of them together.
[bio31-s5.6.0] Food: grass is the main density-dependent limit. As rabbit numbers rise, the grass is grazed shorter and competition increases.
[bio31-s5.6.1] Predation by foxes, stoats and birds of prey removes many young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.2] Disease: myxomatosis causes crashes when the population is dense, because the virus spreads fastest between crowded animals.
[bio31-s5.6.3] Space: burrows need well-drained soil, for example on banks and slopes, which limits where warrens (burrow systems) can be dug.
[bio31-s5.6.4] Wet winters, an abiotic and density-independent factor, can flood burrows and kill young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.5] The carrying capacity of the field for rabbits is the population that these factors together allow it to support over time.
[bio31-s5.6.6] Diagram: a simple graph of a population rising and levelling off at a horizontal dashed line labelled “carrying capacity (K)”, with arrows labelled “limiting factors (environmental resistance)” pressing down on the curve.

CANDIDATES bio31-s7: Energy and nutrient transfer
[bio31-s7.0.b] Every ecosystem sorts its organisms by how they get energy and nutrients. Producers bring energy in, consumers pass it along, and decomposers return the nutrients to the soil or water.
[bio31-s7.0.0] Producers are autotrophs: organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly by photosynthesis, using light energy. They include green plants, algae, phytoplankton (microscopic floating algae) and photosynthetic bacteria.
[bio31-s7.0.1] Producers are the entry point of energy and matter into every food chain, because no other group can turn light energy and inorganic substances into organic food. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio31-s7.0.2] Consumers are heterotrophs, meaning they cannot make their own food and obtain energy and nutrients by eating other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers, e.g. rabbits, slugs, caterpillars and limpets.
[bio31-s7.0.3] Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, e.g. thrushes, frogs and crabs. Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers, e.g. sparrowhawks, foxes and herons.
[bio31-s7.0.4] A top (apex) consumer is at the end of a chain and is not normally eaten. Carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat plants and animals, e.g. badgers, foxes and humans.
[bio31-s7.0.5] Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, feed on dead organisms and waste. They secrete enzymes onto the dead matter and absorb the products (saprophytic nutrition), and so release minerals and CO₂ for producers to reuse.
[bio31-s7.0.6] Detritivores are animals that eat dead organic matter (detritus), e.g. earthworms and woodlice. They break it into smaller pieces, which gives decomposers a larger surface to act on, so decomposition is faster. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio31-s7.1.b] A food chain shows who eats whom, and each step is a trophic (feeding) level. Following the chain shows how energy and nutrients are passed on from one organism to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.0] A trophic (feeding) level is the position an organism occupies in a food chain, according to how it obtains its energy. Producers are trophic level 1, primary consumers level 2, secondary consumers level 3 and tertiary consumers level 4.
[bio31-s7.1.1] A food chain is a sequence of organisms in which each is eaten by the next, showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one trophic level to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.2] Arrows point from the organism that is eaten towards the organism that eats it, which is the direction of energy flow ("is eaten by").
[bio31-s7.1.3] Irish examples, one per ecosystem. Grassland: grass → rabbit → fox. Hedgerow or woodland: oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk. Rocky shore: seaweed → periwinkle → crab → herring gull.
[bio31-s7.1.4] Two more Irish examples. Freshwater: phytoplankton → water flea → minnow → heron (or pike). Sea: phytoplankton → zooplankton → herring → seal.
[bio31-s7.1.5] Grazing food chains start with living producers. Detritus (decomposer) food chains start with dead organic matter, e.g. dead leaves → earthworm → blackbird. Much of the energy in a woodland passes through detritus chains.
[bio31-s7.1.6] Food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels, because so much energy is lost at each level that too little is left to support a further level.
[bio31-s7.2.b] Real organisms rarely eat just one thing or get eaten by just one thing. A food web joins the separate food chains of an ecosystem into one network, which is why it is a more realistic picture.
[bio31-s7.2.0] A food web is a set of interlinked food chains in an ecosystem. It is more realistic than a single chain because most organisms eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator.
[bio31-s7.2.1] An organism may also feed at more than one trophic level, as an omnivore does, or as a fox does when it eats rabbits and also beetles.
[bio31-s7.2.2] Example grassland and hedgerow web to draw: grass → rabbit, grass → slug and grass → caterpillar; grass seeds → wood mouse; slug → thrush and caterpillar → thrush; rabbit → fox; wood mouse → fox and wood mouse → barn owl; thrush → sparrowhawk.
[bio31-s7.2.3] Decomposers (bacteria, fungi and earthworms) feed on dead material from every level of this web, because every organism eventually dies or produces waste.
[bio31-s7.2.4] Food webs show interdependence: a change in one population affects many others, directly through feeding and indirectly through competition for the same food. For example, the fox and the barn owl both eat wood mice, so they compete.
[bio31-s7.2.5] Diagram: the food web drawn with arrows pointing towards the consumer, organisms placed in rows by trophic level, and decomposers shown at the side receiving arrows from all levels.
[bio31-s7.3.b] Energy enters an ecosystem once, as sunlight, and then dwindles as it is passed along a food chain. Most of it is lost at every step, and this explains both the shape of food chains and how we should feed people.
[bio31-s7.3.0] Energy enters ecosystems as light. Producers capture only a small fraction of the light that reaches them, around 1–2%, and store it as chemical energy in organic molecules. The rest is reflected, passes through leaves, or is lost as heat.
[bio31-s7.3.1] On average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to, and stored in, the next. The figure varies, roughly from 5% to 20%, so about 90% is lost at each transfer.
[bio31-s7.3.2] Respiration releases energy for movement, growth, active transport and keeping warm, and this ends up as heat lost to the surroundings. Birds and mammals lose the most, because they keep a constant body temperature.
[bio31-s7.3.3] Roots, bark, wood, bones and fur are left uneaten. Undigested food is egested as faeces, e.g. cellulose eaten by herbivores, and some energy is lost in excretory products such as urea. The energy in uneaten parts, faeces and dead bodies passes to decomposers, not to the next level.
[bio31-s7.3.4] Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuous input of energy from the Sun.
[bio31-s7.3.5] Worked example (illustrative, using the 10% figure, not measured data): grass in a field stores 10 000 kJ, the rabbits that eat it store about 1 000 kJ, and the foxes about 100 kJ. A fourth level would store about 10 kJ, too little to support a population.
[bio31-s7.3.6] So there are fewer top predators, they often need large territories, and they are especially vulnerable to habitat loss.
[bio31-s7.3.7] Consequence for food production: eating plants directly, a shorter chain, uses the energy captured by crops more efficiently than feeding crops to animals and then eating the animals. A given area of land can therefore feed more people on plant-based food.
[bio31-s7.4.b] Matter, unlike energy, passes along a food chain in the form of the food organisms eat. Some is lost at each level, but the ecosystem as a whole does not lose it, because decomposers send it back to producers.
[bio31-s7.4.0] Nutrients (matter) pass between trophic levels when one organism eats another. The carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids of the food are digested, absorbed and built into the consumer's own body (assimilated).
[bio31-s7.4.1] The elements involved are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (in all biomolecules), nitrogen (in proteins and nucleic acids), phosphorus (in DNA, ATP and phospholipids), and minerals such as calcium and iron.
[bio31-s7.4.2] The amount of matter passed on also decreases at each level. Much of the food eaten is respired (carbon leaves as CO₂, hydrogen as water), egested in faeces, or excreted (nitrogen as urea or ammonia). So biomass (the mass of living material) also decreases up a food chain.
[bio31-s7.4.3] Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the ecosystem. Decomposers break down dead bodies, faeces and urine, releasing CO₂ and minerals such as nitrate, phosphate and ammonium that producers absorb and reuse, so nutrients cycle.
[bio31-s7.4.4] Contrast: energy flows one way through the ecosystem and is lost as heat, whereas nutrients cycle and are reused again and again.
[bio31-s7.4.5] Persistent substances that are not broken down or excreted, such as the insecticide DDT and mercury, pass up the food chain with the food and build up in the bodies of organisms at each level. They reach the highest concentrations in top consumers, which is called biomagnification.
[bio31-s7.4.6] Biomagnification is how DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey, which sit at the top of food chains and so carry the highest doses.
[bio31-s7.4.7] Diagram: a flow diagram of one food chain, with energy arrows getting narrower at each level, heat-loss arrows from every level, and a separate loop of nutrient arrows from all levels to decomposers and back to producers.
[bio31-s7.5.b] A food chain or web is a model: a simplified picture that lets you work out what happens when one population goes up or down. Following a set method keeps the prediction logical.
[bio31-s7.5.0] The method has five steps. 1. Find the organisms that eat the changed species (its predators).
[bio31-s7.5.1] 2. Find what the changed species eats (its food).
[bio31-s7.5.2] 3. Find its competitors, the species that share its food.
[bio31-s7.5.3] 4. Predict the direction of each change, giving reasons.
[bio31-s7.5.4] 5. Note other effects, e.g. on plants and habitat.
[bio31-s7.5.5] Worked prediction using the food web above, if a disease such as myxomatosis (a viral disease of rabbits) kills most of the rabbits: (a) grass is grazed less and grows taller, providing more food and cover for slugs, caterpillars and wood mice.
[bio31-s7.5.6] Continued: (b) foxes lose their main prey, so they eat more wood mice and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds, and fox numbers may fall. (c) Wood mice decline from the extra fox predation, so barn owls, which compete with foxes for wood mice, have less food.
[bio31-s7.5.7] Real outcomes depend on how flexible each predator's diet is, because a predator that can switch to other food will be affected less.
[bio31-s7.6.b] Changes spread through a web both when a species is removed and when one is added. Because the model is simplified, its predictions have limits.
[bio31-s7.6.0] Removing a top predator can cause a trophic cascade, a chain of effects passing down the food web. In Ireland, with no wolves, deer numbers increase, they browse young trees, and the woodland cannot regenerate, which affects every species that depends on the woodland.
[bio31-s7.6.1] Adding a species also spreads through a web. An invasive filter-feeder such as the zebra mussel removes phytoplankton at the base of a lake food web, reducing food for zooplankton and for the fish that eat them. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio31-s7.6.2] Food chains and webs simplify real feeding, because many links are left out and diets change with the season and with age.
[bio31-s7.6.3] They usually show no numbers or amounts of energy, which pyramids do, and decomposers are often left out. There is more on models in general in Models.

CANDIDATES bio31-s8: Pyramids of numbers
[bio31-s8.0.b] A food chain tells you who eats whom, but not how many of each organism there are. A pyramid of numbers adds the counts, giving a quick picture of how an ecosystem is built level by level.
[bio31-s8.0.0] A pyramid of numbers is a diagram showing the number of organisms at each trophic level (feeding level) of a food chain or food web in an area at one time. Each level is drawn as a horizontal bar whose width is proportional to the number of organisms.
[bio31-s8.0.1] The producers (organisms that make their own food, such as plants) form the bottom bar. The primary, secondary and tertiary consumers (the animals that eat the level below) are drawn above them in order.
[bio31-s8.0.2] In most ecosystems the pyramid is upright: there are many producers, fewer primary consumers, fewer still secondary consumers, and very few top consumers. For example, many grass plants → fewer grasshoppers → fewer frogs → one heron; or grass → rabbits → foxes.
[bio31-s8.0.3] Numbers usually decrease up the pyramid because about 90% of energy is lost at each trophic level, so each level can support fewer organisms than the one below.
[bio31-s8.0.4] A second reason is size: predators are usually larger than their prey, so each predator needs to eat many prey, which means the prey must be more numerous.
[bio31-s8.0.5] Pyramids, like food chains, are usually restricted to three or four levels. So little energy reaches the top level that it cannot support a further level of consumers. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio31-s8.1.b] Ecologists use pyramids of numbers because they turn a food chain into something they can measure, compare and use to make predictions. Drawing one is a short, step-by-step skill.
[bio31-s8.1.0] Feeding relationships: a pyramid shows a food chain quantitatively, that is, how many of each organism there are. It also shows how many prey each predator depends on.
[bio31-s8.1.1] Comparison: it lets two ecosystems, or one ecosystem at different times, be compared, because the bars for each level can be set side by side.
[bio31-s8.1.2] Prediction: it helps to predict the effect of a change in one level on the others. For example, if a disease reduces the primary consumers, the secondary consumers will decline because they have less food.
[bio31-s8.1.3] It also shows why top predators are few and vulnerable: there are only a few of them, so a small loss in their food supply or numbers has a large effect.
[bio31-s8.1.4] How the numbers are found: by sampling the area, for example counting plants in quadrats (square frames laid on the ground) and animals caught in traps or nets, then scaling up to the whole area.
[bio31-s8.1.5] Each organism is then assigned to a trophic level. There is more on sampling methods in Investigating an Ecosystem.
[bio31-s8.1.6] To draw one, step 1: write out the food chain and count, or take from the data, the organisms at each level. Step 2: draw a horizontal bar for each level, with the producers at the bottom, all centred on a vertical line.
[bio31-s8.1.7] Step 3: make each bar's width proportional to the number. Alternatively use a scale, or a logarithmic scale (one that handles very large numbers), or draw it “not to scale” and say so. Step 4: label each bar with the trophic level, the organism and the number.
[bio31-s8.1.8] Diagram: upright pyramid of numbers for grass → grasshopper → frog → heron, with these labels: producer, primary consumer, secondary consumer, tertiary consumer, organism name on each bar, number (illustrative) on each bar.
[bio31-s8.2.b] Pyramids of numbers are not always the same shape. The shape depends on how big the producers are and whether the top level is made of parasites, so you need to recognise three cases.
[bio31-s8.2.0] Upright pyramid: the usual shape, wide at the base and narrowing to the top. It occurs when the producers are small and numerous and each consumer is larger than its prey.
[bio31-s8.2.1] Inverted pyramid (narrow base): when one large producer supports many small herbivores, the base is narrower than the level above. For example, one oak tree supports thousands of caterpillars, which support a few blue tits, which support one sparrowhawk, so the second bar is wider than the first.
[bio31-s8.2.2] Another narrow-base example: one rose bush supports many caterpillars or aphids, which support a few blackbirds, which support one hawk. One big plant provides enough food for many small animals.
[bio31-s8.2.3] Because the upper levels still narrow, the narrow-base pyramid is spindle- or diamond-shaped rather than a full upside-down triangle.
[bio31-s8.2.4] Inverted at the top: when the top level is made of parasites (organisms that live on or in a host), which are smaller and more numerous than their host, the top bar is wider than the one below.
[bio31-s8.2.5] Examples of the parasite shape: grass → rabbits → fleas, or one tree → birds → many lice and fleas on each bird.
[bio31-s8.2.6] Diagram: three pyramids side by side. Upright: grass → grasshopper → frog → heron, narrow-base: oak tree → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawk, top-heavy with parasites: grass → rabbits → fleas. Label each level (illustrative numbers).
[bio31-s8.3.b] Counting organisms is a simple idea but it gives a limited picture of an ecosystem. Each of the following weaknesses is a possible exam answer.
[bio31-s8.3.0] They take no account of the size of organisms: one oak tree counts the same as one blade of grass. The shape can therefore be inverted and misleading about how much food each level provides.
[bio31-s8.3.1] The numbers at different levels can differ so much, for example millions of grass plants and one fox, that the pyramid cannot be drawn to scale.
[bio31-s8.3.2] It is hard to decide what counts as an individual for some organisms, for example grass and clover plants that spread by runners (stems growing along the ground), or a clump of moss.
[bio31-s8.3.3] Organisms that feed at more than one trophic level are hard to place. Examples are omnivores, which eat both plants and animals, such as a fox eating rabbits and beetles, or a badger.
[bio31-s8.3.4] Counting is difficult and usually based on samples. Small, hidden, nocturnal or very mobile animals are hard to count accurately, so the numbers are estimates.
[bio31-s8.3.5] It shows the numbers at one moment only, so it changes with the seasons, for example there are many more caterpillars in early summer.
[bio31-s8.3.6] It does not show the amount of biomass (the mass of living material) or the energy at each level, and it usually leaves out decomposers (organisms such as fungi and bacteria that break down dead matter).
[bio31-s8.3.7] The fix for the size problem is a pyramid of biomass, which takes the mass of organisms into account. There is more on this in Pyramids of biomass.

CANDIDATES bio32-s1: Collection and identification
[bio32-s1.0.b] An ecological investigation studies living things in their natural surroundings, and here the surroundings are a grassland habitat. The first job is to find out which organisms live there, because every later measurement is built on that list.
[bio32-s1.0.0] The aim is to find out which organisms live in a chosen grassland habitat (the place where a community of organisms lives), to collect and identify them, and so to build a species list. The surveys, abiotic measurements and feeding-relationship work are all based on that list.
[bio32-s1.0.1] Data gathered first-hand in the field, by your own counting, measuring and observing, are called primary data. Secondary data would come from books or other people’s work.
[bio32-s1.0.2] 1. A preliminary visit to choose and map the study area (the mapping is done in the Surveys note).
[bio32-s1.0.3] 2. Observe, collect and identify the organisms, which is the stage covered here.
[bio32-s1.0.4] 3. A qualitative survey (which species are present, giving a species list) and a quantitative survey (how many of each) are carried out in the Surveys note.
[bio32-s1.0.5] 4. Measure abiotic factors (non-living factors such as light and temperature) and relate them to the distribution and abundance of a species, as in the Abiotic factors note.
[bio32-s1.0.6] 5. Work out feeding and symbiotic relationships (close partnerships between different species), as in the Biotic factors and relationships note.
[bio32-s1.0.7] 6. Present the results and draw conclusions.
[bio32-s1.1.b] Before collecting anything you visit the site once to plan, because what you can find depends on the place, the time of year and the gear you bring.
[bio32-s1.1.0] On the preliminary visit, walk over the site and note its boundaries and main features: hedges, trees, paths, wet patches, slope and gate. These features create different microhabitats (small areas with their own conditions) that you will need to sample.
[bio32-s1.1.1] Get permission from the landowner, check access and hazards, and choose suitable dates, so that the work is legal and safe before anyone arrives with equipment.
[bio32-s1.1.2] Organisms change with the season, so collect over several visits if possible, for example late spring to early autumn, when most plants flower and most invertebrates (animals without backbones) are active.
[bio32-s1.1.3] Collecting equipment: pitfall traps (jars, trowel, flat stones or lids), pooter, sweep net, beating tray and stick, Tullgren funnel, collecting jars and labelled specimen tubes, and white trays on which small animals show up clearly.
[bio32-s1.1.4] Identification and recording equipment: hand lens, identification keys and field guides, camera or phone, notebook and pencil, and labels.
[bio32-s1.1.5] Protection and measuring equipment: gloves, tape measures, and quadrats (square frames used in the Surveys note).
[bio32-s1.2.b] When you compare two areas or two times, the comparison is only fair if the collecting effort is the same each time. That means deciding what you change, what you measure and what you keep constant.
[bio32-s1.2.0] The independent variable (the one you choose to change) is the area or time sampled.
[bio32-s1.2.1] The dependent variable (the one you measure) is the kinds and numbers of organisms caught.
[bio32-s1.2.2] The controlled variables (kept the same) are the type and number of traps, the trapping time, the number of sweeps, the time of day and the weather, so that the collections are a fair comparison.
[bio32-s1.2.3] No true control is possible in a field study, because the conditions in the field can’t be held constant. This is why you control as many variables as you can and repeat the collection.
[bio32-s1.3.b] Not every organism can or should be caught. Large, fast or delicate organisms are studied by looking, and animals that hide are detected from the traces they leave.
[bio32-s1.3.0] Direct observation (looking at the organism itself) is used for organisms that are large, fast or that should not be caught, such as birds, rabbits, foxes and butterflies, and for all plants, which are identified where they grow and not dug up.
[bio32-s1.3.1] Use binoculars for birds and mammals at a distance, and a hand lens (about ×10) for small features such as the hairs or veins of a leaf.
[bio32-s1.3.2] Indirect signs show animals that are rarely seen: droppings, tracks in mud, burrows, nests, feathers, molehills and wormcasts.
[bio32-s1.3.3] Feeding signs are indirect signs too: nibbled leaves, holes in leaves made by slugs or caterpillars, and broken snail shells beside a stone used by a song thrush.
[bio32-s1.3.4] Record each observation at once, so nothing is forgotten: the organism (or ‘unknown species A’ with a sketch or photograph until it is identified) and where it was found (its microhabitat, for example under a stone or on a dandelion leaf).
[bio32-s1.3.5] Also record the time, the weather and what the organism was doing, such as feeding on what.
[bio32-s1.3.6] Photographs record organisms without removing them and can be checked later against keys, so record the location and date with each image.
[bio32-s1.4.b] Many grassland invertebrates (animals without backbones) walk over the soil surface and are hard to spot. A pitfall trap catches them as they travel, with no effort from the collector.
[bio32-s1.4.0] A pitfall trap is a jar or plastic cup sunk into a hole in the ground so that its rim is exactly level with the soil surface. Animals walking across the ground fall in and can’t climb the smooth sides.
[bio32-s1.4.1] A flat stone or lid raised on small stones covers the trap, which keeps out rain and stops birds and other predators taking the catch. Bait, such as a small piece of meat or fruit, can be added.
[bio32-s1.4.2] Pitfall traps catch ground-dwelling (crawling) invertebrates: ground beetles, spiders, woodlice, slugs, ants and millipedes.
[bio32-s1.4.3] Check traps at least every 24 hours, ideally early morning, because predators such as ground beetles and spiders will eat the other animals in the trap.
[bio32-s1.4.4] Remove all traps at the end of the study so that no animal is trapped later.
[bio32-s1.4.5] Diagram: a pitfall trap in section: jar in the soil with rim level with the surface, raised flat stone cover on small supports, bait, and animals inside. Label soil surface, jar, cover, bait.
[bio32-s1.5.b] Tiny animals living in soil and leaf litter (the layer of dead leaves on the ground) are too small to pick out by hand. A Tullgren funnel gets them to leave the sample by themselves.
[bio32-s1.5.0] A Tullgren funnel extracts small animals from a sample of soil or leaf litter.
[bio32-s1.5.1] The sample sits on a wire mesh (gauze) in a funnel under a lamp, a light bulb about 10–20 cm above it.
[bio32-s1.5.2] The heat and light dry the sample from the top, so the animals move down away from the heat, light and dryness, through the mesh and into a collecting beaker below. The lamp drives the animals away; it does not attract them.
[bio32-s1.5.3] The beaker holds damp tissue paper to keep the animals alive, or ethanol if they are to be preserved for identification. Leave the funnel for several hours to a day.
[bio32-s1.5.4] A Tullgren funnel catches mites, springtails, small beetles and their larvae, and small worms.
[bio32-s1.5.5] The mesh must be fine enough to hold back the soil, and too hot a lamp kills the animals before they leave.
[bio32-s1.5.6] Diagram: a Tullgren funnel: lamp, soil or leaf-litter sample, wire gauze, funnel, collecting beaker with damp tissue (or ethanol), and an arrow showing animals moving down away from the heat and light.
[bio32-s1.6.b] Earthworms and mammals need their own methods, because earthworms burrow too deeply for pitfall traps and mammals need careful, humane handling.
[bio32-s1.6.0] Earthworms are collected by digging a measured block of soil and sorting it by hand on a white tray, or by watering a marked area with a mustard-powder solution, which irritates them so that they come to the surface.
[bio32-s1.6.1] Rinse earthworms in clean water at once.
[bio32-s1.6.2] A small-mammal trap is a baited box with dry bedding and food inside. The door closes behind the animal, which is unharmed.
[bio32-s1.6.3] Small-mammal traps are used only under teacher supervision. Check them at least every few hours and release the animal where it was caught.
[bio32-s1.6.4] Some mammal species are protected by law, so trapping may need a licence. Mammals in grassland are more often recorded by observation and signs, because trapping them is restricted.
[bio32-s1.7.b] Many invertebrates live on stems and leaves rather than on the ground. Two methods knock them off the plants so they can be counted.
[bio32-s1.7.0] A sweep net is a strong net with a fine cloth bag on a stout frame and handle. Sweep it quickly through long grass and low plants in a series of side-to-side arcs while walking slowly, so that animals on the plants are knocked into the bag.
[bio32-s1.7.1] After sweeping, fold the bag over to close it, then empty it onto a white tray or into a jar.
[bio32-s1.7.2] A sweep net catches grasshoppers, aphids, flies, beetles, spiders, caterpillars and leafhoppers.
[bio32-s1.7.3] Use a fixed number of sweeps (for example ten) in each area so that catches can be compared.
[bio32-s1.7.4] A sweep net doesn’t work in wet grass, which soaks the bag and damages the animals.
[bio32-s1.7.5] A beating tray is a white sheet or tray (or an upturned umbrella) held under a branch of a hedge, shrub or tree at the grassland edge. The branch is struck sharply with a stick a set number of times.
[bio32-s1.7.6] Animals living on the branch fall onto the tray, where they are easy to see against the white. A beating tray catches caterpillars, beetles, spiders, ladybirds and shield bugs, which are then collected with a pooter.
[bio32-s1.8.b] Small, fast animals are hard to pick up without crushing them. A pooter lets you suck them gently into a container.
[bio32-s1.8.0] A pooter is a clear container (jar or tube) closed by a stopper through which two tubes pass.
[bio32-s1.8.1] The user places the end of the long inlet tube next to a small animal and sucks on the mouthpiece tube. The animal is drawn up the inlet tube into the container.
[bio32-s1.8.2] A piece of fine gauze (muslin) over the inner end of the mouthpiece tube stops the animal or debris being sucked into the user’s mouth.
[bio32-s1.8.3] A pooter is used for small, fast-moving animals (small insects, spiders, springtails) from a beating tray, sweep net, the ground or bark.
[bio32-s1.8.4] Empty it often, because animals inside can harm each other.
[bio32-s1.8.5] Diagram: a pooter: container, stopper, inlet tube, mouthpiece tube, gauze over the inner end of the mouthpiece tube, arrows for the air flow and the animal entering.
[bio32-s1.9.b] Flying insects escape from ground traps and sweep nets, so they need methods of their own. One works by day with a net and one by night with a lamp.
[bio32-s1.9.0] A butterfly net, a light, fine-meshed net on a long handle, catches flying insects such as butterflies and bees. Hold the net closed with a twist of the bag and release the insect after identification.
[bio32-s1.9.1] Bees and wasps can sting, so many are identified by observation instead of being caught.
[bio32-s1.9.2] A light trap (moth trap) is a bright lamp, often ultraviolet, above a box with egg trays inside, left on overnight.
[bio32-s1.9.3] Night-flying insects, mainly moths, are attracted to the light, fall into the box and settle in the trays. They are identified and released next morning, away from birds, which would otherwise eat them.
[bio32-s1.10.b] Once animals are caught, they must be kept alive, named and recorded quickly, because the record is the data the whole investigation depends on.
[bio32-s1.10.0] Place animals in labelled jars or specimen tubes with a little vegetation, keep them cool and shaded, and identify them as soon as possible on a white tray with a hand lens.
[bio32-s1.10.1] Record for each catch the name (or ‘species A’), the number caught, the method and place of capture, and the date and time. These are the raw primary data used in the Surveys note.
[bio32-s1.10.2] Return every animal to the place where it was caught, after identification.
[bio32-s1.10.3] Collect plants only when they are common and can’t be identified where they grow, taking a single leaf or flower rather than the whole plant.
[bio32-s1.11.b] Once you have an organism, you need its name. An identification key gets you there by asking a short series of questions about what you can see.
[bio32-s1.11.0] An identification key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by working through a series of questions or statements about its visible features.
[bio32-s1.11.1] A dichotomous key (‘dichotomous’ means dividing in two) gives a pair of contrasting statements at each step, called a couplet.
[bio32-s1.11.2] The user chooses the statement that fits the organism, and follows the number or name it leads to, until a name is reached.
[bio32-s1.11.3] Good keys use features that are easy to see and don’t vary between individuals or with age or season: number of legs, number of body parts, wings present or absent, shell present or absent, leaf shape and arrangement, flower colour and number of petals.
[bio32-s1.11.4] Good keys avoid size, which varies with age, and vague words such as ‘large’, because two users might judge them differently.
[bio32-s1.12.b] Here is a key to five grassland invertebrates, written as numbered couplets. Work through it with an animal in mind, then see how such a key is built.
[bio32-s1.12.0] 1. Legs present: go to 2. No legs: go to 4.
[bio32-s1.12.1] 2. Three pairs of legs (6 legs) and body in three parts (head, thorax, abdomen): insect (for example a ground beetle). More than 6 legs: go to 3.
[bio32-s1.12.2] 3. Four pairs of legs (8 legs) and body in two parts: spider. Seven pairs of legs (14 legs) and body flattened and covered with overlapping plates: woodlouse.
[bio32-s1.12.3] 4. Body made of many ring-like segments: earthworm. Body not segmented, soft and slimy: go to 5.
[bio32-s1.12.4] 5. Shell present: snail. No shell: slug. Using the key: an animal with 14 legs goes 1, then 2, then 3, then woodlouse.
[bio32-s1.12.5] To make a key, list the organisms, then choose a feature that splits them into two groups.
[bio32-s1.12.6] Keep splitting each group with a new feature until each organism stands alone, then check the key by keying out each organism in turn to see that it reaches the right name.
[bio32-s1.12.7] A tree diagram (branching chart) of the same splits is another way to show a key.
[bio32-s1.13.b] Keys are not the only help. Several other aids check or speed up an identification, and you still need a plan for organisms you can’t name.
[bio32-s1.13.0] Illustrated field guides and identification charts (for example of wild flowers, grasses, insects and birds) show pictures to compare with your specimen. You can also compare it with labelled specimens or photographs, or ask an expert such as a teacher or naturalist.
[bio32-s1.13.1] Image-recognition apps on a phone are an aid, but their suggestions must be checked against a key or guide, because they are often wrong for small invertebrates.
[bio32-s1.13.2] A hand lens or a stereo (dissecting) microscope shows small features that decide between similar species.
[bio32-s1.13.3] Identify each organism to species if possible. Where that isn’t possible, name the group (for example ‘a ground beetle’) and give it a code (species A, B …) used consistently throughout the study, so that it can still be counted in the surveys.
[bio32-s1.14.b] Knowing what to expect makes identification quicker. These are the plants and animals typically found in an Irish grassland.
[bio32-s1.14.0] Typical plants: grasses (for example perennial ryegrass in pasture), daisy, dandelion, creeping buttercup, white clover, ribwort plantain and, in damp patches, rushes.
[bio32-s1.14.1] Daisy and dandelion grow as flat rosettes of leaves close to the ground, which helps them survive mowing and grazing.
[bio32-s1.14.2] Typical invertebrates: earthworms, slugs, snails, ground beetles, spiders, woodlice (in damp places under stones or logs), ants, aphids, grasshoppers, ladybirds, caterpillars, butterflies and bumblebees.
[bio32-s1.14.3] Typical vertebrates (animals with backbones): rabbits, the Irish hare, foxes, badgers, hedgehogs, wood mice, pygmy shrews, and birds such as the song thrush, blackbird and kestrel.
[bio32-s1.15.b] The methods above suit grassland. In other habitats the organisms and conditions differ, so you choose different methods to match.
[bio32-s1.15.0] Woodland: beating trays under branches and pitfall traps in the leaf litter are the main methods. Leaf-litter samples go in a Tullgren funnel, trees are identified by leaf, bark and fruit, and the layers (canopy, shrub, herb and ground layers) are recorded separately.
[bio32-s1.15.1] Rocky shore: animals are mostly collected by hand from under seaweed, in rock pools and under stones (turned back the right way up), or simply counted where they are, as many are attached (barnacles, limpets, mussels). Seaweeds are identified with a key.
[bio32-s1.15.2] Rocky shore work is done at low tide, ideally a spring low tide. Check tide tables first, and start at the lowest point as the tide goes out.
[bio32-s1.15.3] Freshwater (stream or pond): a pond net is swept through the water and vegetation.
[bio32-s1.15.4] In a stream, kick sampling is used: the net is held on the bed just downstream while the stones upstream are disturbed with the feet for a fixed time, so that animals are washed into the net.
[bio32-s1.15.5] The catch from kick sampling is emptied into a white tray of water and identified, for example mayfly, stonefly and caddis fly larvae, freshwater shrimps, snails and leeches.
[bio32-s1.15.6] A plankton net (very fine mesh) is towed slowly through the water to collect microscopic organisms.
[bio32-s1.15.7] Freshwater animals are kept in water from the same habitat and returned quickly.
[bio32-s1.16.b] Fieldwork takes you away from the classroom, so you must plan for risks on land and near water.
[bio32-s1.16.0] Work in groups, never alone, and tell someone where you are going. Wear suitable clothes and footwear for the weather.
[bio32-s1.16.1] Take care near livestock, electric fences, nettles and brambles. Wear gloves when handling soil, leaf litter and traps, cover cuts, and wash hands before eating.
[bio32-s1.16.2] Check for ticks after working in long grass, because ticks can carry the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio32-s1.16.3] Be aware of anyone with an allergy to bee or wasp stings.
[bio32-s1.16.4] Near water, never work alone, and check the depth and the current before entering a stream.
[bio32-s1.16.5] At the shore, know the tide times, watch for incoming tides and take care on wet, slippery rocks covered with seaweed.
[bio32-s1.16.6] Cover cuts and wash hands after contact with fresh water, which can carry the bacteria that cause leptospirosis (Weil’s disease), spread in the urine of rats.
[bio32-s1.17.b] Collecting can damage the habitat you are studying. A code of conduct protects the habitat and its organisms.
[bio32-s1.17.0] Get permission from the landowner, close gates, and keep disturbance and trampling to a minimum.
[bio32-s1.17.1] Replace stones and logs the way they were found, because animals beneath them depend on the damp, dark conditions.
[bio32-s1.17.2] Handle animals gently and return them to where they were caught.
[bio32-s1.17.3] Don’t pick or uproot plants unnecessarily, and never collect protected or rare species.
[bio32-s1.17.4] Remove all traps and litter at the end of the study.
[bio32-s1.18.b] A species list from one afternoon is never complete. Each method, and each visit, has built-in limits that you should recognise and reduce.
[bio32-s1.18.0] Each collection method catches only some kinds of organism: pitfall traps catch only animals that walk on the ground, and sweep nets only animals on tall plants. Several methods must be used to build a complete species list.
[bio32-s1.18.1] Catches depend on the weather, the time of day and the season. Many animals are nocturnal or are active only in warm, dry or damp conditions, so one visit misses many species.
[bio32-s1.18.2] Repeat collections on several days and at different times, under similar conditions when comparing areas.
[bio32-s1.18.3] Animals in traps may escape or be eaten by others before they are counted, so check traps often. Birds may take the catch from uncovered traps.
[bio32-s1.18.4] Misidentification, especially of small invertebrates and of grasses that are not in flower, is a source of error. Check identifications with a second key or guide, or with the teacher.
[bio32-s1.18.5] Disturbance by the collectors (trampling, noise) changes animals’ behaviour and damages the habitat.

CANDIDATES bio32-s2: Surveys
[bio32-s2.0.b] A survey is a planned way of finding out what lives in a habitat (the place where a community of organisms lives). There are two kinds: one asks 'which species?' and the other asks 'how many?'.
[bio32-s2.0.0] A qualitative survey records which species are present in the habitat (a species list), without measuring how many of each, so it gives the 'what' but not the 'how much'.
[bio32-s2.0.1] A qualitative survey is built from all the observations and collections made during the fieldwork. There is more on this in Collection and identification.
[bio32-s2.0.2] A qualitative survey can add a subjective estimate of abundance (how many there are) using a scale such as DAFOR: Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare, judged by eye for each species.
[bio32-s2.0.3] The DAFOR scale is quick, but different observers give different grades, so it isn't objective (it depends on the person's opinion, not on a measurement).
[bio32-s2.0.4] A quantitative survey measures how many of each species there are, as numbers, percentages or densities: percentage frequency, percentage cover, density per m², or an estimate of population size.
[bio32-s2.0.5] Quantitative data can be compared between areas and analysed mathematically, so it can be used, for example, to calculate a diversity index (a single number describing how varied a community is), such as Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio32-s2.0.6] Distribution means where in the habitat a species is found; abundance means how many there are. Surveys measure both.
[bio32-s2.1.b] Counting every organism in a habitat is almost never possible, so samples (small parts of the habitat) are taken and the results used to estimate the whole. How the samples are chosen decides whether the estimate can be trusted.
[bio32-s2.1.0] A sample must be representative, meaning it reflects the whole habitat: it must be large enough and chosen without bias (without favouring particular spots).
[bio32-s2.1.1] Random sampling is used when the habitat looks uniform (the same all over), so that every point has an equal chance of being chosen.
[bio32-s2.1.2] 1. Lay two long tape measures at right angles along two edges of the study area to form the axes of a grid.
[bio32-s2.1.3] 2. Generate pairs of random numbers (with a calculator, a phone app or random-number tables) as coordinates, for example (7, 23) means 7 m along one tape and 23 m along the other.
[bio32-s2.1.4] 3. Walk to each coordinate and place the quadrat (the square sampling frame) there, with its corner on the point.
[bio32-s2.1.5] Throwing a quadrat over the shoulder is not truly random and is unsafe, so don't use it.
[bio32-s2.2.b] Random sampling suits an even habitat, but conditions often change from one side of a habitat to the other. Then samples are placed in an orderly way, and enough of them must be taken to give a reliable result.
[bio32-s2.2.0] Systematic sampling places samples at regular intervals, for example a quadrat every 2 m along a line. It is used where conditions change across the habitat (a gradient, for example from a hedge into open field), to show how species change along it; this is a transect.
[bio32-s2.2.1] The more samples, the more reliable the estimate, because chance differences between individual spots average out.
[bio32-s2.2.2] Take quadrats one at a time and keep a running mean (or a running total of species found).
[bio32-s2.2.3] When adding more quadrats no longer changes the mean (or adds new species), enough have been taken. In a grassland this is usually at least 10 to 20 quadrats.
[bio32-s2.3.b] A quadrat is the basic tool for sampling plants. Different designs suit different measurements, and a few rules keep the counting fair.
[bio32-s2.3.0] A quadrat is a square frame of known area used to sample plants and slow-moving or attached animals (ones that can't escape or move while you count). School grassland quadrats are usually 0.5 m × 0.5 m (0.25 m²) or 1 m × 1 m (1 m²).
[bio32-s2.3.1] A gridded quadrat is divided by strings or wires into 100 equal small squares (10 × 10), each 1% of the area, which makes percentages easy to read off.
[bio32-s2.3.2] A point quadrat (point frame) is a frame on legs holding a row of usually 10 long pins (needles) that can be lowered vertically through the vegetation. Each pin that touches a species counts as one 'hit' for that species.
[bio32-s2.3.3] Rule 1: lay the quadrat flat on the ground at the chosen point without moving it towards or away from any plant, so you don't choose which plants fall inside.
[bio32-s2.3.4] Rule 2: use the same size of quadrat throughout, so counts from different quadrats can be compared.
[bio32-s2.3.5] Rule 3: decide in advance how to count plants on the edge (for example count a plant only if more than half of it is inside, or count only those on the top and left edges), and apply the rule every time so no plant is counted twice or missed.
[bio32-s2.3.6] Diagram: a gridded quadrat (100 squares) placed at a random coordinate on a grid laid out with two tape measures at right angles; and a point quadrat with 10 pins, with one pin touching a leaf (a hit).
[bio32-s2.4.b] Percentage frequency tells you how widespread a species is across the samples. It asks only 'is it there or not?' in each quadrat.
[bio32-s2.4.0] Percentage frequency is the percentage of all the quadrats sampled in which a species is present: percentage frequency = (number of quadrats containing the species ÷ total number of quadrats) × 100.
[bio32-s2.4.1] Only presence or absence is recorded in each quadrat, so it is quick and objective, and works for any plant (or attached animal).
[bio32-s2.4.2] It doesn't show how many there are: one daisy or a hundred in a quadrat both count as 'present'.
[bio32-s2.4.3] It depends on quadrat size, because a larger quadrat is more likely to contain the species.
[bio32-s2.4.4] Worked example (sample data): 20 quadrats were placed at random. Daisy was present in 12, dandelion in 5 and white clover in 16.
[bio32-s2.4.5] Daisy: 12 ÷ 20 × 100 = 60%. Dandelion: 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%. White clover: 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80%.
[bio32-s2.4.6] Results are shown in a table and as a bar chart with one bar per species, with percentage frequency on the y-axis.
[bio32-s2.5.b] Percentage cover is the percentage of the ground area of a quadrat covered by a species, viewed from directly above. It measures how much space a species takes up, not how many individuals there are.
[bio32-s2.5.0] Percentage cover is used for plants that are hard to count as individuals, such as grasses, clover and mosses, which spread to form mats.
[bio32-s2.5.1] Gridded-quadrat method: count the small squares in which the species covers more than half the square; each square is 1%. Repeat in each quadrat and find the mean percentage cover.
[bio32-s2.5.2] Worked example (sample data): in five quadrats white clover covered 30, 45, 25, 40 and 35 squares; total = 175; mean percentage cover = 175 ÷ 5 = 35%.
[bio32-s2.5.3] Point-quadrat method: lower each pin and record every species it touches. Percentage cover = (number of hits on the species ÷ total number of pins lowered) × 100.
[bio32-s2.5.4] Worked example (sample data): the 10-pin frame was placed at 10 random points, so 100 pins were lowered; grass was touched by 72 pins and white clover by 23.
[bio32-s2.5.5] Grass cover = 72 ÷ 100 × 100 = 72%; clover cover = 23%.
[bio32-s2.5.6] The total cover of all species in a quadrat can be more than 100%, because plants overlap in layers (for example clover leaves above grass).
[bio32-s2.5.7] Percentage cover estimated by eye is subjective. The grid and point methods are more objective, and the point method is the most objective because a pin either touches or doesn't.
[bio32-s2.6.b] Density is the number of individuals per unit area, usually per m². Once you know the density, you can scale up to estimate how many plants live in the whole habitat.
[bio32-s2.6.0] Count the individuals of a species that can be counted separately (for example daisy or dandelion rosettes) in each quadrat. Density = total number counted ÷ total area sampled.
[bio32-s2.6.1] Estimated population = density × total area of the habitat.
[bio32-s2.6.2] The area of the habitat is found by measuring its length and width with tape measures or a trundle wheel (a measuring wheel you roll along the ground).
[bio32-s2.6.3] Worked example (sample data): five quadrats of 0.25 m² contained 4, 7, 3, 6 and 5 daisies. Total = 25 daisies in 5 × 0.25 = 1.25 m².
[bio32-s2.6.4] Density = 25 ÷ 1.25 = 20 daisies per m² (or: mean per quadrat 5 ÷ 0.25 m² = 20 per m²).
[bio32-s2.6.5] The field is 50 m × 40 m = 2000 m², so the estimated population = 20 × 2000 = 40 000 daisies.
[bio32-s2.6.6] Counting individuals is the most informative measure but is slow, and impossible for grasses and other spreading plants, which need percentage cover instead.
[bio32-s2.7.b] A transect is a line across a habitat along which samples are taken at regular intervals. It is the tool for showing how species change when conditions change gradually.
[bio32-s2.7.0] Transects are used where there is a gradient in conditions, to show how the distribution of species changes along it: for example from under a hedge or tree into open grassland, across a trampled path, from a wet hollow to drier ground, or up a rocky shore.
[bio32-s2.7.1] Line transect, step 1: stretch a rope or tape measure tightly across the area at ground level.
[bio32-s2.7.2] Line transect, step 2: at regular intervals (for example every 1 m), record every species touching the line (or directly below it).
[bio32-s2.7.3] Line transect, step 3: record the distance for each species.
[bio32-s2.7.4] A line transect shows where each species occurs (distribution) but not how many, because it only records presence at each point.
[bio32-s2.7.5] A transect is a systematic, not random, sample: it is chosen to cross the gradient.
[bio32-s2.7.6] Several parallel transects give more reliable results, because one line might happen to cross an unusual patch.
[bio32-s2.8.b] A line transect only tells you where species are. A belt transect adds quadrats along the line, so it also tells you how abundant each species is.
[bio32-s2.8.0] Belt transect: quadrats are placed along the line, either side by side all the way (continuous belt transect) or at regular intervals (interrupted belt transect, for example a quadrat every 2 m).
[bio32-s2.8.1] In each quadrat of a belt transect, percentage cover, frequency or numbers are recorded, so a belt transect shows abundance as well as distribution.
[bio32-s2.8.2] Abiotic factors (non-living conditions such as light or soil moisture) are measured at each quadrat to relate them to the species. There is more on this in Abiotic factors.
[bio32-s2.8.3] Diagram: a belt transect from a hedge into open grassland: tape measure from the hedge, quadrats at 2 m intervals, with distances labelled.
[bio32-s2.9.b] Mobile animals move out of a quadrat before you can count them. Instead you catch, mark and release some, then use the proportion of marked animals in a second catch to work out the total population.
[bio32-s2.9.0] Mobile animals can't be counted in quadrats, so their population is estimated by capture–mark–recapture.
[bio32-s2.9.1] 1. Catch a sample with a suitable method (for example pitfall traps, sunken containers, for ground beetles or woodlice).
[bio32-s2.9.2] 2. Count them (M) and mark each harmlessly, for example with a small dot of non-toxic, quick-drying, waterproof paint on the underside of a woodlouse or the wing case of a beetle.
[bio32-s2.9.3] 3. Release them where they were caught and leave enough time (for example 24 hours) for them to mix back into the population.
[bio32-s2.9.4] 4. Catch a second sample in the same way and place; count the total caught (C) and the number that are marked (R).
[bio32-s2.9.5] Estimated population size: N = (M × C) ÷ R. This works because the fraction of the second sample that is marked (R ÷ C) is assumed to equal the fraction of the whole population that is marked (M ÷ N).
[bio32-s2.9.6] Worked example (sample data): 30 woodlice were caught, marked and released. Next day 40 were caught, 8 of them marked. N = (30 × 40) ÷ 8 = 1200 ÷ 8 = 150 woodlice.
[bio32-s2.9.7] Second example (sample data): M = 48, C = 50, R = 12; N = 2400 ÷ 12 = 200.
[bio32-s2.10.b] The capture–recapture formula only gives a good estimate if certain conditions hold, and it is easy to mix up the letters. When an exact population isn't needed, a simpler count can be enough.
[bio32-s2.10.0] N must be larger than both M and C, because the population includes every marked animal and every animal caught in the second sample. If it isn't, you have made an error.
[bio32-s2.10.1] R is the number of marked animals in the second sample, not the number marked in the first sample.
[bio32-s2.10.2] Assumption: there are no births, deaths, immigration (animals arriving) or emigration (animals leaving) between the two samples, so the population stays the same size.
[bio32-s2.10.3] Assumption: marked animals mix fully and randomly with the rest, and marked and unmarked animals are equally likely to be caught.
[bio32-s2.10.4] Assumption: the mark doesn't harm the animal, make it easier for predators to see, or wash or rub off.
[bio32-s2.10.5] If marks are lost, R is too small and N is overestimated, because fewer marked animals are recognised in the second sample. The assumptions are rarely fully met in practice.
[bio32-s2.10.6] For comparisons only, a relative count is often enough: for example the number of grasshoppers caught per ten sweeps of a sweep net, or the number of beetles per pitfall trap per day, compared between two areas using the same method.
[bio32-s2.11.b] In ecology a model is a simplified representation of a habitat that describes it and can be used to explain or predict. Here the model is a labelled map with data, built from the primary data (results you collected yourself).
[bio32-s2.11.0] Size: measure the length and width (or the sides of an irregular area) with long tape measures or a trundle wheel, and calculate the area, for example 50 m × 40 m = 2000 m².
[bio32-s2.11.1] Map: draw a sketch map to scale, with a north arrow, showing the boundary, hedges, trees, paths, gates, wet and dry areas, any slope (and which way it faces), the positions of the quadrats, transects and traps, and where the main species were found.
[bio32-s2.11.2] Species: the species list from the qualitative survey, with the quantitative results (percentage frequency, cover, density, population estimates) for the main species.
[bio32-s2.11.3] Abiotic factors: the mean values measured across the habitat (for example light intensity, soil pH, soil water content, air and soil temperature, aspect and slope), with differences between areas marked on the map.
[bio32-s2.11.4] Biotic factors (the effects of living things): the feeding and symbiotic relationships observed, shown as food chains or a food web drawn from the species found.
[bio32-s2.11.5] Biotic factors also include grazing, trampling and human management such as mowing or fertilising.
[bio32-s2.11.6] Using the model: it can explain why species are where they are (for example rushes only in the wet hollow) and predict the effect of a change (for example if mowing stopped, taller grasses would shade out daisies).
[bio32-s2.11.7] Limits of the model: it is a snapshot of one season and of the parts sampled, so it may not hold at other times of year or in unsampled areas.
[bio32-s2.12.b] Raw counts are hard to interpret. Tables and graphs show patterns in species abundance and distribution at a glance.
[bio32-s2.12.0] Record results in tables with headings and units: quadrat number or distance along the transect, species, and the measure used.
[bio32-s2.12.1] Bar charts compare species, for example the percentage frequency of each.
[bio32-s2.12.2] For a transect, plot each species' abundance against distance, or draw a kite diagram.
[bio32-s2.12.3] In a kite diagram, for each species a band is centred on a horizontal line; its width at each point along the transect is proportional to the species' abundance there, so the bands show at a glance where each species is common.
[bio32-s2.12.4] Diagram: a kite diagram for two species along a 10 m transect from a hedge, with distance on the x-axis.
[bio32-s2.13.b] A survey can be set up as a fair test, with an aim, a hypothesis (a testable prediction), and variables. Field studies differ from lab experiments because conditions can't be fully controlled.
[bio32-s2.13.0] Aim: to estimate the abundance and distribution of named species (for example daisy, white clover, grass and woodlice) in a grassland.
[bio32-s2.13.1] Hypothesis (example): 'Daisies are more abundant in the short-mown part of the field than in the long-grass part.'
[bio32-s2.13.2] Independent variable (the one you change or compare) when two areas are compared: the area (for example mown vs unmown).
[bio32-s2.13.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): the abundance of the species (density, percentage cover or frequency).
[bio32-s2.13.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the quadrat size and type, the number of quadrats, the method of placing them (random coordinates), the counting rule, the observer, the date and the weather.
[bio32-s2.13.5] There is no true control in a field study, because the two areas can't be made identical in every other way.
[bio32-s2.13.6] Conclusion: state the measured abundance of each species and compare the areas using the means.
[bio32-s2.13.7] Say whether the results support the hypothesis. A difference shows correlation with the area, not proof of the cause.
[bio32-s2.14.b] No survey is perfect. Knowing where errors come from lets you say how reliable your results are and how to improve them.
[bio32-s2.14.0] Too few samples, or samples not placed at random (choosing 'typical' spots), give an unrepresentative estimate; use random coordinates and enough quadrats.
[bio32-s2.14.1] Misidentification of species, especially grasses and small invertebrates, is a source of error.
[bio32-s2.14.2] Estimates of percentage cover by eye are subjective, and inconsistent edge rules change counts.
[bio32-s2.14.3] Results vary with the season and the time of day (plants flower and animals are active at different times), so a single survey is a snapshot.
[bio32-s2.14.4] The assumptions of capture–recapture are rarely fully met.
[bio32-s2.14.5] Trampling during the survey damages the plants being sampled; avoid trampling the quadrat sites before they are sampled.
[bio32-s2.14.6] Reliability improves by repeating the survey (more quadrats, more transects, several dates) and calculating means, because repeats reduce the effect of chance.

CANDIDATES bio32-s3: Abiotic factors
[bio32-s3.0.b] Abiotic factors are the non-living features of an environment, such as light, temperature and soil. To explain why a species lives where it does, you must measure them reliably, and a few rules apply whichever factor you measure. There is more on abiotic factors as limiting factors in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio32-s3.0.0] The specification groups abiotic factors as air (climatic) factors, ground (topographic) factors, aquatic (water) factors and edaphic (soil) factors. “Topographic” means the shape of the land, such as slope and aspect, and “edaphic” means to do with the soil.
[bio32-s3.0.1] The specification names water, oxygen, space, temperature, soil, aspect, wind speed and slope, so you need an instrument and a method for each of these.
[bio32-s3.0.2] Measure abiotic factors at the same places as the quadrats (square frames that mark out a sample area) or transect points (sampling points along a line), and at the same time as the survey, so that each measurement can be linked to the species found there.
[bio32-s3.0.3] Many factors (light, temperature, wind, humidity) change during the day and with the weather. Take all readings within a short period under similar conditions, and record the time and weather, so that readings from different places can be compared fairly.
[bio32-s3.0.4] Repeat each reading (e.g. three times) and use the mean, which reduces the effect of one odd reading.
[bio32-s3.0.5] Electronic probes connected to a data logger (a device that stores readings automatically) can record factors such as temperature, light intensity, humidity, pH and dissolved oxygen continuously over hours or days, and at several points at once. This removes reading errors and shows changes over time.
[bio32-s3.0.6] Calibrate meters before use, which means checking them against a known standard, e.g. a pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH, so that its readings are accurate.
[bio32-s3.1.b] Air factors describe the climate around the organisms. Light and temperature both affect plant growth and animal activity, and each has its own instrument.
[bio32-s3.1.0] Light intensity is the energy for photosynthesis (how plants make food), so it affects plant growth and which plants can grow in shade; it also affects animal activity.
[bio32-s3.1.1] Light intensity is measured with a light meter (lux meter), in lux.
[bio32-s3.1.2] Hold the light sensor horizontally, facing up, at the same height each time (e.g. at ground level or at the top of the vegetation), keep your shadow off it, and take readings quickly, as light changes with cloud cover.
[bio32-s3.1.3] Air temperature controls the rate of enzyme reactions (the chemical reactions in cells), so it affects the activity and growth of plants and of cold-blooded animals, whose body temperature follows their surroundings.
[bio32-s3.1.4] Air temperature is measured with a thermometer or temperature probe, in °C, held in the shade at a set height above the ground (e.g. 1 m) so that direct sunlight doesn’t heat it and give a false high reading.
[bio32-s3.1.5] A maximum–minimum thermometer left on site records the highest and lowest temperatures over a period, e.g. 24 hours, so you do not have to be there at the hottest and coldest moments.
[bio32-s3.2.b] Moving air, water vapour and rain all change how quickly organisms lose or gain water. Each is measured with a different instrument.
[bio32-s3.2.0] Wind speed increases water loss by evaporation and transpiration (loss of water vapour from leaves), cools organisms, can damage tall plants, and makes flying difficult for insects.
[bio32-s3.2.1] Wind speed is measured with an anemometer (a cup anemometer or a hand-held digital one), in metres per second (m/s) or km/h, held at a set height facing into the wind.
[bio32-s3.2.2] Take the mean of several readings, as wind gusts. Wind direction is found with a wind vane or a compass.
[bio32-s3.2.3] Humidity (water in the air) affects water loss from plants and from animals with moist skins; slugs and woodlice need humid places because they dry out quickly.
[bio32-s3.2.4] Humidity is measured with a hygrometer or humidity probe, as relative humidity (%).
[bio32-s3.2.5] Rainfall (water supply) is measured with a rain gauge: a funnel draining into a measuring cylinder, set in an open place so that nothing shelters it, in mm, read at the same time each day.
[bio32-s3.3.b] The shape of the land decides how much water drains away and how much sunlight a place gets. Space decides how many organisms can fit in a habitat. Slope, aspect and space are all measured directly in the field.
[bio32-s3.3.0] Slope affects drainage: water runs off steep slopes, so they are drier and soils are thinner, while hollows at the bottom collect water. Slope also affects the amount of sunlight received.
[bio32-s3.3.1] Slope is measured with a clinometer (an instrument for measuring angles), in degrees. Two people of the same eye height (or two poles marked at the same height) stand up and down the slope a set distance apart.
[bio32-s3.3.2] One person sights the other’s eye level (or the mark) through the clinometer and reads the angle.
[bio32-s3.3.3] Aspect is the compass direction a slope faces, measured with a compass, because direction decides how much sun the slope gets.
[bio32-s3.3.4] In Ireland (northern hemisphere) a south-facing slope receives more direct sunlight than a north-facing one, so it is warmer and drier and plants there start growing earlier in spring.
[bio32-s3.3.5] Space is the room available for organisms to live, grow, find shelter, nest or attach.
[bio32-s3.3.6] Measure the area of the habitat (length × width with tapes or a trundle wheel), and the percentage of bare ground in each quadrat (squares of a gridded quadrat with no plants), which shows the space available for new seedlings.
[bio32-s3.3.7] Density (individuals per m², from surveys) shows how crowded a species is. Shortage of space is a cause of competition. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio32-s3.4.b] Soil factors are called edaphic. Soil temperature and pH both help decide which plants and soil organisms can live in a place, and each is measured on a small soil sample or with a probe.
[bio32-s3.4.0] Soil temperature affects root growth, germination and the activity of soil organisms and decomposers (organisms that break down dead material).
[bio32-s3.4.1] Push a soil thermometer or temperature probe into the soil to a set depth (e.g. 10 cm), leave it for a minute or two to settle, and read it in °C.
[bio32-s3.4.2] Soil pH affects which plants grow (by changing which minerals are available) and which soil organisms live there; earthworms are scarce in acid soils.
[bio32-s3.4.3] Soil pH method, step 1: take a small soil sample from a set depth.
[bio32-s3.4.4] Soil pH method, step 2: put about 1 cm depth of soil in a test tube, add about the same amount of barium sulfate (which makes the clay particles settle, so that the liquid clears), then distilled water and a few drops of universal indicator.
[bio32-s3.4.5] Soil pH method, step 3: stopper and shake, then leave to settle.
[bio32-s3.4.6] Soil pH method, step 4: compare the colour of the liquid with the universal indicator colour chart to read the pH.
[bio32-s3.4.7] Alternatively, mix the soil with distilled water and use a calibrated pH meter, rinsing the probe in distilled water between samples. Use distilled water, as tap water has its own pH and would change the result.
[bio32-s3.5.b] Soil water decides which plants and soil animals can live in a place. It is measured by drying a soil sample and finding how much mass it loses.
[bio32-s3.5.0] Soil water content affects which plants grow (rushes grow in wet soil) and soil animals (earthworms need moist soil); waterlogged soil lacks air.
[bio32-s3.5.1] Soil water content method, step 1: weigh an empty evaporating dish.
[bio32-s3.5.2] Soil water content method, step 2: add a soil sample and weigh again, to find the fresh mass of soil by subtraction.
[bio32-s3.5.3] Soil water content method, step 3: heat in an oven at about 100–105 °C (hot enough to evaporate water, not to burn humus) for 24 hours.
[bio32-s3.5.4] Soil water content method, step 4: cool in a desiccator (so the soil doesn’t reabsorb water from the air) and reweigh.
[bio32-s3.5.5] Soil water content method, step 5: repeat the heating, cooling and weighing until the mass stays the same (constant mass), showing that all the water has gone.
[bio32-s3.6.b] The water lost on drying is the water the soil held, so the calculation compares the mass before and after heating.
[bio32-s3.6.0] Percentage water = (fresh mass − dry mass) ÷ fresh mass × 100. It is worked out from the fresh mass, which is the mass before drying.
[bio32-s3.6.1] Worked example: fresh soil 50.0 g; dry soil 38.0 g at constant mass. Water = 12.0 g. Percentage water = 12.0 ÷ 50.0 × 100 = 24%.
[bio32-s3.6.2] A soil-moisture probe (meter) gives a quick reading in the field, but less accurately than drying the soil.
[bio32-s3.7.b] Soil is a mixture of solid particles and air spaces. Measuring the air content tells you how well roots and soil animals can breathe.
[bio32-s3.7.0] Soil air content matters because roots and soil animals need oxygen for respiration. Air spaces are larger in sandy soils and smaller in clay and waterlogged soils.
[bio32-s3.7.1] Soil air content method, step 1: find the volume of an empty can by filling it with water from a graduated cylinder.
[bio32-s3.7.2] Soil air content method, step 2: push the can, open end down, into the soil until it is full, dig it out and level the soil with the rim.
[bio32-s3.7.3] Soil air content method, step 3: put a volume of water equal to the can’s volume in a large graduated cylinder.
[bio32-s3.7.4] Soil air content method, step 4: add the soil, and stir with a rod to release the trapped air.
[bio32-s3.7.5] Soil air content method, step 5: read the new level.
[bio32-s3.7.6] Volume of air = (volume of water + volume of soil) − final reading. Percentage air = volume of air ÷ volume of soil × 100.
[bio32-s3.7.7] Worked example: can volume 100 cm³; 100 cm³ water; reading after adding soil and stirring 185 cm³. Air = 200 − 185 = 15 cm³; percentage air = 15 ÷ 100 × 100 = 15%.
[bio32-s3.8.b] Humus is the organic part of soil. Because it burns away when heated strongly and the mineral part does not, the loss in mass measures how much humus there was.
[bio32-s3.8.0] Humus is the dark, partly decomposed remains of dead organisms in soil. It holds water and minerals and feeds decomposers.
[bio32-s3.8.1] Humus method, step 1: weigh a crucible (a small heat-proof pot).
[bio32-s3.8.2] Humus method, step 2: add dry soil from the water-content method and weigh it.
[bio32-s3.8.3] Humus method, step 3: heat it strongly over a Bunsen burner (lid partly on) until the humus burns away (about 15 minutes).
[bio32-s3.8.4] Humus method, step 4: cool in a desiccator and reweigh.
[bio32-s3.8.5] Humus method, step 5: repeat until constant mass, so that you know all the humus has burned away.
[bio32-s3.8.6] Percentage humus = loss in mass ÷ mass of dry soil × 100, so it uses the dry mass because the water has already been removed.
[bio32-s3.8.7] Worked example: dry soil 38.0 g; after burning, 34.2 g. Loss = 3.8 g; percentage humus = 3.8 ÷ 38.0 × 100 = 10% of the dry soil.
[bio32-s3.9.b] Soil texture describes the sizes of the particles in the soil. A simple settling test separates them into layers.
[bio32-s3.9.0] Soil texture method: shake soil with water in a graduated cylinder and leave it to settle.
[bio32-s3.9.1] The particles settle in layers, largest at the bottom: stones and gravel, then sand, silt and clay, with humus floating on top.
[bio32-s3.9.2] The proportions of the layers show the soil type, such as sandy or clay soil.
[bio32-s3.10.b] In water, temperature, dissolved oxygen and pH are measured. Temperature matters for oxygen because cold water holds more of it.
[bio32-s3.10.0] Water temperature is measured with a thermometer or probe in °C, at a set depth. It affects how much oxygen water holds (cold water holds more) and the activity of aquatic organisms.
[bio32-s3.10.1] Dissolved oxygen is measured with an oxygen meter (probe), in mg per litre, or with a chemical test kit.
[bio32-s3.10.2] Animals such as stonefly larvae need high oxygen. Low oxygen is a sign of organic pollution, because decomposers use up the oxygen as they break down the waste.
[bio32-s3.10.3] Water pH is measured with a pH meter or universal indicator paper, as for soil.
[bio32-s3.11.b] Moving water, cloudy water and salty water each create different conditions for organisms. Rate of flow is measured with a simple timed float.
[bio32-s3.11.0] Rate of flow (current speed): mark two points a set distance apart along a straight stretch of stream (e.g. 10 m); time a float (e.g. an orange or a dog biscuit) from one point to the other.
[bio32-s3.11.1] Repeat the timing three times and take the mean time; speed = distance ÷ time.
[bio32-s3.11.2] Worked example: mean time 25 s over 10 m: 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 m/s.
[bio32-s3.11.3] Fast flow carries away animals that can’t cling on and keeps the water well oxygenated.
[bio32-s3.11.4] Turbidity (cloudiness) is measured with a Secchi disc, a black-and-white disc on a marked rope, which is lowered until it is no longer visible; the depth is recorded.
[bio32-s3.11.5] Cloudy water lets less light reach water plants, so they photosynthesise less.
[bio32-s3.11.6] Salinity (saltiness, in seawater, rock pools and estuaries) is measured with a conductivity or salinity meter. Rock pools become saltier as water evaporates in sunshine.
[bio32-s3.12.b] You must be able to investigate quantitatively (using numbers) how an abiotic factor affects the distribution (where) and abundance (how many) of a species. The standard example is light and daisies on a belt transect (a strip across a habitat, sampled with quadrats at set intervals).
[bio32-s3.12.0] Aim: to investigate quantitatively how light intensity affects the distribution and abundance of the daisy in a grassland, along a belt transect from under a hedge or tree into the open field.
[bio32-s3.12.1] Hypothesis (a testable prediction): “The number of daisies per quadrat increases as light intensity increases.”
[bio32-s3.12.2] Reason for the hypothesis: the daisy is a low-growing rosette plant that needs plenty of light for photosynthesis, and is shaded out where light is low.
[bio32-s3.12.3] Independent variable: light intensity. It varies naturally along the transect, so it is measured, not set by the student.
[bio32-s3.12.4] Dependent variable: the abundance of daisies (number per quadrat, or percentage cover).
[bio32-s3.12.5] Controlled variables: quadrat size, the interval between quadrats, the counting rule, the height and direction of the light sensor, the time of day (all readings within a short period) and the weather.
[bio32-s3.13.b] A field study cannot hold everything else constant. This limits what the results can prove, so it is worth knowing before you plan the method.
[bio32-s3.13.0] No true control is possible: other factors also change along the transect, such as soil water content, leaf litter, trampling, and competition from taller plants near the hedge. Measure these too where possible, so that their effect can be considered.
[bio32-s3.13.1] This is why a field investigation shows a correlation (two things changing together), not proof of cause.
[bio32-s3.13.2] Equipment: two long tape measures (one for the transect), a gridded quadrat, a light meter, pegs, a recording sheet, and a soil-moisture probe or soil-sampling equipment for the second factor.
[bio32-s3.13.3] Other suitable pairs of factor and species: soil water content and rushes (more in wet ground); trampling or soil compaction across a path and plantain or grass cover; soil pH and a named plant; height above low tide on a rocky shore and the cover of each seaweed.
[bio32-s3.14.b] The method lays quadrats at regular intervals along the transect, then counts daisies and measures light at each one.
[bio32-s3.14.0] Step 1: stretch a tape measure from the base of the hedge (0 m) straight out into the open field, e.g. for 10 m.
[bio32-s3.14.1] Step 2: place a gridded quadrat at 0 m and then at every 2 m along the tape (an interrupted belt transect, because it samples at intervals rather than along the whole line).
[bio32-s3.14.2] Step 3: in each quadrat, count the daisy rosettes (or the squares in which daisy covers more than half, for percentage cover).
[bio32-s3.14.3] Step 4: at each quadrat, hold the light meter at ground level, facing up, and take three readings in quick succession; record the mean in lux.
[bio32-s3.14.4] Do all the light readings within a few minutes, ideally under even cloud, because light changes quickly when clouds move.
[bio32-s3.15.b] Repeating the transect and recording results in a table make the data reliable and ready to graph. Fieldwork also carries small risks to manage.
[bio32-s3.15.0] Step 5: measure the second factor (e.g. soil water content) at each quadrat, so that its effect can be compared with that of light.
[bio32-s3.15.1] Step 6: repeat the whole transect at least twice more, parallel to the first and a few metres away, and calculate the mean count at each distance.
[bio32-s3.15.2] Step 7: record the results in a table, and plot a scatter graph of the number of daisies (y-axis) against light intensity (x-axis), or plot both against distance from the hedge.
[bio32-s3.15.3] Safety: take care at hedges with thorns, nettles and brambles; wear gloves when handling soil; wash hands afterwards; avoid trampling the quadrat sites before they are sampled.
[bio32-s3.15.4] Diagram: a belt transect running from a hedge into open grassland with quadrats every 2 m and a light meter at one quadrat. Label the hedge, 0 m, the tape, the quadrats, the light meter and the distances.
[bio32-s3.16.b] Plotting the data shows whether daisy numbers rise with light. The sample data below are illustrative, an example of what results might look like, not real measurements.
[bio32-s3.16.0] Sample data: distance 0 m: light 800 lux, 0 daisies; 2 m: 2500 lux, 1 daisy; 4 m: 6000 lux, 4 daisies; 6 m: 9500 lux, 9 daisies; 8 m: 12 000 lux, 12 daisies; 10 m: 12 500 lux, 11 daisies.
[bio32-s3.16.1] Expected result: daisies are absent or few in deep shade close to the hedge, and become more abundant as light intensity increases into the open field.
[bio32-s3.16.2] Daisies level off once light no longer limits them; other factors, such as space or competition with grass, then limit them.
[bio32-s3.16.3] The scatter graph shows a positive correlation: as light intensity rises, so does the number of daisies.
[bio32-s3.17.b] A conclusion must say whether the data support the hypothesis and how far they can be trusted. Naming the sources of error shows you understand the limits of fieldwork.
[bio32-s3.17.0] Conclusion: the results support the hypothesis that daisy abundance increases with light intensity along this transect.
[bio32-s3.17.1] Because soil water and leaf litter also changed, the result shows a correlation, not proof that light alone is the cause.
[bio32-s3.17.2] A laboratory experiment growing daisies at different light intensities, with other factors controlled, could test the cause.
[bio32-s3.17.3] Sources of error: light intensity changes rapidly with cloud cover, so readings at different quadrats may not be comparable (take readings quickly, or use data loggers at several points at once).
[bio32-s3.17.4] Other sources of error: a single transect may not be representative (use several); counting errors and inconsistent edge rules; misidentification of small daisy rosettes; other factors varying along the transect; the survey is a snapshot of one day and season.
[bio32-s3.17.5] Primary data are the student’s own counts and measurements. They can be compared with secondary data (e.g. published descriptions of where daisies grow) when drawing conclusions.
[bio32-s3.18.b] Each habitat has its own key abiotic factors. A rocky shore, a woodland and a freshwater stream show how different factors dominate.
[bio32-s3.18.0] Rocky shore: the main factor is the time spent out of water at low tide (exposure), which rises with height up the shore and brings drying, temperature change and, in rock pools, salinity change.
[bio32-s3.18.1] This produces zonation, bands of species at different heights. Brown seaweeds (wracks) on Irish shores are zoned from the top down: channelled wrack (highest, survives longest out of water), spiral wrack, bladder wrack and knotted wrack (middle shore), serrated wrack (lower shore), and kelps (at and below the low-tide mark).
[bio32-s3.18.2] Zonation on a rocky shore is measured with a belt transect from low to high water, with height measured using a ranging pole and level.
[bio32-s3.18.3] Woodland: light intensity under the canopy (lowest in summer when trees are in leaf), humidity, leaf-litter depth, soil humus and pH are the key factors.
[bio32-s3.18.4] Many woodland floor plants (e.g. bluebells) grow and flower in spring before the canopy closes, while enough light still reaches the ground.
[bio32-s3.18.5] Freshwater: dissolved oxygen, temperature, rate of flow and pH are the key factors.
[bio32-s3.18.6] Fast, cold streams are rich in oxygen and support stonefly and mayfly larvae; still or polluted water low in oxygen supports fewer species.

CANDIDATES bio32-s4: Biotic factors and relationships
[bio32-s4.0.b] Biotic factors are the effects that living organisms have on each other. In a grassland they include food supply, predation, grazing, competition, parasites and disease, pollination and decomposition, and human activities such as grazing livestock, mowing and fertilising. Grazing and mowing show how one biotic factor shapes which plants can live in a habitat.
[bio32-s4.0.0] Grazing by rabbits, cattle or sheep and mowing keep grass short. This lets low rosette plants (plants whose leaves lie flat close to the ground) such as daisy, dandelion and plantain get light, and stops shrubs and trees from invading.
[bio32-s4.0.1] If grazing or mowing stops, taller grasses shade out the rosette plants because they grow above them and take the light. In time scrub (a tangle of woody shrubs, e.g. brambles, hawthorn) invades.
[bio32-s4.1.b] Other living things work in the grassland soil and on its flowers. Some recycle dead material back into the ground, and others carry pollen between flowers.
[bio32-s4.1.0] Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) and detritivores (animals that eat dead organic matter, e.g. earthworms, woodlice, and slugs in part) break down dead plants, dung and dead animals, returning minerals to the soil so that plants can take them up again.
[bio32-s4.1.1] Decomposition recycles carbon and nitrogen, and there is more on this in The carbon cycle, The nitrogen cycle and Bacteria and fungi.
[bio32-s4.1.2] Earthworms also drag leaves underground, mix and aerate the soil (let air into it), and improve drainage.
[bio32-s4.1.3] Pollinators such as bumblebees, hoverflies and butterflies feed on the nectar and pollen of clover, dandelion and buttercup flowers, and carry pollen between them, which is how these plants are pollinated.
[bio32-s4.2.b] To work out who eats whom in a grassland, you gather primary data, meaning information you collect yourself in the field. You then check it against what other people have already recorded.
[bio32-s4.2.0] Direct observation of animals feeding shows a feeding relationship at the moment it happens.
[bio32-s4.2.1] Feeding signs are evidence left behind: nibbled grass, holes in leaves, and broken snail shells at a thrush’s stone.
[bio32-s4.2.2] Droppings and pellets show what an animal ate, e.g. undigested fur and bones in the pellets of birds of prey.
[bio32-s4.2.3] The habitat where each animal was caught is a clue, e.g. aphids found on the stems they feed on.
[bio32-s4.2.4] Your findings are then checked against secondary sources (information collected by others), such as field guides, for what each species eats.
[bio32-s4.3.b] Every feeding relationship starts with a green plant that makes food. Animals then feed on the plants, and on each other, in layers called trophic levels (feeding levels).
[bio32-s4.3.0] Producers in the grassland are grasses, clover, daisy, dandelion, buttercup and other green plants, which make food by photosynthesis.
[bio32-s4.3.1] Primary consumers (herbivores, which eat plants) in the grassland: rabbits and hares graze grass and clover; slugs and snails eat leaves; grasshoppers and caterpillars chew leaves; aphids suck sap from stems; bumblebees take nectar and pollen.
[bio32-s4.3.2] Secondary and higher consumers (carnivores, which eat animals, and omnivores, which eat both): ladybirds eat aphids; spiders catch flies, grasshoppers and other insects; ground beetles eat slugs and other invertebrates.
[bio32-s4.3.3] Song thrushes eat snails (smashing their shells on a stone) and earthworms; blackbirds eat earthworms and insects; hedgehogs eat slugs, beetles and earthworms.
[bio32-s4.3.4] Badgers eat mainly earthworms; foxes eat rabbits, mice and birds; kestrels eat wood mice, pygmy shrews and large insects.
[bio32-s4.3.5] Pygmy shrews eat small invertebrates such as beetles, spiders and woodlice.
[bio32-s4.4.b] A food chain follows one line of feeding, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder. Because most animals eat more than one thing, the chains join up into a food web.
[bio32-s4.4.0] Worked example of grassland food chains: grass → rabbit → fox; clover leaves → slug → song thrush; plant sap → aphid → ladybird → (insect-eating bird); grass seeds and invertebrates → wood mouse → kestrel.
[bio32-s4.4.1] These chains connect into a food web. For example, the fox eats rabbits, wood mice and birds, so it is on several chains.
[bio32-s4.4.2] The earthworm feeds on dead leaves and is eaten by thrushes, blackbirds, badgers and hedgehogs, so it links the dead material in the soil to several animals above it.
[bio32-s4.4.3] Diagram: a grassland food web of 8–12 of the species listed above, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder.
[bio32-s4.5.b] A food web shows that organisms depend on each other, so a change in one population spreads through the whole community.
[bio32-s4.5.0] The web shows interdependence: a change in one population affects others.
[bio32-s4.5.1] For example, if disease kills many rabbits, foxes eat more wood mice and birds, so those populations fall, and the grass grows taller because fewer rabbits graze it, shading out daisies.
[bio32-s4.5.2] An omnivore or a predator with several prey is less affected than one with a single food source, because it can switch to another food when one becomes scarce. There is more on predicting changes from webs in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio32-s4.6.b] Predation is the best-known feeding relationship between animals. Predators have features that help them find, catch and kill their prey.
[bio32-s4.6.0] Predation is a feeding relationship in which one animal, the predator, kills and eats another, the prey.
[bio32-s4.6.1] Grassland examples of predation: fox (predator) and rabbit (prey); kestrel and wood mouse; ladybird and aphid; song thrush and snail.
[bio32-s4.6.2] The fox has acute hearing and smell to find prey, forward-facing eyes for judging distance, speed and sharp teeth, and hunts mostly at night, when rabbits feed.
[bio32-s4.6.3] The kestrel hovers into the wind while scanning the ground with very keen eyesight, then drops onto its prey, gripping it with sharp talons and tearing it with a hooked beak.
[bio32-s4.6.4] Spiders build webs to trap flying insects.
[bio32-s4.7.b] Prey animals need to spot danger early, escape, or avoid being noticed or eaten in the first place.
[bio32-s4.7.0] Rabbits have eyes on the sides of the head, giving an almost all-round field of view, and large mobile ears, so they can detect a predator coming from any direction.
[bio32-s4.7.1] Rabbits feed in groups so that some can watch for danger, thump the ground with a hind foot and flash the white underside of the tail as a warning, run in zigzags, and shelter in burrows.
[bio32-s4.7.2] Snails withdraw into their shells for protection.
[bio32-s4.7.3] Ladybirds have bright warning colours and a bad taste, so predators learn to avoid them.
[bio32-s4.7.4] Many caterpillars and grasshoppers are green, camouflaged against the grass, so predators find them hard to see.
[bio32-s4.8.b] Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together in a repeating cycle, because each population depends on the other.
[bio32-s4.8.0] 1. When prey are plentiful, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise.
[bio32-s4.8.1] 2. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.2] 3. With less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.3] 4. With fewer predators, prey numbers recover, and the cycle repeats.
[bio32-s4.8.4] Predator numbers peak after prey numbers, because predators need time to breed once food is plentiful. There are usually fewer predators than prey.
[bio32-s4.8.5] The classic data are the fur-trading records of the snowshoe hare and the lynx in Canada, which rose and fell in cycles of about ten years.
[bio32-s4.8.6] Diagram: a graph of predator and prey numbers against time: two out-of-step waves, the predator curve lower and peaking after the prey curve.
[bio32-s4.9.b] Predators do more than eat prey. They also help to set how many prey a habitat holds.
[bio32-s4.9.0] Predators usually take the old, sick, young or weak prey, which are the easiest to catch.
[bio32-s4.9.1] The effect of predation increases as prey become more numerous, because prey are easier to find. This helps keep the prey population near the habitat’s carrying capacity (the largest population the habitat can support). There is more on this in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio32-s4.9.2] Removing a predator can let its prey increase until it overgrazes its food.
[bio32-s4.10.b] Organisms compete when they need the same resource and there is not enough for all. Competition can be sorted by who is competing and by how the resource is shared out.
[bio32-s4.10.0] Competition is the struggle between organisms for a resource that is in short supply. Plants compete for light, water, minerals and space; animals compete for food, water, space or territory, shelter and mates.
[bio32-s4.10.1] If the resource is plentiful, there is no competition.
[bio32-s4.10.2] Intraspecific competition is between members of the same species. It is the most intense kind, because they have exactly the same needs, e.g. daisy rosettes crowding each other for space and light, or male blackbirds or robins defending territories that contain food and nest sites.
[bio32-s4.10.3] Interspecific competition is between different species that need the same resource, e.g. grasses and daisies competing for light, or rabbits and sheep grazing the same grass.
[bio32-s4.10.4] Contest competition: there is an active physical contest for the resource and the winner takes it, e.g. birds fighting over territory, or stags fighting over mates.
[bio32-s4.10.5] Scramble competition: each competitor gets as much as it can of a shared resource, so when it is scarce all get too little, e.g. seedlings crowded together all grow weak and thin, or aphids crowded on a stem.
[bio32-s4.11.b] Competition has a cost for the organisms that lose, and this affects the whole population. Organisms also have features that help them win.
[bio32-s4.11.0] Competition reduces the growth, survival and breeding of the losers, and so limits population size. Its effect grows as the population becomes more crowded.
[bio32-s4.11.1] Between species, one may exclude the other from the habitat, or each may survive by using the resources in a slightly different way (different niches, meaning different roles or ways of living in the habitat). There is more on this in The niche.
[bio32-s4.11.2] Adaptations that help organisms compete: fast growth, and tall stems or climbing (e.g. ivy, brambles) in plants competing for light; deep roots for water; rapid breeding; aggressive defence of territory in animals.
[bio32-s4.12.b] Two examples show interspecific competition at work: one in the grassland and one from Ireland.
[bio32-s4.12.0] In short, grazed or mown grass, daisies and other rosette plants get enough light. Where grass is left long, it outgrows and shades them, and they disappear (interspecific competition for light).
[bio32-s4.12.1] Irish example: grey squirrels, introduced to Ireland at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford, in 1911, compete with the native red squirrel for food, especially seeds and nuts.
[bio32-s4.12.2] Grey squirrels are larger, can digest acorns (which red squirrels digest poorly), eat hazelnuts before they ripen, and carry squirrelpox virus, which kills red squirrels but rarely harms grey squirrels. Red squirrels have disappeared from much of the area greys occupy.
[bio32-s4.12.3] Where pine marten numbers have recovered in Ireland, grey squirrels have declined and red squirrels have returned.
[bio32-s4.13.b] Some organisms do not just compete or eat each other but live together. This close partnership is called symbiosis, and it is sorted into three types by who benefits.
[bio32-s4.13.0] Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship in which two organisms of different species live together.
[bio32-s4.13.1] There are three types, named by who benefits: mutualism (both benefit, +/+), commensalism (one benefits and the other is unaffected, +/0) and parasitism (one benefits and the other is harmed, +/−).
[bio32-s4.13.2] Pollinators and flowers benefit each other (food for the insect, pollination for the plant), so the relationship is mutualistic, but the two don’t live together, so many biologists don’t call it symbiosis.
[bio32-s4.14.b] In mutualism both partners gain. The first example is a bacterium that gives clover a supply of nitrogen, which is why clover can enrich grassland.
[bio32-s4.14.0] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) live in root nodules (small swellings) of clover and other legumes. The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonium, which the plant uses to make amino acids and proteins.
[bio32-s4.14.1] The plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (from photosynthesis) and a protected home.
[bio32-s4.14.2] Active nodules are pink inside, from a pigment (leghaemoglobin) that controls oxygen levels for the bacteria.
[bio32-s4.14.3] This is why clover grows well in soils poor in nitrogen and enriches grassland. There is more on this in The nitrogen cycle.
[bio32-s4.14.4] To observe: dig up a clover plant, wash the roots and examine the nodules with a hand lens; cut one to see the pink colour.
[bio32-s4.15.b] Mutualism is common wherever one partner can do something the other cannot. Here are three more examples, and in each the partners swap food or shelter for something they lack.
[bio32-s4.15.0] Cellulose-digesting microorganisms (bacteria, protists and fungi) live in the rumen (first stomach) of cattle and sheep, and in the caecum (a pouch of the gut) of rabbits. They digest the cellulose of grass, which the animal has no enzyme to digest.
[bio32-s4.15.1] The microorganisms get a warm, food-rich home, and the animal gets the products (fatty acids) as food. Rabbits also eat some of their own soft droppings to absorb nutrients released by these microorganisms.
[bio32-s4.15.2] Mycorrhizal fungi grow around and into the roots of most grassland plants and trees. The fungal hyphae (thread-like filaments) spread far through the soil, absorbing water and minerals (especially phosphate) and passing them to the plant; the plant supplies the fungus with sugars.
[bio32-s4.15.3] Lichens (e.g. on walls, rocks and tree bark around the grassland) are a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) living together. The alga photosynthesises and supplies sugars; the fungus gives shelter, holds water and absorbs minerals.
[bio32-s4.15.4] Bacteria in the human gut are another example of mutualism. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.b] In commensalism one partner gains and the other is neither helped nor harmed. It is easy to confuse with mutualism, so check whether the second partner really gets nothing.
[bio32-s4.16.0] Commensalism: one partner benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
[bio32-s4.16.1] Mosses and lichens growing on the bark of trees get a place in the light without harming the tree.
[bio32-s4.16.2] Many bacteria living on human skin feed on dead skin cells and sweat without harming the host. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.3] Seeds with hooks (e.g. burdock) are carried in an animal’s fur, and robins follow a gardener or grazing cattle to catch the invertebrates they disturb; both are commensal relationships, since the animal or gardener is unaffected.
[bio32-s4.17.b] A parasite lives on or in another organism, the host, and feeds on it. Unlike a predator, it usually keeps its host alive.
[bio32-s4.17.0] Parasitism: the parasite lives in or on the host and feeds on it, harming it. A parasite usually weakens its host without killing it quickly, since it depends on the host.
[bio32-s4.17.1] Grassland ectoparasites (parasites living on the outside of the host): fleas on rabbits and hedgehogs; ticks on sheep, deer and humans. Ticks are also vectors of Lyme disease, meaning they carry it from host to host.
[bio32-s4.17.2] The rabbit flea carries the myxoma virus that causes myxomatosis, which has killed very large numbers of rabbits.
[bio32-s4.17.3] Grassland endoparasites (parasites living inside the host): liver fluke in sheep and cattle on wet pasture; roundworms and tapeworms in the gut of grazing animals. There is more on liver fluke and tapeworms in Pathogens.
[bio32-s4.17.4] Plant parasites: rust fungi on grasses and cereals.
[bio32-s4.17.5] Yellow rattle, a meadow plant, is a partial parasite: it photosynthesises but taps the roots of grasses for water and minerals, weakening the grasses so that other wild flowers can grow. For this reason it is sown when restoring wildflower meadows.
[bio32-s4.17.6] Ivy is not a parasite: it climbs trees but makes its own food and takes nothing from them.
[bio32-s4.18.b] A parasite usually weakens its host rather than killing it quickly, but parasites and disease can still change how many hosts there are, because weakened hosts breed less and are caught more easily.
[bio32-s4.18.0] Parasites and disease weaken hosts, making them easier prey and less able to breed.
[bio32-s4.18.1] Parasites spread fastest when hosts are crowded, so their effect increases with population density, like predation.
[bio32-s4.19.b] The signs show the effect on each partner: + for benefits, − for harmed, and 0 for unaffected. Use them to compare the five relationships.
[bio32-s4.19.0] Predation (+/−): a predator kills and eats its prey, e.g. fox and rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.1] Competition (−/−): both organisms lose some of a scarce resource, e.g. grass and daisy for light, or grey and red squirrel.
[bio32-s4.19.2] Mutualism (+/+): both benefit, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules.
[bio32-s4.19.3] Commensalism (+/0): one benefits and the other is unaffected, e.g. mosses on tree bark.
[bio32-s4.19.4] Parasitism (+/−): the host is harmed but usually not killed quickly, e.g. a flea on a rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.5] Predation and parasitism are both +/−. The difference is that a predator kills its prey at once and eats it, while a parasite lives on a living host over a long time.

CANDIDATES bio34-s2: The nitrogen cycle
[bio34-s2.0.b] Every living thing needs nitrogen, yet the huge store in the air is out of reach for almost all of them. Nitrogen therefore has to be converted into usable forms and then recycled, or life would run out of it.
[bio34-s2.0.0] Living things need nitrogen to make amino acids and proteins, nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and chlorophyll (the green pigment in plants). Minerals goes into why plants need it and the signs of deficiency.
[bio34-s2.0.1] About 78% of the air is nitrogen gas (N₂), but plants and animals cannot use it. The two atoms are held together by a very strong triple bond that few organisms can break.
[bio34-s2.0.2] Plants can absorb nitrogen only as ions dissolved in soil water, mainly nitrate (NO₃⁻) and also ammonium (NH₄⁺). So nitrogen must be converted, or fixed, before it enters food chains, and recycled from dead matter.
[bio34-s2.0.3] The forms to recognise are nitrogen gas (N₂); ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium (NH₄⁺); nitrite (NO₂⁻); nitrate (NO₃⁻); and organic nitrogen, which is nitrogen built into proteins, nucleic acids and urea (the waste molecule in mammals' urine).
[bio34-s2.1.b] Nitrogen fixation converts nitrogen gas from the air into nitrogen compounds that living things can use, such as ammonium, ammonia or nitrate. The biological route is carried out by bacteria, either living freely or in partnership with plants.
[bio34-s2.1.0] Free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil and in water convert N₂ to ammonium, which they use to make their own proteins. When they die, decomposition releases the nitrogen to the soil.
[bio34-s2.1.1] Other nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in root nodules (small swellings on the roots) of legumes, the pea family, for example clover, peas and beans. They fix nitrogen for the plant.
[bio34-s2.1.2] The bacteria give the plant ammonium, which it uses to make amino acids. The plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates from photosynthesis and a protected, low-oxygen home. Both benefit, so this is mutualism.
[bio34-s2.1.3] Fixation takes a lot of energy, supplied as ATP from the bacteria's respiration, and uses an enzyme that oxygen damages. This is why nodules keep oxygen levels low: they contain a red pigment that binds oxygen, so active nodules are pink inside.
[bio34-s2.1.4] Legumes grow well in nitrogen-poor soil because their bacteria supply the nitrogen. When they die or are ploughed in, the nitrogen in their tissues enriches the soil.
[bio34-s2.1.5] Clover grown with grass, and legumes in crop rotation (growing different crops in turn on the same field), reduce the need for nitrogen fertiliser. This is why farmers grow clover.
[bio34-s2.2.b] Bacteria are not the only route from nitrogen gas to usable nitrogen. Lightning does a little of it naturally, and humans do a great deal of it in factories.
[bio34-s2.2.0] Atmospheric fixation by lightning: the energy of lightning makes nitrogen react with oxygen to form nitrogen oxides, which dissolve in rain and reach the soil as nitrate. This is a small contribution compared with biological fixation.
[bio34-s2.2.1] Industrial fixation by the Haber process: nitrogen from the air combines with hydrogen (made mainly from natural gas) at high temperature and pressure to make ammonia.
[bio34-s2.2.2] The ammonia is turned into nitrogen fertilisers such as ammonium nitrate and urea, which farmers spread to give crops usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.2.3] Human activity, mainly the Haber process, now fixes at least as much nitrogen each year as all natural processes on land. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s2.3.b] Once nitrogen is in the soil as nitrate or ammonium, it moves into living things and along food chains. Assimilation means the plant builds absorbed nitrogen into its own molecules.
[bio34-s2.3.0] Uptake: plant roots absorb nitrate and ammonium from soil water. Nitrate is taken in by active transport, against its concentration gradient, using ATP, and travels to the leaves in the xylem (the tubes that carry water up the plant).
[bio34-s2.3.1] Assimilation: the plant uses the nitrogen, together with sugars from photosynthesis, to make amino acids, then proteins, and also nucleic acids and chlorophyll.
[bio34-s2.3.2] Feeding: animals get nitrogen only by eating plants or other animals. They digest the proteins into amino acids and build their own proteins from them.
[bio34-s2.3.3] Excretion and death: animals excrete excess nitrogen (in mammals, as urea in urine). Faeces, urine, dead plants and dead animals return organic nitrogen to the soil.
[bio34-s2.4.b] Dead matter and wastes are full of organic nitrogen that plants cannot use directly. Decomposition is the step that turns it back into a usable form.
[bio34-s2.4.0] Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms, faeces and urine. They digest the proteins and other nitrogen compounds and release the nitrogen as ammonia, which dissolves in soil water as ammonium (NH₄⁺). This step is often called ammonification (putrefaction).
[bio34-s2.4.1] Without decomposition, the nitrogen in dead matter would be locked up and the soil would run out of usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.4.2] Decomposition is fastest in warm, moist, aerated soil (soil with air spaces), because these conditions suit the decomposers. There is more on this in The carbon cycle.
[bio34-s2.4.3] Ammonium is the starting point for nitrification. Some ammonium is also taken up directly by plants.
[bio34-s2.5.b] Ammonium from decomposition is not the form plants take up most. Nitrifying bacteria in the soil convert it to nitrate in two steps.
[bio34-s2.5.0] Nitrification is the conversion of ammonium into nitrate by nitrifying bacteria in the soil, in two steps. First, one group of nitrifying bacteria oxidises ammonium (NH₄⁺) to nitrite (NO₂⁻). Second, a second group oxidises nitrite to nitrate (NO₃⁻).
[bio34-s2.5.1] Nitrifying bacteria are aerobic: they need oxygen. Nitrification is therefore fastest in well-drained, well-aerated soil. Ploughing and draining help it, while waterlogging (soil with all its air spaces filled with water) stops it.
[bio34-s2.5.2] Nitrifying bacteria are autotrophic (they make their own food): they get energy from these chemical reactions (chemosynthesis) rather than from light or from eating other organisms.
[bio34-s2.5.3] Nitrate is the form plants absorb most. It is also very soluble and is not held by soil particles, so heavy rain easily washes it out of the soil (leaching) into ground water, rivers and lakes. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s2.6.b] Nitrification builds up nitrate in the soil. Denitrification is the opposite journey, returning nitrogen to the air and completing the cycle.
[bio34-s2.6.0] Denitrification is the conversion of nitrate in the soil back into nitrogen gas (N₂) by denitrifying bacteria. This returns nitrogen to the atmosphere and completes the cycle.
[bio34-s2.6.1] Denitrifying bacteria work in anaerobic conditions (no oxygen), such as waterlogged, compacted or poorly drained soils and the mud of lakes and marshes. They use nitrate in place of oxygen in respiration.
[bio34-s2.6.2] Denitrification removes usable nitrogen from the soil, lowering fertility, so farmers drain wet land to stop it.
[bio34-s2.6.3] It also gives off some nitrous oxide (N₂O), a greenhouse gas (one that traps heat in the atmosphere). There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s2.6.4] In wetlands and water treatment, denitrification is useful because it removes excess nitrate before it reaches rivers.
[bio34-s2.7.b] Fixation, decomposition, nitrification and denitrification are the four named processes of the cycle. Comparing them side by side shows what each converts, who does it, and what it needs.
[bio34-s2.7.0] Fixation: N₂ becomes ammonium. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living or in nodules) do it. It is anaerobic inside a nodule, and it adds usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.7.1] Decomposition: organic nitrogen becomes ammonium. Bacteria and fungi do it. It is aerobic or anaerobic, and it recycles nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.7.2] Nitrification: ammonium becomes nitrite and then nitrate. Nitrifying bacteria do it. It needs oxygen, and it makes the form plants use most.
[bio34-s2.7.3] Denitrification: nitrate becomes N₂. Denitrifying bacteria do it. It needs no oxygen, and it removes usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.8.b] A model is useful because it lets you work out what happens when conditions change. It also has limits, and exams may ask you to name them.
[bio34-s2.8.0] Prediction (a): if a field becomes waterlogged, nitrification stops and denitrification increases, so soil nitrate falls and plants become nitrogen-deficient.
[bio34-s2.8.1] Prediction (b): if a farmer grows clover, fixation increases, so there is more soil nitrogen the next season.
[bio34-s2.8.2] Prediction (c): heavy rain after fertiliser is spread leaches nitrate into a river. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s2.8.3] Limitation: the model shows the processes but not their rates or amounts.
[bio34-s2.8.4] Limitation: it does not show where nitrogen is lost, such as by leaching or by ammonia escaping from slurry (liquid animal waste), unless these are added.
[bio34-s2.8.5] Limitation: real soils contain many more microorganisms and steps than the four named groups. There is more on general limitations of models in Models.
