CANDIDATES bio21-s1: Structures and systems for response
[bio21-s1.0.b] Every living thing has to cope with a changing world, from a predator appearing to a drop in water supply. Detecting a change and reacting to it is called response, and it is the starting point for every system in this subsection.
[bio21-s1.0.0] Response is a characteristic of all living things: organisms detect stimuli (changes in their internal or external environment) and react to them in ways that help them survive.
[bio21-s1.0.1] The general pathway is stimulus → receptor (detects it) → coordinator (processes the information) → effector (a muscle or gland) → response.
[bio21-s1.0.2] In other words, a receptor notices the change, a coordinator decides what to do, and an effector carries it out, so the body reacts in a useful way rather than at random.
[bio21-s1.1.b] The nervous system is the body's fast communication network. It links the places that detect changes to the places that act on them.
[bio21-s1.1.0] Receptors in the sense organs (such as the skin, eye and ear) detect stimuli. Here sense organs are named only as receptors, with no structure needed.
[bio21-s1.1.1] Neurons (nerve cells) carry fast electrical impulses from the receptors to the brain and spinal cord, which act as the coordinator.
[bio21-s1.1.2] The brain and spinal cord send impulses along neurons to muscles and glands, which act as effectors, so the body can respond.
[bio21-s1.1.3] Nervous responses are fast and short-lived, because electrical impulses travel quickly and stop as soon as the stimulus ends. There is more on this in The Nervous System.
[bio21-s1.1.4] Diagram: a nervous pathway from receptor to coordinator to effector. Label the sense organ, sensory neuron, brain and spinal cord, motor neuron and muscle or gland.
[bio21-s1.2.b] The hormonal system is a second way of coordinating a response. Instead of electrical impulses along neurons, it uses chemical messengers carried in the blood.
[bio21-s1.2.0] Glands release hormones (chemical messengers) directly into the blood, rather than along neurons.
[bio21-s1.2.1] Hormones travel in the blood to the target organs, the organs that have the means to respond to them.
[bio21-s1.2.2] Hormonal responses are slower and longer-lasting than nervous responses, because a chemical has to be carried round the body in the blood and takes time to be cleared away.
[bio21-s1.2.3] Examples: adrenaline, released in danger, and insulin, released after a meal. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio21-s1.2.4] Diagram: a gland releasing a hormone into a blood vessel, with an arrow to a target organ. Label the gland, hormone, blood vessel and target organ.
[bio21-s1.3.b] Detecting a stimulus and deciding what to do achieves nothing unless the body moves. The musculoskeletal system, made of muscles and bones, does the moving.
[bio21-s1.3.0] Muscles are the effectors of most nervous responses, so most nerve impulses to effectors end at a muscle.
[bio21-s1.3.1] Muscles contract (shorten) and pull on bones at joints, the places where bones meet, to move the body.
[bio21-s1.3.2] Example: withdrawing a hand from heat, carried out by muscles pulling on the bones of the arm. There is more on this in The Musculoskeletal System.
[bio21-s1.3.3] Diagram: a muscle attached to a bone across a joint. Label the bone, the joint and the muscle.
[bio21-s1.4.b] Some stimuli are not changes in temperature or danger from outside but invaders inside the body. The immune system is the response system that deals with them.
[bio21-s1.4.0] The immune system responds to pathogens (disease-causing organisms) and other foreign antigens (molecules the body recognises as not its own).
[bio21-s1.4.1] White blood cells respond in two ways: they engulf bacteria, or they produce antibodies (proteins made against a specific foreign antigen). There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio21-s1.5.b] Responses rarely use just one system. A single danger can involve the nervous, hormonal and musculoskeletal systems one after another.
[bio21-s1.5.0] In danger, the nervous system stimulates the adrenal glands (hormone-releasing glands) to release adrenaline, so a nerve signal triggers a hormone response.
[bio21-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the muscles for action, so the body is ready to act.
[bio21-s1.5.2] The musculoskeletal system then carries out the movement, as the effector.
[bio21-s1.6.b] Plants have no nervous system or muscles. They respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and through their structure.
[bio21-s1.6.0] Because plants cannot run away from danger, their protection depends on features built into their structure and on chemicals they make.
[bio21-s1.7.b] An anatomical adaptation is a feature of a plant's structure that protects it. These features work against grazing animals, insects and pathogens (disease-causing organisms).
[bio21-s1.7.0] Thorns, spines and prickles deter grazing animals, since they make the plant painful to eat: holly leaves have spines, brambles have prickles and cacti have spines.
[bio21-s1.7.1] A thick waxy cuticle (a waterproof layer on the surface) and bark form barriers to pathogens and to water loss, so microbes find it hard to get in and water stays inside.
[bio21-s1.7.2] Stinging hairs inject irritants into anything that touches them, as in nettles.
[bio21-s1.7.3] Hairy leaves deter insects.
[bio21-s1.7.4] Diagram: a labelled leaf showing the waxy cuticle and a spiny leaf margin, such as holly.
[bio21-s1.8.b] Plants also protect themselves by making chemicals. Some make a plant unpleasant or poisonous to eat, and others fight infection.
[bio21-s1.8.0] Toxic or bad-tasting chemicals deter herbivores (plant-eating animals): nicotine in tobacco, caffeine in tea and coffee plants, and tannins in oak leaves.
[bio21-s1.8.1] Nettle stings contain histamine and formic acid, which is why the stinging hairs of nettles act as both an anatomical and a chemical defence.
[bio21-s1.8.2] Some plants produce antimicrobial chemicals when infected, which limit the spread of a pathogen.
[bio21-s1.9.b] Plants also react to changes in their surroundings. Some of these responses are slow and some are quick.
[bio21-s1.9.0] Guard cells (the pair of cells around each stoma, or leaf pore) close the stomata when water is short, reducing water loss. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio21-s1.9.1] The sensitive plant, Mimosa pudica, folds its leaves rapidly when touched, a fast response by a plant.
[bio21-s1.10.b] Putting the two side by side shows why plants rely on protective adaptations: plants cannot move away from danger, as humans can, and their responses are generally slower.
[bio21-s1.10.0] Speed: human responses can be fast, through nerves, whereas plant responses are generally slower.
[bio21-s1.10.1] Mechanism: humans use nerves and hormones, whereas plants use chemicals and their structure.
[bio21-s1.10.2] Protection: plants cannot move away from danger, so their protective adaptations matter more.

CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio24-s1: Flower structure and pollination
[bio24-s1.0.b] Flowering plants reproduce sexually by means of flowers, which produce the gametes (sex cells) and bring about pollination (the transfer of pollen) and fertilisation (the joining of male and female gametes). Three structures carry the process from gamete to new plant: the flower, the seed and the fruit.
[bio24-s1.0.0] The flower is a short shoot whose parts are modified leaves arranged in rings (whorls) on the receptacle, the tip of the flower stalk. Its job is to produce gametes, bring about pollination and fertilisation, and then produce seeds and fruits.
[bio24-s1.0.1] The flower makes the male and female gametes and brings about pollination. It is the only one of the three structures that exists before fertilisation, because the seed and fruit develop from flower parts afterwards.
[bio24-s1.0.2] The seed, a fertilised and developed ovule, contains the embryo (a new plant) with a food store and a protective coat. The fruit, a developed ovary, protects the seeds and helps to disperse them, that is, spread them away from the parent plant.
[bio24-s1.0.3] Seed detail is in Seeds; dispersal is in Fruit and seed dispersal.
[bio24-s1.0.4] Many flowers are bisexual (hermaphrodite), meaning they have both male and female parts in the same flower, e.g. buttercup.
[bio24-s1.0.5] Some plants have separate male and female flowers on the same plant, e.g. hazel, whose male flowers hang in catkins and whose small female flowers have red stigmas. In some species male and female flowers are on different plants, e.g. holly.
[bio24-s1.1.b] A flower is built in rings, listed here from the outside inwards. The outer parts hold the flower together, protect it and, in many species, attract the animals that carry its pollen.
[bio24-s1.1.0] Receptacle: the swollen tip of the flower stalk, to which all the other flower parts are attached, so it holds the flower together. For example, in strawberry it swells to form the fleshy part of the fruit.
[bio24-s1.1.1] Sepal: a small, usually green, leaf-like part in the outer ring; together the sepals form the calyx. Sepals enclose and protect the flower while it is a bud, and some photosynthesise (make food using light).
[bio24-s1.1.2] Petal: inside the sepals; together the petals form the corolla. In insect-pollinated flowers the petals are large, coloured and often scented, which attracts insects.
[bio24-s1.1.3] Many insect-pollinated petals have lines or patches (nectar guides) that lead insects to the nectar. In wind-pollinated flowers petals are small, dull or absent, because there is no need to attract animals.
[bio24-s1.1.4] Nectary: a gland, usually at the base of the petals, that secretes nectar, a sugary liquid that rewards visiting insects so that they keep coming back. Pollen is also eaten by some insects, e.g. bees collect it to feed their larvae.
[bio24-s1.2.b] The male part of the flower is the stamen. Its job is to make pollen, the package that carries the male gametes, and to hold it where a pollinator can pick it up.
[bio24-s1.2.0] Stamen: the male part, made of an anther on a stalk, the filament, so that the pollen is held up where it can be picked up. The stamens together form the androecium.
[bio24-s1.2.1] Anther: produces pollen grains by meiosis (a cell division that halves the chromosome number), so the pollen grains are haploid, with one set of chromosomes. Each pollen grain contains the male gamete nuclei. When ripe the anther splits open to release the pollen.
[bio24-s1.2.2] Filament: the stalk that holds the anther in position, where insects brush against it or out in the wind. It also carries water and nutrients to the anther through a small vascular strand (a thin tube of transport tissue).
[bio24-s1.2.3] Diagram: a stamen drawn on its own. Labels: anther, filament.
[bio24-s1.3.b] The female part of the flower is the carpel. It catches pollen, provides a route for the male gametes to reach the egg cell, and protects the ovules that become seeds.
[bio24-s1.3.0] Carpel: the female part, made of the stigma, style and ovary. A flower may have one carpel or many; the carpels together form the gynoecium.
[bio24-s1.3.1] Stigma: the tip of the carpel that receives (catches) pollen. It is sticky in insect-pollinated flowers, so pollen carried on an insect adheres, and large and feathery in wind-pollinated grasses, so it can catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s1.3.2] Style: the stalk joining the stigma to the ovary. It holds the stigma in a position to receive pollen, and the pollen tube (the thin tube a pollen grain grows) grows down through it to the ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.3] Ovary: the swollen base of the carpel. It contains one or more ovules and protects them, so the developing seeds are not exposed. After fertilisation the ovary develops into the fruit.
[bio24-s1.3.4] Ovule: a structure inside the ovary that contains the egg cell (female gamete), made by meiosis, so it is haploid. After fertilisation the ovule develops into the seed. The micropyle is a tiny pore at one end of the ovule through which the pollen tube usually enters.
[bio24-s1.3.5] Diagram: a carpel drawn on its own. Labels: stigma, style, ovary.
[bio24-s1.3.6] Diagram: a half-flower (vertical section) of an insect-pollinated flower such as buttercup or wallflower. Labels: receptacle, sepal, petal, nectary, stamen (anther, filament), carpel (stigma, style, ovary), ovule.
[bio24-s1.4.b] Pollination must happen before fertilisation can take place. Flowers can be self-pollinated or cross-pollinated, and several features favour cross-pollination.
[bio24-s1.4.0] Pollination is the transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma of a flower of the same species. It is only the arrival of pollen: fertilisation, the fusion of gamete nuclei in the ovule, comes later.
[bio24-s1.4.1] Self-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of the same flower or of another flower on the same plant. Cross-pollination: pollen is transferred to a stigma of a flower on a different plant of the same species.
[bio24-s1.4.2] Cross-pollination combines genes from two different parents, so the offspring show more genetic variation. The species is better able to adapt to change, such as new diseases or climate, and avoids the harmful effects of inbreeding (breeding between close relatives).
[bio24-s1.4.3] Self-pollination needs no second plant or pollinator and is more certain, but it gives less variation. It suits isolated plants and plants in places with few pollinators.
[bio24-s1.4.4] Ways flowers favour cross-pollination: the stamens and stigmas of a flower ripen at different times; self-incompatibility, where pollen landing on a stigma of the same plant fails to grow a pollen tube; separate male and female flowers (hazel) or separate male and female plants (holly).
[bio24-s1.4.5] In primrose two flower forms exist: pin flowers, with a long style and low anthers, and thrum flowers, with a short style and high anthers. Insects carry pollen between the two forms, so the pollen reaches a different plant.
[bio24-s1.4.6] Agents of pollination (the carriers of pollen): insects (most showy flowers), wind (grasses and many trees, e.g. hazel, oak and birch), and less often other animals (birds and bats in other parts of the world) or water. There is more on this in Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants.
[bio24-s1.5.b] The flower's structure matches the agent of pollination, whether an animal or the wind. Each type of flower is built to make the transfer of pollen as reliable as possible.
[bio24-s1.5.0] In insect-pollinated plants the flower attracts the pollinator (colour, scent, nectar guides), rewards it (nectar and pollen), and places its anthers and stigma where the insect's body touches them.
[bio24-s1.5.1] Pollen sticks to the insect at one flower and is rubbed onto the sticky stigma of the next flower of the same species it visits.
[bio24-s1.5.2] Many insects visit only one species on a foraging trip, having learned the flower they are faithful to, so pollen is carried to the right species.
[bio24-s1.5.3] Plant and pollinator both benefit, because the plant is pollinated and the insect is fed: this is a mutualism (a relationship in which both partners gain). There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio24-s1.5.4] In wind-pollinated plants the flower's job is to release very large amounts of pollen into moving air and to catch pollen on a large stigma. There is nothing to attract animals, so there are no showy petals, scent or nectar.
[bio24-s1.5.5] Sustainability link: many crops (e.g. apples, strawberries and oilseed rape) and wild plants depend on insect pollinators, especially bees. Bee numbers are falling through habitat loss, pesticides and disease.
[bio24-s1.5.6] Ireland's response is the All-Ireland Pollinator Plan, first published in 2015 and coordinated by the National Biodiversity Data Centre. It encourages councils, farmers, schools and gardeners to provide food and nesting places for pollinators, e.g. by leaving wildflowers and mowing less often.
[bio24-s1.5.7] Health link: wind-pollinated plants, especially grasses, release huge numbers of small, light pollen grains that cause hay fever (allergic rhinitis) in early summer.
[bio24-s1.6.b] Once pollen reaches a stigma of the right species, a sequence of events leads to fertilisation. Afterwards the flower parts change into the seed and fruit.
[bio24-s1.6.0] Fertilisation in five steps: 1. A pollen grain of the same species lands on the stigma.
[bio24-s1.6.1] 2. It grows a pollen tube down through the style to the ovary, digesting a path with enzymes.
[bio24-s1.6.2] 3. The tube enters an ovule, usually through the micropyle, and carries the male gamete nuclei to it.
[bio24-s1.6.3] 4. Fertilisation: a male gamete nucleus fuses with the egg cell nucleus to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes, the first cell of the new plant).
[bio24-s1.6.4] 5. The zygote divides by mitosis (ordinary cell division that makes identical cells) to form the embryo.
[bio24-s1.6.5] Double fertilisation: a second male nucleus fuses with two other nuclei in the ovule (the polar nuclei) to form the endosperm nucleus, which divides to form the endosperm food store.
[bio24-s1.6.6] After fertilisation the ovule becomes the seed, and its outer layers (integuments) become the testa, the seed coat. The ovary becomes the fruit, and its wall becomes the fruit wall (pericarp). The petals, stamens and usually the sepals wither and fall off.
[bio24-s1.6.7] Diagram: a carpel in section, with a pollen grain on the stigma, the pollen tube growing down the style and entering an ovule through the micropyle. Labels: pollen grain, pollen tube, style, ovary, ovule, egg cell, micropyle.

CANDIDATES bio24-s2: Investigation: insect- and wind-pollinated plants
[bio24-s2.0.b] A flower's structure follows from how its pollen travels. Insects must be lured in and rewarded, so insect-pollinated flowers advertise; the wind needs no persuading, so wind-pollinated flowers drop the advertising. Each point gives the insect-pollinated flower first and the wind-pollinated flower second, and the flower parts are named as in Flower structure and pollination.
[bio24-s2.0.0] Petals: insect-pollinated flowers have large, brightly coloured petals, often with nectar guides (markings that lead the insect towards the nectar), to attract insects.
[bio24-s2.0.1] Wind-pollinated flowers have small, green or dull petals, or none, because there is no animal to attract and large petals would block pollen in the wind.
[bio24-s2.0.2] Scent and nectar: insect-pollinated flowers are usually scented and have nectaries (the glands that produce nectar, a sugary liquid), so the insect is rewarded for visiting.
[bio24-s2.0.3] Wind-pollinated flowers have no scent and no nectaries, because there is no visitor to reward.
[bio24-s2.0.4] Flowers as a whole: insect-pollinated flowers are usually showy, single or in visible clusters, so that insects can spot them.
[bio24-s2.0.5] Wind-pollinated flowers are small and inconspicuous, often in catkins (dangling spikes of tiny flowers) or spikes held high above the leaves, where the wind can reach them.
[bio24-s2.0.6] Many wind-pollinated trees, e.g. hazel, flower before their leaves open, so the leaves don't block the pollen.
[bio24-s2.0.7] Examples: insect-pollinated plants include buttercup, wallflower, foxglove and primrose; wind-pollinated plants include grasses (e.g. ryegrass), hazel, oak, birch and plantain.
[bio24-s2.1.b] The anther (the pollen-producing part of the stamen, held up on a stalk called the filament) and the stigma (the sticky or feathery top of the carpel that receives pollen) are positioned to suit the carrier. Insects touch the flower's parts; the wind blows past them.
[bio24-s2.1.0] Position of anthers: insect-pollinated anthers are inside the flower, held firmly on short or stiff filaments where a visiting insect brushes against them, so pollen rubs onto its body.
[bio24-s2.1.1] Wind-pollinated anthers hang outside the flower on long, thin, flexible filaments, loosely attached and often swinging freely, so the wind shakes pollen out.
[bio24-s2.1.2] Stigma: insect-pollinated stigmas are small, sticky and inside the flower, where the insect's body touches them and pollen sticks to them.
[bio24-s2.1.3] Wind-pollinated stigmas are large, feathery (branched) and hang outside the flower, giving a big surface area to catch pollen from the air.
[bio24-s2.1.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled drawings of a buttercup or wallflower half-flower (large petal, nectary, anther inside, small sticky stigma) and a wind-pollinated grass flower (feathery stigmas, anthers on long filaments hanging outside small green bracts). Label bracts only as bracts (small green scales).
[bio24-s2.2.b] The pollen itself also differs. Insects deliver pollen accurately, whereas the wind scatters it at random, and the grains are built for the different journeys.
[bio24-s2.2.0] Amount of pollen: insect-pollinated flowers produce relatively small amounts, because insects carry pollen directly from flower to flower.
[bio24-s2.2.1] Wind-pollinated flowers produce very large amounts, because most pollen carried by the wind never reaches a stigma of the same species, so many grains are needed for a few to land.
[bio24-s2.2.2] Pollen grains: insect pollen grains are larger, often with a sticky or oily coat and a rough or spiky surface that clings to insect hairs.
[bio24-s2.2.3] Wind pollen grains are small, light, dry and smooth, so they are carried easily and far by the wind and don't clump together.
[bio24-s2.2.4] Diagram: an insect pollen grain (larger, with a spiky or sculptured surface) drawn beside a wind pollen grain (smaller and smooth), as seen under the microscope.
[bio24-s2.3.b] This is a comparative observation study, not a controlled experiment. No factor is changed, so there is no treatment and no control group; the two flower types are compared with each other.
[bio24-s2.3.0] Aim: to compare the structures of an insect-pollinated flower and a wind-pollinated flower, and relate each structure to its function in pollination.
[bio24-s2.3.1] Example hypothesis: “The insect-pollinated flower will have larger, coloured petals, nectaries and a sticky stigma inside the flower; the wind-pollinated flower will have small or no petals, anthers and feathery stigmas hanging outside the flower, and smaller, smoother pollen grains.”
[bio24-s2.3.2] What differs and what is recorded: the factor being compared is the method of pollination (insect or wind). Qualitative features (recorded in words) are colour, scent, presence of nectar, whether the stigma is sticky or feathery, whether anthers and stigma are inside or outside the flower, and pollen surface.
[bio24-s2.3.3] Quantitative features (measured as numbers) are the number of each part, the lengths of filaments and styles, petal size and pollen grain diameter.
[bio24-s2.3.4] Keeping the comparison fair: use fully open, mature flowers in which the anthers are shedding pollen, so every flower is at the same stage. Examine several flowers of each species (at least three) and average the measurements, to reduce the effect of one unusual flower.
[bio24-s2.3.5] Measure every flower in the same way, with the same instruments and microscope magnification, so the two types can be compared fairly.
[bio24-s2.3.6] Equipment: fresh insect-pollinated flowers (e.g. buttercup or wallflower) and wind-pollinated flowers (e.g. a flowering grass, or hazel catkins in late winter); white tile; scalpel or sharp blade; forceps; mounted needle; hand lens (×10) or stereo (dissecting) microscope; ruler (mm) or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.3.7] Also needed: microscope slides and coverslips; dropper with water; compound light microscope; clear sticky tape; gloves; pencil and paper.
[bio24-s2.4.b] The method moves from the whole flower, to its separated parts, to the pollen under the microscope, and repeats everything for the second flower type.
[bio24-s2.4.0] 1. Examine each whole flower with the hand lens. Record its colour, size and whether it has a scent. Note whether the anthers and stigmas are inside the flower or hang outside it.
[bio24-s2.4.1] 2. Remove the parts of the insect-pollinated flower from the outside in, using forceps, laying each ring (sepals, petals, stamens, carpels) in order on the white tile. Count the parts in each ring and record them.
[bio24-s2.4.2] 3. Look at the base of the petals for nectaries (shiny or moist patches). Touch the stigma lightly with a mounted needle to see if it is sticky.
[bio24-s2.4.3] 4. Measure the length of the filaments, style and petals with a ruler (or on graph paper) for each flower. Repeat for at least three flowers of each species and calculate the means.
[bio24-s2.4.4] 5. Repeat steps 1 to 4 with the wind-pollinated flower, using the stereo microscope for the small grass flowers. Note the bracts, the long hanging filaments and the feathery stigmas.
[bio24-s2.4.5] 6. Cut a vertical section of the insect-pollinated flower with the scalpel on the tile (a half-flower) and draw it, labelled.
[bio24-s2.4.6] 7. Pollen: tap a ripe anther over a drop of water on a clean slide, or press clear sticky tape onto the anther and stick the tape on a slide. Add a coverslip and examine under the light microscope at low, then high, power.
[bio24-s2.4.7] 8. Draw a few grains of each type at the same magnification and compare their size and surface; estimate their diameter (e.g. against the field of view or an eyepiece scale). Note how easily pollen comes off the anther and whether it clumps (sticky) or spreads (dry).
[bio24-s2.5.b] Scalpels, pollen, some plants and glass slides each carry a risk, so the practical needs a precaution for each.
[bio24-s2.5.0] Cut away from the body on a white tile with a sharp scalpel; carry and store blades safely, so nobody is cut.
[bio24-s2.5.1] Pollen can trigger hay fever or asthma. Students with pollen allergies should avoid handling wind-pollinated flowers or wear a mask and gloves, and the room should be ventilated.
[bio24-s2.5.2] Some plants are poisonous or irritant: foxglove is poisonous if eaten, and buttercup sap can irritate the skin. Wear gloves, don't put plant material near the mouth, and wash hands after the practical.
[bio24-s2.5.3] Collect only common plants, with the landowner's permission; don't pick protected or rare wild flowers.
[bio24-s2.5.4] Microscope slides and coverslips are fragile glass; dispose of breakages in a glass bin.
[bio24-s2.6.b] Recording results in a table puts the two flowers side by side, so each difference can be seen and then explained.
[bio24-s2.6.0] Results table: one column for each species (insect-pollinated and wind-pollinated), with rows for petal colour and size, scent, nectaries, number of stamens, filament length, anther position, stigma type and position, style length, amount of pollen released, and pollen size and surface.
[bio24-s2.6.1] Expected primary data (collected by you) for the insect-pollinated flower: large coloured petals, nectaries, scent, anthers on short or stiff filaments inside the flower, a small sticky stigma, and larger, sticky, spiky or sculptured pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.6.2] Expected primary data for the wind-pollinated flower: small green bracts or no petals, no nectar or scent, anthers dangling outside the flower on long filaments that shed clouds of dry pollen when tapped, feathery stigmas outside the flower, and smaller, smooth, dry pollen grains.
[bio24-s2.7.b] A conclusion links what was seen to why it matters for pollination, and secondary data add evidence the class could not collect itself.
[bio24-s2.7.0] Conclusion: relate each observed structure to its function, using the adaptations above. For example, the feathery stigma of the grass gives a large surface to catch airborne pollen, and the sticky stigma of the buttercup collects pollen rubbed off an insect's body. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio24-s2.7.1] Secondary data used to support the conclusions: scanning electron micrographs (SEM images) of pollen grains, which show surface detail that a light microscope can't resolve, such as spikes and ridges on insect pollen and smooth surfaces on grass pollen.
[bio24-s2.7.2] Other secondary data: published pollen counts, which show the huge numbers of grass pollen grains in the air in early summer; and labelled diagrams and descriptions of other species in textbooks or plant identification guides.
[bio24-s2.7.3] Primary data are collected by the student through observing and measuring; secondary data are collected by others. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio24-s2.8.b] Every practical has weaknesses. Each limitation below has a matching improvement, and you should be able to give both.
[bio24-s2.8.0] Only a few flowers of one or two species are examined, so the results may not represent all insect- or wind-pollinated plants; examine more flowers and more species, and compare with secondary data.
[bio24-s2.8.1] Flowers at different stages (bud, open, withering) differ in size and pollen release; use flowers at the same stage.
[bio24-s2.8.2] Grass flowers are very small, so measurements with a ruler are imprecise; use a stereo microscope or graph paper.
[bio24-s2.8.3] Pollen size estimated against the field of view is approximate; a calibrated eyepiece scale gives more precise sizes.
[bio24-s2.8.4] The light microscope can't show fine surface detail, hence the use of SEM images.
[bio24-s2.8.5] Judgements of colour, scent and stickiness are qualitative and subjective, because different people judge differently; have two people record them.
[bio24-s2.8.6] Damage during dissection can break parts or knock off pollen; handle flowers gently with forceps.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio24-s4: Fruit and seed dispersal
[bio24-s4.0.b] Dispersal is the carrying of fruits and seeds away from the parent plant. Plants cannot move, so dispersal is how they spread their offspring, and it gives the young plants a much better chance of surviving.
[bio24-s4.0.0] Dispersal reduces competition between the young plants and the parent, and among the young plants themselves, for light, water, minerals and space, because seeds spread out are not all drawing on the same patch of soil.
[bio24-s4.0.1] Dispersal lets the species colonise new areas and new habitats, so it can grow beyond the place where the parent happens to live.
[bio24-s4.0.2] Dispersal spreads the population out, so a local disaster, such as disease, fire or flooding, is less likely to destroy all of it.
[bio24-s4.0.3] Seeds that fall directly under the parent are usually shaded and short of water and minerals, so few survive. Plants produce many seeds because most are lost during dispersal or eaten.
[bio24-s4.0.4] Sustainability link: the ability of some plants to spread their seeds widely makes them invasive (they take over) when introduced to new countries, e.g. Himalayan balsam, an invasive plant along Irish rivers with exploding seed pods.
[bio24-s4.1.b] Dispersal works through fruits, so it helps to know what a fruit is. The type of fruit a plant makes decides which dispersal method suits it.
[bio24-s4.1.0] A fruit is a mature ovary (the part of the flower that holds the ovules) containing seeds; its wall is the pericarp. It protects the seeds and is adapted to disperse them. Botanically, many foods called vegetables are fruits, e.g. tomatoes, cucumbers and pea pods.
[bio24-s4.1.1] Fleshy (succulent) fruits have a soft, juicy pericarp, often brightly coloured and sweet when ripe, e.g. blackberry, tomato and cherry; they are mostly dispersed by animals that eat them.
[bio24-s4.1.2] Dry fruits have a hard or papery pericarp, e.g. pea pod, hazelnut and sycamore; they are dispersed by wind, water, animals carrying them, or by splitting open.
[bio24-s4.1.3] A false fruit is one in which other parts of the flower, not only the ovary, form the fruit, e.g. strawberry, where the fleshy part is the receptacle (the swollen top of the flower stalk).
[bio24-s4.2.b] Wind carries a fruit or seed further the more slowly it falls. Wind-dispersed plants therefore make fruits or seeds that are light and have a large surface area compared with their mass.
[bio24-s4.2.0] Adaptations: fruits or seeds are small and light, or have wings or parachutes that increase their surface area, so they fall slowly and are carried further by the wind.
[bio24-s4.2.1] Parachutes: dandelion and thistle fruits each carry a parachute of fine hairs that lets them float long distances.
[bio24-s4.2.2] Wings: sycamore has paired winged fruits and ash has single winged fruits. The wing makes the fruit spin like a helicopter blade as it falls, slowing it so the wind carries it away from the parent.
[bio24-s4.2.3] Censer (pepper-pot) mechanism: the poppy's dry fruit, a capsule, has a ring of pores near the top. When the wind sways the stalk, the tiny seeds are shaken out a few at a time, like pepper from a shaker.
[bio24-s4.2.4] Dust-like seeds: orchid seeds are so tiny and light that they are blown like dust.
[bio24-s4.3.b] Plants growing beside or on water use it to carry their offspring. The key adaptation is the ability to float, so the fruit is carried rather than sinking.
[bio24-s4.3.0] Adaptations: fruits or seeds that float, because they contain air spaces or have a light, waterproof covering, so they can be carried by streams, rivers or the sea.
[bio24-s4.3.1] Coconut: the fruit has a thick, fibrous, air-filled husk and a waterproof outer layer, so it floats for long periods and is carried by ocean currents to other shores and islands.
[bio24-s4.3.2] Alder: a tree of riverbanks whose small fruits contain air and float, and are carried downstream to settle on new banks.
[bio24-s4.4.b] Some plants use animals as couriers by offering them food. The animal eats the fruit, and the seeds travel inside it before being left somewhere new.
[bio24-s4.4.0] Fleshy fruits are eaten by birds and mammals. The animal digests the fleshy pericarp, but the seeds have a tough testa (the hard seed coat) or a hard stone that resists digestion. 
[bio24-s4.4.1] The seeds pass out in the faeces, often far from the parent, and the droppings act as a supply of minerals.
[bio24-s4.4.2] Adaptations: bright colours (often red, black or purple) when ripe, attracting birds; sweet, juicy, nutritious flesh as a reward for the animal.
[bio24-s4.4.3] Unripe fruit is green and sour while the seeds are unripe, so the fruit isn't eaten too early, before the seeds can survive and grow.
[bio24-s4.4.4] Examples: blackberry, holly berries and rowan berries (eaten by birds), and tomato.
[bio24-s4.4.5] Animal caching: squirrels and jays bury acorns and hazelnuts as winter stores and fail to recover some, which then grow into new trees.
[bio24-s4.5.b] Other plants do not offer food. Instead their fruits hitch a ride on the outside of an animal.
[bio24-s4.5.0] Fruits have hooks or sticky surfaces that catch on the fur or feathers of passing animals (or on human clothing). They fall or are brushed off later, away from the parent.
[bio24-s4.5.1] Examples: burdock has hooked burs; goosegrass (cleavers) has small fruits covered in hooked hairs.
[bio24-s4.5.2] Technology link: the hooked burs of burdock inspired Velcro, invented by the Swiss engineer George de Mestral after he found burs stuck to his dog's coat.
[bio24-s4.6.b] A few plants need no outside carrier. The fruit itself throws the seeds out.
[bio24-s4.6.0] Some dry fruits split open forcefully as they dry, flinging the seeds out: gorse and pea pods twist and burst in dry weather, and Himalayan balsam pods explode when touched.
[bio24-s4.7.b] Each method of dispersal goes with a matching set of adaptations and named examples. Learn them as method, adaptation and example together.
[bio24-s4.7.0] Wind: wings, parachutes and light seeds (sycamore, ash, dandelion, poppy).
[bio24-s4.7.1] Water: floating, air-filled fruits (coconut, alder).
[bio24-s4.7.2] Animal, internal: fleshy, coloured, edible fruits with indigestible seeds (blackberry, holly).
[bio24-s4.7.3] Animal, external: hooks (burdock, goosegrass).
[bio24-s4.7.4] Self-dispersal: exploding pods (gorse, pea).
[bio24-s4.7.5] Diagram: labelled drawings of one fruit for each method: sycamore (wing and seed), dandelion (parachute of hairs and the fruit containing a seed), burdock bur (hooks) and blackberry (fleshy fruit and seeds). A coconut in section (fibrous, air-filled husk and seed) is optional.

CANDIDATES bio5-s1: Characteristics of living things
[bio5-s1.0.b] Biology is the study of living things, called organisms. No single feature separates living from non-living, so the specification lists eleven characteristics: organisation, response, respiration, nutrition, excretion, reproduction, growth, metabolism, homeostasis, heredity and cellular basis. Together they define life.
[bio5-s1.0.0] An organism shows all eleven characteristics at some stage of its life, though not necessarily all at once. A dormant seed or a hibernating animal shows very little response or growth, but it is still alive.
[bio5-s1.0.1] Some non-living things show one or two characteristics. A crystal grows, but only by adding material to its outside, not by making new material inside cells.
[bio5-s1.0.2] A fire seems to 'use oxygen', 'release energy' and 'spread', but it has no cells, no heredity and no control of its reactions, so it is not alive.
[bio5-s1.0.3] Something is alive only if it shows the full set, not just one or two features. Viruses are the main borderline case. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio5-s1.1.b] Living things are not random mixtures of chemicals: they are built in an ordered way, and the basic building block is the cell. Two of the eleven characteristics, organisation and cellular basis, describe this.
[bio5-s1.1.0] Organisation: living things have a highly ordered structure. In multicellular organisms (those made of many cells), cells are grouped into tissues, tissues into organs and organs into organ systems, each level carrying out particular jobs.
[bio5-s1.1.1] Even a single cell has organised parts called organelles (small structures inside a cell, each with its own job). There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio5-s1.1.2] Cellular basis: all living things are made of one or more cells. The cell is the smallest unit that can carry out all the processes of life, so it is the basic unit of structure and function.
[bio5-s1.1.3] Unicellular organisms (e.g. bacteria, yeast, Amoeba) are one cell that carries out every life process. Multicellular organisms (e.g. humans, oak trees) have many specialised cells, each adapted to one job.
[bio5-s1.1.4] All cells share a cell membrane, cytoplasm (the jelly-like fluid inside the cell), ribosomes (which make proteins) and DNA as their genetic material. This shared basis is evidence that all life has a common origin.
[bio5-s1.1.5] All cells arise only from pre-existing cells, by cell division. New cells are never made from non-living material.
[bio5-s1.1.6] These points form the cell theory, developed in the 19th century. Matthias Schleiden concluded that all plants are made of cells (1838); Theodor Schwann extended this to animals (1839); Rudolf Virchow stated that all cells come from pre-existing cells (1855).
[bio5-s1.2.b] Every organism needs a supply of food to stay alive and to build and repair its body. Nutrition is how it gets that food, and organisms fall into two groups depending on whether they make it or take it in.
[bio5-s1.2.0] Nutrition: the way an organism obtains food, which supplies the energy and the raw materials (e.g. carbon, nitrogen, minerals) it needs.
[bio5-s1.2.1] Autotrophic nutrition: the organism makes its own food from simple inorganic substances (ones not made by living things, such as carbon dioxide and water). Photosynthetic autotrophs (plants, algae, some bacteria) use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio5-s1.2.2] A few bacteria are chemosynthetic: instead of light, they use energy from chemical reactions to make their food, so they can live where there is no sunlight.
[bio5-s1.2.3] Heterotrophic nutrition: the organism takes in ready-made food made by other organisms, e.g. animals eat plants or other animals.
[bio5-s1.2.4] Fungi and many bacteria are also heterotrophs: they absorb food from dead matter (saprophytes) or from a living host (parasites). There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio5-s1.3.b] Food is only useful once its stored energy can be released in a form cells can use. Respiration does this inside every living cell, and it is easily confused with breathing.
[bio5-s1.3.0] Respiration: the controlled release of energy from food, mainly glucose, inside every living cell. The energy is trapped in ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy), which powers the cell's activities.
[bio5-s1.3.1] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. It releases much more energy per glucose than anaerobic respiration.
[bio5-s1.3.2] Anaerobic respiration happens without oxygen and releases less energy, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio5-s1.3.3] In human muscle, anaerobic respiration produces lactic acid. In yeast and plant cells it produces ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[bio5-s1.3.4] Respiration is not the same as breathing. Breathing is the movement of air into and out of the lungs (gas exchange), while respiration is a chemical process in cells.
[bio5-s1.3.5] Plants respire all the time, day and night, because every living cell needs energy continuously. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio5-s1.4.b] Living cells carry out thousands of chemical reactions at once. Metabolism is the name for all of them, and they fall into two types: those that build up and those that break down.
[bio5-s1.4.0] Metabolism: the sum of all the chemical reactions in an organism. Every reaction is controlled by an enzyme (a protein that speeds up a reaction).
[bio5-s1.4.1] Anabolic reactions build large molecules from smaller ones and need energy, e.g. photosynthesis (glucose made from carbon dioxide and water) and protein synthesis from amino acids.
[bio5-s1.4.2] Catabolic reactions break large molecules into smaller ones and release energy, e.g. respiration, and the digestion of starch to maltose.
[bio5-s1.4.3] In short, anabolic means building up and using energy, while catabolic means breaking down and releasing energy. There is more on this in Biomolecules — The Chemicals of Life.
[bio5-s1.5.b] Chemical reactions in cells make some products that the body cannot use. Excretion removes them before they cause harm.
[bio5-s1.5.0] Excretion: the removal of the waste products of metabolism, which are substances made in cells that are harmful or not needed. If they built up they would poison cells or upset conditions such as pH.
[bio5-s1.5.1] Human examples: carbon dioxide from respiration is excreted by the lungs. Urea, made in the liver from excess amino acids, is excreted by the kidneys in urine. Excess water and salts are excreted by the kidneys and in sweat.
[bio5-s1.5.2] Plant examples: oxygen from photosynthesis and carbon dioxide from respiration diffuse out through the stomata (the tiny pores in leaves). Some waste is stored in old leaves and lost when they fall.
[bio5-s1.5.3] Excretion is not egestion. Egestion is the removal of undigested food (faeces), which was never inside body cells and so is not a product of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.b] Cells work properly only when the conditions around them stay within narrow limits, even if the outside world changes. Homeostasis is the control that keeps them steady.
[bio5-s1.6.0] Homeostasis: the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment, such as body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance and pH, despite changes outside or inside the organism.
[bio5-s1.6.1] It matters because enzymes work only within narrow ranges of temperature and pH, so a large change would slow or stop the reactions of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.6.2] For example, human core body temperature is kept at about 37 °C, and insulin lowers blood glucose after a meal. There is more on the mechanism, negative feedback, in Homeostasis.
[bio5-s1.7.b] Organisms must cope with a changing environment and must also produce new individuals, because every organism eventually dies. Response and reproduction cover these two needs.
[bio5-s1.7.0] Response: the ability to detect a change in the internal or external environment (a stimulus) and react to it, which helps the organism survive.
[bio5-s1.7.1] Animals respond quickly through the nervous system (e.g. pulling a hand away from a hot object) and more slowly with hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood).
[bio5-s1.7.2] Plants respond more slowly, mainly through chemicals and their structure, e.g. guard cells close the stomata when water is short, which reduces water loss. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio5-s1.7.3] Reproduction: the production of new individuals of the same species. Every organism eventually dies, so reproduction is needed for the species to continue.
[bio5-s1.7.4] Asexual reproduction: one parent and no gametes (sex cells). The offspring are genetically identical to the parent (clones), e.g. binary fission in bacteria and budding in yeast.
[bio5-s1.7.5] Sexual reproduction: two gametes fuse at fertilisation to form a zygote (the first cell of the new organism). The offspring are genetically different from the parents and from each other, which gives variation, e.g. humans and flowering plants.
[bio5-s1.8.b] Organisms get bigger as they develop, and they pass their features on to the next generation. Growth and heredity describe these two processes.
[bio5-s1.8.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass. It happens from within, by making new materials (anabolism, the building reactions of metabolism), by cell division (mitosis) increasing the number of cells, and by cells enlarging and becoming specialised.
[bio5-s1.8.1] Plants keep growing throughout life at regions of dividing cells at the root and shoot tips. Most animals grow to a fixed adult size.
[bio5-s1.8.2] Heredity: the passing of genetic information, carried by genes made of DNA, from parents to offspring, so offspring inherit their parents' characteristics. 'Like produces like', e.g. a cat's offspring are always kittens.
[bio5-s1.8.3] Genes code for proteins, which control an organism's traits (its characteristics). Variation arises from sexual reproduction and from mutations (changes in DNA). There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance.
[bio5-s1.9.b] Here is the full set in one place for quick revision. An organism shows all eleven at some stage of its life.
[bio5-s1.9.0] Organisation: highly ordered structure, from cells up to organ systems.
[bio5-s1.9.1] Response: detecting a stimulus and reacting to it.
[bio5-s1.9.2] Respiration: releasing energy from food inside cells.
[bio5-s1.9.3] Nutrition: obtaining food for energy and raw materials.
[bio5-s1.9.4] Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism.
[bio5-s1.9.5] Reproduction: producing new individuals of the same species.
[bio5-s1.10.b] These are the remaining five characteristics, covering growth, chemistry, stability, inheritance and cells.
[bio5-s1.10.0] Growth: a permanent increase in size and mass.
[bio5-s1.10.1] Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism.
[bio5-s1.10.2] Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment relatively constant.
[bio5-s1.10.3] Heredity: passing genetic information from parents to offspring.
[bio5-s1.10.4] Cellular basis: made of cells, which arise only from pre-existing cells.
