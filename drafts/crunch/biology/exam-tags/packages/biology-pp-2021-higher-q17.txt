CANDIDATES bio19-s1: CNS and PNS
[bio19-s1.0.b] The nervous system detects stimuli (changes in the surroundings or inside the body) and coordinates responses by carrying nerve impulses. To do this it is split into two divisions: one that makes decisions and one that carries messages.
[bio19-s1.0.0] The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and spinal cord, the control centre of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.1] The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is all the nerves outside the CNS, so it reaches every part of the body.
[bio19-s1.0.2] The CNS receives information, processes it and sends out instructions, which is why it is the part that decides what to do.
[bio19-s1.0.3] The PNS carries impulses between the CNS and the rest of the body, so it is the communication line that links the CNS to receptors and effectors.
[bio19-s1.0.4] A neuron is a nerve cell; a nerve is a bundle of many neuron fibres wrapped in connective tissue, so one nerve carries many separate signals at once.
[bio19-s1.0.5] A nerve impulse is an electrochemical signal travelling along a neuron, which is how information moves around the body.
[bio19-s1.1.b] Information has to travel into the CNS and instructions have to travel out again. The PNS does both jobs using two kinds of neuron, and its fibres run through the body bundled in nerves.
[bio19-s1.1.0] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect stimuli) to the CNS, so the CNS learns what is happening.
[bio19-s1.1.1] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors, which are muscles and glands, so the CNS's instructions produce a response such as a movement or a secretion.
[bio19-s1.1.2] Nerve fibres of the PNS run through the body inside nerves, which carry them to almost every tissue.
[bio19-s1.1.3] Humans have 31 pairs of spinal nerves, which leave the spinal cord between the vertebrae (the bones of the backbone).
[bio19-s1.1.4] Each spinal nerve joins the cord by two roots, short branches that attach it to the spinal cord, so sensory and motor fibres enter and leave by separate routes.
[bio19-s1.1.5] The dorsal (back) root carries sensory fibres into the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.1.6] The ventral (front) root carries motor fibres out of the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.b] A neuron's cell body (the part containing the nucleus) can sit in different places depending on the type of neuron. Knowing where each type lies tells you which part of the nervous system it belongs to.
[bio19-s1.2.0] Sensory neuron cell bodies lie in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on the dorsal root just outside the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.2.1] Motor neuron cell bodies lie in the grey matter of the CNS, inside the cord, and their fibres leave through the ventral root.
[bio19-s1.2.2] Interneurons (neurons that link one neuron to another) lie entirely within the CNS, so they never leave the brain or spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.3.b] The spinal cord runs from the base of the brain down the back, inside the vertebral column, which protects it. It is the main route between the brain and the rest of the body.
[bio19-s1.3.0] The spinal cord relays impulses between the body and the brain, so sensory information travels up to the brain and instructions travel back down.
[bio19-s1.3.1] It carries out spinal reflexes, which are fast automatic responses, such as the withdrawal reflex when you touch something painful. There is more on this in The reflex action.
[bio19-s1.4.b] Cut straight across (a transverse section), the spinal cord shows a clear pattern of grey and white tissue around a central canal. The two tissues differ because they are made of different parts of neurons.
[bio19-s1.4.0] A central canal, containing cerebrospinal fluid (a watery fluid that cushions the CNS), lies at the centre of the cord.
[bio19-s1.4.1] Around the canal is an H- or butterfly-shaped core of grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.2] White matter surrounds the grey matter.
[bio19-s1.4.3] Grey matter is made of cell bodies and unmyelinated interneurons (interneurons without a fatty myelin sheath), which is why it looks grey.
[bio19-s1.4.4] White matter is made of myelinated axons (long fibres coated in myelin); it is white because myelin is fatty.
[bio19-s1.4.5] In the brain the arrangement is reversed: the cerebral cortex is grey matter on the outside, with white matter beneath.
[bio19-s1.4.6] Diagram: transverse section of the spinal cord labelling central canal, grey matter, white matter, dorsal root, dorsal root ganglion, ventral root and spinal nerve.
[bio19-s1.5.b] The brain and spinal cord control the whole body, so they need strong protection. The CNS is protected by bone, membranes and fluid.
[bio19-s1.5.0] The brain is protected by the skull (cranium), a rigid bony case.
[bio19-s1.5.1] The spinal cord is protected by the vertebrae, which form a bony tunnel around it.
[bio19-s1.5.2] Three membranes, the meninges, surround the brain and spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.5.3] Cerebrospinal fluid lies between the inner meninges and fills the brain's cavities and the central canal.
[bio19-s1.5.4] Cerebrospinal fluid cushions the CNS against shock, so a knock to the head or back is absorbed, and it supplies the CNS with nutrients.
[bio19-s1.5.5] Meningitis is inflammation of the meninges, usually caused by bacterial or viral infection.
[bio19-s1.6.b] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain and the part that handles most of its higher functions, such as thought and voluntary movement.
[bio19-s1.6.0] The cerebrum is the largest part of the brain, divided into left and right cerebral hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum.
[bio19-s1.6.1] The cerebral cortex, its outer layer, is grey matter deeply folded to increase its surface area, and so the number of neurons.
[bio19-s1.6.2] Each cerebral hemisphere controls the opposite side of the body, so the left hemisphere controls the right side and vice versa.
[bio19-s1.6.3] The cerebrum controls voluntary movement (movement you choose to make) and interprets the senses.
[bio19-s1.6.4] It also governs memory, thought, language, intelligence and personality.
[bio19-s1.7.b] Smooth, accurate movement needs a part of the brain dedicated to coordinating it. That is the cerebellum.
[bio19-s1.7.0] The cerebellum lies at the back of the brain, below the cerebrum, and also has a folded surface.
[bio19-s1.7.1] It coordinates muscular movement and controls balance and posture.
[bio19-s1.8.b] Some vital actions must carry on without you thinking about them, even during sleep. The medulla oblongata controls these.
[bio19-s1.8.0] The medulla oblongata lies at the base of the brain, where it joins the spinal cord.
[bio19-s1.8.1] It controls involuntary actions (those that happen without conscious control), including breathing rate, heart rate and blood pressure.
[bio19-s1.8.2] It controls reflexes such as swallowing, coughing and sneezing.
[bio19-s1.9.b] The hypothalamus is a small region that keeps conditions in the body steady and connects the nervous system to the hormone system.
[bio19-s1.9.0] The hypothalamus lies below the cerebrum and above the pituitary gland, to which it is connected.
[bio19-s1.9.1] It controls homeostasis (keeping the internal environment steady): body temperature, water balance, hunger and thirst. There is more on this in Homeostasis.
[bio19-s1.9.2] It links the nervous and hormonal systems by controlling the pituitary gland, so nerve signals can lead to hormone release.
[bio19-s1.10.b] You may be asked to label the brain, so know where each part sits relative to the others.
[bio19-s1.10.0] Diagram: vertical section of the brain labelling cerebrum, cerebral cortex, corpus callosum, cerebellum, medulla oblongata, hypothalamus and pituitary gland, with the spinal cord continuing below.

CANDIDATES bio19-s2: The reflex action
[bio19-s2.0.b] Every reflex, and every nervous response, is built from the same three parts: something that triggers it, something that detects it and something that acts. A reflex begins with a stimulus that a receptor detects and ends with a response from an effector.
[bio19-s2.0.0] A stimulus is a change in the internal or external environment that can be detected, such as heat on the skin or an object approaching the eye.
[bio19-s2.0.1] A receptor detects the stimulus. It is a cell or nerve ending that is sensitive to that particular type of change, and it starts the impulse.
[bio19-s2.0.2] An effector, a muscle or a gland, carries out the response. A muscle responds by contracting; a gland responds by secreting.
[bio19-s2.1.b] Some situations need a response so quickly that there is no time to think. A reflex action is the body's built-in solution: a response that is fixed and does not wait for a decision.
[bio19-s2.1.0] A reflex action is a rapid, automatic, involuntary response to a stimulus, happening without conscious thought. Involuntary means you do not choose it or control it.
[bio19-s2.1.1] Reflexes protect the body from harm, for example pulling a hand from a hot object or blinking when something approaches the eye, so damage is limited before you even notice.
[bio19-s2.1.2] Reflexes also maintain posture and normal body functions; coughing and sneezing clear the airways, for example.
[bio19-s2.1.3] Reflexes are inborn, so they are not learned. You are born able to do them.
[bio19-s2.1.4] The same stimulus always produces the same response, which is what makes a reflex automatic and predictable.
[bio19-s2.2.b] A reflex works because the nerve pathway involved is short and fixed. The reflex arc is the pathway an impulse (the electrical signal carried by nerve cells) follows in a reflex action.
[bio19-s2.2.0] The pathway runs receptor, sensory neuron, interneuron in the grey matter of the spinal cord, motor neuron, effector. The sensory neuron carries the impulse in, the motor neuron carries it out, and the interneuron connects them.
[bio19-s2.2.1] The sensory neuron enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root (the back root of the spinal nerve); its cell body lies in the dorsal root ganglion, a swelling on that root.
[bio19-s2.2.2] The motor neuron leaves the spinal cord through the ventral root (the front root), carrying the impulse out to the effector.
[bio19-s2.2.3] Diagram: reflex arc drawn through a transverse section of the spinal cord, labelling receptor in the skin, sensory neuron, dorsal root ganglion, interneuron, grey matter, white matter, motor neuron, ventral root and effector muscle, with arrows showing the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s2.3.b] Touching a hot object shows the whole reflex arc at work. Follow the impulse from the finger to the muscle that pulls the hand away.
[bio19-s2.3.0] 1. Pain and heat receptors in the skin of the finger are stimulated by the hot object.
[bio19-s2.3.1] 2. An impulse travels along a sensory neuron to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.3.2] 3. It passes across a synapse (the tiny gap between two neurons) to an interneuron in the grey matter.
[bio19-s2.3.3] 4. It passes across a synapse to a motor neuron.
[bio19-s2.3.4] 5. The motor neuron carries the impulse to the biceps, the effector.
[bio19-s2.3.5] 6. The biceps contracts and the hand is pulled away from the object.
[bio19-s2.4.b] A reflex is faster than a voluntary action because of the route it takes. The brain is not left out, though: it is told what happened a moment later.
[bio19-s2.4.0] The impulse takes a short route through the spinal cord, crossing few synapses. Each synapse adds a small delay, so fewer synapses means a faster response.
[bio19-s2.4.1] It does not wait for a decision by the brain, which is why reflexes are rapid.
[bio19-s2.4.2] Interneurons also carry impulses up the spinal cord to the brain, so pain is felt just after the hand has moved.
[bio19-s2.4.3] The brain can sometimes override a reflex, for example when a person holds on to a hot plate to avoid dropping it.
[bio19-s2.5.b] The knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is the simplest reflex arc, because it leaves out the interneuron. It is a useful contrast with the withdrawal reflex.
[bio19-s2.5.0] Tapping the tendon below the kneecap stretches the thigh muscle, which is the stimulus.
[bio19-s2.5.1] The sensory neuron synapses directly with the motor neuron in the spinal cord, with no interneuron, so the pathway has only one synapse.
[bio19-s2.5.2] The leg kicks. Doctors use this reflex to test the nervous system, because a normal response shows the reflex pathway is working.
[bio19-s2.6.b] Not every reflex passes through the spinal cord. Some are controlled by the brain instead, but they are still rapid, automatic and involuntary.
[bio19-s2.6.0] Some reflexes are controlled by the brain rather than the spinal cord.
[bio19-s2.6.1] Examples are blinking, coughing, sneezing and the pupil narrowing in bright light.

CANDIDATES bio19-s3: Neurons
[bio19-s3.0.b] Your body has to pass information quickly between receptors (cells that detect changes), the brain and spinal cord, and muscles and glands. Neurons are the cells that do this job, and there are three types, each with its own role.
[bio19-s3.0.0] A neuron, or nerve cell, is the basic unit of the nervous system; it carries electrochemical nerve impulses (tiny electrical-and-chemical signals that travel along the cell).
[bio19-s3.0.1] Neurons are long and thin, so a single cell can connect distant parts of the body, for example the skin of the foot and the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.0.2] Sensory neurons carry impulses from receptors (cells that detect a stimulus) to the CNS (the central nervous system: brain and spinal cord).
[bio19-s3.0.3] Motor neurons carry impulses from the CNS to effectors (muscles or glands, which carry out the response).
[bio19-s3.0.4] Interneurons, also called relay or connector neurons, carry impulses between neurons within the CNS, so they link the incoming and outgoing pathways.
[bio19-s3.1.b] Every neuron is built from the same three main regions: a cell body, branched dendrites that receive signals, and an axon that sends them on. The shape of each region suits its job.
[bio19-s3.1.0] The cell body contains the nucleus and most of the cytoplasm and organelles, which keeps the cell alive.
[bio19-s3.1.1] The cell body also makes the neurotransmitter (the chemical that passes the signal to the next cell) and the proteins the neuron needs.
[bio19-s3.1.2] Dendrites are short, branched extensions that receive impulses from other neurons or receptors and carry them towards the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.3] Branching gives dendrites a large surface, so one neuron can receive signals from many others.
[bio19-s3.1.4] The axon is a long single fibre that carries impulses away from the cell body.
[bio19-s3.1.5] The axon's length lets impulses reach distant effectors, for example a muscle in the foot.
[bio19-s3.2.b] Many axons are wrapped in a fatty coat that makes impulses travel faster. Fast conduction matters because the body must respond quickly, for example when you pull your hand from something hot.
[bio19-s3.2.0] The myelin sheath is a fatty insulating layer around many axons, so the impulse does not leak away along the way.
[bio19-s3.2.1] It speeds up the impulse, which jumps between the small gaps in the sheath called nodes of Ranvier.
[bio19-s3.2.2] Schwann cells are cells that wrap around the axon in the PNS (the peripheral nervous system: the nerves outside the brain and spinal cord), their membrane forming the myelin sheath.
[bio19-s3.3.b] At its far end an axon has to hand the impulse on to the next cell, whether another neuron, a muscle or a gland. The axon terminals are built for this hand-over.
[bio19-s3.3.0] The axon ends in swollen synaptic knobs, also called axon terminals.
[bio19-s3.3.1] Neurotransmitter vesicles are tiny sacs in the synaptic knobs that store neurotransmitter, so a supply is ready to be released.
[bio19-s3.3.2] The neurotransmitter is released to pass the impulse to the next cell. There is more on this in Neurotransmission.
[bio19-s3.4.b] A motor neuron carries the command from the CNS out to an effector. Its structure is arranged to collect many inputs inside the CNS and then send one signal a long way out.
[bio19-s3.4.0] A motor neuron has its cell body at one end, inside the CNS, with many dendrites.
[bio19-s3.4.1] Its many dendrites receive input from many interneurons, so one motor neuron can combine signals from many sources.
[bio19-s3.4.2] It has one long axon, which ends in axon terminals on a muscle or gland, so the axon reaches the effector wherever it lies in the body.
[bio19-s3.5.b] A sensory neuron brings information in from a receptor to the CNS. It has a different layout from a motor neuron, with its cell body off to one side.
[bio19-s3.5.0] A sensory neuron has a long fibre, the dendron, which carries impulses from the receptor to the cell body. A dendron is not the same as a dendrite, which is short and branched.
[bio19-s3.5.1] Its cell body sits to one side of the fibre, in a ganglion (a cluster of cell bodies) outside the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.2] A shorter axon carries the impulse on into the CNS.
[bio19-s3.5.3] The long dendron reaches from a receptor, for example in the skin of the foot, to the spinal cord.
[bio19-s3.5.4] The cell body lies off the main pathway, so it does not slow the impulse.
[bio19-s3.6.b] Between the sensory and motor neurons sits a third type that links them. Interneurons make the CNS a network rather than a set of separate wires.
[bio19-s3.6.0] Interneurons are short, with many branched connections, so each can link with many other neurons.
[bio19-s3.6.1] They are found only in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.6.2] They connect sensory neurons to motor neurons and link many neurons together.
[bio19-s3.7.b] You are often asked to label a neuron diagram or to say which type is shown. Practise drawing both types so that you can place every part and show the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.7.0] Diagram: a motor neuron and a sensory neuron, labelling cell body, nucleus, dendrites, dendron (sensory), axon, myelin sheath, Schwann cell, node of Ranvier, axon terminals, neurotransmitter vesicles, and an arrow for the direction of the impulse.
[bio19-s3.8.b] The importance of myelin shows when it is damaged. Multiple sclerosis is a disease in which this happens.
[bio19-s3.8.0] Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disease (the immune system attacks the body's own tissue) in which the body's immune system damages the myelin sheath in the CNS.
[bio19-s3.8.1] Damaged myelin slows or blocks impulses, because the insulation that lets them jump between the nodes of Ranvier is lost.
[bio19-s3.8.2] Symptoms include problems with movement, balance and vision.

CANDIDATES bio21-s3: Nervous vs hormonal coordination
[bio21-s3.0.b] The cells of the body need to act together when something changes, such as a hot surface or a rise in blood sugar. Coordination is the process of linking stimuli (changes in the surroundings or inside the body) with responses so the body works as a whole. Humans have two coordinating systems: the nervous system and the hormonal (endocrine) system.
[bio21-s3.0.0] The nervous system coordinates using electrical impulses carried along nerve cells (neurons), so it can act within a fraction of a second.
[bio21-s3.0.1] The hormonal system coordinates using chemical messengers called hormones, which are released into the blood and carried around the body.
[bio21-s3.0.2] Both systems link a stimulus to a response, so the body works as a whole rather than as separate cells acting alone.
[bio21-s3.1.b] The two systems do the same job in different ways. The differences come down to what carries the message and where it goes, and from these follow how fast and how long the effect lasts. Each point below is a pair: nervous first, then hormonal.
[bio21-s3.1.0] Nature of the message: nervous coordination uses electrical impulses along neurons, with chemical neurotransmitters (chemicals that pass the message across the gap between neurons) at synapses; hormonal coordination uses chemical hormones.
[bio21-s3.1.1] Route: nervous messages travel along neurons directly to particular cells; hormones travel in the blood to all parts of the body, because the blood reaches every organ.
[bio21-s3.1.2] Speed: nervous coordination is very fast, with impulses arriving in a fraction of a second; hormonal coordination is slower, taking seconds to hours, because hormones must travel in the blood.
[bio21-s3.1.3] Duration: nervous effects are short-lived and end when the impulses stop; hormonal effects are longer-lasting because hormones stay in the blood until they are broken down, mainly in the liver.
[bio21-s3.1.4] Area affected: nervous effects are localised to the muscle or gland the neuron reaches; hormonal effects are widespread, reaching every target cell with receptors for the hormone, possibly in many organs.
[bio21-s3.1.5] Effectors (the parts that carry out the response): nervous coordination acts on muscles and glands; hormonal coordination acts on target cells in organs and tissues.
[bio21-s3.1.6] Typical uses: nervous coordination suits rapid responses such as reflexes and movement; hormonal coordination suits long-term processes such as growth, metabolism, puberty and the menstrual cycle, and steady control of blood glucose.
[bio21-s3.2.b] Despite their differences, the two systems share the same basic design. Both pass information to cells using chemicals, and both keep conditions in the body steady.
[bio21-s3.2.0] Both use chemical messengers that bind to specific receptors (molecules that fit the messenger, like a lock and key) on target cells: neurotransmitters in the nervous system and hormones in the hormonal system.
[bio21-s3.2.1] Both carry information from receptors (which detect a change) or control centres (which decide what to do) to effectors (which carry out the response).
[bio21-s3.2.2] Both take part in homeostasis (keeping the internal environment steady) through negative feedback, in which a change triggers a response that reverses that change.
[bio21-s3.3.b] The two systems are not rivals. They are linked, and many responses use both: the nervous system acts first and fast, and the hormonal system follows with a longer-lasting effect.
[bio21-s3.3.0] The hypothalamus links the two systems: it is part of the brain, which is nervous tissue, but it controls the pituitary gland, which releases hormones.
[bio21-s3.3.1] In danger, nerve impulses stimulate the adrenal glands to release adrenaline, so a fast nervous response is followed by a longer-lasting hormonal one.
[bio21-s3.3.2] Touching a hot plate is nervous coordination: it is a reflex, takes under a second, and affects one arm.
[bio21-s3.3.3] Rising blood glucose is hormonal coordination: the hormone insulin is released, it takes minutes to hours, and it affects cells throughout the body.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio25-s2: Osmosis in food preservation and plant health
[bio25-s2.0.b] Food goes off because microorganisms feed on it, and microorganisms need water to live. Adding a lot of salt or sugar turns osmosis against them: it pulls water out of their cells.
[bio25-s2.0.0] Food spoils mainly because bacteria and fungi (moulds and yeasts) grow on it, digesting it and making it unsafe or unpleasant to eat. Like all cells, they need water for their enzymes to work and to grow and reproduce.
[bio25-s2.0.1] Mechanism, step 1: a high concentration of salt or sugar is added to the food.
[bio25-s2.0.2] 2. The food's water is now a concentrated (hypertonic) solution compared with the cytoplasm (the watery contents) of any microorganism on it.
[bio25-s2.0.3] 3. Water leaves the microbial cells by osmosis, moving from the more dilute cytoplasm to the more concentrated solution outside.
[bio25-s2.0.4] 4. The cells are dehydrated and plasmolysed (the cell membrane shrinks away from the cell wall; bacteria and fungi have cell walls), so their enzymes can't work and they can't grow or reproduce; many die.
[bio25-s2.0.5] The salt or sugar also binds water in the food, so less free water is available to any microbe.
[bio25-s2.0.6] Preserving by drying (e.g. dried fruit and dried fish) works by removing water directly, which has the same effect on microbes. It is related to osmosis but is not osmosis itself.
[bio25-s2.1.b] Three traditional methods preserve food with salt, sugar or acid. Salt and sugar work by osmosis, but acid works in a different way, by its low pH.
[bio25-s2.1.0] Salting: meat and fish are rubbed with dry salt or soaked in strong brine (salt solution), e.g. bacon, ham and salted fish. Salt also draws water out of the food itself by osmosis, drying it further, so even less water is left for microbes.
[bio25-s2.1.1] Sugaring: fruit is preserved in a high concentration of sugar, e.g. jam, marmalade and candied fruit; fruit is also stored in heavy syrup.
[bio25-s2.1.2] Honey keeps for a very long time because its sugar concentration is so high that microbes lose water to it instead of growing.
[bio25-s2.1.3] Pickling: vegetables kept in vinegar are preserved mainly by the low pH of the acid (ethanoic acid), which stops enzymes and microbes working, not by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.1.4] Brine pickles (salt solutions) combine osmosis with acid made by fermentation (microbes breaking down sugars in the vegetable), so both effects protect the food.
[bio25-s2.2.b] Osmotic preservation is useful but not perfect. To evaluate it, weigh what it does well against where it fails and what it does to our health.
[bio25-s2.2.0] Advantages: it is cheap, simple and traditional; it needs no refrigeration, so it was used for long journeys and winter stores; and it works against both bacteria and fungi.
[bio25-s2.2.1] Limits: some microorganisms tolerate high salt or sugar concentrations, especially moulds and yeasts, so jam can still grow mould on its surface once opened and should be refrigerated.
[bio25-s2.2.2] Preservation fails if the salt or sugar concentration is too low, because there is then not enough of a concentration difference to draw water out of microbes. For example, reduced-sugar jams spoil faster.
[bio25-s2.2.3] Osmotic preservation slows microbial growth but doesn't sterilise the food (kill all microbes). It is usually combined with other methods, such as cooking, sealing, refrigeration, smoking and acids.
[bio25-s2.2.4] Health link: salted foods add much salt to the diet, and high salt intake is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.
[bio25-s2.2.5] Sugary preserves add sugar, which is linked to tooth decay and obesity. So salting and sugaring are used less today, and refrigeration and freezing more. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio25-s2.2.6] Osmosis also affects food preparation: salting sliced vegetables, e.g. cucumber and aubergine, draws water out of them.
[bio25-s2.2.7] Limp vegetables, e.g. celery and lettuce, become crisp again when placed in cold water, because their cells take in water by osmosis and become turgid (swollen and firm with water).
[bio25-s2.3.b] Plant cells stay firm because they are filled with water taken in by osmosis, and this water pressure does several jobs for the plant.
[bio25-s2.3.0] Healthy plants keep their cells turgid (full of water and pushing outwards) by taking in water by osmosis into the vacuole (the large sap-filled space) of each cell.
[bio25-s2.3.1] The turgor pressure of cells pressing against their walls supports non-woody plants, leaves and flowers, holding them upright and spreading leaves to the light for photosynthesis.
[bio25-s2.3.2] Turgor pressure drives cell enlargement: young cells expand as water enters their vacuoles, so plant growth depends on water uptake by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.3.3] Guard cells (the pair of cells around each stoma, the tiny pore in a leaf) open the stomata when they become turgid and close them when they become flaccid (soft and limp from losing water), controlling gas exchange and water loss. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.3.4] Wilting: when water is lost from the leaves by transpiration (evaporation from the leaf) faster than the roots take it up, the cells lose water, become flaccid, and the plant wilts.
[bio25-s2.3.5] Wilting closes the stomata, so less carbon dioxide gets in and it reduces photosynthesis and growth. It is reversed if water is supplied soon enough; prolonged wilting kills the plant.
[bio25-s2.4.b] Roots rely on the soil water being more dilute than their own cells. If that balance flips, osmosis runs the wrong way and the plant loses water even though the soil is wet.
[bio25-s2.4.0] Roots take in water by osmosis because the soil water is normally more dilute than the cell sap (the solution in the vacuole) of the root cells. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.4.1] If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the contents of the root cells, water stops entering the roots and can leave them by osmosis. The root cells become plasmolysed, the plant cannot take up enough water, and it wilts and may die, even in moist soil.
[bio25-s2.4.2] Causes of salty soil water: flooding of coastal land by seawater (e.g. storm surges), and salt spread on roads in icy weather, which damages roadside trees and hedges.
[bio25-s2.4.3] Irrigation (watering crops artificially) in hot, dry regions is another cause: water evaporates and leaves salts behind in the soil (salinisation), lowering crop yields.
[bio25-s2.4.4] Fertiliser burn: applying too much fertiliser, especially concentrated fertiliser close to roots or on dry soil, makes the soil solution too concentrated, so water leaves the roots and the leaves turn brown and scorched.
[bio25-s2.4.5] Advice to avoid fertiliser burn: apply the recommended amount, spread it evenly and water it in. Salt was used in the past as a weedkiller for the same reason.
[bio25-s2.4.6] Some plants of salty habitats, such as salt marshes and seashores, survive because their root cells hold a higher solute concentration than the salty soil water, so water still enters by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.4.7] Sustainability link: soil salinisation from irrigation, and seawater flooding linked to rising sea levels and stronger storms, reduce the land available for crops. Careful irrigation and drainage reduce salinisation.

CANDIDATES bio31-s7: Energy and nutrient transfer
[bio31-s7.0.b] Every ecosystem sorts its organisms by how they get energy and nutrients. Producers bring energy in, consumers pass it along, and decomposers return the nutrients to the soil or water.
[bio31-s7.0.0] Producers are autotrophs: organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly by photosynthesis, using light energy. They include green plants, algae, phytoplankton (microscopic floating algae) and photosynthetic bacteria.
[bio31-s7.0.1] Producers are the entry point of energy and matter into every food chain, because no other group can turn light energy and inorganic substances into organic food. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio31-s7.0.2] Consumers are heterotrophs, meaning they cannot make their own food and obtain energy and nutrients by eating other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers, e.g. rabbits, slugs, caterpillars and limpets.
[bio31-s7.0.3] Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, e.g. thrushes, frogs and crabs. Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers, e.g. sparrowhawks, foxes and herons.
[bio31-s7.0.4] A top (apex) consumer is at the end of a chain and is not normally eaten. Carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat plants and animals, e.g. badgers, foxes and humans.
[bio31-s7.0.5] Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, feed on dead organisms and waste. They secrete enzymes onto the dead matter and absorb the products (saprophytic nutrition), and so release minerals and CO₂ for producers to reuse.
[bio31-s7.0.6] Detritivores are animals that eat dead organic matter (detritus), e.g. earthworms and woodlice. They break it into smaller pieces, which gives decomposers a larger surface to act on, so decomposition is faster. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio31-s7.1.b] A food chain shows who eats whom, and each step is a trophic (feeding) level. Following the chain shows how energy and nutrients are passed on from one organism to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.0] A trophic (feeding) level is the position an organism occupies in a food chain, according to how it obtains its energy. Producers are trophic level 1, primary consumers level 2, secondary consumers level 3 and tertiary consumers level 4.
[bio31-s7.1.1] A food chain is a sequence of organisms in which each is eaten by the next, showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one trophic level to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.2] Arrows point from the organism that is eaten towards the organism that eats it, which is the direction of energy flow ("is eaten by").
[bio31-s7.1.3] Irish examples, one per ecosystem. Grassland: grass → rabbit → fox. Hedgerow or woodland: oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk. Rocky shore: seaweed → periwinkle → crab → herring gull.
[bio31-s7.1.4] Two more Irish examples. Freshwater: phytoplankton → water flea → minnow → heron (or pike). Sea: phytoplankton → zooplankton → herring → seal.
[bio31-s7.1.5] Grazing food chains start with living producers. Detritus (decomposer) food chains start with dead organic matter, e.g. dead leaves → earthworm → blackbird. Much of the energy in a woodland passes through detritus chains.
[bio31-s7.1.6] Food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels, because so much energy is lost at each level that too little is left to support a further level.
[bio31-s7.2.b] Real organisms rarely eat just one thing or get eaten by just one thing. A food web joins the separate food chains of an ecosystem into one network, which is why it is a more realistic picture.
[bio31-s7.2.0] A food web is a set of interlinked food chains in an ecosystem. It is more realistic than a single chain because most organisms eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator.
[bio31-s7.2.1] An organism may also feed at more than one trophic level, as an omnivore does, or as a fox does when it eats rabbits and also beetles.
[bio31-s7.2.2] Example grassland and hedgerow web to draw: grass → rabbit, grass → slug and grass → caterpillar; grass seeds → wood mouse; slug → thrush and caterpillar → thrush; rabbit → fox; wood mouse → fox and wood mouse → barn owl; thrush → sparrowhawk.
[bio31-s7.2.3] Decomposers (bacteria, fungi and earthworms) feed on dead material from every level of this web, because every organism eventually dies or produces waste.
[bio31-s7.2.4] Food webs show interdependence: a change in one population affects many others, directly through feeding and indirectly through competition for the same food. For example, the fox and the barn owl both eat wood mice, so they compete.
[bio31-s7.2.5] Diagram: the food web drawn with arrows pointing towards the consumer, organisms placed in rows by trophic level, and decomposers shown at the side receiving arrows from all levels.
[bio31-s7.3.b] Energy enters an ecosystem once, as sunlight, and then dwindles as it is passed along a food chain. Most of it is lost at every step, and this explains both the shape of food chains and how we should feed people.
[bio31-s7.3.0] Energy enters ecosystems as light. Producers capture only a small fraction of the light that reaches them, around 1–2%, and store it as chemical energy in organic molecules. The rest is reflected, passes through leaves, or is lost as heat.
[bio31-s7.3.1] On average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to, and stored in, the next. The figure varies, roughly from 5% to 20%, so about 90% is lost at each transfer.
[bio31-s7.3.2] Respiration releases energy for movement, growth, active transport and keeping warm, and this ends up as heat lost to the surroundings. Birds and mammals lose the most, because they keep a constant body temperature.
[bio31-s7.3.3] Roots, bark, wood, bones and fur are left uneaten. Undigested food is egested as faeces, e.g. cellulose eaten by herbivores, and some energy is lost in excretory products such as urea. The energy in uneaten parts, faeces and dead bodies passes to decomposers, not to the next level.
[bio31-s7.3.4] Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuous input of energy from the Sun.
[bio31-s7.3.5] Worked example (illustrative, using the 10% figure, not measured data): grass in a field stores 10 000 kJ, the rabbits that eat it store about 1 000 kJ, and the foxes about 100 kJ. A fourth level would store about 10 kJ, too little to support a population.
[bio31-s7.3.6] So there are fewer top predators, they often need large territories, and they are especially vulnerable to habitat loss.
[bio31-s7.3.7] Consequence for food production: eating plants directly, a shorter chain, uses the energy captured by crops more efficiently than feeding crops to animals and then eating the animals. A given area of land can therefore feed more people on plant-based food.
[bio31-s7.4.b] Matter, unlike energy, passes along a food chain in the form of the food organisms eat. Some is lost at each level, but the ecosystem as a whole does not lose it, because decomposers send it back to producers.
[bio31-s7.4.0] Nutrients (matter) pass between trophic levels when one organism eats another. The carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids of the food are digested, absorbed and built into the consumer's own body (assimilated).
[bio31-s7.4.1] The elements involved are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (in all biomolecules), nitrogen (in proteins and nucleic acids), phosphorus (in DNA, ATP and phospholipids), and minerals such as calcium and iron.
[bio31-s7.4.2] The amount of matter passed on also decreases at each level. Much of the food eaten is respired (carbon leaves as CO₂, hydrogen as water), egested in faeces, or excreted (nitrogen as urea or ammonia). So biomass (the mass of living material) also decreases up a food chain.
[bio31-s7.4.3] Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the ecosystem. Decomposers break down dead bodies, faeces and urine, releasing CO₂ and minerals such as nitrate, phosphate and ammonium that producers absorb and reuse, so nutrients cycle.
[bio31-s7.4.4] Contrast: energy flows one way through the ecosystem and is lost as heat, whereas nutrients cycle and are reused again and again.
[bio31-s7.4.5] Persistent substances that are not broken down or excreted, such as the insecticide DDT and mercury, pass up the food chain with the food and build up in the bodies of organisms at each level. They reach the highest concentrations in top consumers, which is called biomagnification.
[bio31-s7.4.6] Biomagnification is how DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey, which sit at the top of food chains and so carry the highest doses.
[bio31-s7.4.7] Diagram: a flow diagram of one food chain, with energy arrows getting narrower at each level, heat-loss arrows from every level, and a separate loop of nutrient arrows from all levels to decomposers and back to producers.
[bio31-s7.5.b] A food chain or web is a model: a simplified picture that lets you work out what happens when one population goes up or down. Following a set method keeps the prediction logical.
[bio31-s7.5.0] The method has five steps. 1. Find the organisms that eat the changed species (its predators).
[bio31-s7.5.1] 2. Find what the changed species eats (its food).
[bio31-s7.5.2] 3. Find its competitors, the species that share its food.
[bio31-s7.5.3] 4. Predict the direction of each change, giving reasons.
[bio31-s7.5.4] 5. Note other effects, e.g. on plants and habitat.
[bio31-s7.5.5] Worked prediction using the food web above, if a disease such as myxomatosis (a viral disease of rabbits) kills most of the rabbits: (a) grass is grazed less and grows taller, providing more food and cover for slugs, caterpillars and wood mice.
[bio31-s7.5.6] Continued: (b) foxes lose their main prey, so they eat more wood mice and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds, and fox numbers may fall. (c) Wood mice decline from the extra fox predation, so barn owls, which compete with foxes for wood mice, have less food.
[bio31-s7.5.7] Real outcomes depend on how flexible each predator's diet is, because a predator that can switch to other food will be affected less.
[bio31-s7.6.b] Changes spread through a web both when a species is removed and when one is added. Because the model is simplified, its predictions have limits.
[bio31-s7.6.0] Removing a top predator can cause a trophic cascade, a chain of effects passing down the food web. In Ireland, with no wolves, deer numbers increase, they browse young trees, and the woodland cannot regenerate, which affects every species that depends on the woodland.
[bio31-s7.6.1] Adding a species also spreads through a web. An invasive filter-feeder such as the zebra mussel removes phytoplankton at the base of a lake food web, reducing food for zooplankton and for the fish that eat them. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio31-s7.6.2] Food chains and webs simplify real feeding, because many links are left out and diets change with the season and with age.
[bio31-s7.6.3] They usually show no numbers or amounts of energy, which pyramids do, and decomposers are often left out. There is more on models in general in Models.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio34-s1: The carbon cycle
[bio34-s1.0.b] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the elements living things are built from have to be reused. Carbon is the clearest example: it moves round and round between organisms and their surroundings.
[bio34-s1.0.0] A biogeochemical cycle, or nutrient cycle, is the continuous movement of a chemical element between living things (bio) and the non-living environment (geo): the air, water, rocks and soil.
[bio34-s1.0.1] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the same atoms are used again and again. Energy, by contrast, flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.0.2] Carbon is the element at the centre of every biomolecule (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids; see Biomolecules and metabolism), so every organism needs a constant supply. Producers (organisms that make their own food, such as plants) take it in as CO₂; consumers get it already built into food.
[bio34-s1.0.3] A store (reservoir) is a place where carbon is held: the atmosphere (as CO₂, and a little methane, CH₄); living organisms (biomass, the mass of living material); dead organic matter in soil; the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate); rocks such as limestone and chalk (calcium carbonate); and fossil fuels.
[bio34-s1.0.4] A process, or flux, is whatever moves carbon from one store to another. Stores are the boxes of the cycle and processes are the arrows between them.
[bio34-s1.0.5] CO₂ makes up only about 0.04% of the air (over 420 parts per million), yet it is the source of carbon for almost all life on Earth, because photosynthesis starts from it.
[bio34-s1.1.b] Carbon dioxide leaves the air in only a few ways. By far the most important is photosynthesis, but dissolving in water and shell building also lock carbon away.
[bio34-s1.1.0] Photosynthesis by plants, algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating algae) fixes CO₂ into glucose and then into the rest of their biomass, so they act as carbon sinks (stores that take in more carbon than they release).
[bio34-s1.1.1] Photosynthesis is the only major route by which carbon enters living things, so nearly all the carbon in a food chain started as CO₂ taken in by a producer.
[bio34-s1.1.2] Dissolving: CO₂ dissolves in the surface of the oceans, lakes and rivers, and aquatic producers take it up for photosynthesis.
[bio34-s1.1.3] Shell and skeleton building: many marine organisms (e.g. corals, molluscs and some phytoplankton) combine dissolved carbonate with calcium to make calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.
[bio34-s1.1.4] When these organisms die, their shells and skeletons settle on the sea floor and, over millions of years, form limestone and chalk. These rocks are very long-term stores because the carbon stays locked in them for so long.
[bio34-s1.2.b] Once carbon is in a producer it does not stay there. It passes along the food chain and into the dead matter that every organism leaves behind.
[bio34-s1.2.0] Feeding: carbon in the producer's biomass passes to herbivores and then carnivores when they eat and digest it. At each step most of it is respired away (broken down to release energy, giving off CO₂) rather than built into the consumer's body.
[bio34-s1.2.1] Each step in a food chain is a trophic level (a feeding level). There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.2.2] Egestion and excretion: carbon leaves animals in faeces and in wastes such as urea, and enters the dead organic matter store, as do shed leaves and dead organisms. This store is the food supply for decomposers.
[bio34-s1.3.b] Carbon dioxide is put back into the air by respiration, by decomposers, by burning and by geological release. If it were not returned, producers would run out of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.3.0] Respiration by plants, animals and microorganisms breaks down glucose and releases CO₂. Details are in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio34-s1.3.1] Plants respire all the time, day and night, so the respiration arrow must start from plants as well as animals. Over a growing season photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the plant as a whole gains carbon.
[bio34-s1.3.2] Combustion: burning biomass (wood, peat, forest fires) or fossil fuels combines the carbon with oxygen and releases it as CO₂ very quickly.
[bio34-s1.3.3] Natural geological release: volcanoes release CO₂ from rocks, and the weathering of limestone releases some. This is small compared with the biological fluxes and with fossil-fuel burning.
[bio34-s1.4.b] Dead organisms, faeces and fallen leaves contain a lot of carbon. Decomposers are the living things that break this matter down and return its carbon to the air, so the cycle can keep turning.
[bio34-s1.4.0] Decomposers are bacteria and fungi. They feed saprophytically (on dead organic matter): they secrete enzymes that digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products, and respire them, releasing CO₂ and minerals.
[bio34-s1.4.1] Say that decomposers digest the matter and then respire the products. The CO₂ comes from their respiration, not from the matter breaking down into CO₂ directly.
[bio34-s1.4.2] Without decomposers, carbon would stay locked in dead matter and the CO₂ supply for photosynthesis would run out. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi, and soil microbiomes are covered in The microbiome.
[bio34-s1.4.3] Decomposers work fastest when it is warm, moist and well aerated, because they respire aerobically (using oxygen).
[bio34-s1.4.4] In cold, waterlogged, acidic or oxygen-poor places decomposition is very slow, so dead matter builds up, e.g. as peat in bogs.
[bio34-s1.4.5] Anaerobic decomposition: where there is no oxygen (waterlogged soils, bogs, rice paddies, landfill, slurry tanks and the stomachs of cattle and sheep), some microorganisms break organic matter down to methane (CH₄) instead of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.4.6] The methane-producing microorganisms are archaea, a separate group of single-celled organisms from bacteria (see Domains of life). Methane in the air is slowly converted to CO₂.
[bio34-s1.5.b] Fossil fuels are a store of carbon that the natural cycle leaves almost untouched. People now burn them, which is why they matter so much to the carbon cycle.
[bio34-s1.5.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) are the remains of organisms that lived millions of years ago and were buried before they could decompose fully.
[bio34-s1.5.1] Coal formed mainly from land plants in ancient swamps. Oil and natural gas formed mainly from tiny marine organisms (plankton) buried in sea-floor mud. Heat and pressure over millions of years changed them into fuels.
[bio34-s1.5.2] The carbon in fossil fuels was fixed by photosynthesis long ago, so they store ancient solar energy and carbon. Peat is an early stage of the same process, which is why it is sometimes counted with them.
[bio34-s1.5.3] In the natural cycle almost no carbon leaves this store.
[bio34-s1.5.4] Burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution moves carbon locked away for millions of years into the atmosphere in a few centuries, far faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can take it back, so atmospheric CO₂ rises. Consequences are in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.b] Whether a store helps to lower or raise atmospheric CO₂ depends on whether carbon is flowing in or out. Photosynthesis shows how plants act as sinks; here the sink and source distinction is applied to every store and to the overall balance of the cycle.
[bio34-s1.6.0] A store acts as a sink when its inputs exceed its outputs, so it gains carbon, and as a source when its outputs exceed its inputs, so it releases carbon.
[bio34-s1.6.1] The same store can switch between the two: a growing forest is a sink; a mature forest roughly balances; a burning or felled forest is a source. A healthy wet bog is a sink; a drained or burning bog is a source.
[bio34-s1.6.2] The main natural sinks on a carbon-cycle diagram are land plants (forests and grassland, with the soil carbon under them); algae and phytoplankton in the oceans (with dissolving CO₂ and shells sinking to the sea floor); bogs and other wetlands; and limestone and fossil fuels as very long-term stores.
[bio34-s1.6.3] These sinks are compared, and the strategies that use them are described, in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.4] Before large-scale fossil-fuel burning, the CO₂ taken up by photosynthesis and the oceans each year was roughly balanced by the CO₂ released by respiration, decomposition and natural fires. Atmospheric CO₂ therefore stayed nearly constant, at about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution.
[bio34-s1.6.5] Deforestation and fossil-fuel burning have tipped this balance, so more CO₂ is now added than removed.
[bio34-s1.6.6] Seasonal pattern: atmospheric CO₂ falls each northern summer, when photosynthesis exceeds respiration over the large land area of the northern hemisphere, and rises each winter, when respiration and decomposition exceed photosynthesis. This happens on top of the long-term rise.
[bio34-s1.7.b] A model represents the cycle in a diagram and in words, and is used to explain and predict. It is only as good as its limits allow.
[bio34-s1.7.0] Diagram: draw boxes for the stores: carbon dioxide in the air; green plants and algae; animals; dead organisms and wastes; decomposers (bacteria and fungi); fossil fuels; oceans.
[bio34-s1.7.1] Diagram arrows, each labelled with its process: photosynthesis (air → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (plants and animals → dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → decomposers); respiration (plants, animals and decomposers → air).
[bio34-s1.7.2] Diagram arrows, continued: fossilisation (dead matter → fossil fuels, over millions of years); combustion (fossil fuels and wood → air); dissolving (air ↔ oceans).
[bio34-s1.7.3] Prediction (a): cutting down and burning a forest removes photosynthesis and adds combustion and the decomposition of the remains, so atmospheric CO₂ rises.
[bio34-s1.7.4] Prediction (b): draining a bog lets air in, so aerobic decomposers break down the peat and release CO₂.
[bio34-s1.7.5] Prediction (c): if decomposers were killed (e.g. by soil made very acidic or polluted), dead matter would accumulate, minerals would not be returned to the soil, and plant growth would eventually fall.
[bio34-s1.7.6] Limitation: a diagram shows the processes but not how much carbon moves or how fast. It also treats each store as one box, although the oceans, soils and forests each hold carbon for very different lengths of time.
[bio34-s1.7.7] Limitation: it simplifies feedbacks, e.g. warming speeds up decomposition, which releases more CO₂. It is also a snapshot that hides seasonal and long-term change. There is more on this in Models.

CANDIDATES bio6-s3: Classification
[bio6-s3.0.b] There are so many kinds of living thing that biologists sort them into groups, the way a library sorts books into sections, shelves and titles. The groups sit inside one another in a fixed set of levels.
[bio6-s3.0.0] Classification is the grouping of organisms according to their shared characteristics. Taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
[bio6-s3.0.1] Organisms are placed in a hierarchy of ranks (taxa, the levels of grouping), from broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
[bio6-s3.0.2] Moving down the ranks, each group has fewer members, which share more features and are more closely related. This is why two species in the same genus are far more alike than two in the same kingdom.
[bio6-s3.0.3] Worked example, human: domain Eukarya, kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Primates, family Hominidae, genus Homo, species Homo sapiens.
[bio6-s3.1.b] The species is the smallest rank, so it needs a clear definition and a universal name. Every species gets a two-part scientific name, which is called binomial nomenclature.
[bio6-s3.1.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (young that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, a mule, is infertile, so they are different species.
[bio6-s3.1.1] Limitation of the species definition: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually (e.g. bacteria), because they do not interbreed at all, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding.
[bio6-s3.1.2] Binomial nomenclature: every species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name (capital first letter) followed by the species name (lower case), printed in italics or underlined when handwritten.
[bio6-s3.1.3] Examples of binomial names: Homo sapiens (human), Escherichia coli (a gut bacterium), Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould).
[bio6-s3.1.4] The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature; his Species Plantarum (1753) used it for all plants.
[bio6-s3.1.5] Species in the same genus are closely related, which is why they share the first part of their name. The lion (Panthera leo) and tiger (Panthera tigris) share the genus Panthera.
[bio6-s3.2.b] Before sorting living things into kingdoms, biologists ask one basic question about their cells: is there a nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) or not? The answer splits cellular life into two big groups.
[bio6-s3.2.0] The first division of cellular life is by cell type. Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (the small working parts of a cell). Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, and membrane-bound organelles. There is more on this in Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
[bio6-s3.2.1] Two kingdoms are prokaryotic, Bacteria and Archaea, each forming its own domain. Four kingdoms are eukaryotic, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, all in the domain Eukarya.
[bio6-s3.2.2] The distinction became clear only once the electron microscope showed cell interiors, because the details inside prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are too small to see with a light microscope.
[bio6-s3.3.b] Both prokaryotic kingdoms are single-celled with no nucleus, so cell structure alone does not separate them. One way of telling them apart is the chemistry of their cell walls, and another is rRNA. Each is described by cell type, number of cells, cell wall, nutrition and examples.
[bio6-s3.3.0] Bacteria: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls of peptidoglycan (a mesh of sugars and amino acids). They reproduce by binary fission (one cell splits into two).
[bio6-s3.3.1] Bacterial nutrition varies. Bacteria are autotrophic (make their own food), either photosynthetic or chemosynthetic, or heterotrophic (take in food made by others), either saprophytic (feeding on dead matter) or parasitic (feeding on a living host).
[bio6-s3.3.2] Bacteria examples: Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus (used to make yoghurt) and Streptococcus.
[bio6-s3.3.3] Archaea: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls that have no peptidoglycan. This difference in cell wall is one way of telling them apart from bacteria. Many live in extreme environments.
[bio6-s3.3.4] Archaea examples: methanogens (methane-producing archaea) in cattle digestive systems, and Halobacterium in salt lakes.
[bio6-s3.4.b] The four eukaryotic kingdoms all have a nucleus. They are separated by how many cells they have, what their cell walls are made of, how they get food and how they store carbohydrate.
[bio6-s3.4.0] Protista: eukaryotic and mostly unicellular, though some are multicellular algae such as seaweeds (kelp). They have no tissues (groups of similar cells doing one job) of the kind found in plants and animals. Some are photosynthetic (algae) and some are heterotrophic.
[bio6-s3.4.1] Protista includes eukaryotes that do not belong in the other three eukaryotic kingdoms. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (causes malaria) and Phytophthora infestans (causes potato blight, which caused the Great Famine in Ireland, 1845–1849).
[bio6-s3.4.2] Fungi: eukaryotic and mostly multicellular, made of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium (a tangled mass of threads), though yeasts are unicellular. They have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise, and their cell walls are made of chitin.
[bio6-s3.4.3] Fungi are heterotrophic: they secrete enzymes onto food and absorb the digested products, living as saprophytes or parasites. They store carbohydrate as glycogen and reproduce by spores. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), mushrooms and Penicillium.
[bio6-s3.4.4] Plantae: eukaryotic and multicellular, with cell walls of cellulose and chloroplasts (where photosynthesis happens). They are autotrophic by photosynthesis, store carbohydrate as starch, and mostly do not move. Examples: mosses, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants such as the daisy and oak.
[bio6-s3.4.5] Animalia: eukaryotic and multicellular, with no cell walls. They are heterotrophic, taking in (ingesting) food, and store carbohydrate as glycogen. Most can move and have nerves and muscles. Examples: sponges, earthworms, insects, fish and mammals.
[bio6-s3.5.b] Classification by kingdom and domain only works for things made of cells. Viruses fall outside it.
[bio6-s3.5.0] Viruses are not in any kingdom or domain, because they are not cells. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio6-s3.6.b] Grouping by appearance can mislead, so modern classification also asks which organisms share a recent ancestor. This evolutionary family-tree approach is called phylogeny.
[bio6-s3.6.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms. Phylogenetic classification groups organisms by how closely related they are by descent from common ancestors, rather than by appearance alone.
[bio6-s3.6.1] A phylogenetic tree shows these relationships. Each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor, and the closer two species' branches join, the more recent their common ancestor and the more closely related they are.
[bio6-s3.6.2] Species in the same genus share a very recent common ancestor; genera in the same family share an older one. This is how the ranks of the hierarchy reflect evolutionary history.
[bio6-s3.6.3] Example: the lion and tiger (genus Panthera) are more closely related to each other than either is to the domestic cat (Felis catus), but all three are in the cat family (Felidae).
[bio6-s3.6.4] The dog (Canis familiaris, also written Canis lupus familiaris) is in a different family, Canidae, and all four animals are in the order Carnivora.
[bio6-s3.6.5] Evidence used to work out phylogeny includes shared anatomical structures with the same basic plan, e.g. the five-digit limb of mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians; embryology (the study of early development); and fossils. There is more on this in Evolution: The Origins of Life.
[bio6-s3.6.6] The most powerful evidence is comparison of DNA base sequences and protein amino-acid sequences: the fewer the differences, the more closely related the organisms, because less time has passed for differences to build up.
[bio6-s3.6.7] Appearance can mislead. Whales and dolphins look like fish but are mammals: they breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young on milk.
[bio6-s3.6.8] Diagram: a phylogenetic tree for lion, tiger, domestic cat and dog, with the nodes for genus Panthera, family Felidae and order Carnivora labelled.
[bio6-s3.7.b] Classification is not fixed. Each time scientists gained a new tool for looking at living things, the groupings were redrawn, which is why you will meet different numbers of kingdoms in the history of the subject.
[bio6-s3.7.0] Early systems had two kingdoms, plants and animals. Linnaeus, in the 18th century, classified organisms by visible features.
[bio6-s3.7.1] Microscopes revealed single-celled organisms that fitted neither kingdom. In 1866 Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista.
[bio6-s3.7.2] In 1969 Robert Whittaker proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. Monera held all the prokaryotes.
[bio6-s3.7.3] Fungi were separated from plants in Whittaker's system because they have chitin walls, no chlorophyll and absorb their food.
[bio6-s3.7.4] rRNA sequencing (reading the sequence of a molecule found in ribosomes) by Carl Woese showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups. This gave three domains (1990).
[bio6-s3.7.5] The six kingdoms used in this course followed, with Monera split into Bacteria and Archaea. There is more on this in Domains of life.
[bio6-s3.7.6] Systems change because new technology provides new evidence, e.g. the light microscope, the electron microscope, biochemistry and DNA sequencing. Classification is revised to reflect evolutionary relationships more accurately. It is a model that scientists keep testing and improving.
[bio6-s3.8.b] DNA sequencing, reading the order of bases in an organism's DNA, is now the strongest tool for classification. It is still moving organisms between groups and adding new ones.
[bio6-s3.8.0] DNA sequencing and whole-genome comparisons are now the main evidence for classification, and have moved many organisms. Phytophthora (potato blight) was long classed as a fungus because it grows as threads. Its DNA and its cellulose cell walls show that it belongs with the protists (water moulds).
[bio6-s3.8.1] DNA evidence placed the giant panda in the bear family.
[bio6-s3.8.2] Environmental DNA sequencing, which sequences all the DNA in a soil or water sample, has revealed huge numbers of microorganisms that have never been grown in a laboratory. These add new branches to the tree of life.
[bio6-s3.8.3] Asgard archaea were first reported in 2015 from DNA in sediment near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vents in the Arctic Ocean. They share many genes with eukaryotes.
[bio6-s3.8.4] Some scientists argue that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, which would mean only two primary domains. This is still debated, so classification remains a work in progress.
[bio6-s3.8.5] DNA barcoding uses a short standard gene sequence to identify species quickly, e.g. to identify species from tiny samples or to detect mislabelled food.
[bio6-s3.9.b] A dichotomous key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by answering a series of either/or questions about what you can see. The worked key below identifies six invertebrates (animals without backbones).
[bio6-s3.9.0] A dichotomous key identifies an organism by a series of steps. Each step gives a choice between two contrasting statements (a couplet) about a visible feature. Each statement leads either to a name or to the number of the next couplet.
[bio6-s3.9.1] Worked example: a key to six invertebrates.
[bio6-s3.9.2] 1. Legs present → go to 2; legs absent → go to 4.
[bio6-s3.9.3] 2. Three pairs of legs → ladybird (an insect); more than three pairs of legs → go to 3.
[bio6-s3.9.4] 3. Four pairs of legs → spider; seven pairs of legs → woodlouse.
[bio6-s3.9.5] 4. Shell present → snail; no visible shell → go to 5.
[bio6-s3.9.6] 5. Body divided into rings (segments) → earthworm; body not divided into rings → slug.
[bio6-s3.10.b] To use a key, start at couplet 1 and follow the numbers until you reach a name. Practise tracing a route for each organism so you can do it in the exam.
[bio6-s3.10.0] Using the key: an animal with eight legs goes 1 (legs present) → 2 (more than three pairs) → 3 (four pairs) → spider. Eight legs means four pairs, which is why it ends at spider.
[bio6-s3.10.1] Write the route for one more organism: the slug goes 1 → 4 → 5 → slug, because it has no legs, no visible shell and a body that is not divided into rings.
[bio6-s3.11.b] You may have to build a key of your own, or use the features of the kingdoms to place an unknown organism. Both rely on picking features that give a clear yes or no.
[bio6-s3.11.0] To construct a key, list the organisms, choose a feature that splits them into two groups, and keep splitting each group in two until each organism is on its own.
[bio6-s3.11.1] Use features that are easy to observe and do not vary between individuals or seasons, e.g. number of legs, not size or colour, because size and colour change with age and season and would give unreliable answers.
[bio6-s3.11.2] A key for n organisms needs n − 1 couplets. For example, the six-invertebrate key above has five couplets.
[bio6-s3.11.3] To classify an unknown organism to a kingdom, use the features in order. Does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a bacterium or an archaeon, separated by cell-wall chemistry or rRNA.
[bio6-s3.11.4] If it has a nucleus, ask further questions. Is it multicellular with tissues? What is its cell wall made of? Is it autotrophic or heterotrophic?
[bio6-s3.11.5] Worked example: a newly found organism is eukaryotic, is made of hyphae with chitin walls, and absorbs food from dead leaves. It is in the kingdom Fungi.
[bio6-s3.12.b] Classification is more than tidy labelling. It makes biology easier to study, communicate and apply to health, farming and conservation.
[bio6-s3.12.0] It organises the enormous diversity of life into manageable groups, making it easier to study and to find information.
[bio6-s3.12.1] Scientific names are universal and unique, which avoids the confusion of common names. The robin of Europe (Erithacus rubecula) and the robin of North America (Turdus migratorius) are different species in different genera.
[bio6-s3.12.2] It shows evolutionary relationships, so features of a new organism can be predicted from its relatives. For example, relatives of a plant that produces a useful drug may produce similar compounds.
[bio6-s3.12.3] Health: correctly identifying a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) decides the treatment. A bacterial infection may respond to an antibiotic, but a fungal or viral one will not.
[bio6-s3.12.4] Agriculture and the environment: identifying pests, invasive species and endangered species is needed for control and conservation, and for measuring biodiversity (the variety of living things in an area).

CANDIDATES bio7-s1: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
[bio7-s1.0.b] Biologists sort all cells into two types, depending on whether the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. The names themselves tell you the difference, and you need the units to talk about how big cells are.
[bio7-s1.0.0] A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments wrapped in their own membrane). Its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. “Pro” means before and “karyon” means kernel or nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.1] A eukaryotic cell has its DNA enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and it has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. “Eu” means true, so eukaryote means “true nucleus”. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.2] Cells are far too small for millimetres to be a handy unit, so their sizes are given in micrometres. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres) and 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.
[bio7-s1.1.b] Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells look very different, but both are living cells that must do the same basic jobs. So they share a core set of features.
[bio7-s1.1.0] Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves the cell, and cytoplasm, the fluid where many of the cell's reactions take place.
[bio7-s1.1.1] Both have ribosomes (the tiny structures that make proteins), and both use DNA as the genetic material, reading it with the same genetic code.
[bio7-s1.1.2] Both respire, releasing energy from food, and both use ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy) as the energy currency.
[bio7-s1.1.3] These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor, because such an unusual set of identical features is unlikely to have arisen separately.
[bio7-s1.2.b] The most important difference between the two cell types is how the DNA is stored. This affects where the DNA sits, what shape it has and what it is packed with.
[bio7-s1.2.0] Nucleus: prokaryotes have none. Their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Eukaryotes have a true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores (small channels through the envelope).
[bio7-s1.2.1] Number and shape of chromosomes: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome (a closed loop of DNA). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes (DNA with two ends).
[bio7-s1.2.2] Packaging: eukaryotic DNA is wound around histone proteins, which help to pack it. In bacteria the circular chromosome is not wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s1.2.3] Prokaryotes often also have plasmids, small extra rings of DNA separate from the main chromosome. Plasmids can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance, which is why resistance can spread between bacteria.
[bio7-s1.2.4] Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast), not in all eukaryotes, so do not say that eukaryotes have none or that all have them.
[bio7-s1.3.b] Eukaryotic cells divide their work between membrane-bound organelles, whereas prokaryotes have to do everything without them. Ribosomes are the one structure both have, but they differ in size.
[bio7-s1.3.0] Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none, so no mitochondria, no chloroplasts and no other membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.3.1] Without organelles, prokaryotes carry out respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it (folds of the membrane that increase its area).
[bio7-s1.3.2] Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. This is one more way these organelles resemble prokaryotic cells.
[bio7-s1.3.3] Ribosomes: both cell types have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). The S number is a measure of size.
[bio7-s1.3.4] Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they stop bacteria making proteins and harm bacteria but not human cells, which use 80S ribosomes in their cytoplasm.
[bio7-s1.4.b] The remaining differences are about the whole cell: how big it is, what surrounds it, and how the organism is built and reproduces.
[bio7-s1.4.0] Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so they are roughly ten times larger in length.
[bio7-s1.4.1] Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one, made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells have chitin walls, and animal cells have none.
[bio7-s1.4.2] Some prokaryotes have extra outer structures: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum, a whip-like tail, for movement. There is more on the full bacterial cell in Microorganisms.
[bio7-s1.4.3] Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular (made of a single cell) and divide by binary fission, in which one cell splits in two. Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis.
[bio7-s1.4.4] Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotes are protists, fungi, plants and animals.
[bio7-s1.5.b] How a cell looks in a micrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope) depends on the microscope, because a light microscope cannot show very small structures but an electron microscope can. Knowing what each shows lets you identify a cell.
[bio7-s1.5.0] Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure. It is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
[bio7-s1.5.1] Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm and the cell membrane as a line. Plant cells also show the cell wall, a large vacuole (a fluid-filled sac) and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.5.2] Electron micrographs show what the light microscope cannot. In prokaryotes they show the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall.
[bio7-s1.5.3] In eukaryotes, electron micrographs show the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
[bio7-s1.5.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule, flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), with the eukaryotic cell drawn much larger.
[bio7-s1.6.b] You may be given a micrograph and asked whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and to justify the answer. Work through the same checks in order.
[bio7-s1.6.0] 1. Check for a nucleus. If one is present, the cell is eukaryotic, because only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nucleus.
[bio7-s1.6.1] 2. Check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size. A cell of about 1–10 µm suggests a prokaryote, since eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm.
[bio7-s1.6.2] 3. Look for membrane-bound organelles, which only eukaryotes have.
[bio7-s1.6.3] 4. For a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts, which mean a plant cell, or their absence, which suggests an animal cell. There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.

CANDIDATES bio7-s2: Microscopy
[bio7-s2.0.b] Microscopes do two different jobs: they make things look bigger, and they let you see fine detail. Students often mix these up, but only the second one limits what a light microscope can show.
[bio7-s2.0.0] Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the real object, so a ×100 image is 100 times the real size.
[bio7-s2.0.1] Resolution (also called resolving power) is the ability to show two points that are close together as separate points. It decides how much detail can be seen.
[bio7-s2.0.2] Magnifying an image beyond the resolution limit only makes it bigger and blurrier, not more detailed, because the two close points have already merged into one blur.
[bio7-s2.0.3] Total magnification of a light microscope = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification. For example, a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.
[bio7-s2.1.b] The light (optical) microscope passes light through a thin specimen and uses glass lenses to enlarge the image. These parts form the image and bring it into focus.
[bio7-s2.1.0] Eyepiece (ocular) lens: the lens you look through. It magnifies the image from the objective, usually ×10.
[bio7-s2.1.1] Objective lenses: mounted on a revolving nosepiece so they can be swapped. They are usually ×4 (scanning), ×10 (low power) and ×40 (high power), sometimes ×100 (oil immersion). They produce the first magnified image.
[bio7-s2.1.2] Stage with clips: the platform that holds the slide in place over the hole through which light passes.
[bio7-s2.1.3] Coarse focus knob: moves the stage (or tube) a long way quickly, so it is used for focusing at low power only.
[bio7-s2.1.4] Fine focus knob: moves the stage or tube a small distance to give a sharp image. It is the only focus used at high power.
[bio7-s2.2.b] Light has to reach the specimen in the right amount and direction, and the instrument needs a stable frame. These parts do that.
[bio7-s2.2.0] Light source: a built-in lamp, or a mirror that reflects light up through the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.1] Condenser: a lens under the stage that focuses light onto the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.2] Diaphragm (iris): adjusts the amount of light reaching the specimen. Less light often improves contrast for pale, unstained cells, which are nearly transparent and look washed out in bright light.
[bio7-s2.2.3] Arm and base: support the microscope. The arm is used for carrying it.
[bio7-s2.2.4] Diagram: a light microscope labelled eyepiece, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source (or mirror), coarse focus knob, fine focus knob, arm and base.
[bio7-s2.3.b] The method is built to protect the slide and the lenses. You always start on the lowest power, which has the widest view and the most room between lens and slide.
[bio7-s2.3.0] 1. Carry the microscope with one hand on the arm and the other under the base, so its weight is supported.
[bio7-s2.3.1] 2. Switch on the light and turn the lowest-power objective into place.
[bio7-s2.3.2] 3. Clip the slide on the stage with the specimen over the hole, so light can pass through it.
[bio7-s2.3.3] 4. Looking from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide without touching it. Watching from the side stops you grinding the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.3.4] 5. Looking through the eyepiece, focus by moving the objective and slide apart until the image is clear.
[bio7-s2.3.5] 6. Use the fine focus to sharpen the image, and the diaphragm to adjust the light.
[bio7-s2.3.6] 7. Move the part to be studied to the centre of the field of view (the circle of specimen you can see), so it stays in view when you increase magnification.
[bio7-s2.3.7] 8. Turn the next objective into place and use only the fine focus.
[bio7-s2.3.8] 9. Record the total magnification with every drawing.
[bio7-s2.4.b] The image behaves in predictable ways as you change objective, and one safety rule matters for your eyes.
[bio7-s2.4.0] The image is inverted, meaning upside down and reversed left to right. This means moving the slide to the left moves the image to the right.
[bio7-s2.4.1] At higher magnification the field of view is smaller and the image is dimmer, so more light is needed.
[bio7-s2.4.2] At high power the objective is very close to the slide, which is why the coarse focus must not be used: it could crash the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.4.3] Never use a mirror to reflect direct sunlight into the microscope, as it can damage the eye.
[bio7-s2.5.b] To view a specimen with light passing through it, you mount it on a glass slide. The technique below is the general method that any slide uses.
[bio7-s2.5.0] A wet mount: the specimen is placed in a drop of water or stain on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip (a thin square of glass).
[bio7-s2.5.1] The specimen must be very thin, ideally one layer of cells thick, so that light can pass through it and the cells do not overlap.
[bio7-s2.5.2] Stains add contrast to transparent structures by colouring some parts more than others. Iodine solution is used for plant cells: it stains the nucleus yellow-brown and turns starch blue-black. Methylene blue is used for animal cells: it stains the nucleus dark blue.
[bio7-s2.5.3] Lowering the coverslip: touch one edge of the coverslip to the liquid at an angle of about 45° and lower it slowly with a mounted needle. This pushes air out ahead of it and avoids air bubbles, which appear as black-edged circles and can hide cells.
[bio7-s2.5.4] The coverslip keeps the specimen flat, stops it drying out, and protects the objective lens from the liquid. Excess liquid is removed by touching filter paper or a paper towel to the edge.
[bio7-s2.5.5] Stain can also be added after the coverslip is on. Put a drop at one edge and draw it under the coverslip with filter paper held at the opposite edge (irrigation). There is more on the full procedures in Investigation: plant and animal cells.
[bio7-s2.6.b] A light microscope cannot resolve anything much smaller than 0.2 µm. Electron microscopes get round this by using electrons instead of light, so they show far finer detail.
[bio7-s2.6.0] An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by electromagnets instead of glass lenses. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, so resolution is far better.
[bio7-s2.6.1] The specimen is in a vacuum, because air would scatter the electrons, so it must be dead. The image is viewed on a screen and recorded digitally. It is black and white, and colour is added afterwards (false colour).
[bio7-s2.6.2] Transmission electron microscope (TEM): electrons pass through an extremely thin, stained section of a specimen. It gives a flat (2D) image of internal structure at the highest magnifications, e.g. mitochondria, ribosomes, nuclear pores and viruses.
[bio7-s2.6.3] Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a beam of electrons scans across the surface of a specimen coated with a very thin layer of metal, e.g. gold. Electrons bouncing off it form an image. It gives a 3D image of surfaces, e.g. the surface of cells, pollen grains and insects.
[bio7-s2.6.4] The SEM has lower resolution than the TEM and shows no internal structure unless the specimen is broken open.
[bio7-s2.6.5] The first electron microscope was built in Germany in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska.
[bio7-s2.7.b] The two types differ in how they work, what they can resolve and what they demand of the specimen. Compare them point by point.
[bio7-s2.7.0] Radiation: light for the light microscope, electrons for the electron microscope. Lenses: glass for the light microscope, electromagnets for the electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.1] Maximum useful magnification: about ×1000–1500 for the light microscope, compared with hundreds of thousands of times or more for electron microscopes.
[bio7-s2.7.2] Resolution: about 0.2 µm (200 nm) for the light microscope, compared with less than 1 nm for the TEM, roughly a thousand times better.
[bio7-s2.7.3] So the light microscope shows the nucleus, cell wall, vacuole and chloroplasts. Ribosomes, nuclear pores, the inner structure of mitochondria and viruses are too small and need an electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.4] Specimens: the light microscope can view living or dead specimens, with colour visible. The electron microscope needs dead specimens in a vacuum, and its images are black and white.
[bio7-s2.7.5] Preparation and cost: light microscopes are simple, quick, cheap and portable. Electron microscopes need complex preparation (thin sectioning, metal coating) that can create artefacts, structures that are not really in the living cell. They are very expensive, large, and need trained operators.
[bio7-s2.8.b] Microscopy has a long history and is still developing. Newer techniques tag, scan or freeze specimens to reveal what older methods could not.
[bio7-s2.8.0] Robert Hooke described and named “cells” from thin slices of cork in his book Micrographia (1665).
[bio7-s2.8.1] Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using his own single-lens microscopes, was the first to see and describe bacteria and other single-celled organisms, in the 1670s.
[bio7-s2.8.2] Fluorescence microscopy: specific molecules are tagged with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins that glow when lit with a particular colour of light. This shows exactly where a protein is in a living cell.
[bio7-s2.8.3] Green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a jellyfish, earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.
[bio7-s2.8.4] Confocal laser scanning microscopy: a laser scans one thin plane of the specimen at a time, giving sharp images that a computer combines into a 3D model.
[bio7-s2.8.5] Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy overcomes the light microscope’s normal resolution limit. It earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William Moerner.
[bio7-s2.8.6] Cryo-electron microscopy: samples are flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice instead of being dried and stained, so molecules keep their natural shape. It shows the structure of proteins and viruses in near-atomic detail.
[bio7-s2.8.7] Cryo-electron microscopy earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson.
[bio7-s2.8.8] Cryo-electron microscopy was used to show the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which helped vaccine design.
[bio7-s2.9.b] Microscopes are everyday tools in medicine, research and forensics, and modern imaging adds automation.
[bio7-s2.9.0] Uses in health and technology: examining blood smears and biopsies to diagnose disease, including spotting cancer cells; identifying pathogens; research into how cells and viruses work; and forensic science, e.g. comparing hairs and fibres.
[bio7-s2.9.1] Digital cameras and computer image analysis now allow images to be measured, shared and analysed automatically.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.

CANDIDATES bio7-s4: Investigation: plant and animal cells
[bio7-s4.0.b] This investigation compares plant cells with animal cells. You gather first-hand (primary) data with a light microscope, then add second-hand (secondary) data from scanning electron microscope images. A fair comparison needs a clear aim, a prediction and tight control of variables.
[bio7-s4.0.0] Aim: to observe and compare the structures of plant cells (onion epidermis, the thin outer skin layer) and animal cells (human cheek cells) using a light microscope, and to use scanning electron microscope (SEM) images to see structures the light microscope cannot show.
[bio7-s4.0.1] Hypothesis (a testable prediction): plant cells will show a cell wall and a large vacuole, which animal cells lack. Both will show a cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus.
[bio7-s4.0.2] Independent variable (the one thing you change): the type of cell, plant or animal.
[bio7-s4.0.3] Dependent variable (what you observe or measure): the structures observed, whether present or absent, and their shape and arrangement.
[bio7-s4.0.4] Controlled variables (kept the same so the comparison is fair): the same microscope and total magnification (×100 and ×400), the same light setting, a fresh thin specimen one cell layer thick, and the same volume of stain and staining time.
[bio7-s4.0.5] Control: an unstained wet mount (specimen in a drop of water) of each cell type, viewed in the same way. It shows what the stain adds, meaning which structures become visible, and that the structures seen are in the cells and not produced by the stain.
[bio7-s4.1.b] You need a short list of apparatus, and several of the items carry real hazards: sharp blades, stains, thin glass and body cells. Good practice is part of the method, so know the precaution for each hazard.
[bio7-s4.1.0] Equipment: light microscope; clean glass slides and coverslips; onion; scalpel or knife and white tile; forceps; mounted needle; dropper (pipette); filter paper or paper towel; iodine solution; methylene blue stain; sterile cotton buds; beaker of disinfectant for used buds; distilled water; gloves and safety glasses; pencil and paper for drawings.
[bio7-s4.1.1] Use only your own cheek cells, with a new sterile cotton bud. Put used buds, and slides after use, into disinfectant to prevent spreading infection, and wash hands afterwards.
[bio7-s4.1.2] Iodine solution and methylene blue stain skin and clothes and can irritate eyes, so wear gloves and safety glasses and wipe up spills.
[bio7-s4.1.3] Cut away from your fingers on a white tile with the scalpel, and carry scalpels pointing down so the blade cannot injure anyone if you trip.
[bio7-s4.1.4] Hold slides and coverslips by their edges. Coverslips are very thin and break easily, so dispose of broken glass in the glass bin, not the ordinary bin, where it could cut someone.
[bio7-s4.1.5] Keep water away from the microscope's electric lamp and cable to avoid electric shock. Never point a microscope mirror at direct sunlight, as this can damage the eye.
[bio7-s4.2.b] Onion epidermis peels off in a layer only one cell thick, so light passes through it and the cells can be seen clearly. The steps below prepare a stained slide and an unstained control. There is more on lowering the coverslip and focusing in Microscopy.
[bio7-s4.2.0] 1. Place a few drops of water in the centre of a clean slide.
[bio7-s4.2.1] 2. Cut an onion into quarters and remove one fleshy scale leaf.
[bio7-s4.2.2] 3. Snap the scale leaf and use forceps to peel the thin, transparent epidermis from its inner (concave) surface.
[bio7-s4.2.3] 4. Cut a piece about 1 cm square and lay it flat in the water, unfolding it with the mounted needle so there are no folds or overlaps.
[bio7-s4.2.4] 5. Add one or two drops of iodine solution and leave it for a few minutes to stain.
[bio7-s4.2.5] 6. Lower a coverslip at 45° with the mounted needle, which avoids trapping air bubbles, then blot excess liquid from the edges with filter paper.
[bio7-s4.2.6] 7. Prepare an unstained slide the same way, using water only, as the control.
[bio7-s4.2.7] 8. Examine under low power (×100), then high power (×400). Make a labelled pencil drawing of a few cells and record the total magnification.
[bio7-s4.3.b] Cheek cells are collected by scraping the lining of your mouth, then stained so they show up. Because they come from a person, hygiene steps are built into the method. There is also an alternative for schools that do not use cheek cells.
[bio7-s4.3.0] 1. Gently rub the inside of your own cheek with a clean, sterile cotton bud.
[bio7-s4.3.1] 2. Smear the bud across the centre of a clean slide in a thin layer.
[bio7-s4.3.2] 3. Immediately put the used bud into the beaker of disinfectant to prevent spreading infection.
[bio7-s4.3.3] 4. Add one or two drops of methylene blue and leave for one to two minutes.
[bio7-s4.3.4] 5. Lower a coverslip at 45° and blot the excess with filter paper.
[bio7-s4.3.5] 6. Prepare an unstained slide with a drop of water, as the control.
[bio7-s4.3.6] 7. Examine under low power, then high power. Look for flat, separate cells, avoiding clumps, which are hard to interpret. Make a labelled pencil drawing and record the magnification.
[bio7-s4.3.7] Alternative where a school does not use cheek cells: a prepared (commercial) slide of human cheek cells or another animal tissue, examined in the same way.
[bio7-s4.4.b] Onion bulb cells have no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), so this optional extension uses a leaf instead.
[bio7-s4.4.0] Mount a single leaf of a moss or of Elodea (Canadian pondweed) in a drop of water, without stain. These leaves are only one or two cells thick, so light passes through them.
[bio7-s4.4.1] The green chloroplasts are visible without any stain, which onion bulb cells do not have.
[bio7-s4.5.b] The stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm stand out, and the shape of the cells tells you whether they are plant or animal. You should be able to describe what each slide looks like and explain why.
[bio7-s4.5.0] Onion epidermis: cells are regular, brick-shaped or rectangular, and fitted closely together in rows. Each cell is outlined by a clear, rigid cell wall.
[bio7-s4.5.1] In onion cells the nucleus is stained yellow-brown by iodine and is often seen near the edge of the cell. The cytoplasm stains pale yellow and forms a thin layer pushed to the edge by a large central vacuole (a fluid-filled sac), which looks clear.
[bio7-s4.5.2] Onion cells have no chloroplasts, because the bulb grows underground and does not photosynthesise.
[bio7-s4.5.3] Cheek cells: cells are flat and irregular in shape, rounded or many-sided, lying singly or in small overlapping groups. Each has a cell membrane but no cell wall.
[bio7-s4.5.4] In cheek cells the nucleus is small, central and stained dark blue, and the cytoplasm stains pale blue. There is no large vacuole and no chloroplasts.
[bio7-s4.5.5] Unstained controls: the cells are almost transparent. The onion cell walls are faintly visible but the nucleus is hard to see, and the cheek cells are barely visible. So stains make the nucleus and cytoplasm visible by adding contrast.
[bio7-s4.5.6] Structures the light microscope cannot show: ribosomes, the internal detail of mitochondria, and nuclear pores. Mitochondria are too small to see clearly at ×400.
[bio7-s4.5.7] Diagram: pencil drawings of (i) a few onion epidermis cells labelled cell wall, cell membrane (pressed against the wall), cytoplasm, vacuole and nucleus, and (ii) two or three cheek cells labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm and nucleus, each with the total magnification (e.g. ×400) written beside it.
[bio7-s4.6.b] Secondary data is information collected by someone else, here scanning electron microscope (SEM) images. They reveal detail the light microscope cannot, but they have limits of their own. You also need to be able to work out real cell size from an image.
[bio7-s4.6.0] Sources: published SEM images (micrographs) of onion epidermis, of cheek (epithelial) cells, and of other cells, from textbooks or reputable scientific image libraries. Each has a scale bar or magnification. Record the source of each image so others can check it.
[bio7-s4.6.1] What SEM images add: a three-dimensional view of the cell surface, at much higher magnification and resolution (the ability to separate fine detail) than the light microscope.
[bio7-s4.6.2] For example, SEM images show the regular, box-like shape of the onion cells and the outlines of their walls, and the flat, thin, overlapping shape of cheek cells and the texture of their surfaces.
[bio7-s4.6.3] Limits of SEM images: they show surfaces only, not internal organelles, which need TEM (transmission electron microscope) images. They show dead, metal-coated cells, so preparation can create artefacts (features that are not really in the living cell). Any colour is false colour added afterwards.
[bio7-s4.6.4] Measuring size from an image: actual size = image size ÷ magnification. Measure the image with a ruler in mm and convert to µm (1 mm = 1000 µm).
[bio7-s4.6.5] Worked example: an onion cell measures 40 mm long on an image magnified ×200. 40 ÷ 200 = 0.2 mm = 200 µm.
[bio7-s4.6.6] With a scale bar, measure the bar, then calculate: actual size = (image size of the cell ÷ image length of the bar) × the length the bar represents.
[bio7-s4.6.7] Comparing the data: the light microscope shows living-cell features in colour (with stain) and at low cost. The SEM confirms the shapes and surfaces in 3D at much greater detail. Using both gives a fuller and more reliable picture than either alone.
[bio7-s4.7.b] Your results can now be compared with the hypothesis. The differences between the two cell types fit what each cell does, so you should be able to link structure to function.
[bio7-s4.7.0] Both cell types have a cell membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus, so both are eukaryotic (their DNA is enclosed in a nucleus).
[bio7-s4.7.1] The onion cells have a cell wall, which gives their fixed, regular shape and support, and a large vacuole, which keeps the cell turgid (firm and full of water). The cheek cells have neither, so they have a flexible, irregular shape. This supports the hypothesis.
[bio7-s4.7.2] The cheek cells are lining (epithelial) cells: flat and thin, forming a protective layer that is continually rubbed off and replaced, which is why they come away easily.
[bio7-s4.7.3] The onion epidermis is a protective outer layer of tightly fitting cells with strong walls.
[bio7-s4.7.4] The onion has no chloroplasts because the bulb is a food store, not a photosynthetic organ.
[bio7-s4.8.b] Slide preparation is fiddly, and small mistakes give misleading images. For each problem, know what you would see and how to fix it.
[bio7-s4.8.0] Air bubbles (black-edged circles) can be mistaken for cells or can hide them. Lower the coverslip at 45°.
[bio7-s4.8.1] Folded or overlapping tissue, or a thick smear, makes cells hard to see because light cannot pass through. Use a flat piece of epidermis one cell layer thick, and a thin smear.
[bio7-s4.8.2] Too much stain darkens everything and hides detail; too little gives poor contrast. Use one or two drops and blot the excess.
[bio7-s4.8.3] The specimen drying out under the lamp distorts the cells. Add a drop of water at the edge of the coverslip.
[bio7-s4.8.4] Poor focus or wrong light settings: focus with the fine focus at high power and adjust the diaphragm (which controls how much light reaches the specimen).
[bio7-s4.8.5] Drawings depend on the observer, so compare them with other students' slides and with the SEM images. Examine several cells and several fields of view, not just one, so your conclusion does not rest on a single unusual cell.

CANDIDATES bio8-s6: Water
[bio8-s6.0.b] Water is the most abundant substance in living things, and almost every role it plays comes from the shape and charge of its molecule. Learn the properties here first, because the later blocks all rely on them.
[bio8-s6.0.0] Water is the most abundant substance in living things. It makes up about 60% of the mass of an adult human body and most of the mass of cytoplasm (the jelly-like contents of a cell).
[bio8-s6.0.1] Many plant tissues and jellyfish are over 90% water. Organisms die quickly without water, which shows how much of life depends on it.
[bio8-s6.0.2] A water molecule, H₂O, is polar, meaning it has uneven charge: the oxygen end carries a slight negative charge (δ−) and the hydrogen ends carry slight positive charges (δ+).
[bio8-s6.0.3] Because of these opposite charges, neighbouring water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, weak attractions between the δ+ hydrogen of one molecule and the δ− oxygen of another.
[bio8-s6.0.4] Because it is polar, water is an excellent solvent: ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻) and polar molecules (e.g. glucose, amino acids) dissolve in it, as the charged water molecules surround them. Lipids are non-polar and do not dissolve.
[bio8-s6.0.5] Hydrogen bonding gives water a high specific heat capacity, so it takes a lot of energy to raise its temperature, because the energy must first break hydrogen bonds. It also gives water a high latent heat of vaporisation, so a lot of energy is absorbed when it evaporates.
[bio8-s6.0.6] Hydrogen bonding makes water molecules stick to each other (cohesion) and to other surfaces (adhesion).
[bio8-s6.0.7] Diagram: two or three water molecules with δ+ on the hydrogens, δ− on the oxygen, and a dotted hydrogen bond between them.
[bio8-s6.1.b] Chemical reactions in cells need the reacting molecules to meet. Water gives them a place to do that, and it also takes part in some of the reactions itself.
[bio8-s6.1.0] Almost all metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of life) take place in solution in water, in the cytoplasm and inside organelles. This is because dissolved molecules can move, collide and meet enzymes.
[bio8-s6.1.1] Water is also a reactant, meaning it is used up in a reaction. It is split in photosynthesis, releasing oxygen, and it is used in every hydrolysis reaction (breaking a bond by adding water), including digestion.
[bio8-s6.1.2] Water is a product, meaning it is made by a reaction, in respiration and in every condensation reaction (joining molecules together with the loss of water).
[bio8-s6.2.b] Because so many substances dissolve in water, it can carry them from place to place. This works in animals and plants, and also across the surface of every cell.
[bio8-s6.2.0] In animals, blood plasma (the liquid part of blood) is mainly water. It carries dissolved glucose, amino acids, mineral ions, hormones, urea and carbon dioxide around the body; lymph and tissue fluid are also watery.
[bio8-s6.2.1] Urea (a waste product) and excess salts are excreted dissolved in water as urine, so water carries waste out of the body as well as supplies in.
[bio8-s6.2.2] In plants, water carries dissolved minerals up the xylem (the vessels that carry water) from the roots to the leaves. Sucrose and amino acids move through the phloem (the vessels that carry food).
[bio8-s6.2.3] Cohesion between water molecules holds the column of water together in the xylem, so it can be pulled upwards as one continuous stream. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio8-s6.2.4] Substances move into and out of cells by diffusion through the watery fluid around them, because dissolved substances can spread out through water.
[bio8-s6.3.b] Living things work best within a narrow temperature range. Water helps keep them there, thanks to its high specific heat capacity and high latent heat of vaporisation.
[bio8-s6.3.0] High specific heat capacity: water heats up and cools down slowly, so the water in an organism's body buffers it against sudden temperature changes. Lakes and seas give aquatic organisms a stable temperature.
[bio8-s6.3.1] Cooling by evaporation: because water absorbs a lot of heat when it evaporates, sweating in mammals cools the skin, panting cools dogs, and transpiration (evaporation of water from leaves) cools leaves. There is more on sweating in Homeostasis.
[bio8-s6.3.2] Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats and forms an insulating layer on ponds and lakes, letting organisms survive beneath it in winter.
[bio8-s6.4.b] Enzymes only work properly at the right pH, so cells need to keep acidity steady. Water is the medium in which this control happens.
[bio8-s6.4.0] pH depends on the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺) in a solution; pure water is neutral, at pH 7. Acids and bases exert their effects only when they are dissolved in water, because that is when they release ions.
[bio8-s6.4.1] Buffers, such as hydrogencarbonate and phosphate ions, work dissolved in body water. They keep the pH of cells and blood almost constant, so enzymes keep their shape and activity.
[bio8-s6.4.2] Water is the medium by which excess H⁺ ions are removed. The kidneys excrete them dissolved in urine.
[bio8-s6.4.3] Carbon dioxide, which forms an acid in solution, is carried in the blood plasma to the lungs and breathed out, so the acid does not build up in the blood.
[bio8-s6.5.b] Water moves in and out of cells by osmosis, so cells can swell or shrink if the surrounding fluid changes. Osmoregulation is how organisms keep this under control, and animals and plants deal with it differently.
[bio8-s6.5.0] Osmoregulation is the control of the water and salt (solute) concentration of body fluids.
[bio8-s6.5.1] Water moves into or out of cells by osmosis, from a region of higher water concentration to a region of lower water concentration, through a selectively permeable membrane. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio8-s6.5.2] Animal cells placed in pure water swell and may burst; in strong salt solution they shrink. So the concentration of the blood and tissue fluid must be kept steady.
[bio8-s6.5.3] In humans the kidneys do this, adjusting the volume of water in urine under the control of ADH (a hormone).
[bio8-s6.5.4] Plant cells have a strong cell wall, so water entering by osmosis makes them turgid (swollen and firm) instead of bursting. Turgor pressure supports non-woody plants and leaves and opens stomata (the pores in leaves).
[bio8-s6.5.5] When water is lost, plant cells become flaccid (soft and limp) and the plant wilts.
[bio8-s6.5.6] Single-celled freshwater organisms such as Amoeba constantly gain water by osmosis, because their surroundings are more dilute than their cytoplasm. They expel the excess with a contractile vacuole.
[bio8-s6.6.b] Beyond reactions, transport, temperature, pH and osmoregulation, water has several further roles in living things.
[bio8-s6.6.0] Lubrication: synovial fluid lubricates joints, and mucus and saliva help swallowing.
[bio8-s6.6.1] Support: the fluid pressure of a hydrostatic skeleton supports soft-bodied animals such as earthworms, because water cannot easily be compressed.
[bio8-s6.6.2] Habitat: water is the habitat of aquatic organisms.
[bio8-s6.6.3] Fertilisation: some organisms need water for fertilisation. Moss and fern sperm swim in water to reach the egg.
