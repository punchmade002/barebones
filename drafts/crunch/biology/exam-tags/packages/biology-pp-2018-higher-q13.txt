PART (b)(i) (10 marks) subsections: bio11-s7
MODEL ANSWER: Law of Segregation: Inherited traits are controlled by pairs of factors (genes/alleles), which separate from each other during gamete formation, so each gamete receives only one of each pair.
Law of Independent Assortment: When gametes form, the members of one pair of factors (alleles) separate independently of the members of another pair, so either member of one pair can combine randomly with either member of the other pair.

PART (b)(iii) (3 marks) subsections: bio11-s9
MODEL ANSWER: Linked genes are on the same chromosome and are inherited together, so they do not assort independently. The TtYy parent would make mostly (only) two gamete types, TY and ty. The progeny would be mostly (or all) the parental types, tall yellow and dwarf green, with no or few tall green and dwarf yellow recombinants. The 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio would not be obtained, and there would be less variation.

PART (c)(i) (10 marks) subsections: bio12-s2
MODEL ANSWER: Evolution is the inherited (genetic) change in the characteristics of a population or species over time (over many generations), in response to changes in the environment.

PART (c)(ii) (3 marks) subsections: bio12-s2
MODEL ANSWER: Organisms produce more offspring than can survive, so there is competition (a struggle for existence) for limited resources. Individuals show inherited variation. Those best adapted to the environment survive and reproduce more (survival of the fittest). They pass on their favourable characteristics, so these become more common in the next generation, and over many generations new species may form.

PART (c)(iii) (11 marks) subsections: bio12-s3
MODEL ANSWER: Source: fossils (the fossil record).
Fossils show structures changing over time: the horse fossils over 50 million years run from the small, dog-sized Hyracotherium with four toes on its front feet to the modern horse, which is larger and stands on a single toe, with teeth suited to grazing.
The change is related to the environment: it matches a change from forest to open grassland. Also, older rocks contain only simple organisms and more complex ones appear in younger rocks in a consistent order.

CANDIDATES bio10-s1: Nucleotides, DNA and RNA
[bio10-s1.0.b] Cells need a way to store instructions for building and running themselves. They use nucleic acids, long molecules made by joining many small units, and the unit that is repeated is the nucleotide. There are four kinds of nucleotide, which differ in their base.
[bio10-s1.0.0] Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are large molecules (polymers) built from many small units called nucleotides joined in a chain, much as beads are threaded on a string.
[bio10-s1.0.1] DNA is deoxyribonucleic acid; RNA is ribonucleic acid. Both contain the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus.
[bio10-s1.0.2] Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base (a nitrogen-containing molecule) attached to the sugar.
[bio10-s1.0.3] The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen atom than ribose, which is where its name comes from: deoxy means an oxygen has been removed.
[bio10-s1.0.4] Each nucleic acid has four bases. DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). RNA: adenine (A), uracil (U), cytosine (C) and guanine (G); uracil takes the place of thymine.
[bio10-s1.0.5] Adenine and guanine are larger, double-ringed bases, called purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are smaller, single-ringed bases, called pyrimidines.
[bio10-s1.0.6] A nucleotide is the building block of DNA and RNA, and its base is one letter of the genetic information. The order, or sequence, of bases along a strand carries the information, just as the order of letters makes a word.
[bio10-s1.0.7] Modified nucleotides also carry energy. There is more on this in ATP.
[bio10-s1.0.8] Diagram: one nucleotide drawn as a circle (phosphate) joined to a pentagon (sugar) joined to a rectangle (base), labelled phosphate, deoxyribose or ribose, and nitrogenous base.
[bio10-s1.1.b] One nucleotide holds one letter of information, so to store a whole message the cell must link many of them together. The way they link leaves a strong chain with the bases free to carry the code.
[bio10-s1.1.0] Nucleotides join by bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next. These bonds are made in condensation reactions, in which a molecule of water is removed for each bond.
[bio10-s1.1.1] The repeating sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate chain is the sugar–phosphate backbone. The bonds within it are strong covalent bonds, not hydrogen bonds.
[bio10-s1.1.2] The bases stick out from the backbone, one per sugar. The backbone is the same in every DNA molecule; only the sequence of bases differs, so the bases carry the information and the backbone gives strength and support.
[bio10-s1.2.b] DNA is not one chain but two, held together in a regular pattern. This shape explains how DNA can store information and be copied accurately.
[bio10-s1.2.0] DNA is double-stranded: two strands of nucleotides lie side by side, with the sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside and the bases pointing inwards, like the rails and rungs of a ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.1] The bases pair up: adenine always pairs with thymine (A–T) and cytosine always pairs with guanine (C–G). These are complementary base pairs.
[bio10-s1.2.2] Each pair is one purine with one pyrimidine, so every rung is the same width and the ladder stays an even shape.
[bio10-s1.2.3] The bases in a pair are held together by hydrogen bonds (weak attractions between molecules): two between A and T, and three between C and G.
[bio10-s1.2.4] Each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated, or unzipped, when DNA is copied or read. There are millions of hydrogen bonds, so the molecule as a whole is stable.
[bio10-s1.2.5] The two linked strands are twisted around each other into a spiral called a double helix, like a twisted ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.6] Because pairing is fixed, the two strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand decides the sequence of the other. For example, the strand ATGCCTAG pairs with TACGGATC.
[bio10-s1.2.7] Diagram: a short section of DNA drawn as a straight ladder of about four base pairs, labelled sugar (deoxyribose), phosphate, sugar–phosphate backbone, base pairs A–T and C–G, and hydrogen bonds; with a sketch of the twisted double helix.
[bio10-s1.3.b] Because A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G, the amounts of the bases in double-stranded DNA are linked. This lets you work out the percentage of every base from just one.
[bio10-s1.3.0] In double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of C equals the amount of G, because every A is paired with a T and every C with a G.
[bio10-s1.3.1] For example, if 20% of the bases in a DNA sample are adenine, then 20% are thymine. That leaves 60% shared equally between cytosine and guanine, so each is 30%.
[bio10-s1.4.b] The order of bases in DNA is the cell's set of instructions. DNA is kept in different places in different kinds of cell.
[bio10-s1.4.0] DNA stores the genetic information (genes) that controls the cell's activities by coding for proteins. There is more on genes and proteins in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio10-s1.4.1] DNA is copied before a cell divides, so each new cell receives the full set of information, which means that it is passed from parents to offspring.
[bio10-s1.4.2] In eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), DNA is found mostly in the nucleus, in the chromosomes, with small amounts in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio10-s1.4.3] In bacteria, DNA forms a loop in the cytoplasm and is also found in small rings called plasmids.
[bio10-s1.5.b] The structure of DNA was worked out by combining physical evidence with model building. It is a good example of how scientists build on each other's data.
[bio10-s1.5.0] The double-helix model was published by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953. It was based on X-ray diffraction images of DNA (patterns made when X-rays pass through DNA, which reveal its shape) made by Rosalind Franklin, working with Maurice Wilkins.
[bio10-s1.6.b] DNA stays safe in the nucleus, so the cell needs a working copy of its instructions to take out and use. That working copy is RNA, a similar molecule with a few key differences.
[bio10-s1.6.0] RNA is usually single-stranded: one chain of nucleotides with its own sugar–phosphate backbone. RNA molecules are much shorter than DNA molecules.
[bio10-s1.6.1] RNA nucleotides contain ribose instead of deoxyribose, and uracil instead of thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine (A–U) whenever RNA pairs with DNA or with another RNA.
[bio10-s1.6.2] A single strand can fold back on itself where its bases pair up, giving some RNA molecules a fixed shape.
[bio10-s1.6.3] RNA uses the information in DNA to make proteins, so it acts as the go-between for DNA and the protein-making machinery.
[bio10-s1.6.4] Three main types of RNA are named here, with no more detail. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries a copy of a gene from the nucleus to a ribosome (the structure that makes proteins).
[bio10-s1.6.5] Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids to the ribosome; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms part of the ribosome. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s1.6.6] RNA is made in the nucleus and works in the cytoplasm, so it carries information out to where proteins are made. It is found in the nucleus (including the nucleolus), the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.6.7] Diagram: a short single RNA strand of about four nucleotides, labelled ribose, phosphate, backbone and bases, including uracil.
[bio10-s1.7.b] Put side by side, DNA and RNA differ in structure, stability and job. The differences fit their roles: DNA is a long-term store and RNA is a short-lived working copy.
[bio10-s1.7.0] Strands: DNA is a double-stranded helix, which suits long-term storage; RNA is usually single-stranded.
[bio10-s1.7.1] Sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose; RNA contains ribose.
[bio10-s1.7.2] Bases: DNA has A, T, C and G; RNA has A, U, C and G, with uracil instead of thymine.
[bio10-s1.7.3] Pairing: A–T in DNA; A–U in RNA.
[bio10-s1.7.4] Size and life span: DNA is very long and stable and is kept for the life of the cell; RNA molecules are shorter and are broken down after use, so each copy is only a temporary message.
[bio10-s1.7.5] Location: DNA is mainly in the nucleus, with some in mitochondria and chloroplasts; RNA is in the nucleus, the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.7.6] Function: DNA stores the genetic information; RNA carries and uses that information to make proteins.

CANDIDATES bio11-s5: Monohybrid crosses
[bio11-s5.0.b] A genetic cross predicts what offspring a pair of parents can have. It works only because of how alleles (the different versions of a gene) are shared out when gametes (sex cells) are made and joined.
[bio11-s5.0.0] Each body cell carries two alleles of each gene, one on each chromosome of a homologous pair (two matching chromosomes, one inherited from each parent).
[bio11-s5.0.1] Each gamete carries only one of the two alleles, because meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes) separates the two chromosomes of each homologous pair.
[bio11-s5.0.2] At fertilisation the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) again has two alleles, one from each parent, so the pair is restored.
[bio11-s5.0.3] There is more on this in the subsections on chromosome structure and genes, mitosis and meiosis, and the key concepts of information transfer, where allele, homozygous, heterozygous, pure-breeding and carrier are defined.
[bio11-s5.1.b] Before you can set out any cross you must work out which gametes each parent can make. This depends only on whether the parent is homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous (two different alleles).
[bio11-s5.1.0] A homozygous individual (TT or tt) makes only one type of gamete for that gene: T from TT, or t from tt, because both of its alleles are the same.
[bio11-s5.1.1] A heterozygous individual (Tt) makes two types of gamete, T and t, in equal numbers, half of its gametes each, because the two chromosomes separate at random in meiosis.
[bio11-s5.1.2] True-breeding is another name for pure-breeding (homozygous): crossed with one like itself, it always gives offspring with the same trait.
[bio11-s5.2.b] A monohybrid cross is the simplest kind of genetic cross, because it follows just one characteristic at a time. Labels for the generations let you say clearly which individuals you mean.
[bio11-s5.2.0] A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of one trait, controlled by one gene with two alleles.
[bio11-s5.2.1] Unlinked means the gene is considered on its own, without other genes on the same chromosome affecting the result. There is more on this in Linkage.
[bio11-s5.2.2] P (parental generation) are the parents crossed.
[bio11-s5.2.3] F1 (first filial generation) are their offspring, for example the plants grown from the seeds of the P cross.
[bio11-s5.2.4] F2 (second filial generation) are the offspring of crossing F1 individuals with each other.
[bio11-s5.2.5] “First generation” means the offspring of the cross being modelled, so you only need to work out the offspring of one cross.
[bio11-s5.3.b] Symbols are shorthand for alleles. A key at the start tells the examiner what each symbol means, so your answer can be read without guessing.
[bio11-s5.3.0] Use the first letter of the dominant trait: a capital for the dominant allele and a lower-case letter for the recessive allele, e.g. T = tall, t = dwarf.
[bio11-s5.3.1] Use the letters a question gives, because the marking scheme is written with them.
[bio11-s5.3.2] Avoid letters whose capital and lower case look alike when handwritten, because the examiner cannot tell which allele you mean: C/c, O/o, S/s, W/w, P/p, K/k, V/v, X/x, Z/z.
[bio11-s5.3.3] Always state a key first, e.g. “T = allele for tall, t = allele for dwarf”.
[bio11-s5.4.b] Every line is shown, in this order, because marks are given for each. Working in the same sequence every time also stops you missing a step.
[bio11-s5.4.0] Step 1: the key, so that the examiner knows what each letter means.
[bio11-s5.4.1] Step 2: the parents' phenotypes (the visible traits), e.g. tall × dwarf.
[bio11-s5.4.2] Step 3: the parents' genotypes (the alleles they carry), e.g. TT × tt.
[bio11-s5.4.3] Step 4: the gametes, each different allele each parent can pass on, written once and usually circled (T from TT; t from tt; T and t from Tt).
[bio11-s5.4.4] Step 5: the Punnett square (a grid that combines the gametes), or fertilisation lines joining every gamete of one parent to every gamete of the other.
[bio11-s5.4.5] Step 6: the offspring genotypes, with their ratio, such as 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.
[bio11-s5.4.6] Step 7: the offspring phenotypes, with their ratio, such as 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
[bio11-s5.5.b] A Punnett square is a grid that shows every possible fertilisation between the gametes of two parents. It makes sure you do not miss a combination.
[bio11-s5.5.0] One parent's gametes go across the top and the other parent's gametes down the side.
[bio11-s5.5.1] Each box takes the allele from its column and the allele from its row, with the dominant allele written first (Tt, not tT), so that identical genotypes are always written the same way.
[bio11-s5.5.2] Each box is one equally likely outcome of fertilisation; with two gametes from each parent, each box is a 1 in 4 (25%) chance.
[bio11-s5.5.3] Diagram: a 2 × 2 Punnett square for Tt × Tt: gametes T and t across the top (labelled “gametes of parent 1”), T and t down the side (labelled “gametes of parent 2”); boxes TT, Tt, Tt, tt; each box labelled with its phenotype (tall, tall, tall, dwarf).
[bio11-s5.6.b] A cross predicts chances, not certainties. Each fertilisation is a separate chance event, so the ratio describes what is expected on average.
[bio11-s5.6.0] A ratio is a prediction of the proportions expected, not an exact count.
[bio11-s5.6.1] Each fertilisation is independent, so in a family where the first child is affected, the chance for the next child is unchanged.
[bio11-s5.6.2] A 3 : 1 ratio is a probability, not a guarantee that a family of four has exactly one affected child.
[bio11-s5.6.3] Ratios can be given as a ratio (3 : 1), a fraction (3/4), a percentage (75%) or a probability (0.75, or “a 3 in 4 chance”).
[bio11-s5.6.4] Observed results come close to the predicted ratio only with large numbers of offspring (e.g. hundreds of pea plants), not in a small human family, because chance has more effect on small samples.
[bio11-s5.7.b] Pea plants: tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). Here two heterozygous tall plants are crossed, and every line of the standard layout is shown.
[bio11-s5.7.0] Key: T = tall, t = dwarf.
[bio11-s5.7.1] Parents' phenotypes: tall × tall.
[bio11-s5.7.2] Parents' genotypes: Tt × Tt (both heterozygous, so each makes two types of gamete).
[bio11-s5.7.3] Gametes: T, t × T, t, because each heterozygous parent makes both types.
[bio11-s5.7.4] Punnett square: TT, Tt / Tt, tt.
[bio11-s5.7.5] Offspring genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, because Tt arises in two boxes of the square.
[bio11-s5.7.6] Offspring phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 dwarf (75% tall, 25% dwarf), because TT and both Tt boxes are tall.
[bio11-s5.7.7] Of the tall offspring, 1/3 are homozygous (TT) and 2/3 heterozygous (Tt), because the three tall offspring are TT, Tt and Tt.
[bio11-s5.8.b] In the cross above there are two different ratios, one for genotypes and one for phenotypes. They differ because of dominance.
[bio11-s5.8.0] The genotype ratio (1 : 2 : 1) differs from the phenotype ratio (3 : 1) because TT and Tt look the same: the dominant allele masks the recessive one.
[bio11-s5.8.1] A recessive trait reappears in offspring of parents who both show the dominant trait only if both parents are heterozygous, because only then can each parent pass on a recessive allele.
[bio11-s5.9.b] Crossing a true-breeding tall plant with a true-breeding dwarf plant is the simplest case, because each parent makes only one type of gamete.
[bio11-s5.9.0] Parents' phenotypes: true-breeding tall × true-breeding dwarf.
[bio11-s5.9.1] Parents' genotypes: TT × tt (both homozygous, so true-breeding).
[bio11-s5.9.2] Gametes: T × t, because each parent makes only one type.
[bio11-s5.9.3] Offspring genotypes: all Tt (heterozygous), because each gets T from one parent and t from the other.
[bio11-s5.9.4] Offspring phenotypes: all (100%) tall, because the dominant T masks t.
[bio11-s5.9.5] This is Mendel's P → F1 cross; crossing these F1 plants together (Tt × Tt) gives the F2 result of the heterozygous × heterozygous example above, in which the dwarf trait reappears.
[bio11-s5.9.6] Mendel's own figures are in Mendel's Laws.
[bio11-s5.10.b] In this cross a heterozygous tall plant is crossed with a dwarf plant. It gives a different ratio from the first example, because one parent can pass on only the recessive allele.
[bio11-s5.10.0] Parents' phenotypes: tall × dwarf.
[bio11-s5.10.1] Parents' genotypes: Tt × tt (one heterozygous parent, so only the Tt parent makes two types of gamete).
[bio11-s5.10.2] Gametes: T, t × t, because the tt parent can give only t.
[bio11-s5.10.3] Punnett square (2 × 1, or 2 × 2 with t written twice): Tt, tt.
[bio11-s5.10.4] Offspring genotypes: 1 Tt : 1 tt.
[bio11-s5.10.5] Offspring phenotypes: 1 tall : 1 dwarf (50% each), because Tt is tall and tt is dwarf.
[bio11-s5.11.b] With one gene and two alleles there are only three genotypes, TT, Tt and tt, so only six different crosses are possible. Knowing all six lets you recognise any monohybrid cross quickly.
[bio11-s5.11.0] Cross 1: TT × TT → all TT → all tall, because both parents can only pass on T.
[bio11-s5.11.1] Cross 2: TT × Tt → 1 TT : 1 Tt → all tall.
[bio11-s5.11.2] Cross 3: TT × tt → all Tt → all tall, because T masks t.
[bio11-s5.11.3] Cross 4: Tt × Tt → 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt → 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
[bio11-s5.11.4] Cross 5: Tt × tt → 1 Tt : 1 tt → 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
[bio11-s5.11.5] Cross 6: tt × tt → all tt → all dwarf, because both parents can only pass on t.
[bio11-s5.12.b] You can also work from the offspring back to the parents. The ratio of offspring tells you which genotypes the parents must have had.
[bio11-s5.12.0] Offspring in a 3 : 1 ratio mean both parents are heterozygous, because only Tt × Tt gives this ratio.
[bio11-s5.12.1] A 1 : 1 ratio means one parent is heterozygous and the other homozygous recessive, so only one parent makes two kinds of gamete.
[bio11-s5.12.2] Any offspring showing the recessive trait mean each parent carries at least one recessive allele, because an offspring needs two recessive alleles, one from each parent.
[bio11-s5.13.b] An organism showing the dominant trait could be TT or Tt, and you cannot tell by looking. A test cross finds out by crossing it with a recessive organism.
[bio11-s5.13.0] A test cross finds the unknown genotype of an organism showing the dominant trait (TT or Tt) by crossing it with a homozygous recessive (tt) organism.
[bio11-s5.13.1] The tt parent can give only t, so each offspring's phenotype shows directly which allele came from the unknown parent.
[bio11-s5.13.2] If all offspring show the dominant trait (all tall), the unknown parent is almost certainly TT (TT × tt → all Tt).
[bio11-s5.13.3] If any offspring show the recessive trait (about half dwarf), the unknown parent is Tt (Tt × tt → 1 : 1), because it must have passed on a recessive allele.
[bio11-s5.13.4] Many offspring are needed to be confident that a TT result is not chance.
[bio11-s5.14.b] Cystic fibrosis is an inherited disorder that follows the same rules as the pea plants. Two parents who do not have the disease can still have an affected child, if both are carriers.
[bio11-s5.14.0] Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on an autosome (a chromosome that is not a sex chromosome).
[bio11-s5.14.1] Key: F = normal allele, f = cystic fibrosis allele, with the normal allele dominant.
[bio11-s5.14.2] Parents: two unaffected carriers (people who carry the recessive allele but do not have the disease).
[bio11-s5.14.3] Parents' genotypes: Ff × Ff.
[bio11-s5.14.4] Gametes: F, f × F, f, because each carrier makes both types.
[bio11-s5.14.5] Offspring genotypes: 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff.
[bio11-s5.14.6] Offspring phenotypes: 3 unaffected : 1 with cystic fibrosis, because only ff has the disease.
[bio11-s5.14.7] Each child has a 1 in 4 (25%) chance of having cystic fibrosis, a 1 in 2 (50%) chance of being a carrier, and a 1 in 4 chance of being neither.
[bio11-s5.14.8] Why the mutation causes the disease is in Mutations, and its use in genetic counselling is in Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics.

CANDIDATES bio11-s7: Mendel's Laws
[bio11-s7.0.b] Mendel bred pea plants in a monastery garden and worked out the basic rules of inheritance long before anyone knew about genes, chromosomes or DNA.
[bio11-s7.0.0] Gregor Mendel (1822–1884) was an Augustinian friar, later abbot, at the monastery of St Thomas in Brno (then Brünn, in the Austrian Empire; now in the Czech Republic).
[bio11-s7.0.1] He bred garden pea plants (Pisum sativum) between 1856 and 1863, presented his results in 1865 and published them in 1866.
[bio11-s7.0.2] His work was largely ignored until 1900, when it was rediscovered independently by other botanists, including Hugo de Vries and Carl Correns.
[bio11-s7.0.3] He worked before chromosomes, meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes) or DNA were understood, so he called the units of inheritance “factors”. We now call them genes, and their different versions alleles.
[bio11-s7.1.b] Mendel's choice of organism was part of why his experiments worked. Garden peas had four features that made clean, countable results possible.
[bio11-s7.1.0] Peas had many varieties with clear contrasting forms of a trait (for example tall or dwarf, with nothing in between), so every offspring could be sorted into one group or the other.
[bio11-s7.1.1] Peas normally self-pollinate (a flower pollinates itself), so true-breeding lines were available. True-breeding means offspring always look like their parents, generation after generation.
[bio11-s7.1.2] They can be cross-pollinated by hand: the anthers (pollen-producing parts) of one flower are removed and its stigma (the pollen-receiving part) is dusted with pollen from another. This lets the experimenter choose the parents.
[bio11-s7.1.3] They grow quickly and produce many seeds, giving large numbers to count, which is needed to see clear ratios.
[bio11-s7.2.b] Mendel's method was careful and quantitative, which is why his results were clear. He followed simple, clear-cut traits and treated inheritance as something to be counted and calculated.
[bio11-s7.2.0] The seven traits he studied, dominant form first: seed shape (round / wrinkled), seed colour (yellow / green), flower colour (purple / white), pod shape (inflated / constricted), pod colour (green / yellow), flower position (axial, along the stem / terminal, at the tip), stem length (tall / dwarf).
[bio11-s7.2.1] He started with true-breeding parents and studied one trait at a time, then two. Looking at one trait at a time kept the results simple enough to interpret.
[bio11-s7.2.2] He counted large numbers of offspring, because small samples can give misleading ratios by chance.
[bio11-s7.2.3] He recorded results over several generations: P (the parents), F1 (their offspring) and F2 (the offspring of the F1 crossed with each other).
[bio11-s7.2.4] He used mathematics (ratios) to analyse the results, and repeated each cross for several traits to check that the pattern held.
[bio11-s7.3.b] Mendel's crosses gave the same pattern every time. That pattern led him to the idea that each trait is controlled by two factors, one of which can hide the other.
[bio11-s7.3.0] Crossing true-breeding tall with true-breeding dwarf gave an F1 that was all tall; none were dwarf and none were in between.
[bio11-s7.3.1] Self-pollinating the F1 gave an F2 of tall and dwarf in about 3 : 1.
[bio11-s7.3.2] F2 figures: 787 tall : 277 dwarf (2.84 : 1); 5474 round : 1850 wrinkled seeds (2.96 : 1); 6022 yellow : 2001 green seeds (3.01 : 1). Large numbers bring the ratio very close to 3 : 1.
[bio11-s7.3.3] The dwarf trait disappeared in the F1 and reappeared in the F2, so the dwarf “factor” must have been present, hidden, in the F1.
[bio11-s7.3.4] He concluded that each plant has two factors for each trait, one from each parent, and that one (dominant) can mask the other (recessive).
[bio11-s7.4.b] Mendel's first law explains the 3 : 1 ratio. It is about the two alleles of a single gene, and what happens to them when gametes (sex cells) are made.
[bio11-s7.4.0] Statement: “The two alleles (factors) of a gene separate (segregate) from each other during gamete formation, so that each gamete receives only one allele of each gene.”
[bio11-s7.4.1] Shorter accepted form: “Inherited traits are controlled by pairs of factors (alleles), which separate during gamete formation.”
[bio11-s7.4.2] A heterozygous tall plant (Tt, carrying two different alleles) forms two types of gamete, T and t, in equal numbers, so half the gametes carry T and half carry t.
[bio11-s7.4.3] Random fertilisation of these gametes in Tt × Tt gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt and 3 tall : 1 dwarf, the 3 : 1 Mendel observed. There is more on this in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s7.4.4] Chromosome basis (Mendel did not know this): the two alleles of a gene lie at the same locus (position) on the two chromosomes of a homologous pair, the matching pair of chromosomes, one from each parent.
[bio11-s7.4.5] In meiosis the homologous chromosomes separate into different cells, carrying one allele each into each gamete. This is why the alleles segregate.
[bio11-s7.4.6] Diagram: a diploid cell with one homologous pair, one chromosome carrying T and the other t at the same position, dividing by meiosis into gametes, half with T and half with t. Labels: homologous pair, allele T, allele t, locus, meiosis, gametes (haploid).
[bio11-s7.5.b] Mendel's second law deals with two different genes at once. It says that how one gene's alleles are shared out into gametes does not affect how another gene's alleles are shared out.
[bio11-s7.5.0] Statement: “When gametes form, the alleles of one gene separate independently of the alleles of another gene, so either allele of one gene can combine at random with either allele of another gene.”
[bio11-s7.5.1] Equivalent: “Each member of a pair of factors can combine randomly with either member of another pair.”
[bio11-s7.5.2] The law applies to genes on different chromosomes (unlinked genes). Genes close together on the same chromosome tend to be inherited together and break this rule.
[bio11-s7.5.3] A plant heterozygous for seed shape and seed colour (RrYy) forms four types of gamete, RY, Ry, rY and ry, in equal numbers (1/4 each).
[bio11-s7.5.4] This is because whether a gamete gets R or r has no effect on whether it gets Y or y.
[bio11-s7.5.5] Chromosome basis: when homologous pairs line up in meiosis, the way one pair lines up is independent of the way another pair lines up.
[bio11-s7.5.6] With R and Y on different chromosome pairs, half the cells put R with Y (giving RY and ry gametes) and half put R with y (giving Ry and rY gametes), so all four types are made in equal numbers.
[bio11-s7.5.7] Diagram: a cell with two homologous pairs (a long pair carrying R and r, a short pair carrying Y and y) in the two possible arrangements. Arrangement 1 gives RY and ry; arrangement 2 gives Ry and rY. Labels: homologous pairs, alleles, two equally likely arrangements, four gamete types.
[bio11-s7.6.b] A dihybrid cross follows two traits at the same time. Mendel's results from one showed that the two traits were inherited independently of each other.
[bio11-s7.6.0] Mendel's dihybrid cross: true-breeding round yellow × wrinkled green gave an F1 that was all round yellow.
[bio11-s7.6.1] Self-pollinating the F1 gave an F2 of 315 round yellow : 108 round green : 101 wrinkled yellow : 32 wrinkled green (total 556), close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
[bio11-s7.6.2] The new combinations (round green and wrinkled yellow) can only arise if the two genes are inherited independently, because neither parent had those pairings of traits. There is more on this in Dihybrid crosses.
[bio11-s7.7.b] Mendel's biggest contribution was a new way of thinking about heredity. His laws still underpin how we predict the outcome of genetic crosses.
[bio11-s7.7.0] Mendel showed that inheritance is particulate: traits are passed on by separate units that stay unchanged from generation to generation, rather than by blending of the parents' characteristics.
[bio11-s7.7.1] A blended trait could never reappear unchanged, as the dwarf trait did in the F2, so the reappearance is evidence for separate units.
[bio11-s7.7.2] His laws are the basis of the predictions in Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics.

CANDIDATES bio11-s8: Dihybrid crosses
[bio11-s8.0.b] A monohybrid cross follows one gene. A dihybrid cross follows two genes at once, which is how you predict offspring when two traits are inherited together.
[bio11-s8.0.0] A dihybrid cross follows the inheritance of two different traits (two genes) at the same time, e.g. seed shape and seed colour in peas.
[bio11-s8.0.1] “Unlinked” means the two genes are on different chromosomes, so they assort independently (are sorted into gametes without affecting each other) and every combination of their alleles is equally likely in the gametes. There is more on genes on the same chromosome in Linkage.
[bio11-s8.0.2] Key for the worked examples: R = round seed, r = wrinkled; Y = yellow seed, y = green. Capital letters are dominant alleles (the version that shows when present), lower case are recessive.
[bio11-s8.0.3] A genotype always has two letters for each gene (four letters in all), written with the two alleles of one gene together and the dominant first: RrYy, not RYry. Keeping each gene's pair together stops you mixing the genes up.
[bio11-s8.1.b] Before you can fill in a Punnett square you need the gametes (sex cells) each parent can make. This is a short method you can use on any two-gene genotype.
[bio11-s8.1.0] Each gamete gets one allele of each gene, so every gamete has two letters, one for each gene; never two of the same gene: RY, not Rr.
[bio11-s8.1.1] Method: pair each allele of the first gene with each allele of the second gene.
[bio11-s8.1.2] For RrYy: R with Y, R with y, r with Y, r with y, giving RY, Ry, rY, ry, in equal numbers. This equal mixing is the Law of Independent Assortment: the alleles of one gene are sorted into gametes independently of the other gene.
[bio11-s8.1.3] The number of gamete types depends on how many genes are heterozygous (have two different alleles): RRYY gives one type (RY); RRYy gives two (RY, Ry); Rryy gives two (Ry, ry).
[bio11-s8.1.4] RrYy, heterozygous for both genes, gives four (RY, Ry, rY, ry); rryy gives one (ry). Write each different gamete once, however many times it could be made.
[bio11-s8.2.b] The first step in Mendel's style of cross is to breed two pure lines together. The offspring, the F1 (first filial) generation, are all identical, which sets up the F2.
[bio11-s8.2.0] Parents' phenotypes (the visible traits): round yellow × wrinkled green (both true-breeding, meaning homozygous, so they always pass on the same alleles).
[bio11-s8.2.1] Parents' genotypes: RRYY × rryy, so each parent is homozygous for both genes.
[bio11-s8.2.2] Gametes: RY × ry, because each parent has only one type of gamete.
[bio11-s8.2.3] F1 genotype: all RrYy, as every offspring gets one dominant and one recessive allele of each gene.
[bio11-s8.2.4] F1 phenotype: all round yellow (both dominant traits), because the dominant alleles hide the recessive ones.
[bio11-s8.3.b] Crossing two F1 plants is the core dihybrid cross. Both parents are heterozygous for both genes, so each makes four types of gamete and the Punnett square (a grid showing every possible gamete pairing) is 4 × 4.
[bio11-s8.3.0] Parents (F1): round yellow × round yellow. Genotypes: RrYy × RrYy, so both are heterozygous for both genes.
[bio11-s8.3.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × RY, Ry, rY, ry.
[bio11-s8.3.2] Punnett square: 4 × 4 = 16 boxes, each a 1/16 chance, because every gamete pairing is equally likely.
[bio11-s8.3.3] Punnett square, row by row (gametes RY, Ry, rY, ry across the top and down the side). Row 1 (gamete RY): RRYY, RRYy, RrYY, RrYy. Row 2 (gamete Ry): RRYy, RRyy, RrYy, Rryy.
[bio11-s8.3.4] Row 3 (gamete rY): RrYY, RrYy, rrYY, rrYy. Row 4 (gamete ry): RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy.
[bio11-s8.3.5] F2 genotypes (nine kinds): 1 RRYY, 2 RRYy, 2 RrYY, 4 RrYy, 1 RRyy, 2 Rryy, 1 rrYY, 2 rrYy, 1 rryy (total 16). Count repeated genotypes from the squares to get these numbers.
[bio11-s8.4.b] The 16 boxes contain nine genotypes but only four phenotypes, because a dominant allele hides the recessive one. Counting those phenotypes gives the famous dihybrid ratio.
[bio11-s8.4.0] F2 phenotypes: 9 round yellow (at least one R and at least one Y) : 3 round green (at least one R, yy) : 3 wrinkled yellow (rr, at least one Y) : 1 wrinkled green (rryy).
[bio11-s8.4.1] The ratio 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is the signature of a dihybrid F2 with two unlinked genes, each with complete dominance (the heterozygote looks the same as the dominant homozygote). There is more on Mendel's F2 counts, close to this ratio, in Mendel's Laws.
[bio11-s8.5.b] Look at the F2 phenotypes against the original parents. Some combinations of traits appear that neither the P nor the F1 plants showed.
[bio11-s8.5.0] Two of the four F2 phenotypes (round green and wrinkled yellow) are new combinations, found in neither the P nor the F1 generation.
[bio11-s8.5.1] They appear because the genes assort independently, so any seed shape allele can end up with any seed colour allele. They are a source of variation in offspring.
[bio11-s8.5.2] Diagram: the full 4 × 4 Punnett square of the F1 × F1 cross, with gametes labelled on both sides, and the boxes coloured or marked by phenotype (9 round yellow, 3 round green, 3 wrinkled yellow, 1 wrinkled green).
[bio11-s8.6.b] A 16-box square is slow and easy to miscount. Because the two genes are independent, you can work out each gene separately and multiply, which gives a quick check.
[bio11-s8.6.0] Because the genes are independent, the dihybrid result is the two monohybrid results multiplied.
[bio11-s8.6.1] Rr × Rr gives 3/4 round and 1/4 wrinkled; Yy × Yy gives 3/4 yellow and 1/4 green, so each gene is treated as its own monohybrid cross.
[bio11-s8.6.2] So round yellow = 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16, round green = 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16, wrinkled yellow = 3/16, wrinkled green = 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.
[bio11-s8.6.3] Use this to check a Punnett square; show the full square in an exam answer when asked for a genetic cross.
[bio11-s8.7.b] A test cross crosses an individual showing the dominant traits with one that is homozygous recessive for both genes. The recessive parent makes only one type of gamete, so the offspring reveal exactly which gametes the other parent makes.
[bio11-s8.7.0] Round yellow (RrYy) × wrinkled green (rryy) is the dihybrid test cross.
[bio11-s8.7.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × ry, because rryy can only make ry.
[bio11-s8.7.2] Offspring genotypes: RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy (1 : 1 : 1 : 1).
[bio11-s8.7.3] Phenotypes: 1 round yellow : 1 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green (25% each), because each of the four gametes has a 1/4 chance.
[bio11-s8.7.4] A 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 result shows that the dominant-looking parent was heterozygous for both genes and that the genes assort independently. There is more on the same cross with linked genes in Linkage.
[bio11-s8.7.5] The same pattern with other traits: in peas with tall (T) dominant to dwarf (t) and yellow (Y) dominant to green (y), TtYy × ttyy gives 1 tall yellow (TtYy) : 1 tall green (Ttyy) : 1 dwarf yellow (ttYy) : 1 dwarf green (ttyy).
[bio11-s8.8.b] Not every cross has two double heterozygotes. Here one parent is heterozygous for only one gene, so it makes just two gamete types and the square is 4 × 2.
[bio11-s8.8.0] Round yellow × round green: RrYy × Rryy, so the second parent is homozygous recessive for colour and heterozygous for shape.
[bio11-s8.8.1] Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry × Ry, ry.
[bio11-s8.8.2] Punnett square: 4 × 2 = 8 boxes; column Ry: RRYy, RRyy, RrYy, Rryy; column ry: RrYy, Rryy, rrYy, rryy.
[bio11-s8.8.3] Phenotypes: 3 round yellow : 3 round green : 1 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green (3 : 3 : 1 : 1).
[bio11-s8.8.4] Check: 3/4 round × 1/2 yellow = 3/8 round yellow, matching the 3 round yellow out of 8 boxes.
[bio11-s8.9.b] When one gene shows incomplete dominance, the heterozygote has a blend in between the two homozygotes. This changes the phenotype count but the method for crossing two genes stays the same.
[bio11-s8.9.0] Snapdragons: tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t); red (R) and white (W) flower colour show incomplete dominance (RW = pink).
[bio11-s8.9.1] Parents' phenotypes: homozygous dwarf red × tall pink (heterozygous for height). Parents' genotypes: ttRR × TtRW.
[bio11-s8.9.2] Gametes: tR × TR, TW, tR, tW, because the ttRR parent can only make tR while the TtRW parent makes four types.
[bio11-s8.9.3] Offspring: TtRR (tall, red), TtRW (tall, pink), ttRR (dwarf, red), ttRW (dwarf, pink), in a 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ratio.
[bio11-s8.9.4] So 50% of the offspring have pink flowers (two of four genotypes) and 50% are tall.
[bio11-s8.9.5] To the second generation: TTRR × ttWW gives an F1 of all TtRW (tall pink).
[bio11-s8.9.6] F1 × F1 gives 3 tall red : 6 tall pink : 3 tall white : 1 dwarf red : 2 dwarf pink : 1 dwarf white (out of 16).
[bio11-s8.9.7] These are found by multiplying 3/4 tall and 1/4 dwarf by 1/4 red, 1/2 pink and 1/4 white, because the two genes are independent.

CANDIDATES bio11-s9: Linkage
[bio11-s9.0.b] Genes that sit on the same chromosome are passed on together, because a chromosome goes into a gamete as one piece. This is called linkage.
[bio11-s9.0.0] Each chromosome carries many genes in a fixed order along one DNA molecule, and a chromosome passes into a gamete (a sex cell) as one unit, so any two genes on it travel together.
[bio11-s9.0.1] Linked genes are not joined to each other in any special way. They are simply on the same DNA molecule, which is why they are inherited together.
[bio11-s9.0.2] All the genes on one chromosome form a linkage group, so there are as many linkage groups as there are chromosome pairs.
[bio11-s9.0.3] Linked genotypes show the alleles (versions of a gene) on each chromosome together, with a line or slash for the pair of chromosomes. AB/ab means one chromosome carries A and B and its homologue (the matching chromosome of the pair) carries a and b.
[bio11-s9.0.4] Diagram: two vertical bars (a homologous pair), one labelled with A above B, the other with a above b, at matching positions; label “homologous chromosomes”, “linked genes” and “locus”.
[bio11-s9.1.b] Independent assortment means the alleles of one gene are sorted into gametes without affecting the alleles of another gene. That only works if the genes are on separate chromosomes, so linkage is an exception to it.
[bio11-s9.1.0] The Law of Independent Assortment holds only for genes on different chromosomes. The Law of Segregation still applies to each gene, so linkage is an exception to one law, not a refutation of Mendel.
[bio11-s9.1.1] Linked genes do not assort independently: the alleles on one chromosome move together into the same gamete when the homologous chromosomes separate in meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes).
[bio11-s9.1.2] A double heterozygote (heterozygous for both genes) with linked genes, AB/ab, produces only two types of gamete, AB and ab, in equal numbers.
[bio11-s9.1.3] An unlinked double heterozygote produces four types (AB, Ab, aB, ab) instead. The gametes Ab and aB (new combinations) are not formed when the genes are linked, because A and B stay together on one chromosome.
[bio11-s9.1.4] Offspring show only (or mostly) the parental combinations of traits, while the new combinations seen with unlinked genes are missing (or rare).
[bio11-s9.1.5] So linkage gives fewer types of offspring and less variation, and the ratios differ from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 and 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
[bio11-s9.1.6] In real organisms, linked genes are occasionally separated, so a small number of offspring with new combinations can appear. The reason is crossing over, and genes are more likely to stay together the closer they lie on the chromosome.
[bio11-s9.1.7] Diagram: side by side, (a) unlinked genes: two chromosome pairs (A/a on one, B/b on the other) giving four gamete types AB, Ab, aB, ab; (b) linked genes: one chromosome pair, A and B on one homologue, a and b on the other, giving two gamete types AB and ab.
[bio11-s9.2.b] A test cross mates an individual of unknown genotype with a homozygous recessive one, so the offspring reveal which gametes it makes. With linked genes, only two kinds of offspring appear.
[bio11-s9.2.0] Key: A = dominant allele of gene 1, a = recessive; B = dominant allele of gene 2, b = recessive; A and B on the same chromosome.
[bio11-s9.2.1] Parents' phenotypes: AB/ab shows both dominant traits; ab/ab shows both recessive traits. Parents' genotypes: AB/ab × ab/ab.
[bio11-s9.2.2] Gametes: AB, ab × ab. The recessive parent can only give ab, so each offspring's type shows which gamete came from the other parent.
[bio11-s9.2.3] Offspring genotypes: AB/ab (AaBb) and ab/ab (aabb), 1 : 1. Offspring phenotypes: 1 showing both dominant traits : 1 showing both recessive traits.
[bio11-s9.2.4] If the genes were unlinked, the same cross (AaBb × aabb) would give four phenotypes, 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. Linkage removes the two new combinations.
[bio11-s9.2.5] The arrangement matters. If the double heterozygote has the dominant allele of one gene with the recessive allele of the other on each chromosome (Ab/aB), its gametes are Ab and aB, and the test cross gives 1 Aabb : 1 aaBb, i.e. only the two mixed phenotypes.
[bio11-s9.2.6] Always read which alleles are on the same chromosome, because AB/ab and Ab/aB are different genotypes that give different offspring.
[bio11-s9.3.b] Crossing two linked double heterozygotes (the F1, or first-generation offspring) shows how linkage changes the familiar dihybrid ratio.
[bio11-s9.3.0] Parents: AB/ab × AB/ab.
[bio11-s9.3.1] Gametes: AB, ab × AB, ab. Each parent makes only two gamete types because the genes are linked.
[bio11-s9.3.2] Punnett square: AB/AB, AB/ab / AB/ab, ab/ab.
[bio11-s9.3.3] Offspring genotypes: 1 AABB : 2 AaBb : 1 aabb.
[bio11-s9.3.4] Phenotypes: 3 both dominant : 1 both recessive (3 : 1), compared with 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 if the genes were unlinked, because no offspring show a dominant and a recessive trait together.
[bio11-s9.4.b] The fruit fly gives a real case of two genes inherited together. Both genes sit on the same chromosome, so the results of a test cross do not match the unlinked pattern.
[bio11-s9.4.0] In the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, the genes for body colour (grey dominant to black) and wing shape (normal wings dominant to vestigial wings) are on the same chromosome.
[bio11-s9.4.1] A test cross of a fly heterozygous for both (with grey and normal on one chromosome) with a black, vestigial fly gives mostly grey normal-winged and black vestigial-winged offspring, the parental combinations.
[bio11-s9.4.2] Only a few grey vestigial or black normal flies appear, not the 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 expected for unlinked genes, because the genes are usually inherited together.
[bio11-s9.5.b] In an exam you may be given offspring results and asked whether the genes are linked. You decide by comparing them with the ratios expected for unlinked genes.
[bio11-s9.5.0] A dihybrid cross (a cross following two genes) whose offspring show mainly the parental combinations suggests that the two genes are linked.
[bio11-s9.5.1] Fewer phenotype classes than expected, or a ratio far from 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (F1 × F1) or 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 (test cross), also suggests that the two genes are linked, because linked alleles stay together instead of mixing.

CANDIDATES bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio12-s3: Evidence for evolution
[bio12-s3.0.b] Fossils are the actual remains or traces of organisms that lived long ago. To use them as evidence you need to know how they form and how old they are.
[bio12-s3.0.0] A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago, for example bones, shells, teeth, footprints or impressions of leaves.
[bio12-s3.0.1] Most fossils form when an organism is quickly buried in sediment (mud or sand) in water. Its soft parts decay, and minerals gradually replace the hard parts as the sediment becomes sedimentary rock (rock formed from compacted sediment).
[bio12-s3.0.2] Whole organisms are occasionally preserved in amber (hardened tree resin), ice or peat, which is why these finds are rare and valuable.
[bio12-s3.0.3] In undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, deeper layers are older, because new sediment settles on top of old. So fossils in deeper layers are older than those above them.
[bio12-s3.0.4] Radiometric dating measures the decay of radioactive elements in rocks, and gives ages in years rather than just older or younger.
[bio12-s3.0.5] The Earth is about 4.6 billion years old. The oldest widely accepted fossils, of simple bacteria-like cells, are about 3.5 billion years old.
[bio12-s3.0.6] Diagram: a cross-section of rock layers with fossils, the deepest layer labelled oldest and the top labelled youngest, showing simpler forms in the lower layers; optionally, three horse feet from Hyracotherium to the modern horse showing toe reduction.
[bio12-s3.1.b] Laid out in order of age, the fossils show a pattern: life changes over time in a consistent direction. This is what evolution predicts and is why the record counts as evidence.
[bio12-s3.1.0] The oldest rocks contain only simple, single-celled life, so complex organisms had not yet appeared.
[bio12-s3.1.1] More complex organisms appear in younger rocks in a consistent order, for example fish before amphibians, and reptiles before birds and mammals.
[bio12-s3.1.2] Most species that ever lived are extinct, so the living world is only a small part of the history of life.
[bio12-s3.1.3] In many groups a series of fossils shows gradual change over time, which is what you would expect if one form slowly gave rise to another.
[bio12-s3.2.b] Some fossils show features of two different groups. These are called transitional fossils, and Archaeopteryx and Tiktaalik are two examples.
[bio12-s3.2.0] Archaeopteryx was found in Germany in 1861, two years after On the Origin of Species, and is about 150 million years old.
[bio12-s3.2.1] It had feathers and wings like a bird, but also teeth, a long bony tail and clawed fingers like a reptile (a dinosaur). So it shows features of both groups.
[bio12-s3.2.2] Tiktaalik was found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic and is about 375 million years old. It was a fish with scales, fins and gills.
[bio12-s3.2.3] It also had a flat head, a neck, and fin bones like the bones of a limb, so it was intermediate between fish and the first four-limbed land animals.
[bio12-s3.2.4] The team searched rocks of that age because the theory predicted such an animal should have lived then, and the prediction was confirmed. There is more on this in The importance of the theory.
[bio12-s3.3.b] A long series of horse fossils shows gradual change in one group. The record is also incomplete, so you need to know why gaps exist and why they do not weaken the case.
[bio12-s3.3.0] The horse series runs over more than 50 million years, from Hyracotherium, a small animal about the size of a dog with four toes on its front feet, to the modern horse.
[bio12-s3.3.1] The modern horse is much larger, stands on a single toe (the hoof), and has teeth suited to grazing. This matches a change from forest to open grassland.
[bio12-s3.3.2] The fossil record is incomplete, because fossils form only in rare conditions and soft-bodied organisms seldom fossilise.
[bio12-s3.3.3] Many fossils have also been destroyed by erosion or movements of the Earth, and many have not yet been found.
[bio12-s3.3.4] The gaps do not contradict evolution, and every new fossil found has fitted the expected order.
[bio12-s3.4.b] Comparing the bodies of different species shows similarities that are hard to explain unless the species share ancestors. Structures with the same basic plan but different jobs are called homologous.
[bio12-s3.4.0] Homologous structures have the same basic structure because they were inherited from a common ancestor, but are adapted for different functions.
[bio12-s3.4.1] The pentadactyl (five-digit) limb is the classic example: the human arm (grasping), bat wing (flying), whale flipper (swimming) and horse leg (running).
[bio12-s3.4.2] All of them have one upper bone (humerus), two lower bones (radius and ulna), wrist bones (carpals), palm bones (metacarpals) and finger bones (phalanges), differently shaped and sized.
[bio12-s3.4.3] This is divergent evolution: one ancestral form adapted in different directions, so the same bones were modified for different uses.
[bio12-s3.4.4] Diagram: the forelimbs of a human, a bat and a whale side by side, each bone group shaded the same way in all three; label humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges.
[bio12-s3.5.b] Not every similarity points to a shared ancestor, and some structures have lost their original job. Analogous and vestigial structures show two different things.
[bio12-s3.5.0] Analogous structures have the same function but different structures and origins, for example the wing of a bird (bones and feathers) and the wing of an insect (a thin membrane of chitin with no bones).
[bio12-s3.5.1] Analogous structures are not evidence of common ancestry. They show that similar environments select similar solutions, which is called convergent evolution.
[bio12-s3.5.2] Vestigial structures are reduced, with little or no function, but were functional in the organism's ancestors.
[bio12-s3.5.3] Examples are the human coccyx (the remains of a tail), the human appendix (a much-reduced part of the gut, larger in plant-eating mammals), the small pelvic and leg bones inside some whales, and the tiny wings of flightless birds such as the kiwi.
[bio12-s3.5.4] Vestigial structures make sense only if the organism descended from ancestors that used them.
[bio12-s3.6.b] Embryos (animals in the earliest stages of development) can reveal ancestry that adult bodies hide. Vertebrates, the animals with backbones, are a good example.
[bio12-s3.6.0] Comparative embryology compares the development of embryos. The early embryos of all vertebrates (fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals) look very similar: all have a tail and a series of pouches in the throat region (pharyngeal pouches).
[bio12-s3.6.1] In fish these pouches develop into gills; in humans they develop into parts of the ear, jaw and throat, and the tail is reduced to the coccyx.
[bio12-s3.6.2] The shared early stages suggest that all vertebrates inherited the same basic plan of development from a common ancestor, which was then modified in each group.
[bio12-s3.6.3] Limitations: the similarity is in early development, not in adult structures, and embryos do not pass through the adult forms of their ancestors.
[bio12-s3.6.4] In the 19th century Ernst Haeckel published drawings that exaggerated the similarity of embryos; modern photographs confirm the shared features but not the exaggeration.
[bio12-s3.6.5] Diagram: early embryos of a fish, a chicken and a human side by side, labelled tail and pharyngeal pouches.
[bio12-s3.7.b] Evolution predicts that the more closely two species are related, the more similar their DNA and proteins should be. Comparing these molecules tests that prediction independently of anatomy.
[bio12-s3.7.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group; phylogenetic trees show which species share the most recent common ancestors. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s3.7.1] Species closely related by anatomy and fossils should also have the most similar DNA and proteins, because they have had less time to accumulate differences since their common ancestor.
[bio12-s3.7.2] All living things use DNA, the same four bases, almost the same genetic code and the same ATP (the energy-carrying molecule of cells). This is evidence that all life shares one origin.
[bio12-s3.7.3] Human and chimpanzee DNA is about 98–99% identical in comparable sequences; human DNA is less similar to that of a mouse, and less again to that of a fish.
[bio12-s3.7.4] Cytochrome c is a protein used in aerobic respiration in nearly all eukaryotes (organisms whose cells have a nucleus). Its amino-acid sequence is identical in humans and chimpanzees.
[bio12-s3.7.5] It differs by one amino acid between humans and rhesus monkeys, and differs more in more distantly related organisms such as yeast.
[bio12-s3.7.6] The trees built from DNA and proteins largely agree with those built from anatomy and fossils. Independent lines of evidence giving the same pattern are strong support for evolution.
[bio12-s3.7.7] Molecular evidence has also corrected some classifications based on appearance, for example the domains of life (the three largest groupings of living things).
[bio12-s3.8.b] Bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics, and the way this happens is natural selection taking place in front of us. Once you can describe the steps, you can explain it in an exam.
[bio12-s3.8.0] An antibiotic is a drug that kills or stops the growth of bacteria; antibiotics have no effect on viruses. Antibiotic resistance is the ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them.
[bio12-s3.8.1] 1. A bacterial population varies: a few bacteria carry a mutation (or a resistance gene on a plasmid, a small ring of DNA) that makes them resistant, for example by producing an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic.
[bio12-s3.8.2] 2. The antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria.
[bio12-s3.8.3] 3. The resistant bacteria survive and, with less competition, reproduce rapidly by binary fission (splitting in two), as often as every 20 minutes in ideal conditions.
[bio12-s3.8.4] 4. They pass the resistance gene to their offspring, and can pass plasmids carrying it to other bacteria, even of other species.
[bio12-s3.8.5] 5. The population soon becomes mostly resistant, and the antibiotic no longer works.
[bio12-s3.8.6] Diagram: four panels of a bacterial population: mostly non-resistant cells with a few resistant ones (shaded), then antibiotic added and non-resistant cells die, then resistant cells multiply, then the population is mostly resistant.
[bio12-s3.9.b] A common mistake is to think antibiotics create resistance. The evidence shows that resistance is already there, and that selection simply favours it.
[bio12-s3.9.0] The resistance mutations are present before the antibiotic is used: the antibiotic selects them but does not cause them.
[bio12-s3.9.1] In 1952 Joshua and Esther Lederberg showed this by growing bacteria that had never met the antibiotic and finding resistant colonies already among them.
[bio12-s3.9.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many common antibiotics and causes infections that are hard to treat, especially in hospitals.
[bio12-s3.9.3] Natural selection is observed directly, within years, because bacteria have huge populations and very short generation times.
[bio12-s3.9.4] Overuse of antibiotics, for example prescribing them for viral infections or in farm animals, speeds up resistance by applying selection more often. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio12-s3.10.b] Natural selection can be seen in wild populations too, and the way species are spread across the world supports common descent.
[bio12-s3.10.0] Peter and Rosemary Grant studied the medium ground finch on the Galápagos island of Daphne Major. In a drought in 1977, small soft seeds became scarce and mainly large, hard seeds were left.
[bio12-s3.10.1] Most finches died, but birds with deeper (larger) beaks, which could crack the hard seeds, survived more, and their offspring had, on average, deeper beaks than the generation before.
[bio12-s3.10.2] Natural selection was measured happening in one generation.
[bio12-s3.10.3] Biogeography (the distribution of species): species on oceanic islands resemble those on the nearest mainland rather than those on islands with similar conditions elsewhere. The Galápagos species resemble South American species.
[bio12-s3.10.4] This is explained by colonisation from the mainland followed by evolution, not by separate creation for each environment.
[bio12-s3.11.b] Speciation is the formation of new species, and it is evidence that evolution can produce the diversity of life. One route to speciation is when a population is split by a physical barrier.
[bio12-s3.11.0] A species is a group that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so a new species has formed when two populations can no longer interbreed: they are reproductively isolated. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio12-s3.11.1] 1. A population is split by a barrier, such as a sea, a mountain range or a river, so the two groups no longer interbreed.
[bio12-s3.11.2] 2. Different mutations arise in each group.
[bio12-s3.11.3] 3. The environments differ, so natural selection favours different characteristics in each.
[bio12-s3.11.4] 4. Over many generations the two populations become so different that they could not interbreed even if they met; they are now separate species.
[bio12-s3.11.5] Diagram: a flow diagram of geographical speciation: one population, then a barrier divides it, then different mutations and selection on each side, then two species that can't interbreed.
[bio12-s3.12.b] The finches of the Galápagos show geographical speciation in a real place. Different islands acted as separate environments.
[bio12-s3.12.0] More than a dozen species of finch on the Galápagos are descended from a common ancestor that arrived from the South American mainland.
[bio12-s3.12.1] Populations on different islands, eating different foods, evolved beaks of different shapes, for example large, deep beaks for cracking seeds and thin, pointed beaks for catching insects.
[bio12-s3.12.2] Why it is evidence: new species have been seen to form, and the pattern of species (groups of similar species on neighbouring islands) is what the theory predicts.
[bio12-s3.13.b] A barrier is not always needed. In plants, a change in the number of chromosomes can make a new species almost at once.
[bio12-s3.13.0] In plants, a new species can form in one or a few generations when a change in chromosome number (polyploidy, having extra sets of chromosomes) makes offspring unable to breed with the parent species. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s3.13.1] The cordgrass Spartina anglica arose in southern England in the late 1800s from a cross between a native European cordgrass and an introduced American one.
[bio12-s3.13.2] The chromosomes then doubled, which made the hybrid fertile. It is now a separate, widespread species, including on Irish coasts.
[bio12-s3.14.b] Each source of evidence has weak points, but together they make a strong case because they are independent of each other.
[bio12-s3.14.0] Each source has limitations: an incomplete fossil record, embryos similar only early on, and analogous structures that can mislead.
[bio12-s3.14.1] The sources are independent of each other and all point to the same conclusion: common descent with change over time, driven largely by natural selection.
[bio12-s3.14.2] Antibiotic resistance, the finch beaks, the peppered moth and new species such as Spartina anglica show evolution happening now. There is more on the peppered moth in The theory of evolution by natural selection.

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.
