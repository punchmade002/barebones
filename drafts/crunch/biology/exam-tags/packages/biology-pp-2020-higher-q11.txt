PART (b)(v) (15 marks) subsections: bio14-s3
MODEL ANSWER: Pathway 2 is the non-cyclic pathway. Light energises electrons, which leave chlorophyll and pass along carriers (ATP is formed from ADP and Pi as they lose energy). The electrons are trapped by NADP+, which takes two electrons and a proton (H+), forming NADPH (NADP- attracts H+). The chlorophyll, now positively charged, pulls electrons from water: photolysis splits water into protons (H+) and electrons (e-), 2H2O -> 4H+ + 4e- + O2. These electrons pass to the chlorophyll, replacing those it lost. O2 is formed as a by-product. ATP is formed.

PART (c)(iv) (12 marks) subsections: bio15-s4, bio15-s5
MODEL ANSWER: NADH delivers high-energy electrons (and protons) to the start of the chain. The electrons are passed from carrier to carrier along the chain in the inner mitochondrial membrane. At each transfer they lose energy, which is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi. At the end of the chain the low-energy electrons combine with oxygen and H+ to form water (oxygen is the final electron acceptor).

CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio14-s3: The light-dependent reactions
[bio14-s3.0.b] The light-dependent reactions start when light hits a leaf. Before any chemistry can happen, the plant has to catch that light energy and channel it to one special chlorophyll molecule, which uses it to fire off an electron.
[bio14-s3.0.0] Light travels as tiny packets of energy called photons. A pigment is a molecule that absorbs light of particular colours (wavelengths).
[bio14-s3.0.1] The thylakoid membranes hold several pigments: chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and yellow and orange pigments called carotenoids. Each absorbs slightly different colours, so having several widens the range of light the plant can use.
[bio14-s3.0.2] The pigment molecules sit in clusters of hundreds, called light-harvesting or antenna complexes, around a reaction centre. The cluster works like a satellite dish, collecting light over a wide area and focusing it on one point.
[bio14-s3.0.3] When any pigment in the cluster absorbs a photon, the energy is passed from one pigment molecule to the next until it reaches the reaction centre, a special chlorophyll a molecule.
[bio14-s3.0.4] At the reaction centre, the energy raises (excites) one of the chlorophyll's electrons to a high energy level. This energised electron leaves the chlorophyll and is taken up by an electron acceptor, the first carrier of an electron transport chain.
[bio14-s3.0.5] Because an electron carries a negative charge, losing one leaves the reaction-centre chlorophyll positively charged. It must get an electron back before it can fire off another one, and in the non-cyclic pathway it takes that electron from water.
[bio14-s3.1.b] The energised electron now has to go somewhere useful. In the non-cyclic pathway, it travels one way, from water to NADP⁺, and along the way its energy is used to make the two things the second stage of photosynthesis needs: ATP and NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.0] 1. Light energy reaches the reaction centre, and energised electrons leave the chlorophyll.
[bio14-s3.1.1] 2. The electrons are passed along an electron transport chain, a series of electron carriers in the thylakoid membrane. At each hand-over they lose a little energy, like a ball bouncing down a staircase, and this released energy is used to make ATP from ADP and phosphate (Pi).
[bio14-s3.1.2] 3. At the end of the chain, the electrons, now at lower energy, are passed to NADP⁺, a hydrogen carrier. Each NADP⁺ takes two electrons and one proton (H⁺) to form NADPH: NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.3] 4. The chlorophyll is still positively charged, so it strongly attracts electrons and pulls them from water molecules. This splits the water, a step called photolysis (splitting by light, because the energy for it ultimately came from light): 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂.
[bio14-s3.1.4] 5. The electrons from water replace the ones the chlorophyll lost, so it is neutral again and ready to absorb more energy.
[bio14-s3.1.5] The protons (H⁺) from photolysis are used to form NADPH, and they also build up inside the thylakoid, where this crowd of protons is used to power ATP-making. There is more on this in The chloroplast's internal structure.
[bio14-s3.1.6] The oxygen from photolysis is a by-product. It is used in the cell's respiration or released through the stomata.
[bio14-s3.1.7] It is called non-cyclic because the electrons go one way only, from water through the chlorophyll and the chain to NADP⁺, and never return to where they started. The electrons aren't used up; they end up on NADPH.
[bio14-s3.1.8] Products of the non-cyclic pathway: ATP, NADPH and oxygen.
[bio14-s3.2.b] Sometimes the energised electrons take a shortcut. In the cyclic pathway they loop back to the chlorophyll they came from, making ATP on the way but nothing else.
[bio14-s3.2.0] In the cyclic pathway, some energised electrons leaving the reaction-centre chlorophyll pass along carriers of the electron transport chain and then return to the same chlorophyll molecule, instead of going on to NADP⁺.
[bio14-s3.2.1] As they pass along the carriers, they give up their extra energy, which is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio14-s3.2.2] Because the electrons come back, the chlorophyll is neutral again without needing electrons from water. So water is not split, no oxygen is released and no NADPH is made.
[bio14-s3.2.3] Products of the cyclic pathway: ATP only.
[bio14-s3.2.4] Why the plant needs it: the light-independent reactions use more ATP than NADPH, 3 ATP for every 2 NADPH for each CO₂ molecule fixed (built into sugar) in the light-independent reactions, the second stage that makes glucose. The cyclic pathway tops up the extra ATP.
[bio14-s3.2.5] It also runs when NADP⁺ is in short supply because most of it is already loaded as NADPH. The energised electrons then still have somewhere to go.
[bio14-s3.3.b] Exams ask you to tell the two pathways apart. The differences all come back to one thing: where the electrons end up.
[bio14-s3.3.0] Route of the electrons: non-cyclic goes one way, from water to NADP⁺; cyclic goes from the chlorophyll round the carriers and back to the same chlorophyll.
[bio14-s3.3.1] Final electron acceptor: NADP⁺ in the non-cyclic pathway; the chlorophyll molecule itself in the cyclic pathway.
[bio14-s3.3.2] Water: split by photolysis in the non-cyclic pathway, to replace the lost electrons; not needed in the cyclic pathway, because the electrons come back.
[bio14-s3.3.3] Products: non-cyclic makes ATP, NADPH and oxygen; cyclic makes ATP only.
[bio14-s3.3.4] What they share: both start with light energy reaching the reaction centre and an energised electron leaving the chlorophyll, both pass electrons along carriers to make ATP, and both happen on the thylakoid membranes.
[bio14-s3.3.5] Older Irish textbooks and past papers call the cyclic pathway pathway 1 and the non-cyclic pathway pathway 2, and describe NADP⁺ first picking up two electrons (becoming NADP⁻) and then a proton. Recognise these names, but learn the single step to NADPH given above.
[bio14-s3.3.6] Diagram: one energy diagram with chlorophyll at the bottom receiving light from pigment molecules; the electron rising to an acceptor; a downhill chain (ATP made) ending at NADP⁺ → NADPH; water entering below the chlorophyll; a loop back to the chlorophyll for the cyclic pathway (ATP made).
[bio14-s3.4.b] Put together, the light-dependent reactions turn light into a form of energy the rest of the chloroplast can use, and hand it on to the second stage.
[bio14-s3.4.0] Location: the thylakoid membranes (grana) of the chloroplast, because that is where the pigments and electron carriers are held.
[bio14-s3.4.1] Inputs: light energy, water, ADP + Pi and NADP⁺.
[bio14-s3.4.2] Outputs: ATP and NADPH, which pass into the stroma for the light-independent reactions, and oxygen, released as a by-product.
[bio14-s3.4.3] Energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in ATP and in the high-energy electrons carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s3.4.4] The two products have different jobs: ATP carries energy, and NADPH carries electrons and hydrogen to reduce carbon dioxide to glucose. After use, they return as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ to be reloaded. There is more on this in The light-independent reactions.

CANDIDATES bio15-s4: Second-stage process
[bio15-s4.0.b] Glycolysis (the first stage of respiration, in the cytoplasm) only splits glucose part of the way and ends with pyruvate. If oxygen is available, the cell passes pyruvate on to the second stage, and the first job there is a short link step that prepares it for the citric acid cycle.
[bio15-s4.0.0] When oxygen is present, each pyruvate (a 3-carbon molecule, C₃) made in glycolysis moves from the cytoplasm into the matrix of a mitochondrion. The matrix is the fluid-filled space inside the inner membrane.
[bio15-s4.0.1] In the matrix, pyruvate is broken down. One carbon atom is removed as one molecule of carbon dioxide, and hydrogen is removed and picked up by NAD⁺ (a hydrogen carrier), which turns it into NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.2] The remaining two-carbon (acetyl) group is joined to coenzyme A, a carrier molecule, forming acetyl coenzyme A (acetyl CoA, C₂). Per pyruvate: pyruvate + coenzyme A + NAD⁺ → acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH.
[bio15-s4.0.3] Each glucose gives two pyruvate, so this link step happens twice per glucose, producing 2 CO₂ and 2 NADH.
[bio15-s4.1.b] Acetyl CoA is the fuel for the citric acid cycle, which strips the carbon from the acetyl group off as CO₂ and loads hydrogen onto carriers. Because the cycle rebuilds its starting compound every turn, it can keep running as long as acetyl CoA keeps arriving.
[bio15-s4.1.0] The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, is a cycle of enzyme-controlled reactions in the matrix of the mitochondrion, not on the cristae (the folds of the inner membrane).
[bio15-s4.1.1] 1. The acetyl CoA passes its two-carbon acetyl group into the cycle, where it joins a four-carbon compound to form citric acid (C₆), which gives the cycle its name. Coenzyme A is released to collect another acetyl group.
[bio15-s4.1.2] 2. In a series of steps, citric acid is broken down back to the four-carbon compound. This is regenerated to accept the next acetyl group, which is why it is a cycle.
[bio15-s4.1.3] 3. During each turn, two carbon atoms are removed as two molecules of CO₂, so the carbon of the acetyl group leaves as CO₂.
[bio15-s4.1.4] 4. Hydrogen, as protons and electrons, is removed at several steps and loaded onto NAD⁺, forming NADH. A small amount of ATP, one per turn, is also made directly.
[bio15-s4.1.5] The cycle turns twice for each glucose, once per acetyl CoA. With the link step, all six carbon atoms of glucose have now left as six molecules of CO₂, matching the 6CO₂ in the respiration equation. Glycolysis releases none.
[bio15-s4.1.6] The specification's products are CO₂ and H₂O. The CO₂ is released directly. The hydrogen removed is carried by NADH to the electron transport chain, where it ends up in water, so water is not made in the cycle itself.
[bio15-s4.1.7] The cycle does not need oxygen directly, but it stops without oxygen, because NADH cannot be unloaded and NAD⁺ runs out.
[bio15-s4.2.b] Most of the energy in glucose is still locked up in the hydrogen carried by NADH. The electron transport chain releases it in small steps and uses it to make ATP, which is why far more ATP per glucose is made in the second stage than the net 2 ATP of glycolysis. Aerobic respiration (respiration with oxygen) therefore releases many times more energy per glucose than fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.0] The electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers (protein complexes and mobile carriers) in the inner mitochondrial membrane, especially on the folds called cristae.
[bio15-s4.2.1] 1. NADH from glycolysis, the link step and the citric acid cycle delivers its high-energy electrons and protons to the start of the chain. NAD⁺ is released to collect more.
[bio15-s4.2.2] 2. The electrons are passed from carrier to carrier, each at a lower energy level. The energy released at each transfer is used to make ATP from ADP and Pi (phosphate).
[bio15-s4.2.3] 3. Oxygen is the final electron acceptor. At the end of the chain, low-energy electrons combine with oxygen and protons (H⁺) to form water: 4e⁻ + 4H⁺ + O₂ → 2H₂O. It is the only step of respiration using oxygen directly, and the source of the 6H₂O in the equation.
[bio15-s4.2.4] 4. If oxygen is absent, electrons cannot leave the end of the chain, so the whole chain stops. NADH cannot be unloaded, so the citric acid cycle and the link step stop too, and the cell can then only carry out glycolysis and fermentation.
[bio15-s4.2.5] Cyanide is a poison because it blocks the last complex of the chain, which has the same effect as having no oxygen: the chain stops and aerobic ATP production collapses.
[bio15-s4.2.6] 5. The ATP made is exported from the mitochondrion to the cytoplasm and used for other biochemical reactions in the cell, such as synthesis of proteins, active transport and muscle contraction.
[bio15-s4.2.7] Most of the ATP from aerobic respiration is made here, via a proton gradient and ATP synthase. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.b] Respiration can be modelled as two stages joined by carrier molecules that shuttle electrons, hydrogen, acetyl groups and energy around the cell. Putting the stages together shows where each product comes from and where each reactant is used. There is more on the membrane comparison with photosynthesis in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio15-s4.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm: glucose becomes 2 pyruvate, a net 2 ATP is made directly, and NAD⁺ is converted to NADH.
[bio15-s4.3.1] Stage 2 takes place in the mitochondrion. The link step and the citric acid cycle, in the matrix, turn pyruvate into acetyl CoA and then into CO₂, converting NAD⁺ to NADH and making a little ATP directly.
[bio15-s4.3.2] The electron transport chain, on the inner membrane (cristae), unloads NADH to NAD⁺, passes electrons to oxygen to form water, and makes most of the ATP from ADP and Pi.
[bio15-s4.3.3] Transfer molecules carry energy and electrons between the stages. NAD⁺/NADH carries electrons and hydrogen from the breakdown of glucose (glycolysis, link step and cycle) to the electron transport chain, and returns empty. Coenzyme A carries the acetyl group into the cycle and returns.
[bio15-s4.3.4] ATP carries energy from the mitochondrion and cytoplasm to the cell's activities, returning as ADP + Pi to be reloaded. All the transfer molecules are recycled, so only small amounts are needed.
[bio15-s4.3.5] Tracing the atoms: the carbon of glucose leaves as CO₂ in the link step and the cycle. The hydrogen of glucose ends up in water, formed by combining with oxygen breathed in at the electron transport chain. The oxygen breathed in therefore ends up in water, not in CO₂.
[bio15-s4.3.6] Comparison with photosynthesis: in photosynthesis, NADPH and ATP are made in stage 1, using light energy, and used in stage 2 to build glucose. In respiration, NADH is made as glucose is broken down and is used at the end of stage 2 to make ATP.
[bio15-s4.3.7] Diagram: cytoplasm and a mitochondrion (outer membrane, inner membrane with cristae, matrix). Label: glucose, 2 pyruvate, NAD⁺ → NADH, acetyl CoA, CO₂, citric acid cycle (acetyl group + C₄ → citric acid (C₆) → back to C₄), electron transport chain, O₂ + H⁺ → H₂O, ATP, NAD⁺; what each carrier carries.

CANDIDATES bio15-s5: The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase
[bio15-s5.0.b] A mitochondrion is an organelle (a specialised compartment inside a cell). Its inside is divided into distinct regions by two membranes, and each region has a job.
[bio15-s5.0.0] Outer membrane: smooth, it surrounds the mitochondrion and is permeable to small molecules such as pyruvate, ADP and ATP, so these can move freely in and out.
[bio15-s5.0.1] Inner membrane: folded into many cristae (singular crista), which greatly increase its surface area. It holds the electron transport chains and ATP synthase (the enzyme that makes ATP). It is impermeable to protons except through ATP synthase.
[bio15-s5.0.2] Intermembrane space: the narrow space between the outer and inner membranes, into which protons are moved. This is the space "outside the inner membrane" in the specification's wording, not outside the mitochondrion.
[bio15-s5.0.3] Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane. It contains the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle (the earlier stages of respiration that deliver electrons to the chain).
[bio15-s5.0.4] The matrix also holds the mitochondrion's own small loop of DNA and its ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), so the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins.
[bio15-s5.0.5] Diagram: electron-microscope-level drawing of a mitochondrion in section. Label outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae, intermembrane space, matrix, DNA, ribosomes, and ATP synthase drawn as small knobs on the matrix side of the cristae.
[bio15-s5.1.b] The electron transport chain is the machinery that passes electrons along a series of steps, releasing energy at each one. It is built into the inner membrane, so the membrane's structure matters.
[bio15-s5.1.0] The chain is made of a number of large protein complexes embedded in, and spanning, the inner membrane (reaching from one side to the other).
[bio15-s5.1.1] Smaller mobile electron carriers link the complexes. They move within or along the membrane and carry electrons from one complex to the next, so the electrons are handed on step by step.
[bio15-s5.1.2] NADH (the carrier that brings electrons from earlier stages of respiration) delivers electrons to the first complex. The electrons then move from complex to complex, each at a lower energy level.
[bio15-s5.1.3] At the last complex the electrons are passed to oxygen, forming water. There is more on this in Second-stage process.
[bio15-s5.2.b] The electrons do not make ATP directly. Their energy is used to build up a store of protons on one side of the inner membrane, and the protons' flow back drives ATP synthase. The sequence runs in seven steps.
[bio15-s5.2.0] 1. Electrons arrive: high-energy electrons from NADH are transferred to a protein complex in the inner membrane. Electrons are negatively charged, so their arrival gives the complex a negative charge, a charge difference.
[bio15-s5.2.1] 2. Protons are attracted: the charge difference attracts positively charged protons (H⁺) to the complex. The protons are given by donor molecules: NADH releases a proton when it gives up its electrons, and more protons come from the solution in the matrix.
[bio15-s5.2.2] 3. Protons are moved out: the protons pass through the complex from the matrix side to the outside of the inner membrane, into the intermembrane space. Energy from the electrons drives this. It happens at several complexes along the chain as the electrons pass from one to the next. Textbooks call this proton pumping.
[bio15-s5.2.3] 4. A gradient builds: protons accumulate in the narrow intermembrane space, giving a high concentration of protons outside the inner membrane and a low concentration in the matrix. This proton (concentration) gradient is a store of energy, like water held behind a dam. It also makes the intermembrane space more positive and more acidic than the matrix.
[bio15-s5.2.4] 5. Protons flow back: the inner membrane lets protons through only at the enzyme ATP synthase. They flow back down their gradient, from the intermembrane space across the inner membrane into the matrix, through a channel in ATP synthase. They do not pass back through the electron transport complexes.
[bio15-s5.2.5] 6. ATP is made: ATP synthase harnesses the flow of protons. It turns part of the enzyme, like a water wheel or turbine, and uses the energy to join ADP and Pi (inorganic phosphate) into ATP on the matrix side.
[bio15-s5.2.6] 7. Oxygen keeps the system running: oxygen accepts the electrons and protons at the end of the chain, forming water, so electrons keep flowing and protons keep being moved out. Without oxygen, the gradient is not maintained and ATP synthase stops.
[bio15-s5.2.7] Diagram: inner membrane, matrix below, intermembrane space above. Show NADH → NAD⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻; electron arrow; many H⁺ up; ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O; H⁺ down ATP synthase, ADP + Pi → ATP. Label protein complex, mobile electron carrier, inner membrane, intermembrane space, matrix, ATP synthase, proton gradient.
[bio15-s5.3.b] The process in the last block has a name, and its history shows how a new scientific idea can win acceptance.
[bio15-s5.3.0] Making ATP using a proton gradient across a membrane is called chemiosmosis.
[bio15-s5.3.1] Nature of Science: the idea was proposed by the British biochemist Peter Mitchell in 1961 and was at first widely doubted. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978, once experiments had supported it.
[bio15-s5.4.b] Each feature of the mitochondrion's structure suits the way the proton gradient is built and used. Linking a feature to its effect is how you answer structure-and-function questions.
[bio15-s5.4.0] Cristae: folding gives a large area of inner membrane, which holds very many electron transport chains and ATP synthase molecules, so more ATP can be made. Mitochondria in very active cells, such as heart and flight muscle, have more densely packed cristae.
[bio15-s5.4.1] Narrow intermembrane space: its small volume means that protons moved into it quickly build up a high concentration and a steep gradient.
[bio15-s5.4.2] Impermeable inner membrane: the gradient is kept, and protons can return only through ATP synthase, so their energy is not wasted.
[bio15-s5.4.3] Complexes embedded in the membrane in a fixed order: electrons pass quickly and in sequence, and protons are moved in one direction only, across the membrane.
[bio15-s5.4.4] Matrix next to the inner membrane: the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle are right beside the chains, so NADH is delivered quickly. ATP is made into the matrix, from where it is exported to the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s5.4.5] Double membrane: separates the matrix's enzymes and conditions from the cytoplasm. The outer and inner membranes together create the intermembrane space.
[bio15-s5.4.6] Own DNA and ribosomes: the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins, including some of the chain's, and can divide to make more mitochondria when a cell needs more energy, for example in muscles during training.
[bio15-s5.5.b] Chloroplasts (the organelles where photosynthesis happens) make ATP using a very similar mechanism, built into their thylakoid membranes (flattened internal membrane sacs). The two organelles are alike in several ways, then differ in the details.
[bio15-s5.5.0] Both have electron transport chains of protein complexes and mobile electron carriers embedded in a membrane.
[bio15-s5.5.1] Both use the energy of electrons passing along the chain to move protons across the membrane, building a proton gradient, and both make ATP as protons flow back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis).
[bio15-s5.5.2] Both have a large membrane area, from cristae in the mitochondrion or stacked thylakoids in the chloroplast, so each can hold many chains and ATP synthase molecules.
[bio15-s5.5.3] Both organelles have their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio15-s5.6.b] The shared mechanism is fed by different energy and electron sources, and it sits in different membranes and compartments. Compare each feature as a pair.
[bio15-s5.6.0] Source of energy: the mitochondrion uses chemical energy from food (glucose), delivered as electrons by NADH; the chloroplast uses light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll.
[bio15-s5.6.1] Source of electrons: NADH, from glucose, in the mitochondrion; water, via the reaction-centre chlorophyll, in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.2] Final electron acceptor: oxygen, forming water, in the mitochondrion; NADP⁺, forming NADPH, in the chloroplast (non-cyclic pathway).
[bio15-s5.6.3] Oxygen: used at the end of the chain in mitochondria; released by photolysis (the splitting of water) in chloroplasts.
[bio15-s5.6.4] Which membrane: the inner membrane of the envelope (cristae) in the mitochondrion; the thylakoid membrane, a separate internal membrane system, in the chloroplast, not its envelope.
[bio15-s5.6.5] Where protons build up: in the intermembrane space, between the inner and outer membranes, in the mitochondrion; inside the thylakoid space in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.6] Where ATP is made and used: in the matrix, then exported for use all over the cell, in the mitochondrion; in the stroma (the fluid around the thylakoids), where it is used in the light-independent reactions, in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.7] Diagram: side-by-side sketches of a section of the inner mitochondrial membrane and a section of the thylakoid membrane. Each shows its chain, the direction of proton movement, the high-proton compartment (intermembrane space; thylakoid space) and ATP synthase. Label the electron source and final acceptor on each.

CANDIDATES bio30-s1: Plant structures for transport
[bio30-s1.0.b] A plant takes in materials at one end and uses them at the other, so it needs pipes to move them. Two transport tissues, xylem and phloem, do this job, and they join the plant's two main parts: the root system and the shoot system.
[bio30-s1.0.0] A large plant has a small surface area to volume ratio (its outer surface is small compared with the amount of living tissue inside), so diffusion alone cannot supply all its cells. There is more on this in Levels of organisation.
[bio30-s1.0.1] Roots take in water and minerals from the soil, but these are needed in the leaves. Leaves make sugar, but this is needed in the roots, growing tips, flowers, fruits and storage organs, so materials must travel both ways.
[bio30-s1.0.2] Xylem and phloem, the two transport tissues, link the roots and the leaves, so that each end gets what it cannot make or collect for itself.
[bio30-s1.0.3] The root system, everything below ground, anchors the plant, absorbs water and minerals through root hairs, and often stores food, e.g. the swollen root of a carrot.
[bio30-s1.0.4] The shoot system (stem, leaves, buds and flowers) holds the leaves up to the light, carries out photosynthesis and gas exchange, transports materials between roots and leaves, and reproduces.
[bio30-s1.0.5] The two systems are joined by continuous vascular tissue (the transport tissue): the xylem and phloem of the root run up through the stem and branch out into every leaf as the veins, giving an unbroken pipeline from root hair to leaf cell.
[bio30-s1.1.b] A tissue is a group of similar cells doing one job. The root, stem and leaf are each built from three tissue types: dermal, ground and vascular. Exams ask you to label all three on a drawing.
[bio30-s1.1.0] Dermal tissue is the outer protective layer, so it is the first thing to meet the outside world. In young roots, stems and leaves it is the epidermis, usually one cell thick.
[bio30-s1.1.1] In leaves and stems the epidermis is covered by a waxy, waterproof cuticle that cuts water loss and keeps out pathogens. It contains stomata, pores that each lie between two guard cells, for gas exchange.
[bio30-s1.1.2] In roots the epidermis has no cuticle, and some epidermal cells grow out as root hairs for absorbing water and minerals. In woody stems the epidermis is replaced by bark, which is pierced by lenticels.
[bio30-s1.1.3] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant between the dermal and vascular tissue. It is mostly made of living, thin-walled cells with large vacuoles (fluid-filled sacs).
[bio30-s1.1.4] The job of ground tissue depends on its position: photosynthesis in the mesophyll (the middle layer) of the leaf and in green stems; storage of starch and water in the root cortex, stem pith and potato tubers; and support, because its cells are kept firm (turgid) by water pressure.
[bio30-s1.1.5] Vascular tissue is the transport tissue: xylem carries water and dissolved minerals, and phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose.
[bio30-s1.1.6] Xylem and phloem lie together in groups called vascular bundles in the stem and leaf, and in a central core in the root. Xylem also supports the plant.
[bio30-s1.2.b] Xylem is the plant's water pipe. Every feature of its cells, from being hollow to having hard walls, is there so that water can rise from the roots to the leaves without obstruction and without the pipe collapsing.
[bio30-s1.2.0] Xylem carries water and dissolved minerals from the roots, up the stem, to the leaves, in one direction only, which is upwards, because the leaves are where water is lost and used.
[bio30-s1.2.1] Xylem is made of two kinds of cell, both dead and hollow when mature, with no cytoplasm, nucleus or cell membrane, so water flows through them without obstruction.
[bio30-s1.2.2] Xylem vessels are long continuous tubes formed from a column of cells joined end to end, whose end walls have broken down. This makes one open pipe.
[bio30-s1.2.3] Tracheids are single long, narrow cells with tapering ends. Water passes from one tracheid to the next through thin areas in the walls called pits.
[bio30-s1.2.4] Pits in the side walls let water move sideways between neighbouring vessels and tracheids, and out to the surrounding cells, so water can bypass a blocked tube.
[bio30-s1.2.5] The cell walls are thickened with lignin, a hard, waterproof substance, laid down in rings, spirals or as a solid layer with pits.
[bio30-s1.2.6] Lignin stops the vessels collapsing inwards when the water in them is under tension, pulled from above. It makes xylem the main supporting tissue of woody plants: wood is xylem. There is more on this tension in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal view of a xylem vessel (a long open tube with no end walls, spiral or ring thickening of lignin and pits) beside a tracheid (tapering ends, pits); label lignin, pit, no end wall and hollow lumen (the empty space inside).
[bio30-s1.3.b] Phloem is the plant's food-delivery system. Unlike xylem, its cells are alive, and it relies on a partnership between two cell types: the tube that carries the sap and the companion cell that powers it.
[bio30-s1.3.0] Phloem carries dissolved food, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, from the leaves, where it is made, to the parts that use or store it. It can carry food upwards or downwards, because roots and fruits lie on either side of the leaves.
[bio30-s1.3.1] Sieve tubes are columns of living sieve tube elements joined end to end. Their end walls are perforated with many small holes, forming sieve plates, through which the sap flows from one cell to the next.
[bio30-s1.3.2] A mature sieve tube element has a thin layer of cytoplasm but no nucleus and very few organelles, which leaves room for the sap to flow.
[bio30-s1.3.3] Each sieve tube element has a companion cell beside it, joined to it by many fine connections through the walls, so the two can exchange substances.
[bio30-s1.3.4] The companion cell has a nucleus, dense cytoplasm and many mitochondria. It keeps the sieve tube element alive, which has no nucleus of its own, and supplies the ATP (energy) needed to load sucrose into the sieve tube. There is more on this in Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products.
[bio30-s1.3.5] Phloem cell walls are cellulose only, not lignified (not strengthened with lignin), so phloem gives little support. In a woody stem the phloem lies just inside the bark.
[bio30-s1.3.6] Diagram: a longitudinal view of two sieve tube elements end to end, with a sieve plate between them and a companion cell alongside; label sieve tube element, sieve plate, cytoplasm, companion cell and nucleus (in the companion cell only).
[bio30-s1.4.b] Because the two tissues sit side by side but do opposite kinds of job, examiners like to ask for the differences. Each pair of points below sets one feature of xylem against the same feature of phloem.
[bio30-s1.4.0] Cells: xylem vessels and tracheids are dead and hollow, so water can flow through; sieve tube elements and companion cells are living, so they can load sucrose using energy.
[bio30-s1.4.1] Walls: xylem walls are thickened with lignin, which is why they are strong; phloem walls are cellulose only.
[bio30-s1.4.2] End walls: xylem vessels have none, so water flows freely; sieve tubes have sieve plates, perforated walls the sap passes through.
[bio30-s1.4.3] Contents: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries sucrose and amino acids in solution.
[bio30-s1.4.4] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only, from roots to leaves; phloem moves food up or down, from where it is made or stored to where it is used.
[bio30-s1.4.5] Support: xylem supports the plant; phloem gives little support.
[bio30-s1.5.b] The root has to find water in the soil and get it into the plant, so its structure is built for absorption and for holding on. Working from the tip inwards shows how each part suits its job.
[bio30-s1.5.0] The root anchors the plant in the soil, absorbs water and minerals, transports them to the stem, and may store food.
[bio30-s1.5.1] A root cap protects the delicate growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Just behind it is meristematic tissue whose cells divide, so the root grows in length.
[bio30-s1.5.2] Root hairs develop a little further back, on the region that has stopped growing, so they are not torn off as the root moves forward.
[bio30-s1.5.3] Each root hair is a long, thin outgrowth of a single epidermal cell. There are thousands of them, giving a huge surface area for absorption, and they grow between soil particles in close contact with the film of soil water.
[bio30-s1.5.4] The root hair's wall is thin and has no cuticle, so water passes in easily. Its large vacuole holds cell sap more concentrated than the soil water, so water enters by osmosis. Its many mitochondria supply ATP for the active transport of mineral ions.
[bio30-s1.5.5] The root cortex is a wide zone of ground tissue made of large, thin-walled, loosely packed living cells with air spaces between them. Water and minerals pass across it from the root hairs to the xylem, and it stores food, often as starch.
[bio30-s1.5.6] The air spaces in the cortex let oxygen from the soil air reach the cells for respiration. The innermost single layer of the cortex, the endodermis, surrounds the vascular tissue and controls what passes into the xylem.
[bio30-s1.5.7] In the centre of the root the xylem forms a star or X shape, with the phloem in groups between its arms. This central core resists the pulling forces on a root as the shoot is blown about, as the core of a rope does.
[bio30-s1.5.8] Diagram: a transverse section (a slice across) of a young root of a broad-leaved (dicotyledonous) plant, labelled epidermis (dermal tissue), root hair, cortex (ground tissue), endodermis, xylem and phloem (vascular tissue); label the three tissue types by name as well as the structures.
[bio30-s1.6.b] The stem is the link between roots and leaves. It has to hold the leaves up to the light and carry materials in both directions, so it combines strength with transport tissue.
[bio30-s1.6.0] The stem supports the leaves and flowers, holding the leaves up to the light and the flowers up for pollination. It carries water and minerals up in the xylem and food up or down in the phloem, between the roots and the leaves.
[bio30-s1.6.1] The stem may store food, as the potato tuber does, since it is a swollen underground stem, and if it is green it photosynthesises because it contains chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.6.2] The epidermis on the outside of a stem is dermal tissue with a waxy cuticle, which protects the stem and reduces water loss. In a woody stem it is replaced by bark.
[bio30-s1.6.3] The cortex, just inside the epidermis, and the pith in the centre are both ground tissue. Their living cells store food and water and help support the stem when they are turgid (firm with water).
[bio30-s1.6.4] The vascular bundles are arranged in a ring near the outside of the stem. In each bundle the phloem is on the outside and the xylem on the inside.
[bio30-s1.6.5] The ring of strong tissue near the edge resists bending as the stem is blown sideways, as a hollow tube does.
[bio30-s1.6.6] Diagram: a transverse section of a young, non-woody stem, labelled epidermis and cuticle (dermal tissue), cortex and pith (ground tissue), and a vascular bundle with phloem outside and xylem inside (vascular tissue).
[bio30-s1.7.b] Woody stems are wrapped in bark, which is tough but also seals the living cells beneath from the air. Lenticels are the gaps that solve this problem, and they are the plant structure other than stomata through which gases are exchanged.
[bio30-s1.7.0] A lenticel is a small raised pore in the bark of a woody stem. Lenticels are also found on some roots, on potato tubers, and as the speckles on apple skins.
[bio30-s1.7.1] A lenticel is a patch of loosely packed cells with air spaces between them, so gases can pass through easily.
[bio30-s1.7.2] Bark is waterproof and airtight, so the living cells beneath it, including the phloem, would be cut off from air. Lenticels allow gas exchange by diffusion: oxygen in for respiration and carbon dioxide out.
[bio30-s1.7.3] A small amount of water vapour is also lost through lenticels.
[bio30-s1.7.4] Lenticels stay open all the time, because unlike stomata they have no guard cells and cannot close.
[bio30-s1.7.5] Examples: the horizontal lines on the bark of birch and cherry trees are lenticels.
[bio30-s1.8.b] The leaf is the plant's food factory, and its shape and its connections to the stem both follow from that job. Before looking inside, it helps to see what the leaf does and how its outside suits it.
[bio30-s1.8.0] The leaf carries out photosynthesis, gas exchange (CO₂ in and oxygen out, in the light) and transpiration, which pulls water up the plant. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.8.1] The leaf is a thin, flat, broad blade. This gives a large surface area to absorb light and CO₂, and a short distance for gases to diffuse.
[bio30-s1.8.2] The leaf stalk (petiole) holds the blade out towards the light so it catches more of it, and carries the vascular tissue into the blade.
[bio30-s1.8.3] The midrib and veins are vascular bundles that bring water and minerals in and take sucrose away, and their network supports the blade.
[bio30-s1.9.b] A transverse section of a leaf shows layers, each with its own job. Light enters from the top, so the layers are arranged to let light through first and to put the photosynthetic cells where light is strongest.
[bio30-s1.9.0] The waxy cuticle on the upper, and usually the lower, epidermis is waterproof and reduces water loss by evaporation. It is transparent, so light passes through, and it is usually thicker on the upper surface, which faces the sun.
[bio30-s1.9.1] The upper epidermis is one layer of transparent cells with no chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens), letting light through to the cells below, and it has few or no stomata.
[bio30-s1.9.2] The palisade mesophyll is one or two layers of tall, column-shaped cells packed with chloroplasts. It lies just below the upper epidermis, where light is strongest, and most photosynthesis takes place here.
[bio30-s1.9.3] The spongy mesophyll is made of irregular, loosely packed cells with fewer chloroplasts and large air spaces between them. The air spaces connect to the stomata and let CO₂, oxygen and water vapour diffuse through the leaf.
[bio30-s1.9.4] The moist cell surfaces of the spongy mesophyll are where CO₂ dissolves before entering the cells and where water evaporates.
[bio30-s1.9.5] The vascular bundle, or vein, has xylem on the upper side, bringing water and minerals to the cells that need them, and phloem on the lower side, carrying sucrose away.
[bio30-s1.10.b] A leaf needs CO₂ from the air, but any opening to the air also lets water escape. The leaf's answer is to put most of its pores on the underside and to let guard cells control them.
[bio30-s1.10.0] The lower epidermis contains most of the stomata. Each stoma (plural stomata) is a pore between two bean-shaped guard cells, which, unlike other epidermal cells, contain chloroplasts.
[bio30-s1.10.1] The guard cells open and close the pore. There is more on how they do this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s1.10.2] Stomata on the shaded lower surface lose less water than they would on the sunny upper surface, because it is cooler there.
[bio30-s1.10.3] Diagram: a transverse section of a leaf, labelled cuticle, upper epidermis (dermal tissue), palisade and spongy mesophyll with air spaces (ground tissue), vascular bundle with xylem above and phloem below (vascular tissue), lower epidermis, stoma and guard cells.
[bio30-s1.10.4] Diagram: a surface view of the lower epidermis showing a stoma between two guard cells (with chloroplasts) surrounded by irregular epidermal cells; label stoma, guard cell, chloroplast and epidermal cell.

CANDIDATES bio30-s3: Transport of CO₂ and photosynthetic products
[bio30-s3.0.b] Photosynthesis needs a steady supply of carbon dioxide (CO₂), the raw material the plant builds sugar from. A leaf cell has two places to get it from: the air around the plant and its own respiring cells.
[bio30-s3.0.0] A photosynthesising leaf gets CO₂ from two sources: the air outside, entering through the stomata (the tiny pores in the leaf surface), and the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio30-s3.0.1] The air contains only about 0.04% CO₂, so a leaf must exchange large volumes of air to get enough. This is why it has many stomata and a large internal surface.
[bio30-s3.0.2] Every living plant cell respires all the time, day and night, releasing CO₂ from its mitochondria (the organelles where respiration takes place). Plants do not respire only at night.
[bio30-s3.0.3] In the light, the CO₂ released by a mesophyll cell's own mitochondria, and by neighbouring cells, can be used directly by its chloroplasts without ever leaving the leaf. Mesophyll cells are the photosynthesising cells inside the leaf.
[bio30-s3.0.4] Aquatic plants take in CO₂ dissolved in the water around them.
[bio30-s3.1.b] CO₂ has to get from the outside air into the chloroplasts (the green organelles where photosynthesis happens), and it does so in a series of steps with no pump involved. The steps where it moves (2, 3 and 5) are diffusion down a concentration gradient, meaning from where there is more CO₂ to where there is less, and step 4 is the CO₂ dissolving in water.
[bio30-s3.1.0] 1. Photosynthesis uses CO₂ in the chloroplasts, so the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf falls below that of the outside air, setting up a concentration gradient.
[bio30-s3.1.1] 2. CO₂ diffuses in through the open stomata, from the higher concentration outside to the lower concentration inside.
[bio30-s3.1.2] 3. It diffuses through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll (the loosely packed layer of cells in the lower leaf) to the mesophyll cells.
[bio30-s3.1.3] 4. It dissolves in the thin film of water on the moist cell walls, because gases can only enter cells in solution.
[bio30-s3.1.4] 5. It diffuses across the cell wall, cell membrane and cytoplasm into the chloroplasts, where it is used in the light-independent stage (the part of photosynthesis that builds sugar from CO₂). The gradient is kept up because CO₂ is used as fast as it arrives.
[bio30-s3.1.5] Diagram: a transverse section of part of a leaf, with arrows for CO₂ diffusing in through a stoma, through the air spaces and into a palisade cell's chloroplast, and for oxygen and water vapour diffusing out; plus a short arrow from a mitochondrion to a chloroplast, labelled CO₂ from respiration.
[bio30-s3.2.b] Because CO₂ moves only by diffusion, a leaf is built to make that diffusion fast and short. Stomata also act as the gates that decide when CO₂ can enter.
[bio30-s3.2.0] CO₂ moves through the plant by diffusion only, through the air spaces and across moist cell surfaces. It is not carried in the xylem or phloem (the plant's water and food-carrying tubes), and no energy is used to move it.
[bio30-s3.2.1] A thin leaf (so the distance to travel is short), a large surface area, many stomata, a network of air spaces and moist mesophyll cell walls with a large total surface all speed up the diffusion of CO₂.
[bio30-s3.2.2] Stomata control the entry of CO₂: they open in the light, when CO₂ is needed. At Higher Level, the CO₂ concentration inside the leaf also helps control whether they open or close. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s3.3.b] Photosynthesis and respiration both happen in a leaf, and they swap the same gases in opposite directions. Which one wins depends on how much light there is, so the net gas exchange changes through the day.
[bio30-s3.3.0] In bright light, photosynthesis is faster than respiration. The leaf uses more CO₂ than its cells produce, so there is a net uptake of CO₂ through the stomata and a net release of oxygen.
[bio30-s3.3.1] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, only respiration. The plant takes in oxygen and gives out CO₂, through stomata that are mostly closed, through the cuticle (the waxy surface layer) in small amounts, and through lenticels (small pores in woody stems).
[bio30-s3.3.2] At dawn and dusk, in dim light, the rate of photosynthesis equals the rate of respiration, a point called the compensation point. All the CO₂ produced by respiration is used in photosynthesis, so there is no net exchange of CO₂ or oxygen with the air.
[bio30-s3.3.3] Non-green parts, such as roots, woody stems and the inside of fruits and tubers, only respire because they do not photosynthesise. They take in oxygen and release CO₂ by diffusion: roots to the air spaces in the soil, and woody stems through lenticels.
[bio30-s3.3.4] The oxygen made in photosynthesis is a photosynthetic product too. It is used in the plant's own respiration, and the excess diffuses out through the stomata.
[bio30-s3.4.b] The sugar made in a leaf is needed all over the plant, in places that cannot photosynthesise. Translocation is how it gets there, carried in the phloem by a flow that goes from where the sugar is made to where it is used.
[bio30-s3.4.0] Translocation is the transport of dissolved food made in photosynthesis, mainly sucrose and also amino acids, through the phloem sieve tubes (the living tubes of the phloem) from one part of the plant to another.
[bio30-s3.4.1] Sucrose is the form in which sugar is carried, not glucose. Starch is never transported; it is stored, and must be converted to sucrose before it can move.
[bio30-s3.4.2] Amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are made in the leaves from sugars and the nitrates brought up in the xylem. They are carried in the phloem to growing regions, where they are used to make proteins.
[bio30-s3.4.3] A source is a part of the plant where sucrose is made or released into the phloem. This is mainly the photosynthesising leaves, and also storage organs when their stores are being used.
[bio30-s3.4.4] A sink is a part where sucrose is used or stored. Sinks include growing shoot and root tips, which use it in respiration and to make cellulose for new cell walls, and developing flowers, fruits and seeds.
[bio30-s3.4.5] Other sinks are the roots and storage organs, where sucrose is converted to starch, as in potato tubers in summer.
[bio30-s3.5.b] Because sources and sinks are in different places and can swap roles, phloem flow is not fixed in one direction. It is also driven by a living, energy-using process, which sets it apart from water movement in the xylem.
[bio30-s3.5.0] Because sinks can be above or below the leaves, phloem transport goes both up and down the plant, at different times in different sieve tubes.
[bio30-s3.5.1] A part can change role. A potato tuber is a sink while it fills with starch in summer, and a source the next spring, when its starch is converted back to sucrose and carried to the growing new shoots.
[bio30-s3.5.2] Translocation needs energy: at the source, companion cells (the cells beside the sieve tubes) use ATP to load sucrose actively into the sieve tubes. So it happens only in living phloem and stops if the phloem is killed, for example by heat.
[bio30-s3.5.3] There is more on this in Plant structures for transport.
[bio30-s3.6.b] Once sucrose is in the sieve tubes, something must push the sap along. The pressure-flow model explains this as a pressure difference between the source and the sink, set up by moving sucrose in and out and by water following it by osmosis (water moving into a more concentrated solution).
[bio30-s3.6.0] 1. Loading sucrose at the source makes the sap in the sieve tubes there very concentrated.
[bio30-s3.6.1] 2. Water enters the sieve tubes from the nearby xylem by osmosis, because the concentrated sap draws it in, raising the pressure.
[bio30-s3.6.2] 3. At the sink, sucrose is removed from the sieve tubes into the cells that use or store it, so water leaves and the pressure there is lower.
[bio30-s3.6.3] 4. The sap flows along the sieve tubes, through the sieve plates (the perforated walls between sieve tube cells), from the high-pressure source to the low-pressure sink, carrying the dissolved sucrose and amino acids with it.
[bio30-s3.6.4] Diagram: a plant outline with a leaf labelled source, and a root tip, a fruit and a storage organ labelled sink, with phloem arrows for sucrose moving up and down. Beside it, a sieve tube between source and sink cells: sucrose loaded, water entering by osmosis, flow, sucrose unloaded.
[bio30-s3.7.b] We can't watch sucrose moving inside a sieve tube, so the idea that the phloem carries food rests on experiments and observations. Three pieces of evidence all point to the phloem rather than the xylem.
[bio30-s3.7.0] Ringing, or girdling: removing a complete ring of bark from a woody stem removes the phloem, which lies just inside the bark, but leaves the xylem, the wood, intact.
[bio30-s3.7.1] The bark just above the ring swells as sugars build up there, and the tissues below the ring, including the roots, are starved of food. If the roots run out of food, the whole tree dies.
[bio30-s3.7.2] The leaves above the ring do not wilt at first, because the xylem still carries water up.
[bio30-s3.7.3] Animals that strip a ring of bark from trees, such as deer, rabbits and grey squirrels, can kill them in this way. Foresters protect young trees with tree guards.
[bio30-s3.7.4] Aphids are small insects that feed by pushing their needle-like mouthparts into a single sieve tube. The sap that flows into them is rich in sucrose and amino acids.
[bio30-s3.7.5] If an aphid's body is cut away, the sap keeps oozing out of its mouthparts, which shows that the sap is under pressure in the sieve tube.
[bio30-s3.7.6] Radioactive tracers: if a leaf is given CO₂ containing radioactive carbon (¹⁴CO₂), the radioactive carbon soon appears in sucrose, first in the phloem of the leaf, then in the phloem of the stem and in the sinks, and not in the xylem.
[bio30-s3.8.b] Plants move three different kinds of material by three different mechanisms. Comparing them side by side shows where students most often mix them up.
[bio30-s3.8.0] What moves: water and minerals move in the xylem; sucrose and amino acids move in the phloem; CO₂ and oxygen move by diffusion through air spaces, in neither tissue.
[bio30-s3.8.1] Direction: xylem moves water upwards only; phloem moves food up or down, from source to sink; CO₂ moves down its concentration gradient, into the leaf by day and out of it at night.
[bio30-s3.8.2] Driving force: for water, evaporation from the leaves (the transpiration pull), helped at Higher Level by root pressure; for sucrose, active loading at the source and the pressure difference between source and sink; for CO₂, diffusion down a concentration gradient.
[bio30-s3.8.3] Energy used by the plant: none to lift water, which is driven by the sun's energy; ATP for loading sucrose and for absorbing minerals; none for CO₂ diffusion.
