CANDIDATES bio10-s2: Genes, proteins and traits
[bio10-s2.0.b] Three words link what is written in DNA to what an organism looks like and does: gene, protein and trait. Getting each one clear is the first step, because exam answers lose marks when they are mixed up.
[bio10-s2.0.0] A gene is a section of DNA that contains the instructions, or code, for making a particular protein (strictly, a polypeptide, which is a chain of amino acids). A gene is not itself a protein: it is the recipe for one.
[bio10-s2.0.1] Genes are carried on chromosomes (the long DNA molecules in the nucleus), and each chromosome holds many genes, so one chromosome carries the instructions for many different proteins.
[bio10-s2.0.2] A protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a particular shape. About 20 different amino acids are used to make proteins, and different orders of them give different proteins.
[bio10-s2.0.3] A trait is an inherited characteristic of an organism, such as eye colour, blood group, seed shape in peas, or whether a person can make a particular enzyme. The trait is not the protein: it is the visible or measurable result of what the protein does.
[bio10-s2.1.b] A gene's code is turned into a working protein in a fixed chain of events. The order of the code decides the protein's shape, and the shape decides what the protein can do.
[bio10-s2.1.0] The sequence of bases in a gene (the order of the chemical letters along the DNA) determines the sequence of amino acids in the protein it codes for. Change the order of the bases and the order of amino acids can change.
[bio10-s2.1.1] The sequence of amino acids determines how the chain folds, and so the protein's three-dimensional shape. This is why every protein folds into its own shape rather than a random tangle.
[bio10-s2.1.2] The shape determines the protein's function, because other molecules can only fit or bind to the right shape. Examples are the active site of an enzyme (the pocket where the reaction happens) and the binding site of a receptor (the spot that a signal molecule attaches to).
[bio10-s2.1.3] The cell's machinery reads the gene. The code is copied into a messenger molecule, mRNA, which travels from the nucleus to a ribosome (a tiny structure that builds proteins), where the amino acids are joined in the correct order.
[bio10-s2.1.4] Information therefore flows in one direction: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein → trait. There is more on how the copying and joining work in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s2.2.b] Proteins are the link between a gene and a trait because they do most of the work in cells and build much of their structure. Different versions of a gene can therefore give different versions of a trait, and different cells use different genes.
[bio10-s2.2.0] Enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions) control the chemical reactions that make substances such as pigments. If the enzyme is missing or faulty, the substance is not made and the trait changes.
[bio10-s2.2.1] Structural proteins build hair, skin, muscle and connective tissue, for example keratin (hair, nails, skin) and collagen (connective tissue, skin, tendons).
[bio10-s2.2.2] Transport and channel proteins move substances, for example across membranes or around the body. Hormones such as insulin, and receptor proteins, carry signals between cells.
[bio10-s2.2.3] Different versions of a gene, called alleles, have slightly different base sequences. They can give a protein with a different amino acid sequence, a different shape, or no working protein at all, which produces a different version of the trait.
[bio10-s2.2.4] Every body cell contains the same genes, but each cell type uses, or expresses, only some of them. For example, the insulin gene is used to make insulin only in cells of the pancreas, and haemoglobin is made only in developing red blood cells.
[bio10-s2.3.b] Four examples show the whole chain in action, each as gene → protein → trait. In each one, a change to the protein changes the trait that you can see.
[bio10-s2.3.0] Albinism (one form): the gene for the enzyme tyrosinase → tyrosinase is needed to make the dark pigment melanin → people with two non-working copies make little or no melanin, so they have very pale skin, white or very fair hair and light-sensitive eyes.
[bio10-s2.3.1] In albinism the gene codes for the enzyme, not for the pigment: the pigment (melanin) is not a protein, and it is missing only because the enzyme that makes it does not work.
[bio10-s2.3.2] Cystic fibrosis: the CFTR gene → the CFTR protein, a channel in cell membranes that lets chloride ions out of cells → a faulty channel causes thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas, leading to lung infections and poor digestion.
[bio10-s2.3.3] Haemoglobin: the haemoglobin genes → haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen → normal oxygen transport. There is more on how the change arises in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s2.3.4] A changed form of one of the haemoglobin genes gives sickle-cell haemoglobin, which makes red blood cells sickle-shaped when oxygen is low. This causes sickle-cell anaemia.
[bio10-s2.3.5] Seed shape in peas (Mendel's trait): the gene for starch-branching enzyme → the enzyme helps convert sugar into starch in the developing seed → seeds with a working copy are round.
[bio10-s2.3.6] Seeds with two non-working copies (rr) make less starch and keep more sugar. They take up more water while developing, and so wrinkle as they dry out.
[bio10-s2.3.7] Diagram: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein (amino acid chain folded into a shape) → trait, with the albinism example written under it.
[bio10-s2.4.b] The chain from gene to protein to trait is a useful model, but real traits are often more complicated. Genes are not the only thing that shapes an organism.
[bio10-s2.4.0] Many traits are controlled by several genes acting together, such as height and skin colour in humans, so there is no single gene for a trait. Each gene adds a small part of the final result.
[bio10-s2.4.1] The environment also affects many traits. A person's height depends on diet as well as genes, and a plant's height depends on light and nutrients.
[bio10-s2.4.2] The trait, or phenotype (what the organism is actually like), results from genes and environment together. This is why two organisms with the same genes can still look different.

CANDIDATES bio11-s1: Chromosome structure and genes
[bio11-s1.0.b] A chromosome is DNA together with protein, and it carries genes in a fixed linear order. Knowing what it is made of, how it looks in a resting and a dividing cell, and how big it is explains why cells need to pack their DNA.
[bio11-s1.0.0] A chromosome is a single, very long molecule of DNA together with proteins. It is found in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell (a cell with a nucleus) and carries many genes in a fixed linear order along its length.
[bio11-s1.0.1] Chromatin is the name for the DNA and protein material of the chromosomes when the cell is not dividing. The chromosomes are then long, thin and uncoiled, spread through the nucleus, so they cannot be seen as separate threads.
[bio11-s1.0.2] Before a cell divides, the chromatin coils and folds up tightly (condenses), so each chromosome becomes short and thick and is visible under the light microscope when stained. In other words, chromatin and chromosomes are the same material in two states.
[bio11-s1.0.3] Human body cells are diploid (two sets of chromosomes), with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes (chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes) and one pair of sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio11-s1.0.4] Homologous chromosomes are the two chromosomes of a pair. They are the same length, have the centromere (the narrowed region of the chromosome) in the same position and carry the same genes at the same positions, with one chromosome of each pair coming from each parent.
[bio11-s1.0.5] The chromosome number is fixed for a species (46 in humans); a different number does not mean a more complex organism, so you cannot judge complexity by counting chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.0.6] The human genome (all the DNA in one set of 23 chromosomes) is about 3 billion base pairs. The DNA in one human body cell, stretched out, would be about 2 m long, yet it fits in a nucleus a few micrometres across, which is why DNA must be packed.
[bio11-s1.0.7] Prokaryotes (bacteria) differ: a bacterial cell has one circular chromosome in the cytoplasm, not wound around histones (the packing proteins of eukaryotes), plus plasmids (small extra rings of DNA).
[bio11-s1.1.b] Histones are the proteins that pack eukaryotic DNA into a small space. Without them, about 2 m of DNA per cell could not fit in the nucleus or be moved safely when the cell divides.
[bio11-s1.1.0] Histones are small proteins around which the DNA of a eukaryotic chromosome is wound. They are positively charged, while the phosphate groups of the DNA backbone are negatively charged, so DNA binds tightly to them.
[bio11-s1.1.1] The DNA wraps nearly twice around a core of eight histone proteins, forming a bead called a nucleosome. Nucleosomes are joined by short stretches of linker DNA, like beads on a string.
[bio11-s1.1.2] The string of nucleosomes coils and loops further, and before division it condenses into the visible chromosome.
[bio11-s1.1.3] Histones do three jobs: they pack a very long DNA molecule into a small nucleus, they protect the DNA and keep it untangled during division, and they help control which genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.4] DNA wound tightly around histones cannot be reached by the enzymes that read genes, so those genes are switched off; loosely packed regions are active, which means their genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.5] Chemical changes to histones are one of the epigenetic mechanisms (ways of switching genes on or off without changing the DNA sequence). There is more on this in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s1.1.6] Diagram: DNA double helix wound around histone cores to form nucleosomes, then the coiled chromatin fibre, then a condensed chromosome; label DNA, histone, nucleosome, chromatin and chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.b] Before a cell divides it copies its DNA, so each chromosome ends up as two joined copies. Chromatids and the centromere are the names for those copies and for the point that holds them together.
[bio11-s1.2.0] Before a cell divides, each chromosome's DNA is copied, a process called replication. The two identical copies stay joined together; each copy is a chromatid. There is more on this in DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle.
[bio11-s1.2.1] A replicated chromosome therefore consists of two identical sister chromatids, each containing one complete DNA molecule with the same genes and the same alleles (versions of a gene).
[bio11-s1.2.2] The centromere is the constricted (narrowed) region of a chromosome where the two sister chromatids are held together. Spindle fibres (protein threads that move chromosomes in division) attach at the centromere and pull the chromatids apart to opposite poles of the cell.
[bio11-s1.2.3] Once separated, each chromatid is a chromosome in its own right.
[bio11-s1.2.4] The centromere is in a fixed position for each chromosome, dividing it into two arms of equal or different length, so it is not always in the middle. Its position, together with length and banding pattern, is used to identify each chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.5] Before replication, a chromosome is one DNA molecule and one chromatid. After replication it is still one chromosome but has two chromatids, the X shape usually drawn; the two arms of the X are chromatids, not separate chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.2.6] Homologous chromosomes (a pair, one from each parent) may carry different alleles, whereas sister chromatids are identical copies of each other, so they always carry identical alleles.
[bio11-s1.2.7] Diagram: an unreplicated chromosome (single rod) beside a replicated chromosome (X shape); label chromatid, sister chromatids, centromere, arm, and a gene locus marked as a band at the same position on both chromatids. Optionally add a homologous pair of replicated chromosomes, same locus marked on each, labelled homologous chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.3.b] A gene is the unit of heredity, the piece of information that is passed on from cell to cell and parent to offspring. Genes sit at fixed places on chromosomes and, through the proteins they code for, control traits.
[bio11-s1.3.0] A gene is a section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a protein. This means a chromosome is not simply a string of genes: genes are sections of its DNA.
[bio11-s1.3.1] Each gene has a fixed position on a particular chromosome, called its locus (plural loci). The locus of a gene is the same in every member of a species.
[bio11-s1.3.2] A gene carries the information to make one protein, although some genes code instead for RNA molecules that are used directly, such as ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA. So not every gene codes for a protein.
[bio11-s1.3.3] Through the protein it codes for, a gene controls a trait (a characteristic of the organism). The gene is copied and passed to daughter cells in division and to offspring in gametes (sex cells).
[bio11-s1.3.4] Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes spread across the 23 kinds of chromosome; each chromosome carries hundreds to thousands of genes.
[bio11-s1.3.5] Because homologous chromosomes carry the same genes, a body cell has two copies of each autosomal gene. These may be the same or different versions, called alleles. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio11-s1.4.b] Most of the DNA in a human cell does not code for protein. Separating the small coding part from the large non-coding part shows that a chromosome is far more than a list of protein recipes.
[bio11-s1.4.0] Coding DNA is DNA whose base sequence is used to make a protein: the protein-coding parts of genes. Only about 1–2% of human DNA is coding DNA.
[bio11-s1.4.1] Non-coding DNA is all DNA that does not code for protein, about 98% of the human genome. It includes several kinds of sequence, listed in the next points.
[bio11-s1.4.2] Regulatory sequences beside genes act as switches, controlling when, where and how much a gene is expressed (used to make its product).
[bio11-s1.4.3] Genes for functional RNA (rRNA and tRNA) are non-coding DNA, as are introns, the non-coding sections within genes, which are copied into mRNA but removed before it is used to make protein.
[bio11-s1.4.4] Repeated sequences, such as the DNA at centromeres and at the ends of chromosomes (telomeres), protect the chromosome and help it divide correctly. Some non-coding DNA has a function that is still unknown.
[bio11-s1.4.5] Non-coding DNA was once called 'junk DNA', but much of it is now known to have functions, especially in regulating genes. Changes in regulatory DNA can change a trait without changing any protein.
[bio11-s1.4.6] Repeated non-coding sequences vary greatly in length between people, which is why they are used in DNA profiling (identifying individuals from their DNA).
[bio11-s1.4.7] Diagram: a stretch of a chromosome's DNA drawn as a line, with two genes shown as boxes (each with a regulatory region at one end and coding sections separated by an intron), and the DNA between genes labelled non-coding; label gene, coding DNA, non-coding DNA, regulatory sequence and intron.

CANDIDATES bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio15-s1: Respiration as a catabolic process
[bio15-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a constant supply of usable energy. Respiration is the process that gets it, by breaking down food molecules and transferring the energy they hold to ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio15-s1.0.0] Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food, mainly glucose, in every living cell. The energy released is transferred to ATP, so respiration releases energy rather than making it.
[bio15-s1.0.1] Respiration is catabolic, meaning a large molecule such as glucose is broken down into smaller molecules and energy is released. The products are CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂, depending on the conditions.
[bio15-s1.0.2] Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process, because it builds small molecules up into large ones and takes in energy, whereas respiration breaks large ones down.
[bio15-s1.0.3] The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once, as in burning. This is why much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. Energy not trapped as ATP is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
[bio15-s1.0.4] The ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport (moving substances across a membrane using energy) and building large molecules.
[bio15-s1.0.5] Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed (built into sugar).
[bio15-s1.0.6] Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake of carbon by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle. There is more on this in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio15-s1.0.7] Glucose is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down). Glycogen (in animals) and starch (in plants) are first broken down to glucose, and fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired.
[bio15-s1.1.b] Aerobic (with oxygen) respiration breaks glucose down completely, releasing energy. Knowing where the glucose and oxygen come from, and where the carbon dioxide and water go, shows how organisms and their surroundings are linked.
[bio15-s1.1.0] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
[bio15-s1.1.1] Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Its chemicals are those of the photosynthesis equation reversed, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
[bio15-s1.1.2] Source of glucose in plants: photosynthesis, or stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant). In animals: digested food absorbed into the blood, or glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
[bio15-s1.1.3] Source of oxygen in humans: the air, taken in through the lungs and carried to the cells by red blood cells, which is how it reaches every cell.
[bio15-s1.1.4] Source of oxygen in plants: the air, entering through stomata (leaf pores) and lenticels (pores in woody stems); soil air, taken in by the roots; and the plant's own photosynthesis. In aquatic organisms it is oxygen dissolved in water.
[bio15-s1.1.5] Fate of carbon dioxide: it diffuses out of cells. In humans it is carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
[bio15-s1.1.6] Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted. Some is breathed out as water vapour.
[bio15-s1.2.b] When oxygen is scarce or absent, cells can still release some energy from glucose, but not nearly as much. The products depend on the organism.
[bio15-s1.2.0] Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. Because the glucose is only partly broken down, much of the energy stays in the products, so much less energy is released per glucose and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.2.1] In animals, such as human muscle during intense exercise, and in some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
[bio15-s1.2.2] In yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen such as roots in waterlogged soil, the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
[bio15-s1.2.3] Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. In baking, yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise and the ethanol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and wine-making, yeast's ethanol is the useful product. In making yogurt and cheese, bacteria's lactic acid is the useful product.
[bio15-s1.2.4] In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire.
[bio15-s1.2.5] Oxygen debt: after exercise, extra oxygen is needed to remove the lactic acid. It is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose, and the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
[bio15-s1.2.6] Obligate anaerobes respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen, for example the bacteria that cause tetanus. Yeast can respire either way, so it is a facultative anaerobe.
[bio15-s1.3.b] Respiration happens in two stages in different parts of the cell. Stage 1 is the same whether or not oxygen is present, and what happens next depends on oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid inside the cell) of every cell and does not need oxygen. 1. Glucose, with 6 carbon atoms, is split into two molecules of pyruvate, each with 3 carbon atoms. 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
[bio15-s1.3.1] With oxygen, stage 2 takes place in the mitochondria (the organelles where aerobic respiration is completed). 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water.
[bio15-s1.3.2] 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.3] Without oxygen, pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (in animals and some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast and plants). No further ATP is made, so anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s1.3.4] Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria, so their aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane.
[bio15-s1.3.5] Diagram: flow diagram. Glucose (cytoplasm), then glycolysis (small ATP), then 2 pyruvate. Oxygen present: mitochondrion, then CO₂, water, large ATP. No oxygen: cytoplasm, then lactic acid (animals) or ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1, stage 2.
[bio15-s1.4.b] Aerobic and anaerobic respiration start the same way but differ in how far glucose is broken down and how much ATP results. Respiration also contrasts with photosynthesis: respiration is catabolic and happens in all living cells, while photosynthesis is anabolic and happens only in cells with chloroplasts.
[bio15-s1.4.0] Oxygen: needed in aerobic respiration; not needed in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.1] Location: aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria; anaerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm only.
[bio15-s1.4.2] Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O, in aerobic respiration; incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂, in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.3] Energy: a large amount of ATP in aerobic respiration; a small amount, only the ATP made in glycolysis, in anaerobic respiration, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio15-s1.4.4] Stages: stage 1 followed by stage 2 in aerobic respiration; stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.5] Respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂. It happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
[bio15-s1.4.6] Photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂. It happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only in the light.

CANDIDATES bio15-s2: Investigation: conditions for fermentation
[bio15-s2.0.b] Fermentation is a way of releasing energy from sugar without oxygen. This investigation asks what a fermenting microorganism needs before it will do it: sugar, living cells, no oxygen and a suitable temperature.
[bio15-s2.0.0] Fermentation is anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen) by microorganisms. Yeast, a single-celled fungus, ferments sugar to ethanol and carbon dioxide; lactic acid bacteria ferment sugar to lactic acid.
[bio15-s2.0.1] The two products of yeast fermentation are the evidence looked for in this investigation: CO₂, shown by bubbles and by limewater turning milky, and ethanol, shown by the iodoform test.
[bio15-s2.0.2] Condition 1, sugar: it is needed because it is the respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down), so it is the source of energy for fermentation.
[bio15-s2.0.3] Condition 2, living microorganisms: they are needed because their cells contain the enzymes (biological catalysts) that carry out fermentation. Dead cells have no working enzymes.
[bio15-s2.0.4] Condition 3, an anaerobic environment (no oxygen): yeast with oxygen respires mainly aerobically, producing CO₂ and water instead of ethanol, so oxygen must be kept out to make the cells ferment.
[bio15-s2.0.5] Condition 4, a suitable temperature: it lets the enzymes work at a good rate, because enzymes are slow when cold and denatured (permanently changed in shape, so no longer working) when too hot.
[bio15-s2.0.6] Yeast is a facultative anaerobe, meaning it can respire with or without oxygen, so the conditions decide which kind of respiration it carries out. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio15-s2.1.b] A fair test changes one thing at a time. Here you build one flask that has all four conditions and then a set of flasks that each differ from it in a single condition, so any difference in the result can be blamed on that condition.
[bio15-s2.1.0] Aim: to investigate the conditions necessary for fermentation by yeast: the presence of sugar, the presence of living yeast, the absence of oxygen and a suitable temperature.
[bio15-s2.1.1] Hypothesis (example): yeast will ferment glucose, producing carbon dioxide and ethanol, only when sugar and living yeast are present, oxygen is absent and the temperature is warm. Fermentation will be fastest at about 30–40 °C and will stop at high temperature, e.g. 60 °C.
[bio15-s2.1.2] Design: set up one full fermentation flask that meets all four conditions, then a series of flasks each identical except that one condition is missing or changed.
[bio15-s2.1.3] Independent variable (the one you change): the condition changed, which is sugar (present or absent), microorganism (living, dead or none), oxygen (present or absent) or temperature.
[bio15-s2.1.4] Dependent variable (the one you measure): the amount or rate of fermentation, measured as the rate of CO₂ production (bubbles per minute, or cm³ of gas in a set time), whether limewater turns milky, and whether ethanol is present at the end.
[bio15-s2.1.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the volume and concentration of glucose solution, the mass of yeast, the volume of the flask, the time allowed, and the temperature, except in the temperature series.
[bio15-s2.1.6] Control: a flask with glucose solution and yeast that has been boiled for a few minutes and cooled, set up identically. It shows that the CO₂ and ethanol come from living yeast, not from the glucose solution itself.
[bio15-s2.2.b] The apparatus has to do four jobs: hold the mixture, keep air out while letting gas escape, hold the temperature steady, and detect the two products.
[bio15-s2.2.0] Core equipment: conical flasks or boiling tubes; fermentation locks or rubber bungs fitted with delivery tubes; test tubes of limewater (calcium hydroxide solution); a balance and graduated cylinders; a hot plate or kettle to boil water; a water bath or incubator; a thermometer; a stopwatch; labels.
[bio15-s2.2.1] Materials: dried or fresh baker's yeast; glucose or sucrose; distilled water; liquid paraffin or vegetable oil, which will form a layer that keeps air out.
[bio15-s2.2.2] For the ethanol test: a filter funnel and filter paper, test tubes, potassium iodide solution, sodium hypochlorite solution (dilute bleach) and a water bath.
[bio15-s2.2.3] Optional: a gas syringe, or an inverted measuring cylinder in a trough of water, to collect the gas so its volume can be measured.
[bio15-s2.3.b] The full flask is the reference set-up that meets all four conditions. Every other flask is a copy of it with one condition removed or changed, so follow the steps in order.
[bio15-s2.3.0] 1. Boil distilled water for a few minutes and allow it to cool to about 30 °C. Boiling drives out dissolved oxygen and kills unwanted microorganisms; cooling stops the hot water killing the yeast.
[bio15-s2.3.1] 2. Dissolve glucose in the cooled, boiled water to make a glucose solution (e.g. 10 g in 100 cm³). Place it in a conical flask, add a set mass of yeast (e.g. 2 g) and swirl to mix.
[bio15-s2.3.2] 3. Gently pour a layer of liquid paraffin or vegetable oil on top of the solution, about 1 cm deep, to stop oxygen from the air dissolving in it. This makes the conditions anaerobic.
[bio15-s2.3.3] 4. Fit a fermentation lock containing water, or a bung with a delivery tube leading into a test tube of limewater, so that gas can escape but air cannot get in.
[bio15-s2.3.4] 5. Place the flask in a water bath or incubator at about 30 °C, and set up the control flask beside it.
[bio15-s2.3.5] 6. After the yeast has had time to start, about 10–15 minutes, count the bubbles passing through the fermentation lock or into the limewater in one minute, three times. Note when the limewater turns milky.
[bio15-s2.3.6] Alternatively, collect the gas over a set time in a gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder.
[bio15-s2.3.7] 7. Leave the flasks until bubbling stops, a day or more, then test for ethanol.
[bio15-s2.4.b] To show that a condition is needed, remove it from the full flask and see what stops. Each flask below differs from the full flask in one condition only.
[bio15-s2.4.0] Sugar: a flask with yeast in boiled, cooled water only, with no glucose, under oil, at 30 °C. Expected result: very few or no bubbles, limewater stays clear and there is no ethanol. Conclusion: sugar is needed.
[bio15-s2.4.1] Microorganisms: a flask with glucose solution and boiled, dead yeast (the control), and, if wished, a flask with glucose solution only. Expected result: no bubbles, limewater stays clear and there is no ethanol. Conclusion: living microorganisms are needed.
[bio15-s2.4.2] Anaerobic environment: a flask with glucose solution and yeast, made with unboiled water, with no oil layer, open to the air or with air bubbled through it (using an aquarium pump), or shaken often.
[bio15-s2.4.3] Expected result: CO₂ is still produced, from aerobic respiration and often faster, but little or no ethanol is detected. Conclusion: ethanol is made when oxygen is absent.
[bio15-s2.4.4] Suitable temperature: identical full flasks placed at about 5 °C (fridge), 20 °C (room), 30 °C, 40 °C and 60 °C (water baths), each checked with a thermometer.
[bio15-s2.4.5] Expected result: very slow bubbling at 5 °C, where the enzymes are slowed but not denatured and speed up if warmed; faster at 20 °C; fastest at about 30–40 °C.
[bio15-s2.4.6] At 60 °C there is no fermentation, because the enzymes are denatured and the yeast killed, and fermentation does not restart on cooling. Conclusion: a suitable warm temperature is needed.
[bio15-s2.5.b] Bubbles prove only that CO₂ is made, which also happens in aerobic respiration. To show that fermentation really happened you must detect ethanol, and the iodoform test does this by making a visible solid.
[bio15-s2.5.0] 1. When bubbling has stopped, filter a sample of the liquid from each flask into a clean test tube, about 3 cm³. Filtering removes the yeast cells.
[bio15-s2.5.1] 2. Add about 3 cm³ of potassium iodide solution.
[bio15-s2.5.2] 3. Add about 5 cm³ of sodium hypochlorite solution and mix.
[bio15-s2.5.3] 4. Warm the tube gently in a water bath at about 60 °C for a few minutes.
[bio15-s2.5.4] 5. Allow the tube to cool and observe. Test water as a negative control and a little dilute ethanol as a positive control, so you know what no ethanol and definite ethanol look like.
[bio15-s2.5.5] Result: pale yellow crystals of iodoform (triiodomethane), with an antiseptic, hospital smell, form if ethanol is present; no crystals form without ethanol.
[bio15-s2.5.6] Alternative test: acidified potassium dichromate solution turns from orange to green when warmed with ethanol. Many schools avoid it because potassium dichromate is toxic.
[bio15-s2.6.b] The syllabus names bacteria as well as yeast, so the same four conditions can be tested with lactic acid bacteria. The product is different, so the way you detect fermentation changes too.
[bio15-s2.6.0] The specification allows bacteria. Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar lactose in milk to lactic acid, as in making yogurt.
[bio15-s2.6.1] Set up warm milk with a spoonful of live natural yogurt, the source of the bacteria, in clean, closed containers kept warm in an incubator or water bath. Compare with milk without yogurt, and with boiled yogurt.
[bio15-s2.6.2] Measure the fall in pH over several hours, with a pH meter, pH probe or universal indicator, because lactic acid makes the milk more acidic. The milk also thickens as the acid makes its protein clump.
[bio15-s2.6.3] No CO₂ is produced in lactic acid fermentation, so limewater is not used. The same four conditions can be tested.
[bio15-s2.7.b] This practical uses boiling water, corrosive chemicals, flammable ethanol and living cultures. Each hazard has a simple precaution.
[bio15-s2.7.0] Wear safety glasses. Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) is corrosive and irritant to skin and eyes; wash splashes off with plenty of water. Potassium dichromate, if used, is toxic and must be handled with gloves.
[bio15-s2.7.1] Take care with boiling water, the hot plate and water baths, and let the water cool before adding yeast.
[bio15-s2.7.2] Never seal a fermenting flask with a solid bung and no outlet: CO₂ builds up pressure and can force out the bung or crack the glass.
[bio15-s2.7.3] Ethanol is flammable, so keep it away from flames. Never taste the products; laboratory glassware and solutions are not food-safe.
[bio15-s2.7.4] Treat microorganisms with care: wash hands after the practical, and dispose of yeast and bacterial cultures as the teacher directs. Never eat the yogurt made in the laboratory.
[bio15-s2.8.b] Once the flasks have run, record the primary data (the results you collected yourself), compare each flask with the full flask, and decide whether the hypothesis is supported.
[bio15-s2.8.0] Record primary data in a table with one row per flask, giving the condition, the mean bubbles per minute (or volume of gas), whether limewater is milky or clear, and the ethanol test result (crystals or none).
[bio15-s2.8.1] For the temperature series, plot the mean rate on the y-axis against temperature on the x-axis.
[bio15-s2.8.2] Expected results: the full flask bubbles steadily, its limewater turns milky and its iodoform test is positive. The flasks without sugar or without living yeast show no CO₂ and no ethanol. The aerated flask gives CO₂ but little or no ethanol.
[bio15-s2.8.3] Also expected: the rate rises with temperature to a peak at about 30–40 °C and stops at 60 °C. The control shows no activity.
[bio15-s2.8.4] Conclusion: fermentation, the production of ethanol and CO₂, takes place only when sugar and living yeast are present, oxygen is absent and the temperature is suitable. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio15-s2.8.5] Diagram: the fermentation set-up. A conical flask in a water bath, with a thermometer, containing glucose solution and yeast under a layer of oil, fitted with a fermentation lock or a delivery tube into a test tube of limewater.
[bio15-s2.8.6] Label each part. Optionally draw beside it the control flask with boiled yeast.
[bio15-s2.9.b] Your own results are primary data. Secondary data is information collected by others, and using it alongside yours shows whether your conclusion holds up. The conditions you tested are also the ones used in real fermentation.
[bio15-s2.9.0] Pool class results, or compare with published data, to check reproducibility (whether others get the same result). CO₂ production rates can be measured with a CO₂ sensor and data logger, or by the mass lost by a fermenting flask on a balance as CO₂ escapes.
[bio15-s2.9.1] Applications of the conditions: brewers and wine-makers exclude air so that yeast makes ethanol, and control the temperature; bakers keep dough warm so that yeast makes CO₂ quickly to raise it; yogurt makers incubate milk warm with bacteria. There is more on industrial uses in Microorganisms.
[bio15-s2.10.b] Each weakness below would make the results less reliable or the conclusion less certain. For each one, know what goes wrong and how to improve it.
[bio15-s2.10.0] Oxygen can enter if the water was not boiled, the oil layer is broken or the fermentation lock leaks. Some respiration is then aerobic and less ethanol is made.
[bio15-s2.10.1] Yeast can still ferment some sugar when oxygen is present if plenty of sugar is available, so the aerated flask may give a weak positive ethanol test. Compare the amount of crystals, not just their presence.
[bio15-s2.10.2] Bubbles vary in size and some CO₂ dissolves in the solution, so bubble counts are imprecise and underestimate the gas produced. Collecting the gas, or weighing the mass lost, is more accurate.
[bio15-s2.10.3] Limewater turns milky slowly in air, and very large amounts of CO₂ make the milkiness clear again. Set up a limewater tube open to the air for comparison, and observe at set times.
[bio15-s2.10.4] Temperature can drift, so check the water bath regularly. Yeast takes time to start, so begin timing after the same period in every flask.
[bio15-s2.10.5] Yeast samples differ. Use yeast from the same batch, mixed well, for every flask, and repeat each set-up at least three times so a mean can be taken.

CANDIDATES bio16-s1: The cell cycle
[bio16-s1.0.b] Before you can follow the cell cycle you need to know what is being copied and shared out: the chromosomes. How many a cell has, and how they are arranged, decides whether it is diploid or haploid.
[bio16-s1.0.0] A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (the units of inheritance). There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio16-s1.0.1] Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin (DNA with its proteins). Before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio16-s1.0.2] Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent; the alleles (versions of a gene) on them may differ. Human body cells contain 23 such pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.3] Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, so that its chromosomes lie in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46. The 46 counts chromosomes, not pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.4] Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23. Haploid means one complete set, not half a chromosome.
[bio16-s1.0.5] When two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.
[bio16-s1.0.6] Haploid cells are made by meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number; mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s1.0.7] Each species has its own chromosome number: human 2n = 46, the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8, the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.
[bio16-s1.1.b] Cells don't just divide and stop: each one lives through a repeating sequence of events called the cell cycle. It shows how a cell prepares for division and then carries it out.
[bio16-s1.1.0] The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
[bio16-s1.1.1] It has three main activities, in this order. First, growth: the cell makes new proteins and organelles (the working parts of a cell) and gets bigger.
[bio16-s1.1.2] Second, DNA synthesis: every chromosome is copied, so the cell has two copies of its genetic information to hand out.
[bio16-s1.1.3] Third, division: the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells.
[bio16-s1.1.4] The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, about 90% of the cycle, so division is short.
[bio16-s1.1.5] In many human cells grown in the laboratory, one cycle takes about 24 hours.
[bio16-s1.1.6] Interphase is not a resting stage. The cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
[bio16-s1.1.7] One cycle, including division, produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s1.2.b] Not every cell divides at the same pace, and no cell divides without being told it is safe to. Whether a cell cycles depends on its job and on control checks built into the cycle.
[bio16-s1.2.0] Some cells leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, for example most mature nerve cells. Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide.
[bio16-s1.2.1] Other cells divide often throughout life, for example cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems, regions where growth happens).
[bio16-s1.2.2] The cycle is controlled. Proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on.
[bio16-s1.2.3] Signals from other cells (growth factors, chemical messages) tell a cell whether to divide. There is more on how loss of this control leads to cancer in Cancer.
[bio16-s1.2.4] Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle. Large sector: interphase (cell growth and DNA replication). Small sector: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis). Arrow for direction. Higher Level: also label G1, S, G2 and the four stages of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.3.b] Interphase is long and busy, and it has three parts: G1, S and G2. Together they prepare the cell so that division can be quick and accurate.
[bio16-s1.3.0] G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including the enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job.
[bio16-s1.3.1] S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere (the narrow point where they are held together).
[bio16-s1.3.2] G2 (second growth phase): further growth and more organelles, for example mitochondria. The proteins of the spindle (the fibres that later pull chromosomes apart) are made, and ATP is stored for division.
[bio16-s1.3.3] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), for example mature nerve cells.
[bio16-s1.3.4] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.
[bio16-s1.4.b] Mitosis is the division of the nucleus. It runs as one smooth process, but it is split into four stages to make it easier to describe, and each stage has a characteristic look and job.
[bio16-s1.4.0] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process, divided for convenience into four stages in this order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. It is followed by cytokinesis, which is division of the cytoplasm and not a stage of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.4.1] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear membrane breaks down, and spindle fibres (protein tubes called microtubules) begin to form.
[bio16-s1.4.2] In animal cells, two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them. Plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
[bio16-s1.4.3] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
[bio16-s1.4.4] Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first, which gives a V shape.
[bio16-s1.4.5] Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome, so each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio16-s1.4.6] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
[bio16-s1.4.7] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell, 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, shaded to show the two parents). Labels: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow (end of telophase).
[bio16-s1.5.b] You can tell which stage a cell is in just by looking at how its chromosomes are arranged under the microscope. Counting dividing cells then tells you how fast a tissue is dividing.
[bio16-s1.5.0] In stained cells from a growing root tip (for example onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase, seen as a nucleus with no visible chromosomes, because interphase is the longest part of the cycle.
[bio16-s1.5.1] Dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes: condensed and scattered for prophase; in a line across the middle for metaphase.
[bio16-s1.5.2] Two groups moving apart is anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, with new nuclei forming, is telophase.
[bio16-s1.5.3] The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing. For example, 24 dividing cells out of 200 gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%.
[bio16-s1.5.4] A high mitotic index shows a tissue that is dividing rapidly, such as a root tip or a tumour.
[bio16-s1.6.b] After the nucleus has divided, the cell still has to split in two. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and plant and animal cells do it differently because plant cells have a rigid wall.
[bio16-s1.6.0] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.1] In animal cells the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
[bio16-s1.6.2] In plant cells the rigid cell wall can't pinch in. Instead, vesicles (small membrane sacs) from the Golgi apparatus collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate.
[bio16-s1.6.3] The cell plate grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.4] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

CANDIDATES bio17-s2: Transcription and translation
[bio17-s2.0.b] A cell can't send its DNA out to the ribosomes, so it makes a working copy of the gene instead. Three molecules are involved in making and carrying that copy: DNA, the enzyme RNA polymerase and mRNA.
[bio17-s2.0.0] DNA holds the gene, the base sequence (the order of A, T, C and G) that codes for the protein.
[bio17-s2.0.1] In transcription only one of the two strands of the gene is read, the template strand. The DNA is not used up or changed, and it stays in the nucleus, so the original instructions stay safe.
[bio17-s2.0.2] RNA polymerase is the enzyme that carries out transcription. It binds to the DNA at the start of a gene, unwinds and separates the two strands there, and joins free RNA nucleotides into a strand complementary to the template.
[bio17-s2.0.3] RNA polymerase moves along the gene until it reaches the end, building the new strand as it goes.
[bio17-s2.0.4] mRNA (messenger RNA) is a single-stranded copy of one gene's code, a series of codons (groups of three bases), and it carries the code from the nucleus to a ribosome.
[bio17-s2.0.5] mRNA is short-lived and is broken down after use, so a cell can stop making a protein by no longer making its mRNA.
[bio17-s2.1.b] Translation (turning the mRNA code into a chain of amino acids) needs a delivery molecule for the amino acids and a workbench to assemble them on. These are tRNA and the ribosome, which is built from rRNA. Together with mRNA, these are the three types of RNA in protein synthesis.
[bio17-s2.1.0] tRNA (transfer RNA) is a small RNA molecule in the cytoplasm that carries one specific amino acid to the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.1.1] Each tRNA's anticodon (three bases) pairs with a matching codon on the mRNA, so tRNA is the molecule that “translates” codons into amino acids.
[bio17-s2.1.2] tRNA brings amino acids to the ribosome; it does not make them.
[bio17-s2.1.3] rRNA (ribosomal RNA) makes up the ribosome together with proteins. It holds the mRNA and tRNAs in position.
[bio17-s2.1.4] The rRNA of the large subunit also catalyses the formation of peptide bonds (the bonds that join amino acids), so it acts as an enzyme.
[bio17-s2.1.5] The ribosome is the site of translation, made of rRNA and protein in two subunits: a small subunit that binds the mRNA and a large subunit with room for two tRNAs at a time.
[bio17-s2.1.6] The ribosome moves along the mRNA one codon at a time, reading the code in order.
[bio17-s2.1.7] Ribosomes are free in the cytoplasm, making proteins used in the cell, or attached to the rough endoplasmic reticulum (a membrane network studded with ribosomes), making proteins for export or for membranes. There is more on this in Endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus.
[bio17-s2.2.b] Transcription is the making of an mRNA copy of a gene, and it takes place in the nucleus. It works by complementary base pairing, where each base on the DNA attracts one particular partner base.
[bio17-s2.2.0] 1. RNA polymerase attaches to the DNA at the start of the gene, unwinds the double helix there and separates the two strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs.
[bio17-s2.2.1] 2. One strand, the template strand, is read.
[bio17-s2.2.2] 2. Free RNA nucleotides pair with its exposed bases by complementary base pairing: A on DNA pairs with U on RNA, T with A, C with G, and G with C. RNA has U (uracil) where DNA has T.
[bio17-s2.2.3] 3. RNA polymerase joins the RNA nucleotides into a strand of mRNA as it moves along the gene, so the mRNA grows one nucleotide at a time.
[bio17-s2.2.4] 3. Behind it, the DNA strands re-join and rewind, so the DNA is left unchanged.
[bio17-s2.2.5] 4. At the end of the gene, RNA polymerase detaches and the finished mRNA is released.
[bio17-s2.2.6] 4. The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore (a gap in the nuclear envelope) and goes to a ribosome.
[bio17-s2.2.7] Diagram: a section of DNA unwound by RNA polymerase, with the template strand and the mRNA strand growing alongside it; label DNA, template strand, RNA polymerase, free RNA nucleotides, mRNA, base pair A–U, direction of movement.
[bio17-s2.3.b] A tRNA has to do two jobs: carry the right amino acid and recognise the right codon. Its folded shape has one end for each job.
[bio17-s2.3.0] A tRNA is a single strand of about 80 RNA nucleotides that folds back on itself, so some regions lie side by side and can base-pair. Hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in some regions hold the folded molecule in shape.
[bio17-s2.3.1] The folded molecule is a clover-leaf in two dimensions and folds further into an L shape in three dimensions.
[bio17-s2.3.2] At one end is the amino-acid attachment site, where one specific amino acid is attached by a specific enzyme, using energy from ATP.
[bio17-s2.3.3] At the opposite end is a loop with the anticodon: three unpaired bases that pair with a complementary codon on mRNA.
[bio17-s2.3.4] The code is read in codons of three bases, so each tRNA has an anticodon of exactly three bases and matches one codon (three bases of mRNA).
[bio17-s2.3.5] Each tRNA brings one amino acid. The attachment of a specific amino acid to each tRNA with a specific anticodon is what links each codon to its amino acid.
[bio17-s2.3.6] The ribosome holds two tRNAs side by side on neighbouring codons, so the code is read three bases at a time without gaps or overlaps.
[bio17-s2.3.7] Diagram: a clover-leaf tRNA, labelled: single RNA strand, regions of base pairing (hydrogen bonds), anticodon loop with three bases, amino-acid attachment site, amino acid.
[bio17-s2.4.b] Translation is the making of a polypeptide (a chain of amino acids) from the code in mRNA, and it takes place at ribosomes in the cytoplasm. It begins by assembling the ribosome on the mRNA and joining the first two amino acids.
[bio17-s2.4.0] 1. Start: the small ribosomal subunit attaches to the mRNA and moves along it to the start codon, AUG.
[bio17-s2.4.1] 1. A tRNA with the anticodon UAC, carrying methionine, pairs with the start codon, and the large subunit then joins.
[bio17-s2.4.2] 2. A second tRNA, whose anticodon is complementary to the next codon, arrives carrying its amino acid and pairs with that codon in the second position in the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.4.3] 3. A peptide bond forms between the two amino acids, a condensation reaction that releases water. It is catalysed by the rRNA of the ribosome.
[bio17-s2.4.4] 3. The first tRNA's amino acid is now attached to the second amino acid.
[bio17-s2.5.b] Once two amino acids are joined, the same cycle repeats along the mRNA until a stop signal is reached. Cells also run many ribosomes at once to make protein quickly.
[bio17-s2.5.0] 4. The ribosome moves along the mRNA by one codon, three bases. The first tRNA, now without its amino acid, is released into the cytoplasm, where it can pick up another of the same amino acid.
[bio17-s2.5.1] 4. A new tRNA pairs with the codon now in the empty position, because the move has exposed the next codon.
[bio17-s2.5.2] 5. Steps 2 to 4 repeat, the chain growing by one amino acid each time, in the order set by the codons.
[bio17-s2.5.3] 6. End: when a stop codon (UAA, UAG or UGA) is reached, no tRNA matches it, and a protein release factor binds instead.
[bio17-s2.5.4] 6. The polypeptide is released, and the ribosome's subunits separate from the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.5.5] Many ribosomes can translate the same mRNA at once, one behind the other, forming a polysome, so many copies of the protein are made quickly.
[bio17-s2.5.6] Translation also needs energy, both to attach amino acids to their tRNAs (from ATP) and to move the ribosome along the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.5.7] Diagram: a ribosome on an mRNA strand, with two tRNAs paired to neighbouring codons, a growing polypeptide on one, a peptide bond forming and a released tRNA leaving; label mRNA, codon, anticodon, tRNA, amino acid, peptide bond, ribosome (large and small subunits), polypeptide, direction of movement.
[bio17-s2.6.b] A newly made polypeptide is only a chain. It becomes a working protein when it folds into a particular three-dimensional shape, and the shape is set by its amino-acid sequence.
[bio17-s2.6.0] The sequence of amino acids (the primary structure) determines how the chain folds.
[bio17-s2.6.1] Bonds form between amino acids in different parts of the chain, such as hydrogen bonds and, in some proteins, sulphur bridges, giving the protein its particular three-dimensional shape.
[bio17-s2.6.2] Some proteins are made of more than one polypeptide, for example haemoglobin, which has four.
[bio17-s2.6.3] The shape determines the function, for example the active site of an enzyme or the binding site of an antibody or receptor. There is more on this in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio17-s2.6.4] A change in the amino-acid sequence can change the shape and stop the protein working, because the bonds that fold the chain form in different places.
[bio17-s2.6.5] Proteins made on the rough endoplasmic reticulum are folded and modified there and in the Golgi apparatus, then packaged into vesicles (small membrane sacs) for export, such as digestive enzymes and insulin.
[bio17-s2.6.6] In prokaryotes, which have no nucleus, so no nuclear envelope separates the steps, transcription and translation both take place in the cytoplasm, and ribosomes can begin translating an mRNA while it is still being transcribed.
[bio17-s2.7.b] Both processes copy DNA using complementary base pairing, so they are easy to confuse. They differ in what is copied, which enzymes do it, and what happens to the product.
[bio17-s2.7.0] Product: replication makes a complete copy of all the DNA, giving two DNA molecules; transcription makes an RNA copy of one gene, the mRNA.
[bio17-s2.7.1] Strands used: replication uses both strands as templates, so both new molecules are complete; transcription uses only one strand of the gene.
[bio17-s2.7.2] Enzymes: DNA helicase and DNA polymerase in replication; RNA polymerase in transcription, so the enzyme names tell the two processes apart.
[bio17-s2.7.3] Nucleotides and pairing: DNA nucleotides contain deoxyribose (a sugar) and pair A with T; RNA nucleotides contain ribose, and A on DNA pairs with U.
[bio17-s2.7.4] When: replication happens once per cycle, in S phase; transcription happens throughout the cell's life, for whichever genes are needed, many times.
[bio17-s2.7.5] Fate of the product: new DNA stays in the nucleus, but mRNA leaves for the cytoplasm and is broken down after use.
[bio17-s2.8.b] Exam questions often give a DNA sequence and ask for the mRNA, tRNA anticodons and amino acids. You can do this with a fixed five-step method.
[bio17-s2.8.0] 1. Write the mRNA complementary to the template strand (A → U, T → A, C → G, G → C).
[bio17-s2.8.1] 2. Divide the mRNA into codons from the start codon, because the ribosome reads three bases at a time.
[bio17-s2.8.2] 3. Write the tRNA anticodon complementary to each codon, because the anticodon pairs with the codon.
[bio17-s2.8.3] 4. Use the codon table given to find the amino acids.
[bio17-s2.8.4] 5. Stop at a stop codon, because no amino acid is added there.
[bio17-s2.9.b] Here is the method applied to one short template strand, showing each product in turn.
[bio17-s2.9.0] Template DNA TAC AAA CCG ATT gives mRNA AUG UUU GGC UAA, by pairing T with A, A with U and C with G.
[bio17-s2.9.1] The anticodons are UAC, AAA and CCG, with none for the stop codon UAA, because no tRNA matches a stop codon.
[bio17-s2.9.2] The polypeptide is methionine, phenylalanine and glycine, and then the chain stops.
[bio17-s2.9.3] Three codons code for amino acids, so the worked example gives three amino acids joined by two peptide bonds.
[bio17-s2.10.b] You can also model the two processes with paper and cards. A model makes the order of events clear, but it leaves out some of the real chemistry.
[bio17-s2.10.0] Paper strips for DNA, in a “nucleus” area, are copied onto mRNA strips that are carried to a “ribosome” area.
[bio17-s2.10.1] Cards for tRNA have a three-base anticodon on one side and an amino acid on the other, so each card shows what a real tRNA does.
[bio17-s2.10.2] In the role-play, students match anticodons to codons in order and link the amino acids with clips to represent peptide bonds.
[bio17-s2.10.3] Limitations of the role-play: it is slow and flat, and ignores the enzymes, energy and folding, so it shows the order of events but not how they happen chemically.

CANDIDATES bio17-s3: Mutations
[bio17-s3.0.b] DNA is copied every time a cell divides, and the copying is not perfect. A mutation is what is left when a change in the DNA is not put right, and it matters because it is the raw material for new traits.
[bio17-s3.0.0] A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA: either in the base sequence of a gene, called a point or gene mutation, or in the structure of a chromosome, called a chromosomal mutation.
[bio17-s3.0.1] A mutation that changes a gene produces a new allele (a different version of that gene).
[bio17-s3.0.2] Mutations arise spontaneously, mainly as uncorrected errors in base pairing during DNA replication (the copying of DNA before a cell divides), when a wrong base is paired and nobody fixes it.
[bio17-s3.0.3] Mutagens (agents that increase the rate of mutation) include ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive substances) and ultraviolet light, which damage DNA directly.
[bio17-s3.0.4] Other mutagens are chemicals such as those in tobacco smoke, and some viruses.
[bio17-s3.0.5] Repair enzymes correct most damage, so only the errors that escape repair become permanent mutations. There is more on mutagens and cancer in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.b] Whether a mutation matters to a family depends on which cell it happens in. What it does to the organism depends on whether it changes a protein, and for evolution it matters because mutation creates new alleles.
[bio17-s3.1.0] Body-cell (somatic) mutations affect only the individual and the cells descended from the mutated cell, so they are not inherited; they can lead to cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.1] Mutations in cells that form gametes (germ-line mutations) can be passed to offspring, and are then present in every cell of the child because every cell grows from the fertilised egg.
[bio17-s3.1.2] Most mutations are neutral, either because they fall in non-coding DNA (DNA that does not code for a protein) or because they are silent, meaning the protein is not changed.
[bio17-s3.1.3] Many mutations are harmful, because they change or stop a protein.
[bio17-s3.1.4] A few mutations are beneficial in a particular environment, for example lactase persistence (being able to digest milk sugar as an adult).
[bio17-s3.1.5] Mutations giving antibiotic resistance help bacteria survive antibiotics, so they are beneficial to the bacteria in the presence of the drug.
[bio17-s3.1.6] Mutation is the only source of new alleles, and so the ultimate source of variation for natural selection to act on. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio17-s3.2.b] A point mutation is a tiny change at one spot in a gene. Because the genetic code is read in codons (groups of three bases, each coding for one amino acid), the effect depends on exactly what changes. The simplest case is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.2.0] A point mutation is a change in the nucleotide sequence at one point in a gene, usually of one or a few base pairs. This includes small deletions and insertions, not only substitutions.
[bio17-s3.2.1] It occurs when a wrong nucleotide pairs during replication and is not corrected, or when a nucleotide is added or missed out.
[bio17-s3.2.2] At the next replication the change is copied into both strands, so it becomes permanent.
[bio17-s3.2.3] In a substitution one base is replaced by another, so only one codon changes.
[bio17-s3.2.4] Silent substitution: the new codon codes for the same amino acid, because the code is degenerate (several codons can code for the same amino acid), so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.2.5] Missense substitution: the new codon codes for a different amino acid, which may or may not change the protein's shape and function.
[bio17-s3.2.6] Nonsense substitution: the codon becomes a stop codon (a signal to end translation), so the polypeptide is cut short and usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.b] Adding or losing bases is more dangerous than swapping one, because it can shift the way the whole rest of the gene is read.
[bio17-s3.3.0] Insertion or deletion of one base, or of any number of bases that is not a multiple of three, is a frameshift: every codon after the change is read differently, because the code is read in threes without gaps from the start codon.
[bio17-s3.3.1] After a frameshift many amino acids are wrong, and a stop codon often appears early or is lost, so the protein usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.2] A frameshift near the start of a gene is more damaging than one near the end, because more of the protein is changed.
[bio17-s3.3.3] Insertion or deletion of three bases adds or removes one amino acid without a frameshift, so the rest of the protein is normal, but even one missing amino acid can change the folding, as in cystic fibrosis.
[bio17-s3.4.b] To model a mutation, write the mRNA in codons, apply the change, then read each new codon with the codon table. Start with the normal mRNA AUG UUU GGC AAA UAA, which codes for methionine, phenylalanine, glycine and lysine, then stops.
[bio17-s3.4.0] Substitution, silent: GGC changed to GGA; still glycine, so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.4.1] Substitution, missense: UUU changed to UCU; serine instead of phenylalanine.
[bio17-s3.4.2] Substitution, nonsense: AAA changed to UAA, a stop codon; the chain ends after glycine.
[bio17-s3.4.3] Insertion of G after AUG: AUG GUU UGG CAA AUA A… gives methionine, valine, tryptophan, glutamine, isoleucine…; every amino acid after the start is changed and the original stop codon is lost from the reading frame.
[bio17-s3.4.4] Deletion of the first U of UUU: AUG UUG GCA AAU AA… gives methionine, leucine, alanine, asparagine…; this is a frameshift, because the groups of three have moved along.
[bio17-s3.4.5] Deletion of the whole codon UUU: AUG GGC AAA UAA gives methionine, glycine, lysine; one amino acid is missing and the rest is unchanged, so there is no frameshift.
[bio17-s3.4.6] Word analogy for revision: THE CAT ATE THE RAT; substitution THE CAT ATE THE HAT changes one word; insertion THE CXA TAT ETH ERA T gives nonsense words after the change; deletion THE ATA TET HER AT shifts every word after it.
[bio17-s3.5.b] Sickle-cell anaemia shows how a change in a single base can alter a protein and so a whole disease. It is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.5.0] Sickle-cell anaemia is a substitution in the gene for beta-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin, on chromosome 11.
[bio17-s3.5.1] One base change alters the codon for the sixth amino acid from GAG to GUG in mRNA (CTC to CAC in the DNA strand being read), so valine replaces glutamic acid.
[bio17-s3.5.2] The changed haemoglobin, haemoglobin S, molecules stick together into long fibres when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a rigid sickle shape.
[bio17-s3.5.3] Sickled cells block small blood vessels, causing pain and organ damage, and are destroyed quickly, causing anaemia.
[bio17-s3.5.4] Sickle-cell is recessive: people with two copies have the disease; people with one copy (sickle-cell trait) are usually healthy and are more resistant to malaria.
[bio17-s3.5.5] This is why the sickle-cell allele is common in parts of Africa where malaria is common, an example of natural selection.
[bio17-s3.6.b] Cystic fibrosis is the example of a point mutation that is a small deletion. It shows that losing even one amino acid can ruin a protein.
[bio17-s3.6.0] Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7, which codes for a chloride-ion channel protein in cell membranes.
[bio17-s3.6.1] The most common cystic fibrosis mutation, F508del (delta F508), is a deletion of three bases, which removes one amino acid, phenylalanine, at position 508 of the protein.
[bio17-s3.6.2] The faulty protein folds wrongly, is broken down by the cell and does not reach the membrane, giving thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas.
[bio17-s3.6.3] Cystic fibrosis is recessive, and Ireland has the highest rate of cystic fibrosis in the world.
[bio17-s3.6.4] Newer CFTR modulator drugs help the faulty protein fold and work, which shows how knowing the mutation leads to treatment.
[bio17-s3.6.5] Cystic fibrosis is one of the conditions tested for in the newborn heel-prick screening offered to every baby in Ireland. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio17-s3.7.b] A chromosomal mutation changes a large piece of a chromosome, so many genes are affected at once, unlike a point mutation. A letter model makes the four types easy to follow.
[bio17-s3.7.0] A chromosomal mutation is a change in the structure of one or more chromosomes that affects many genes at once.
[bio17-s3.7.1] It occurs when chromosomes break, for example from ionising radiation or chemicals, or from errors when chromosomes are copied or separated in meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes), and the broken pieces are lost or rejoin in the wrong place.
[bio17-s3.7.2] Large changes can be seen under the light microscope in a karyotype (a picture of a cell's chromosomes).
[bio17-s3.7.3] Model: write a chromosome as A B C • D E F G H, where the letters are genes or groups of genes and • is the centromere (the constricted point where the two halves of a chromosome are joined).
[bio17-s3.7.4] A different, non-homologous chromosome (one that is not its partner in a pair) is written M N O • P Q R.
[bio17-s3.8.b] Two of the four types change how many copies of a section there are, one by losing it and one by repeating it.
[bio17-s3.8.0] Deletion: a section breaks off and is lost, so its genes are missing: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E H, with F and G lost.
[bio17-s3.8.1] Deletion is usually harmful, because a whole set of genes is missing.
[bio17-s3.8.2] Duplication: a section is copied twice, so its genes are repeated: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E F E F G H.
[bio17-s3.8.3] Extra copies can upset the balance of gene products (the proteins the genes make); over evolutionary time, duplicated genes can mutate to take on new functions.
[bio17-s3.9.b] The other two types move genes without necessarily losing any. An inversion turns a section around in the same chromosome; a translocation moves a section to another chromosome.
[bio17-s3.9.0] Inversion: a section breaks out, turns through 180 degrees and rejoins, so its genes are in reverse order: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D G F E H.
[bio17-s3.9.1] No genes are lost, so an inversion often has no effect on the person, unless a break falls inside a gene; it can cause problems in meiosis, leading to gametes with missing or extra genes.
[bio17-s3.9.2] Translocation: a section of one chromosome breaks off and joins a different, non-homologous chromosome; in a reciprocal translocation two chromosomes swap sections.
[bio17-s3.9.3] In a reciprocal translocation, A B C • D E F G H and M N O • P Q R become A B C • D E F Q R and M N O • P G H.
[bio17-s3.9.4] If no genes are lost the person may be healthy, but a break can join two genes into a new hybrid (fusion) gene, and carriers can produce gametes with unbalanced sets of genes.
[bio17-s3.9.5] Diagram: each of the four mutations drawn as a chromosome with lettered bands and a centromere, before and after, with the translocation shown between two chromosomes of different sizes.
[bio17-s3.10.b] Each structural type has a real disorder that illustrates it.
[bio17-s3.10.0] Deletion: cri du chat syndrome (French for “cry of the cat”) is caused by deletion of part of the short arm of chromosome 5.
[bio17-s3.10.1] Babies with cri du chat have a high-pitched cry like a cat's, a small head and low birth weight, and later intellectual disability and delayed development.
[bio17-s3.10.2] Duplication: Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A is caused by duplication of a small section of chromosome 17 containing the PMP22 gene.
[bio17-s3.10.3] The extra copy disrupts the insulating myelin sheath of nerves in the limbs, causing gradual weakness of the muscles of the feet and lower legs.
[bio17-s3.10.4] Inversion: an inversion within the gene for blood-clotting factor VIII on the X chromosome breaks the gene and causes nearly half of all cases of severe haemophilia A.
[bio17-s3.10.5] By contrast, a common inversion in chromosome 9 usually has no effect, because no genes are broken. There is more on haemophilia as a sex-linked trait in Sex-linked traits.
[bio17-s3.11.b] The Philadelphia chromosome shows how swapping pieces between chromosomes can create a gene that causes cancer.
[bio17-s3.11.0] The Philadelphia chromosome is found in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), a cancer of white blood cells.
[bio17-s3.11.1] A reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, in a bone-marrow stem cell, joins part of the ABL gene from chromosome 9 to the BCR gene on chromosome 22.
[bio17-s3.11.2] This forms a BCR-ABL fusion gene on a shortened chromosome 22, the Philadelphia chromosome.
[bio17-s3.11.3] The fusion gene codes for an enzyme that is permanently switched on and drives the cell to divide uncontrollably, so it is an oncogene (a gene that causes cancer).
[bio17-s3.11.4] CML is caused by a somatic mutation and is not inherited, because it happens in a body cell and not in a gamete-forming cell.
[bio17-s3.11.5] The Philadelphia chromosome was discovered in Philadelphia, USA, in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford; Janet Rowley showed in 1973 that it arises by translocation.
[bio17-s3.11.6] The drug imatinib blocks the BCR-ABL enzyme. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.12.b] Some conditions come from having the wrong number of chromosomes, not from a change in structure. They are therefore not examples of deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.0] Some conditions are caused by a change in the number of chromosomes, not in their structure, so they are not deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.1] They usually result from non-disjunction: a homologous pair, or two copies of a chromosome, fails to separate during meiosis, so a gamete has one chromosome too many or too few.
[bio17-s3.12.2] Down syndrome: in about 95% of cases there are three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), giving 47 chromosomes, after non-disjunction, usually during egg formation.
[bio17-s3.12.3] The risk of Down syndrome rises with the mother's age.
[bio17-s3.12.4] Features of Down syndrome include a characteristic facial appearance, some degree of intellectual disability and a higher risk of heart defects.
[bio17-s3.12.5] A few per cent of Down syndrome cases are caused instead by a translocation that attaches extra chromosome-21 material to another chromosome; only those cases involve a structural change.
[bio17-s3.13.b] Sex chromosomes can also be gained or lost, and a karyotype is the tool that shows number changes and large structural changes.
[bio17-s3.13.0] Turner syndrome is a change in number with 45 chromosomes, X: one X and no second sex chromosome, female.
[bio17-s3.13.1] Klinefelter syndrome is a change in number with 47 chromosomes, XXY, male.
[bio17-s3.13.2] A karyotype is a photograph of a cell's chromosomes, taken during mitosis when they are condensed, and arranged in homologous pairs by size and shape.
[bio17-s3.13.3] A karyotype shows changes in number (for example three chromosome 21s) and large structural changes (for example the shortened chromosome 22).
[bio17-s3.14.b] The two kinds of mutation differ in size, in how they arise, and in how they are detected. Comparing them in pairs is the quickest way to answer a distinguish question.
[bio17-s3.14.0] Scale: a point mutation changes one or a few bases in one gene; a chromosomal mutation changes the arrangement or number of copies of a large section with many genes.
[bio17-s3.14.1] How they arise: point mutations mainly from mispairing during replication; chromosomal mutations from breakage and wrong rejoining of chromosomes, often during meiosis.
[bio17-s3.14.2] Detection: point mutations need DNA sequencing (reading the order of bases), because they are too small to see; large chromosomal mutations can be seen in a karyotype under the light microscope.
[bio17-s3.14.3] Examples: sickle-cell anaemia (substitution) and cystic fibrosis (three-base deletion); cri du chat (deletion), Charcot–Marie–Tooth type 1A (duplication), severe haemophilia A (inversion), and the Philadelphia chromosome in CML (translocation).

CANDIDATES bio7-s1: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
[bio7-s1.0.b] Biologists sort all cells into two types, depending on whether the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. The names themselves tell you the difference, and you need the units to talk about how big cells are.
[bio7-s1.0.0] A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments wrapped in their own membrane). Its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. “Pro” means before and “karyon” means kernel or nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.1] A eukaryotic cell has its DNA enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and it has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. “Eu” means true, so eukaryote means “true nucleus”. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.2] Cells are far too small for millimetres to be a handy unit, so their sizes are given in micrometres. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres) and 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.
[bio7-s1.1.b] Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells look very different, but both are living cells that must do the same basic jobs. So they share a core set of features.
[bio7-s1.1.0] Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves the cell, and cytoplasm, the fluid where many of the cell's reactions take place.
[bio7-s1.1.1] Both have ribosomes (the tiny structures that make proteins), and both use DNA as the genetic material, reading it with the same genetic code.
[bio7-s1.1.2] Both respire, releasing energy from food, and both use ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy) as the energy currency.
[bio7-s1.1.3] These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor, because such an unusual set of identical features is unlikely to have arisen separately.
[bio7-s1.2.b] The most important difference between the two cell types is how the DNA is stored. This affects where the DNA sits, what shape it has and what it is packed with.
[bio7-s1.2.0] Nucleus: prokaryotes have none. Their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Eukaryotes have a true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores (small channels through the envelope).
[bio7-s1.2.1] Number and shape of chromosomes: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome (a closed loop of DNA). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes (DNA with two ends).
[bio7-s1.2.2] Packaging: eukaryotic DNA is wound around histone proteins, which help to pack it. In bacteria the circular chromosome is not wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s1.2.3] Prokaryotes often also have plasmids, small extra rings of DNA separate from the main chromosome. Plasmids can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance, which is why resistance can spread between bacteria.
[bio7-s1.2.4] Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast), not in all eukaryotes, so do not say that eukaryotes have none or that all have them.
[bio7-s1.3.b] Eukaryotic cells divide their work between membrane-bound organelles, whereas prokaryotes have to do everything without them. Ribosomes are the one structure both have, but they differ in size.
[bio7-s1.3.0] Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none, so no mitochondria, no chloroplasts and no other membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.3.1] Without organelles, prokaryotes carry out respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it (folds of the membrane that increase its area).
[bio7-s1.3.2] Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. This is one more way these organelles resemble prokaryotic cells.
[bio7-s1.3.3] Ribosomes: both cell types have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). The S number is a measure of size.
[bio7-s1.3.4] Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they stop bacteria making proteins and harm bacteria but not human cells, which use 80S ribosomes in their cytoplasm.
[bio7-s1.4.b] The remaining differences are about the whole cell: how big it is, what surrounds it, and how the organism is built and reproduces.
[bio7-s1.4.0] Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so they are roughly ten times larger in length.
[bio7-s1.4.1] Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one, made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells have chitin walls, and animal cells have none.
[bio7-s1.4.2] Some prokaryotes have extra outer structures: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum, a whip-like tail, for movement. There is more on the full bacterial cell in Microorganisms.
[bio7-s1.4.3] Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular (made of a single cell) and divide by binary fission, in which one cell splits in two. Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis.
[bio7-s1.4.4] Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotes are protists, fungi, plants and animals.
[bio7-s1.5.b] How a cell looks in a micrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope) depends on the microscope, because a light microscope cannot show very small structures but an electron microscope can. Knowing what each shows lets you identify a cell.
[bio7-s1.5.0] Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure. It is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
[bio7-s1.5.1] Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm and the cell membrane as a line. Plant cells also show the cell wall, a large vacuole (a fluid-filled sac) and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.5.2] Electron micrographs show what the light microscope cannot. In prokaryotes they show the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall.
[bio7-s1.5.3] In eukaryotes, electron micrographs show the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
[bio7-s1.5.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule, flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), with the eukaryotic cell drawn much larger.
[bio7-s1.6.b] You may be given a micrograph and asked whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and to justify the answer. Work through the same checks in order.
[bio7-s1.6.0] 1. Check for a nucleus. If one is present, the cell is eukaryotic, because only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nucleus.
[bio7-s1.6.1] 2. Check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size. A cell of about 1–10 µm suggests a prokaryote, since eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm.
[bio7-s1.6.2] 3. Look for membrane-bound organelles, which only eukaryotes have.
[bio7-s1.6.3] 4. For a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts, which mean a plant cell, or their absence, which suggests an animal cell. There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.
