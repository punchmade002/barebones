CANDIDATES bio20-s2: Hormonal manipulation
[bio20-s2.0.b] Hormones are chemical messengers that control growth and body functions. Because they act in tiny amounts, people can change how an organism works by adding hormones or blocking them. This is called hormonal manipulation, and it is used in sport, health and agriculture.
[bio20-s2.0.0] Hormonal manipulation is the deliberate use of natural or synthetic (man-made) hormones, or of drugs that block hormones, to change how an organism grows or functions.
[bio20-s2.0.1] It can work by adding a hormone the body lacks or by supplying extra, or by blocking a hormone's release or action, so the body's own control system is deliberately altered.
[bio20-s2.0.2] The syllabus asks you to recognise the impact of these manipulations on organisms, so for each use you need the benefit and the cost.
[bio20-s2.1.b] Some athletes take hormones to gain a physical edge. Two are misused: anabolic steroids, which build muscle, and EPO, which boosts the blood's oxygen supply. Both are dangerous and banned.
[bio20-s2.1.0] Anabolic steroids are synthetic derivatives of testosterone (the male sex hormone). They increase muscle mass and strength and speed up recovery from training, which is why athletes misuse them.
[bio20-s2.1.1] Anabolic steroids cause liver damage, increase the risk of heart disease, and cause acne and mood changes including aggression.
[bio20-s2.1.2] In males, anabolic steroids reduce sperm production, cause infertility and cause shrinking of the testes.
[bio20-s2.1.3] In females, anabolic steroids cause male characteristics to develop, because the drug is a form of a male hormone.
[bio20-s2.1.4] EPO (erythropoietin) is a hormone made by the kidneys that stimulates red blood cell production.
[bio20-s2.1.5] Athletes misuse EPO to increase the oxygen-carrying capacity of the blood, because red blood cells carry oxygen, and so their endurance.
[bio20-s2.1.6] Extra red cells thicken the blood, which increases the risk of clots, heart attack and stroke.
[bio20-s2.1.7] The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) bans these substances in competitive sport and tests athletes through urine and blood samples. Their use is unfair to other competitors.
[bio20-s2.2.b] Doctors use hormones to replace ones the body no longer makes enough of, to control fertility and to relieve symptoms of the menopause such as hot flushes. Each treatment fixes a specific hormone problem.
[bio20-s2.2.0] Insulin injections treat type 1 diabetes, in which the pancreas makes little or no insulin, so blood glucose would otherwise rise too high.
[bio20-s2.2.1] Most insulin used today is human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria (bacteria given the human insulin gene). There is more on this in Genetic Engineering.
[bio20-s2.2.2] The contraceptive pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone, or progesterone alone. These are female sex hormones.
[bio20-s2.2.3] The pill inhibits (reduces) the release of FSH and LH, the pituitary hormones that make an egg mature and be released, so no egg matures and ovulation (release of an egg) is prevented.
[bio20-s2.2.4] Fertility treatment uses hormones to stimulate the ovaries to release eggs, which helps women who do not ovulate normally to become pregnant.
[bio20-s2.2.5] Hormone replacement therapy (HRT) supplies oestrogen, often with progesterone, after the menopause (when the ovaries stop producing these hormones) to relieve symptoms such as hot flushes and to reduce bone loss.
[bio20-s2.2.6] For some women, HRT slightly increases the risk of some cancers and of blood clots, so it carries a risk as well as a benefit.
[bio20-s2.2.7] Thyroxine tablets are given to people whose thyroid gland produces too little thyroxine, replacing the missing hormone.
[bio20-s2.3.b] Farmers use hormones to control breeding, boost yields and manage plants. Animal uses raise welfare and food-safety concerns, while plant hormones are used as weedkillers, rooting aids and ripening agents.
[bio20-s2.3.0] Progesterone, given as a vaginal sponge or implant, synchronises the breeding cycles of cattle and sheep, so a herd can be bred and give birth at the same time.
[bio20-s2.3.1] Growth-promoting hormones increase meat production in cattle.
[bio20-s2.3.2] Bovine somatotropin (BST), a growth hormone from cattle, increases milk yield in cattle.
[bio20-s2.3.3] Growth-promoting hormones and BST are banned in the EU, including Ireland, because of concerns about animal welfare and hormone residues (traces left behind) in food. BST is permitted in the USA.
[bio20-s2.3.4] Synthetic auxins such as 2,4-D are selective weedkillers (they kill some plants but not others). They kill broad-leaved weeds in cereal crops by making them grow uncontrollably.
[bio20-s2.3.5] Auxin rooting powder makes cuttings (pieces cut from a plant) form roots, so new plants can be grown from them.
[bio20-s2.3.6] Ethene gas is used to ripen fruit such as bananas in storage after transport, so fruit can be shipped unripe and ripened when needed.
[bio20-s2.4.b] Hormonal manipulation brings real gains but also real harms. To evaluate it, weigh the benefits against the costs for each use.
[bio20-s2.4.0] Benefits: better health, treatment of disease (such as insulin for type 1 diabetes), increased food production and convenience (such as synchronised breeding and ripening on demand).
[bio20-s2.4.1] Costs: side effects (such as those of anabolic steroids, EPO and HRT), an unfair advantage in sport, animal welfare concerns, hormone residues in food and environmental effects of weedkillers.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio8-s3: Metabolic roles and nutritional sources
[bio8-s3.0.b] Carbohydrates do far more than supply energy. Glucose is fuel, a product of photosynthesis and a building block for storage molecules, and other carbohydrates give structure and carry sugar around organisms.
[bio8-s3.0.0] Respiratory and catabolic role (catabolic means breaking molecules down): glucose is the main respiratory substrate, the fuel respiration uses. It is broken down to release energy, which is trapped as ATP, the cell's energy carrier. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio8-s3.0.1] The brain relies mainly on glucose for its energy, so a steady supply of glucose in the blood matters.
[bio8-s3.0.2] Photosynthetic role: glucose is the product of photosynthesis, the anabolic process (anabolic means building molecules up) in which plants use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio8-s3.0.3] Almost all the carbohydrate in food chains therefore comes from photosynthesis, because plants are the producers that make it first and every consumer depends on them.
[bio8-s3.0.4] Anabolic and storage role: excess glucose is built up into storage polysaccharides (long chains of sugar units), which are starch in plants and glycogen in the liver and muscles of animals.
[bio8-s3.0.5] These stores are broken down again (catabolism) when glucose is needed, so the organism can release it back as fuel.
[bio8-s3.0.6] Structural role: cellulose forms plant cell walls, and chitin forms fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons (the hard outer body casing). The pentose (five-carbon) sugars ribose and deoxyribose are part of RNA and DNA nucleotides.
[bio8-s3.0.7] Transport role: plants move sugar from the leaves to other parts as sucrose, carried in the phloem (the tissue that transports food in plants).
[bio8-s3.1.b] Lipids (fats, oils and related molecules) are used for energy storage, but their uses go much further. They insulate, protect, build membranes, waterproof surfaces and act as regulators.
[bio8-s3.1.0] Respiratory and catabolic role: fats are respired to release energy, especially when glucose and glycogen run low.
[bio8-s3.1.1] A gram of fat releases about twice as much energy as a gram of carbohydrate or protein, so lipid is a compact long-term energy store.
[bio8-s3.1.2] Storage (anabolic): excess food is built into triglycerides (the main type of fat molecule) and stored as fat in adipose tissue, under the skin and around organs in animals.
[bio8-s3.1.3] Plants store oils in seeds, such as sunflower seeds, and in fruits, such as olives.
[bio8-s3.1.4] Insulation: fat under the skin reduces heat loss, as in the thick blubber of whales and seals. The myelin sheath around nerve fibres is rich in lipid and insulates them.
[bio8-s3.1.5] Protection: fat around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against physical damage.
[bio8-s3.1.6] Structural role: phospholipids form the bilayer (double layer) of all cell membranes, including the membranes inside chloroplasts and mitochondria.
[bio8-s3.1.7] Photosynthetic role: the light-absorbing pigments of photosynthesis are held in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast, which are built of phospholipids. Plants also convert some of the glucose made in photosynthesis into oils.
[bio8-s3.2.b] Two further roles of lipids come from their water-repelling nature and from their chemistry as signalling molecules.
[bio8-s3.2.0] Waterproofing: waxes, which are lipids, form the waxy cuticle on leaves, reducing water loss. They also waterproof feathers and fur.
[bio8-s3.2.1] Regulators: steroid hormones such as testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone are lipids, which means not all hormones are proteins.
[bio8-s3.2.2] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fat. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio8-s3.3.b] Proteins have many different jobs in the body, from speeding up reactions to defence, movement and transport. Each role below has a named protein you can give as an example.
[bio8-s3.3.0] Enzymes: almost all enzymes are proteins. They are biological catalysts that speed up the reactions of metabolism (all the chemical reactions in a cell) without being used up, for example amylase digests starch to maltose.
[bio8-s3.3.1] Hormones: some hormones are proteins, for example insulin, made by the pancreas, which lowers blood glucose. Other hormones are lipids.
[bio8-s3.3.2] Immunity: antibodies are proteins made by lymphocytes (B cells, a type of white blood cell). Each binds to a specific antigen (a marker molecule) on a pathogen (a disease-causing organism), marking it for destruction.
[bio8-s3.3.3] Transport of substances in the body: haemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen.
[bio8-s3.3.4] Movement: actin and myosin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they shorten to make the muscle contract.
[bio8-s3.3.5] Energy: protein is respired only when carbohydrate and fat are in short supply.
[bio8-s3.3.6] Excess amino acids (the units proteins are made of) cannot be stored. The liver removes their amino group (deamination), forming urea, which is excreted.
[bio8-s3.3.7] Growth and repair: proteins are the main raw material for new cells and tissues, so children, pregnant women and people recovering from injury need more.
[bio8-s3.4.b] Many proteins sit in the cell membrane (the phospholipid bilayer around every cell) and decide what crosses it and how cells respond to signals. The specification names four kinds.
[bio8-s3.4.0] Membrane proteins are embedded in the phospholipid bilayer. They control what enters and leaves cells and how cells communicate.
[bio8-s3.4.1] Channel proteins form water-filled pores through the membrane that let specific ions or molecules pass by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), for example the sodium and potassium channels of neurons, which allow nerve impulses.
[bio8-s3.4.2] Transporters (carrier proteins) bind a specific molecule and change shape to move it across the membrane.
[bio8-s3.4.3] Some transporters use energy from ATP to move substances against a concentration gradient (active transport), for example the sodium-potassium pump. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio8-s3.4.4] Receptors are proteins on the cell surface, or inside the cell, with a shape that fits one signalling molecule such as a hormone or neurotransmitter. When the signal binds, the cell responds, for example insulin receptors on liver and muscle cells.
[bio8-s3.4.5] This is why a hormone affects only its target cells: only cells with the matching receptor can respond.
[bio8-s3.4.6] Structural proteins give strength and support. Keratin forms hair, nails and the outer skin, and collagen forms skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone. Proteins also form the cytoskeleton, the internal scaffold of cells.
[bio8-s3.4.7] Diagram: a section of cell membrane (phospholipid bilayer) showing a channel protein, a carrier protein, and a receptor protein with its signalling molecule. Label each.
[bio8-s3.5.b] Hormones link the metabolic roles of proteins and lipids, because they are made from both. They let one part of the body control the activity of another.
[bio8-s3.5.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood to target cells, where it regulates their activity. Hormones regulate growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
[bio8-s3.5.1] Hormones are biomolecules from two groups: proteins, such as insulin, and steroid lipids, such as testosterone and oestrogen. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio8-s3.5.2] Hormones work only on cells with the matching receptor proteins, so only target cells respond.
[bio8-s3.5.3] Plants also make chemical regulators, called plant growth regulators, for example auxin.
[bio8-s3.6.b] Knowing the roles of each biomolecule is only half the story. You also need to name the foods that supply each one.
[bio8-s3.6.0] Carbohydrate sources, sugars: fruit, honey, jam, sweets and fizzy drinks, and lactose (milk sugar) in milk.
[bio8-s3.6.1] Carbohydrate sources, starch: bread, pasta, rice, potatoes and breakfast cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.2] Carbohydrate sources, fibre (cellulose): vegetables, fruit, wholegrain cereals and brown bread, and beans.
[bio8-s3.6.3] Lipid sources, fats (mostly saturated): butter, cream, cheese, lard and fatty meat.
[bio8-s3.6.4] Lipid sources, oils (mostly unsaturated): olive and sunflower oils, nuts and seeds, avocados, and oily fish such as salmon and mackerel.
[bio8-s3.6.5] Protein sources, animal: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Protein sources, plant: beans, peas, lentils, nuts, soya and tofu, and cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.6] Animal proteins contain all the essential amino acids (those the body cannot make). Many single plant foods lack one or more, so vegetarian and vegan diets combine a range of plant proteins, for example beans with cereal.
[bio8-s3.7.b] What we eat affects both our health and the environment, so food sources link back to the roles of each biomolecule.
[bio8-s3.7.0] Diets high in saturated fat are linked to raised blood cholesterol and heart disease.
[bio8-s3.7.1] Diets high in free sugars are linked to tooth decay, obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[bio8-s3.7.2] Producing plant protein generally needs less land and water, and causes lower greenhouse gas emissions, than producing meat. There is more on balanced diets in The Digestive System.
