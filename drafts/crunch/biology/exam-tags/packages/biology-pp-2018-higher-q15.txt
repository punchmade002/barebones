CANDIDATES bio21-s2: Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model
[bio21-s2.0.b] Homeostasis is the maintenance of a relatively constant internal environment despite changes in external or internal conditions. The internal environment is the tissue fluid and blood around the cells, so it is the conditions the cells actually live in that are kept steady.
[bio21-s2.0.0] Factors kept constant include body temperature, blood glucose concentration, water and salt balance, blood pH and carbon dioxide level.
[bio21-s2.0.1] Enzymes (the proteins that speed up the body's chemical reactions) work best within a narrow range of temperature and pH, so a drift outside that range slows or stops reactions.
[bio21-s2.0.2] If the concentration of the fluid around a cell changes, the cell gains or loses water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane), which can swell or shrink it.
[bio21-s2.0.3] Steady conditions let cells function whatever the conditions outside the body.
[bio21-s2.0.4] Values fluctuate slightly around a set point (normal level) rather than staying perfectly fixed; for example, normal core body temperature is about 37 °C.
[bio21-s2.1.b] Every homeostatic response follows the same chain: something changes, the change is detected, a decision is made and the body acts. Learning the five named parts lets you analyse any example, however unfamiliar.
[bio21-s2.1.0] Stimulus: a change away from the set point, such as the body getting too hot.
[bio21-s2.1.1] Receptor: detects the change, because the body can only respond to a change it can sense.
[bio21-s2.1.2] Control centre: compares the change with the set point and sends instructions. It is often in the brain, e.g. the hypothalamus (a region at the base of the brain), or in a gland.
[bio21-s2.1.3] Effector: a muscle or gland that carries out the response, because these are the tissues able to act on the instructions.
[bio21-s2.1.4] Response: what the effector does, which returns the factor towards the set point.
[bio21-s2.1.5] In vertebrates, the control centre is linked to receptors and effectors by nervous pathways (neurons, fast) and hormonal pathways (hormones in the blood, slower).
[bio21-s2.2.b] Negative feedback is the way homeostatic systems are kept stable. The word negative means the response works against the change that triggered it, like a thermostat switching the heating off once a room is warm enough.
[bio21-s2.2.0] In negative feedback, the response reverses the original change: a rise is followed by a fall, and a fall by a rise.
[bio21-s2.2.1] Once the factor returns to normal, the stimulus is removed and the response stops, so the body does not overcorrect.
[bio21-s2.2.2] Because the response stops at the set point, the factor oscillates (swings slightly above and below) close to it rather than moving far away.
[bio21-s2.2.3] Diagram: a negative-feedback loop of set point, change, receptor, control centre, effector, response and back towards the set point, with a second branch for a change in the opposite direction.
[bio21-s2.3.b] Positive feedback is the opposite of negative feedback, and knowing it helps you see what makes homeostasis special.
[bio21-s2.3.0] Positive feedback: the response increases the original change, so the change keeps growing, e.g. oxytocin (a hormone) causes stronger and stronger contractions during labour.
[bio21-s2.3.1] Positive feedback is rare and is not used in homeostasis, because it pushes a factor further from the set point instead of holding it steady.
[bio21-s2.4.b] Body temperature is the clearest example of a nervous control system. The hypothalamus is the temperature control centre, and it works with receptors and effectors, linked by fast nervous pathways, that respond to heat or cold.
[bio21-s2.4.0] Stimulus and receptors: receptors in the hypothalamus detect blood temperature, and receptors in the skin detect skin temperature.
[bio21-s2.4.1] Control centre: the hypothalamus compares the temperature with the set point.
[bio21-s2.4.2] Too hot, effector 1: sweat glands produce sweat, which cools the skin as it evaporates (evaporation takes heat from the skin).
[bio21-s2.4.3] Too hot, effector 2: arterioles (small arteries) in the skin widen (vasodilation), so more blood flows near the surface and loses heat.
[bio21-s2.4.4] Too cold, effector 1: muscles shiver, releasing heat from respiration, which warms the body.
[bio21-s2.4.5] Too cold, effector 2: arterioles in the skin narrow (vasoconstriction), so less blood flows near the surface, reducing heat loss.
[bio21-s2.4.6] Behaviour changes, such as adding clothes or seeking shelter, also help restore the temperature.
[bio21-s2.5.b] Blood glucose is kept steady by a hormone rather than by neurons. A hormone is a chemical messenger carried in the blood, so this pathway is slower than the nervous one.
[bio21-s2.5.0] Stimulus: blood glucose rises after a meal, because glucose from digested food enters the blood.
[bio21-s2.5.1] Receptor: cells of the pancreas (a gland beside the stomach) detect the rise.
[bio21-s2.5.2] Response: the pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that lowers blood glucose.
[bio21-s2.5.3] Effect: cells take up glucose, and the liver stores it as glycogen (a storage carbohydrate made from glucose).
[bio21-s2.5.4] Glucose falls back to normal, so less insulin is released. This is negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.5.5] In type 1 diabetes this feedback fails because too little insulin is made, so blood glucose stays high.
[bio21-s2.6.b] In this example the hormone controls its own level. The level of thyroxine (a hormone made by the thyroid gland) is detected, and the thyroxine itself switches off the signal that makes more of it. This makes it a standard example of negative feedback.
[bio21-s2.6.0] Stimulus: thyroxine falls below normal in the blood.
[bio21-s2.6.1] Receptors: the hypothalamus and pituitary gland (a gland at the base of the brain) detect the low level.
[bio21-s2.6.2] Response: the pituitary releases TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone), and TSH makes the thyroid release thyroxine.
[bio21-s2.6.3] High thyroxine inhibits the release of TSH by the pituitary, so thyroxine production falls. This is why the level does not keep rising.
[bio21-s2.7.b] The body must also keep the water concentration of the blood steady, which it does through a hormone released from the pituitary gland.
[bio21-s2.7.0] The hypothalamus detects the water concentration of the blood, and the pituitary adjusts the release of ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
[bio21-s2.7.1] ADH changes how much water the kidneys reabsorb (take back into the blood): when the blood is short of water, more ADH is released, so more water is reabsorbed and less is lost in urine. There is more on this in The Urinary System.
[bio21-s2.8.b] A rise in blood carbon dioxide is detected by the brain, which increases the breathing rate. Faster breathing removes carbon dioxide from the blood, which returns its level towards the set point. There is more on this in The Breathing System.
[bio21-s2.8.0] Carbon dioxide level is one of the factors kept constant, and it is controlled by negative feedback: the brain detects a rise and increases the breathing rate.

CANDIDATES bio22-s1: Pathogens
[bio22-s1.0.b] Some organisms and particles make us ill, and the biology of disease starts with naming them. A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host, the organism it lives in or on.
[bio22-s1.0.0] An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, which is why such diseases can spread through a population.
[bio22-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful, so bacteria and fungi should not be lumped together as 'germs'. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s1.0.2] Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: they invade and destroy host cells or tissues, they release toxins, or they take nutrients from the host.
[bio22-s1.0.3] Invasion means the pathogen destroys cells: viruses burst the cells they reproduce in, and the malaria parasite bursts red blood cells.
[bio22-s1.0.4] Toxins are poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work; the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms.
[bio22-s1.0.5] In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage, for example inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia.
[bio22-s1.1.b] A pathogen only survives if it can keep moving from host to host. So each one is adapted to its host and, often, to a carrier that helps it spread.
[bio22-s1.1.0] Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host.
[bio22-s1.1.1] Many pathogens need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease: the mosquito for malaria and aphids (small sap-sucking insects) for many plant viruses.
[bio22-s1.1.2] The routes by which pathogens pass between hosts are called routes of transmission. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.2.b] A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is neither a cell nor a virus. It is the odd one out among pathogens, because a protein alone causes the disease.
[bio22-s1.2.0] The name comes from proteinaceous infectious particle, a term coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
[bio22-s1.2.1] A prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein, the prion protein, which is found on nerve cells, especially in the brain.
[bio22-s1.2.2] When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction.
[bio22-s1.2.3] Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so that the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope. This is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathy.
[bio22-s1.2.4] Prion diseases take years to appear, are always fatal and have no cure.
[bio22-s1.3.b] Prion diseases have been found in cattle, sheep and humans. Because prions are very hard to destroy, prevention depends on keeping infected material out of the food chain.
[bio22-s1.3.0] Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, mad cow disease) in cattle spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals.
[bio22-s1.3.1] Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans is linked to eating BSE-infected beef and was first described in the UK in 1996.
[bio22-s1.3.2] Scrapie affects sheep. Kuru affected the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
[bio22-s1.3.3] Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants.
[bio22-s1.3.4] The immune system does not attack prions, because they are the body's own protein in a different shape, so the body has no defence against them.
[bio22-s1.3.5] Prevention therefore keeps prions out of the food chain: meat-and-bone meal is banned from the feed of farm animals, and the brain and spinal cord of cattle are removed from food as specified risk material.
[bio22-s1.4.b] A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat. It is an obligate intracellular parasite, meaning it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s1.4.0] Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work.
[bio22-s1.4.1] Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins (molecules on the cell surface that it fits onto), so it infects only certain hosts and tissues: HIV infects helper T cells and influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways.
[bio22-s1.4.2] Human viral diseases include influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) and AIDS (HIV).
[bio22-s1.4.3] Animal viral diseases include foot-and-mouth disease, which affects cattle, sheep and pigs, and avian influenza, which affects birds.
[bio22-s1.4.4] Plant viral disease: tobacco mosaic virus infects tobacco and tomatoes.
[bio22-s1.4.5] Antibiotics don't affect viruses, because viruses have no cell structures for the drugs to target and reproduce inside host cells.
[bio22-s1.5.b] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes with no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, about 1–10 µm long. Only some bacteria are pathogens, but those that are can multiply very quickly inside the body.
[bio22-s1.5.0] Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission (one cell splitting into two) in host tissues, so numbers rise fast.
[bio22-s1.5.1] Most bacteria cause harm by releasing toxins; some invade and destroy tissue.
[bio22-s1.5.2] Most bacterial diseases can be treated with antibiotics, which target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s1.5.3] Antibiotic-resistant strains (bacteria no longer killed by the drug) are increasing, for example MRSA.
[bio22-s1.6.b] Each bacterial disease has a named bacterium and a usual route of spread. Learn them as pairs, and note the plant and cattle examples as well as the human ones.
[bio22-s1.6.0] Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spreads in airborne droplets and mainly affects the lungs.
[bio22-s1.6.1] Food poisoning is caused by Salmonella, which spreads through contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs.
[bio22-s1.6.2] Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae in water contaminated with faeces, and causes severe diarrhoea.
[bio22-s1.6.3] Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani. Its spores in soil enter a wound, and its toxin causes muscle spasms, known as lockjaw.
[bio22-s1.6.4] Lyme disease is caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and is passed on by tick bites, so the tick is the vector.
[bio22-s1.6.5] In plants, fire blight of apple and pear trees is caused by Erwinia amylovora.
[bio22-s1.6.6] In cattle, bovine TB is caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
[bio22-s1.7.b] Fungi are eukaryotes (their cells have a nucleus) with cell walls of chitin. Most are made of thread-like hyphae, and some, such as yeasts, are single cells. Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts.
[bio22-s1.7.0] Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, and the fungus absorbs the products.
[bio22-s1.7.1] Fungi spread by spores, which are often carried by wind, water or contact.
[bio22-s1.7.2] Athlete's foot and ringworm are skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (the protein in skin); ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
[bio22-s1.7.3] Thrush is caused by the yeast Candida albicans in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check.
[bio22-s1.7.4] Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
[bio22-s1.7.5] Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.
[bio22-s1.8.b] Fungi infect plants and animals as well as people. The examples below affect trees, cereal crops and amphibians.
[bio22-s1.8.0] Ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, is killing ash trees across Europe.
[bio22-s1.8.1] Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, is spread by elm bark beetles, which act as vectors.
[bio22-s1.8.2] Cereal rusts such as wheat rust are caused by Puccinia species.
[bio22-s1.8.3] Chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.9.b] Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are neither animals, plants nor fungi (kingdom Protista). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host. Malaria is one example.
[bio22-s1.9.0] Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, which is passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector.
[bio22-s1.9.1] The parasite reproduces first in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia (too few red blood cells).
[bio22-s1.9.2] Malaria is controlled with insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining of standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs such as artemisinin. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.10.b] Other protists cause gut and blood diseases in people, and one protist-like organism caused a famine in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.10.0] Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, which spreads in water or food contaminated with faeces.
[bio22-s1.10.1] African sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei, which is spread by tsetse flies, the vector.
[bio22-s1.10.2] Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus, being thread-like and spread by spores, but it is classed with the protists.
[bio22-s1.10.3] Its spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather, and it rots leaves and tubers.
[bio22-s1.10.4] Potato late blight caused the Irish Great Famine from 1845, because potatoes were the main crop and the disease destroyed them. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.11.b] A parasite lives in or on another organism, its host, feeding on it and harming it. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods. Endoparasites live inside the host.
[bio22-s1.11.0] Tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers.
[bio22-s1.11.1] Humans become infected with tapeworms by eating undercooked infected pork or beef.
[bio22-s1.11.2] Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) is a flatworm of sheep and cattle that damages the liver.
[bio22-s1.11.3] The liver fluke needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.11.4] Onchocerca volvulus is a roundworm spread by blackflies that causes river blindness. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.12.b] Ectoparasites live on the outside of the host. Worms inside the body are treated with drugs, and prevention targets how the parasite reaches us.
[bio22-s1.12.0] Head lice are ectoparasites that feed on blood from the scalp.
[bio22-s1.12.1] Ticks feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio22-s1.12.2] Parasitic worms are treated with anthelmintic drugs that kill them, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s1.12.3] Prevention: cook meat thoroughly, maintain sanitation, and control the second host or vector, because breaking the life cycle stops the parasite spreading.
[bio22-s1.13.b] The six types of pathogen differ in whether they are cells, what genetic material they carry, their size and how they are treated. The treatment follows from the structure, which is why antibiotics work only on bacteria.
[bio22-s1.13.0] Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment.
[bio22-s1.13.1] Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; treated with antivirals, not antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.2] Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; treated with antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.3] Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; from single cells to large networks of hyphae; treated with antifungals.
[bio22-s1.13.4] Protist: eukaryotic and mostly single-celled; DNA; treated with antiprotozoal drugs such as antimalarials.
[bio22-s1.13.5] Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; from millimetres to metres in size; treated with anthelmintics.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio22-s5: White blood cells
[bio22-s5.0.b] The immune system is run by several different kinds of white blood cell, and they all start from the same place. Knowing where each type comes from and matures explains how the body learns to attack invaders but not itself.
[bio22-s5.0.0] White blood cells (leucocytes) are the cells of the immune system. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, which holds the genes they need to make the proteins for their defensive jobs.
[bio22-s5.0.1] All white blood cells develop from stem cells (cells that can divide and become different cell types) in the red bone marrow, the soft tissue inside some bones.
[bio22-s5.0.2] They travel in the blood and lymph (the fluid that drains from tissues), and many leave the capillaries to enter the tissues, because that is where pathogens (disease-causing organisms) are found.
[bio22-s5.0.3] There are two broad groups. Phagocytes (neutrophils and monocytes/macrophages) engulf pathogens, taking them into the cell.
[bio22-s5.0.4] The second group is the lymphocytes (B cells, T cells and natural killer cells). These are small cells with a large round nucleus filling most of the cell and little cytoplasm.
[bio22-s5.0.5] B lymphocytes mature in the bone marrow. T lymphocytes leave the marrow and mature in the thymus, a gland in the chest behind the breastbone (T for thymus).
[bio22-s5.0.6] During maturation, lymphocytes that would attack the body's own cells are removed, so mature lymphocytes respond only to non-self antigens (molecules on a pathogen's surface that the immune system recognises as foreign).
[bio22-s5.1.b] Monocytes are the white blood cells that travel in the blood and then settle in tissues as macrophages. Macrophages are the body's general-purpose cleaners and also the link between the fast, general response and the specific one.
[bio22-s5.1.0] Monocytes are the largest white blood cells, with a large kidney- or bean-shaped nucleus, which makes them easy to tell from lymphocytes under the microscope.
[bio22-s5.1.1] They circulate in the blood for a few days, then move into tissues and develop into macrophages, which can live for months.
[bio22-s5.1.2] Macrophages carry out phagocytosis (engulfing and digesting) of pathogens, dead cells and debris. There is more on the stages in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.1.3] Macrophages are antigen-presenting cells: they display fragments of engulfed pathogens on their surface to activate helper T cells, so they link innate immunity (the fast, non-specific response) and adaptive immunity (the slower, specific one).
[bio22-s5.1.4] Macrophages release chemicals that cause inflammation and fever and attract other white blood cells to the site of infection.
[bio22-s5.1.5] Diagram: a monocyte (large cell, kidney-shaped nucleus) beside a lymphocyte (small cell, large round nucleus, thin rim of cytoplasm). Label nucleus, cytoplasm and cell membrane.
[bio22-s5.2.b] Natural killer (NK) cells are a fast first line of attack against body cells that have gone wrong, either because a virus is hiding inside them or because they have become cancerous. They check cells for a missing marker rather than for a particular pathogen.
[bio22-s5.2.0] Natural killer (NK) cells are lymphocytes of the innate system. They are not specific to one antigen and need no earlier exposure, so they act within hours of an infection, before B and T cells have responded.
[bio22-s5.2.1] Healthy body cells display normal self-marker (MHC) proteins on their surface, which tell the immune system the cell belongs to the body.
[bio22-s5.2.2] Many virus-infected cells and cancer cells display fewer of these markers, or display stress molecules instead. NK cells kill cells showing this missing self pattern.
[bio22-s5.2.3] NK cells also kill cells coated with antibodies, the proteins that bind to antigens.
[bio22-s5.2.4] NK cells release perforin, a protein that makes pores in the target cell's membrane, and enzymes called granzymes that enter through the pores and trigger the cell to self-destruct (apoptosis, programmed cell death).
[bio22-s5.2.5] The dying cell breaks into fragments that macrophages engulf, so the virus inside is destroyed without spreading.
[bio22-s5.2.6] NK cells also release chemicals that activate macrophages, which helps clear the fragments.
[bio22-s5.3.b] B lymphocytes are the cells behind antibodies. Each B cell is committed to one antibody shape, so the body needs a huge range of B cells to cover many different antigens.
[bio22-s5.3.0] B lymphocytes carry antibody molecules in their membrane as receptors, which let them bind a matching antigen. Each B cell and its descendants make only one antibody shape.
[bio22-s5.3.1] B lymphocytes are responsible for antibody-mediated (humoral) immunity, meaning protection by antibodies carried in body fluids. Clonal selection, plasma cells and memory B cells are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.3.2] Plasma cells, the antibody-secreting form of B cells, are larger than resting B cells, with extensive rough endoplasmic reticulum (a network of membranes studded with ribosomes) to make and secrete large amounts of antibody protein.
[bio22-s5.3.3] Antibodies act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, in blood, tissue fluid and mucus, not on pathogens hidden inside cells, because antibodies cannot get into body cells.
[bio22-s5.4.b] T lymphocytes are the cells that control and carry out cell-mediated immunity, the response that deals with infected body cells. Helper T cells are the coordinators that most of the adaptive response depends on.
[bio22-s5.4.0] All T cells have T-cell receptors on their surface that recognise one antigen, but only when it is displayed on a cell's surface (presented), never as a free molecule.
[bio22-s5.4.1] T cells don't make antibodies. That is the job of plasma cells, which come from B cells.
[bio22-s5.4.2] Helper T cells carry the surface marker CD4.
[bio22-s5.4.3] Helper T cells are the coordinators of the adaptive response, signalling with cytokines (chemical messengers between immune cells). Which cells they activate, and in what order, is taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.4.4] Almost every adaptive response depends on helper T cells, so HIV, which infects and destroys CD4 helper T cells, leaves the body unable to fight many infections.
[bio22-s5.5.b] Killer T cells deal with threats that antibodies cannot reach, because the pathogen is hidden inside a body cell. They destroy the whole infected cell.
[bio22-s5.5.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) carry the surface marker CD8.
[bio22-s5.5.1] Killer T cells bind to body cells displaying their matching antigen, such as virus-infected cells, cancer cells and cells of transplanted tissue.
[bio22-s5.5.2] They kill these cells by releasing perforin and granzymes, which triggers apoptosis (the cell's programmed self-destruction).
[bio22-s5.5.3] Killer T cells use the same killing method as NK cells, but only against cells showing their one specific antigen, whereas NK cells look for missing self-markers.
[bio22-s5.5.4] Each killer T cell can kill many target cells in turn.
[bio22-s5.6.b] An immune response needs brakes, and it needs a record of past infections. Suppressor T cells provide the brakes, and memory T cells provide the record that makes a second infection easier to fight.
[bio22-s5.6.0] Suppressor T cells, now usually called regulatory T cells, are the brakes of the immune system.
[bio22-s5.6.1] Suppressor T cells end a response once an infection is cleared, so the body is not damaged by an immune response that carries on. The details are taught in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s5.6.2] They also suppress lymphocytes that react to the body's own antigens, helping to prevent autoimmune disease (the body attacking its own tissues) and allergy.
[bio22-s5.6.3] Memory T cells form from the clone of activated T cells (the identical cells that divide from one activated T cell) and remain in the blood and lymph tissue for years or decades.
[bio22-s5.6.4] On re-exposure to the same antigen, memory T cells quickly produce new helper and killer T cells, giving a faster, stronger cell-mediated response. Memory B cells do the same for antibodies.
[bio22-s5.6.5] Memory cells are why vaccination works: the vaccine exposes you to the antigen safely, so memory cells are ready if the real pathogen arrives.
[bio22-s5.7.b] The outcome asks you to compare the roles of different white blood cells. The first comparison is between the fast, general innate cells and the slower, specific adaptive cells, and then between the ways each one destroys pathogens.
[bio22-s5.7.0] Innate versus adaptive: monocytes, macrophages and NK cells are part of innate immunity, which is non-specific, acts quickly and has no memory.
[bio22-s5.7.1] B and T lymphocytes are adaptive: they are specific to one antigen, slower on first exposure, and they form memory cells.
[bio22-s5.7.2] Macrophages engulf and digest pathogens by phagocytosis. NK and killer T cells kill infected body cells by perforin and apoptosis.
[bio22-s5.7.3] B cells, as plasma cells, secrete antibodies. Helper T cells kill nothing themselves but signal to other cells.
[bio22-s5.7.4] Suppressor T cells regulate the response. Memory cells kill nothing but enable a rapid future response.
[bio22-s5.8.b] Cells also differ in what they attack and in how they recognise it. This explains why different cells are needed to cover both free pathogens and pathogens hidden inside body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.0] Where the target is: antibodies from B cells act on pathogens and toxins outside cells, because antibodies cannot enter body cells.
[bio22-s5.8.1] Killer T and NK cells act on pathogens inside body cells by killing the cell. Macrophages engulf free pathogens.
[bio22-s5.8.2] Recognition: B cells recognise free antigens with membrane antibodies, while T cells recognise only antigens presented on cell surfaces.
[bio22-s5.8.3] NK cells recognise missing or abnormal self-markers, not a specific antigen.
[bio22-s5.8.4] Macrophages recognise general features of pathogens and antibody-coated particles, so they need no specific receptor for each pathogen.
[bio22-s5.9.b] This list puts each cell side by side: whether it is innate or adaptive, whether it is specific, its main role, and where it matures. Use it to compare cells quickly.
[bio22-s5.9.0] Monocyte or macrophage: innate; non-specific; main role is phagocytosis and antigen presentation; matures in the bone marrow, then in tissues.
[bio22-s5.9.1] NK cell: innate; non-specific; kills infected and cancer cells; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.2] B lymphocyte: adaptive; specific; main role is antibodies (via plasma cells) and memory; matures in the bone marrow.
[bio22-s5.9.3] Helper T cell: adaptive; specific; activates B cells, killer T cells and macrophages (CD4); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.4] Killer T cell: adaptive; specific; kills infected, cancer and transplant cells (CD8); matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.5] Suppressor T cell: adaptive; ends responses and prevents attack on self; matures in the thymus.
[bio22-s5.9.6] Memory B and T cells: adaptive; specific; long-lived, giving a fast secondary response.
[bio22-s5.10.b] In a real infection the cells act as a team in sequence. Influenza, a virus that infects the cells lining the airways, shows how the innate and adaptive cells combine.
[bio22-s5.10.0] First, infected cells release interferons (chemical signals that warn neighbouring cells of a virus) and NK cells kill some of them within hours. Macrophages engulf viruses and present antigens, and helper T cells are activated.
[bio22-s5.10.1] Next, killer T cells destroy infected cells while plasma cells release antibodies that neutralise free viruses, so both hidden and free viruses are dealt with.
[bio22-s5.10.2] Finally, suppressor T cells end the response and memory cells remain. Because flu antigens change, memory cells may not recognise next year's strain.

CANDIDATES bio22-s6: Emergence and spread of infectious disease
[bio22-s6.0.b] Disease specialists use precise words for how common a disease is and how fast it is changing. You need these terms to describe any outbreak, and they appear throughout the rest of this subsection.
[bio22-s6.0.0] An emerging infectious disease is one that has newly appeared in a population, or one that was known but is rapidly increasing in incidence (the number of new cases) or in geographic range.
[bio22-s6.0.1] A re-emerging disease is one that had declined and is increasing again, e.g. measles where vaccination rates fall, or TB with drug-resistant strains.
[bio22-s6.0.2] Endemic means always present at a fairly steady level in a region, so the number of cases is predictable from year to year.
[bio22-s6.0.3] An epidemic, or outbreak, is a sudden increase in cases above the expected level in a region.
[bio22-s6.0.4] A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across several continents or worldwide, e.g. COVID-19, which the World Health Organization declared a pandemic on 11 March 2020.
[bio22-s6.1.b] Some terms describe where a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) lives between outbreaks and who can pass it on. These explain why some diseases are so hard to get rid of.
[bio22-s6.1.0] A zoonosis is an infectious disease that passes from animals to humans, e.g. avian influenza or rabies.
[bio22-s6.1.1] Most emerging human diseases are zoonoses, often from wild animals, because people have little immunity to pathogens they have never met. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.1.2] A reservoir is a host population in which a pathogen persists and from which it infects others, e.g. badgers for bovine TB in Ireland, or wild birds for avian influenza.
[bio22-s6.1.3] A carrier is an infected individual who shows no symptoms but can pass on the pathogen, so nobody knows to avoid them.
[bio22-s6.2.b] A pathogen can only cause an outbreak if it can get from one host to the next. There are several routes, and each has typical diseases that use it, in both animals and plants.
[bio22-s6.2.0] Airborne droplets and aerosols (tiny particles hanging in the air) from coughs, sneezes and breath spread influenza, TB, measles and COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.2.1] Wind-borne spores spread ash dieback, cereal rusts and potato blight in plants, because spores are light enough to be carried between fields.
[bio22-s6.2.2] Direct contact, touch and skin to skin, spreads ringworm and athlete's foot.
[bio22-s6.2.3] Indirect contact via contaminated objects and surfaces (fomites), hands, tools and clothing spreads disease. In plants, contact with hands and tools spreads tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s6.2.4] Contaminated food and water, the faecal–oral route (pathogens in faeces reach the mouth), spreads cholera, Salmonella food poisoning and amoebic dysentery.
[bio22-s6.2.5] Body fluids spread HIV and hepatitis B through sexual contact, blood (shared needles and unscreened transfusions) and mother to baby.
[bio22-s6.2.6] Vectors are animals that carry pathogens between hosts: mosquitoes (malaria), ticks (Lyme disease), tsetse flies (sleeping sickness), elm bark beetles (Dutch elm disease), aphids (many plant viruses, e.g. barley yellow dwarf virus) and biting midges (bluetongue virus of sheep and cattle).
[bio22-s6.2.7] In plants, rain splash, soil, infected seed, tubers and cuttings also spread disease. Vegetative propagation (growing new plants from parts of old ones) passes viruses and blight to the next crop, and grafting does too.
[bio22-s6.3.b] Diseases spread when infected organisms, or the things they touch, move. Over short distances this happens through everyday contact, and over a whole country it follows the trade and travel routes of animals and people.
[bio22-s6.3.0] Local spread occurs within a household, school, hospital or farm, between neighbouring fields by wind and rain splash, and between neighbouring farms by animals straying, shared equipment, vehicles and people's boots.
[bio22-s6.3.1] Regional spread occurs when farm animals move through marts (livestock markets) and between farms, when plants and animals are traded within a country or the EU, when wild animals move seasonally, and when people commute and holiday.
[bio22-s6.3.2] In the 2001 foot-and-mouth disease epidemic in Britain, infected sheep were moved through markets across the country before the disease was detected, spreading it to many farms.
[bio22-s6.3.3] Control of that epidemic needed a ban on animal movement and the culling of millions of animals, which shows how costly it is once movement has carried a disease widely.
[bio22-s6.4.b] Modern travel and trade mean a pathogen no longer stays where it started. A disease can reach another continent before anyone knows it is there.
[bio22-s6.4.0] Air travel can carry an infected person to any continent within a day, faster than many diseases take to show symptoms, so travellers can carry infection without knowing it.
[bio22-s6.4.1] COVID-19 reached every continent within months of its first identification in Wuhan, China, in December 2019.
[bio22-s6.4.2] International trade moves pathogens and vectors in plants, wood, soil, live animals and goods. Ash dieback spread through Europe partly in imported young ash plants and was found in Ireland in 2012.
[bio22-s6.4.3] The second, more aggressive epidemic of Dutch elm disease reached Britain from the late 1960s in imported elm logs.
[bio22-s6.4.4] The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus), a vector of several viruses, has spread to many countries in used tyres and plants.
[bio22-s6.4.5] Migrating wild birds carry avian influenza between continents, because infected birds fly long distances without any border controls.
[bio22-s6.5.b] Trade in live animals can move a disease into places where the local species have never met it. Amphibians show how serious this can be.
[bio22-s6.5.0] The pet and wildlife trade spread chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) around the world in the trade in amphibians.
[bio22-s6.5.1] Chytrid is linked to the decline of hundreds of amphibian species and the extinction of some.
[bio22-s6.6.b] Emergence and spread depend on several interrelated factors, and the first is persistence. The longer a host carries a pathogen in a way that can pass on, the more chances it has to infect others.
[bio22-s6.6.0] The longer a host is infectious, the more others it can infect.
[bio22-s6.6.1] Persistence includes long infectious periods, carriers without symptoms, latent infections (the pathogen is present but inactive) that reactivate, and reservoir hosts.
[bio22-s6.6.2] HIV may cause no symptoms for years while the person can still pass it on.
[bio22-s6.6.3] Herpes viruses stay latent in nerve cells for life, so the person is never rid of the virus. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.6.4] TB bacteria can stay dormant (inactive) in the lungs for years and become active later.
[bio22-s6.7.b] Persistence shows up in each kind of host, from a single healthy-looking person to wildlife and crops.
[bio22-s6.7.0] Mary Mallon, known as Typhoid Mary, was born in Co. Tyrone in 1869 and worked as a cook in New York. She carried the typhoid bacterium, Salmonella Typhi, without symptoms and infected many of the families she cooked for.
[bio22-s6.7.1] Health authorities isolated her for most of the rest of her life, because as a carrier she could not be identified by illness.
[bio22-s6.7.2] Bovine TB persists in Irish cattle partly because badgers act as a wildlife reservoir that reinfects herds, so clearing the disease from cattle alone is not enough.
[bio22-s6.7.3] Plant viruses persist in perennial plants (which live for years), weeds and tubers, so infected seed potatoes carry viruses into the next year's crop.
[bio22-s6.7.4] Phytophthora infestans, the cause of potato blight, survives the winter in infected tubers left in the ground or in dumps.
[bio22-s6.8.b] A mutation is a random change in a pathogen's genetic material. Pathogens that reproduce quickly produce huge numbers of changed copies, so some will spread better than the original.
[bio22-s6.8.0] Pathogens with short generation times and large populations produce many mutants, because each new copy is another chance for a change.
[bio22-s6.8.1] RNA viruses mutate fastest because their copying enzyme doesn't proofread (check for copying errors). There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.8.2] Antigenic drift: small mutations gradually change a virus's surface antigens (the molecules the immune system recognises), so memory cells and antibodies from earlier infections or vaccines no longer fit well.
[bio22-s6.8.3] Antigenic drift is why influenza returns every winter and why flu vaccines are updated each year.
[bio22-s6.8.4] Mutations likewise produced new SARS-CoV-2 variants, such as Delta and Omicron, that spread faster or partly escaped immunity, causing new waves of COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.9.b] Mutations can also change what a pathogen is able to do, giving it a new host or defeating a crop's defences.
[bio22-s6.9.0] Antigenic shift (influenza): if two different flu viruses infect the same cell, e.g. in a pig, their RNA segments can mix to form a new strain with surface antigens that no human population has met.
[bio22-s6.9.1] Antigenic shift has caused flu pandemics, e.g. the 2009 H1N1 (swine flu) pandemic, because with no existing immunity the new strain spreads easily.
[bio22-s6.9.2] Mutations can let a pathogen infect a new host species (spillover, e.g. a bird virus gaining the ability to infect mammals), become more transmissible, or overcome resistance bred into crops.
[bio22-s6.9.3] The Ug99 strain of wheat stem rust, identified in Uganda in 1999, overcame resistance genes used in most of the world's wheat varieties.
[bio22-s6.10.b] Resistance means a pathogen or pest survives a drug or chemical that used to kill it. It arises by natural selection (survival of the individuals best suited to their conditions), which happens in four steps. There is more on this in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
[bio22-s6.10.0] 1. Random mutations, or genes passed between bacteria on plasmids (small rings of DNA), make a few individuals resistant.
[bio22-s6.10.1] 2. The antibiotic or chemical kills the susceptible individuals.
[bio22-s6.10.2] 3. The resistant survivors reproduce with less competition.
[bio22-s6.10.3] 4. The next generation is mostly resistant. The chemical doesn't cause the mutation; it selects it.
[bio22-s6.11.b] Resistance arises by natural selection when a drug or chemical kills susceptible individuals, and some practices make it spread faster. The examples cover people, crops and livestock.
[bio22-s6.11.0] Overuse and misuse of antibiotics speed up resistance: use for viral infections (which antibiotics cannot treat), not finishing courses, and use in farm animals.
[bio22-s6.11.1] Poor infection control in hospitals and heavy repeated use of one pesticide also speed up resistance.
[bio22-s6.11.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many antibiotics and spreads in hospitals.
[bio22-s6.11.3] Multidrug-resistant TB, malaria parasites resistant to some antimalarial drugs, and mosquitoes resistant to insecticides, which weakens malaria control.
[bio22-s6.11.4] Fungi that cause crop diseases are resistant to some fungicides, and parasitic worms of sheep and cattle are resistant to some anthelmintics (drugs that kill worms).
[bio22-s6.11.5] Resistant pathogens persist longer in hosts and spread further, linking factors 1 and 3.
[bio22-s6.12.b] A pathogen needs susceptible hosts, meaning individuals with no immunity to it. How many of these there are in a population strongly affects how far and how fast an outbreak goes.
[bio22-s6.12.0] A pathogen spreads only if it can reach susceptible (non-immune) hosts. The higher the proportion of immune individuals, the fewer susceptible hosts there are and the slower the spread. This is herd immunity. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s6.12.1] A pathogen new to a population meets no immunity at all, so it spreads fast and can be very severe, e.g. COVID-19 in 2020, before vaccines.
[bio22-s6.12.2] Smallpox and measles, brought by Europeans to the Americas from the late 1400s, killed very large numbers of Indigenous people, who had never been exposed to them.
[bio22-s6.12.3] Immunity falls when vaccination rates drop, as when measles outbreaks return.
[bio22-s6.12.4] Immunity also falls when protection from vaccination or infection wanes over time, and where many people have weakened immune systems, e.g. from untreated HIV or malnutrition, or the very young and very old.
[bio22-s6.13.b] Plants have no antibody-based immunity like animals, but the same principle applies. What matters is whether any individuals in the crop can resist the pathogen.
[bio22-s6.13.0] In plants, the equivalent is genetic uniformity. A monoculture (a crop of one genetically identical variety) has no resistant individuals, so a pathogen that infects one plant can infect them all.
[bio22-s6.13.1] In Ireland in the 1840s, most of the poor depended on one potato variety, the Lumper, grown from tubers (genetically identical clones) and susceptible to blight.
[bio22-s6.13.2] When Phytophthora infestans arrived in 1845, crops failed across the country in successive years. About one million people died and over a million emigrated in the Great Famine.
[bio22-s6.14.b] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more other hosts they meet, so the further a pathogen travels. Mobility applies to people, livestock, wildlife and vectors.
[bio22-s6.14.0] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more contacts each has and the further the pathogen spreads. The routes are those in local, regional and global spread above.
[bio22-s6.14.1] Crowding in cities, refugee camps, festivals and mass gatherings, travel and migration, and the movement of livestock all increase spread.
[bio22-s6.14.2] Migration of wild animals, such as birds, also moves pathogens into new areas.
[bio22-s6.14.3] Vector mobility: climate warming lets vectors survive further north and for longer each year, carrying disease into new regions.
[bio22-s6.14.4] Bluetongue, a midge-borne virus of sheep and cattle, spread into northern Europe from 2006.
[bio22-s6.14.5] Reducing mobility reduces spread: animal movement bans in foot-and-mouth outbreaks, travel restrictions and quarantine in human epidemics, and import controls on plants. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.15.b] To compare outbreaks and predict whether one will grow, scientists use a single number, R₀. It measures how contagious a pathogen is when nobody has immunity.
[bio22-s6.15.0] The basic reproduction number, R₀ ('R nought'), is the average number of new (secondary) infections caused by one infected individual in a population where everyone is susceptible, with no immunity and no control measures.
[bio22-s6.15.1] R₀ greater than 1: each case causes more than one new case on average, so numbers grow and an epidemic can occur.
[bio22-s6.15.2] R₀ equal to 1: numbers stay steady, which is endemic.
[bio22-s6.15.3] R₀ less than 1: each case causes fewer than one new case, so the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.16.b] Each infected person passes the pathogen to R₀ others, so cases multiply by R₀ every generation of infection (one round of people infecting the next). Working through numbers shows why R₀ above or below 1 matters.
[bio22-s6.16.0] Growth example: with R₀ = 2, one case leads to 2, then 4, then 8, then 16 new cases per generation of infection. After 10 generations, one case has led to 2¹⁰ = 1,024 new cases in that generation.
[bio22-s6.16.1] Decline example: with R₀ = 0.5, 100 cases give 50, then 25, then about 12, and the outbreak fades.
[bio22-s6.16.2] Diagram: a branching tree from one case, giving 2, then 4, then 8 cases (R₀ = 2), beside a single chain with one case per generation (R₀ = 1). Label each generation.
[bio22-s6.17.b] R₀ is not a fixed property of a pathogen. It combines features of the pathogen, the host and the environment, which are the factors already covered.
[bio22-s6.17.0] R₀ depends on how long a host is infectious (persistence), so a longer infectious period raises R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.1] R₀ depends on how many contacts an infected host has per day (mobility and crowding), so more contacts raise R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.2] R₀ depends on the chance that a contact passes on the infection (the transmission route, and mutations that make a pathogen more transmissible).
[bio22-s6.17.3] So R₀ is not fixed for a disease: it differs between populations and settings.
[bio22-s6.18.b] In real outbreaks some people are already immune or control measures are in place. The effective reproduction number measures spread under those real conditions.
[bio22-s6.18.0] The effective reproduction number, R (sometimes written Rt), is the average number of new infections per case at a given time, in a population where some people are immune or control measures are in place.
[bio22-s6.18.1] Approximately, R = R₀ × the proportion still susceptible. For example, R₀ = 3 with half the population immune gives R = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5.
[bio22-s6.18.2] Control aims to bring R below 1, so that each case infects fewer than one other person and the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.19.b] If R₀ tells us how contagious a disease is, we can work out how many people must be immune to stop it spreading. The more contagious the disease, the higher that proportion.
[bio22-s6.19.0] Herd-immunity threshold: to stop sustained spread, the proportion immune must be at least 1 − 1/R₀.
[bio22-s6.19.1] Examples: R₀ = 2 needs 1 − ½ = 50% immune; R₀ = 4 needs 1 − ¼ = 75% immune; R₀ = 10 needs 90% immune.
[bio22-s6.19.2] Measles is one of the most contagious diseases, with R₀ usually quoted as 12–18, so about 95% of people must be immune.
[bio22-s6.19.3] The World Health Organization therefore recommends at least 95% coverage with two doses of measles vaccine.
[bio22-s6.20.b] R₀ comes from a simple model, and a model is only as good as its assumptions. Real outbreaks differ in important ways.
[bio22-s6.20.0] The model's assumptions are that everyone is equally likely to meet everyone else and that R₀ stays constant. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s6.20.1] Real populations are clustered, behaviour changes during outbreaks and new variants change R₀, so estimates are updated as data come in.
[bio22-s6.21.b] The six factors do not work one at a time. Real outbreaks happen when several combine, and other changes in how we live bring pathogens and new hosts into contact.
[bio22-s6.21.0] The factors act together, not one at a time. A mutation that makes a virus more transmissible raises R₀, and a population with low immunity and high mobility then lets it spread widely.
[bio22-s6.21.1] Long persistence in carriers lets a pathogen reach new areas undetected, and resistance to drugs lets infections last longer, raising R₀ further.
[bio22-s6.21.2] Plant example: a genetically uniform crop (low immunity), a pathogen spread by wind and trade (mobility) and survival in tubers (persistence) together explain the speed of the potato famine.
[bio22-s6.21.3] Other drivers increase contact between pathogens and new hosts: clearing forests and expanding farms into wild areas (more contact with wild animals), intensive farming of large numbers of genetically similar animals kept close together, climate change (vectors' ranges), poverty, poor sanitation, and conflict, which weakens health systems.

CANDIDATES bio25-s2: Osmosis in food preservation and plant health
[bio25-s2.0.b] Food goes off because microorganisms feed on it, and microorganisms need water to live. Adding a lot of salt or sugar turns osmosis against them: it pulls water out of their cells.
[bio25-s2.0.0] Food spoils mainly because bacteria and fungi (moulds and yeasts) grow on it, digesting it and making it unsafe or unpleasant to eat. Like all cells, they need water for their enzymes to work and to grow and reproduce.
[bio25-s2.0.1] Mechanism, step 1: a high concentration of salt or sugar is added to the food.
[bio25-s2.0.2] 2. The food's water is now a concentrated (hypertonic) solution compared with the cytoplasm (the watery contents) of any microorganism on it.
[bio25-s2.0.3] 3. Water leaves the microbial cells by osmosis, moving from the more dilute cytoplasm to the more concentrated solution outside.
[bio25-s2.0.4] 4. The cells are dehydrated and plasmolysed (the cell membrane shrinks away from the cell wall; bacteria and fungi have cell walls), so their enzymes can't work and they can't grow or reproduce; many die.
[bio25-s2.0.5] The salt or sugar also binds water in the food, so less free water is available to any microbe.
[bio25-s2.0.6] Preserving by drying (e.g. dried fruit and dried fish) works by removing water directly, which has the same effect on microbes. It is related to osmosis but is not osmosis itself.
[bio25-s2.1.b] Three traditional methods preserve food with salt, sugar or acid. Salt and sugar work by osmosis, but acid works in a different way, by its low pH.
[bio25-s2.1.0] Salting: meat and fish are rubbed with dry salt or soaked in strong brine (salt solution), e.g. bacon, ham and salted fish. Salt also draws water out of the food itself by osmosis, drying it further, so even less water is left for microbes.
[bio25-s2.1.1] Sugaring: fruit is preserved in a high concentration of sugar, e.g. jam, marmalade and candied fruit; fruit is also stored in heavy syrup.
[bio25-s2.1.2] Honey keeps for a very long time because its sugar concentration is so high that microbes lose water to it instead of growing.
[bio25-s2.1.3] Pickling: vegetables kept in vinegar are preserved mainly by the low pH of the acid (ethanoic acid), which stops enzymes and microbes working, not by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.1.4] Brine pickles (salt solutions) combine osmosis with acid made by fermentation (microbes breaking down sugars in the vegetable), so both effects protect the food.
[bio25-s2.2.b] Osmotic preservation is useful but not perfect. To evaluate it, weigh what it does well against where it fails and what it does to our health.
[bio25-s2.2.0] Advantages: it is cheap, simple and traditional; it needs no refrigeration, so it was used for long journeys and winter stores; and it works against both bacteria and fungi.
[bio25-s2.2.1] Limits: some microorganisms tolerate high salt or sugar concentrations, especially moulds and yeasts, so jam can still grow mould on its surface once opened and should be refrigerated.
[bio25-s2.2.2] Preservation fails if the salt or sugar concentration is too low, because there is then not enough of a concentration difference to draw water out of microbes. For example, reduced-sugar jams spoil faster.
[bio25-s2.2.3] Osmotic preservation slows microbial growth but doesn't sterilise the food (kill all microbes). It is usually combined with other methods, such as cooking, sealing, refrigeration, smoking and acids.
[bio25-s2.2.4] Health link: salted foods add much salt to the diet, and high salt intake is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.
[bio25-s2.2.5] Sugary preserves add sugar, which is linked to tooth decay and obesity. So salting and sugaring are used less today, and refrigeration and freezing more. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio25-s2.2.6] Osmosis also affects food preparation: salting sliced vegetables, e.g. cucumber and aubergine, draws water out of them.
[bio25-s2.2.7] Limp vegetables, e.g. celery and lettuce, become crisp again when placed in cold water, because their cells take in water by osmosis and become turgid (swollen and firm with water).
[bio25-s2.3.b] Plant cells stay firm because they are filled with water taken in by osmosis, and this water pressure does several jobs for the plant.
[bio25-s2.3.0] Healthy plants keep their cells turgid (full of water and pushing outwards) by taking in water by osmosis into the vacuole (the large sap-filled space) of each cell.
[bio25-s2.3.1] The turgor pressure of cells pressing against their walls supports non-woody plants, leaves and flowers, holding them upright and spreading leaves to the light for photosynthesis.
[bio25-s2.3.2] Turgor pressure drives cell enlargement: young cells expand as water enters their vacuoles, so plant growth depends on water uptake by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.3.3] Guard cells (the pair of cells around each stoma, the tiny pore in a leaf) open the stomata when they become turgid and close them when they become flaccid (soft and limp from losing water), controlling gas exchange and water loss. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.3.4] Wilting: when water is lost from the leaves by transpiration (evaporation from the leaf) faster than the roots take it up, the cells lose water, become flaccid, and the plant wilts.
[bio25-s2.3.5] Wilting closes the stomata, so less carbon dioxide gets in and it reduces photosynthesis and growth. It is reversed if water is supplied soon enough; prolonged wilting kills the plant.
[bio25-s2.4.b] Roots rely on the soil water being more dilute than their own cells. If that balance flips, osmosis runs the wrong way and the plant loses water even though the soil is wet.
[bio25-s2.4.0] Roots take in water by osmosis because the soil water is normally more dilute than the cell sap (the solution in the vacuole) of the root cells. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.4.1] If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the contents of the root cells, water stops entering the roots and can leave them by osmosis. The root cells become plasmolysed, the plant cannot take up enough water, and it wilts and may die, even in moist soil.
[bio25-s2.4.2] Causes of salty soil water: flooding of coastal land by seawater (e.g. storm surges), and salt spread on roads in icy weather, which damages roadside trees and hedges.
[bio25-s2.4.3] Irrigation (watering crops artificially) in hot, dry regions is another cause: water evaporates and leaves salts behind in the soil (salinisation), lowering crop yields.
[bio25-s2.4.4] Fertiliser burn: applying too much fertiliser, especially concentrated fertiliser close to roots or on dry soil, makes the soil solution too concentrated, so water leaves the roots and the leaves turn brown and scorched.
[bio25-s2.4.5] Advice to avoid fertiliser burn: apply the recommended amount, spread it evenly and water it in. Salt was used in the past as a weedkiller for the same reason.
[bio25-s2.4.6] Some plants of salty habitats, such as salt marshes and seashores, survive because their root cells hold a higher solute concentration than the salty soil water, so water still enters by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.4.7] Sustainability link: soil salinisation from irrigation, and seawater flooding linked to rising sea levels and stronger storms, reduce the land available for crops. Careful irrigation and drainage reduce salinisation.

CANDIDATES bio27-s2: Mechanical breakdown
[bio27-s2.0.b] Before food can be absorbed it has to be broken down in two ways. Mechanical breakdown makes the pieces smaller; chemical breakdown then splits the molecules themselves. This subsection is about the first kind.
[bio27-s2.0.0] Mechanical (physical) digestion breaks food into smaller pieces, or fat into smaller droplets, without changing the molecules: no chemical bonds are broken and no new substances are formed. Chemical digestion, done by enzymes (biological catalysts that speed up reactions), does break bonds. There is more on this in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.0.1] Smaller pieces have a much larger total surface area, so enzymes can act on more of the food at once and digestion is faster. Think of how sugar dissolves faster as powder than as a lump.
[bio27-s2.0.2] Mechanical breakdown also makes food soft and moist enough to swallow, and mixes it with digestive juices (the watery secretions that contain enzymes), so the enzymes reach all of the food.
[bio27-s2.0.3] The four mechanisms are chewing by the teeth (mouth), peristalsis (the whole gut), churning (stomach), and emulsification of fats by bile salts (duodenum, the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s2.1.b] Digestion starts in the mouth, where the teeth cut and crush food into small pieces. Each type of tooth has a shape that suits its job.
[bio27-s2.1.0] Chewing (mastication) breaks food into small pieces and mixes it with saliva. The tongue rolls the moistened food into a bolus (a soft ball of food) for swallowing, and mucus in saliva lubricates the bolus so it slides down easily.
[bio27-s2.1.1] Incisors are at the front of the mouth. They are chisel-shaped with sharp, flat edges, which suits them for biting and cutting food.
[bio27-s2.1.2] Canines, one on each side of the incisors in each jaw, are pointed, so they grip and tear food. Human canines are small compared with those of carnivores (meat-eating animals).
[bio27-s2.1.3] Premolars and molars at the back are broad with ridged surfaces (cusps), for crushing and grinding food. Premolars have two cusps; molars are larger with more cusps.
[bio27-s2.1.4] The jaw moves sideways as well as up and down, so the broad surfaces of the molars can grind food rather than only crush it.
[bio27-s2.1.5] Diagram: (a) the four types of teeth side by side, labelled incisor, canine, premolar and molar, with their roles; (b) a vertical section of a molar labelled crown, root, enamel, dentine, pulp cavity, nerves and blood vessels, gum, cement and jawbone.
[bio27-s2.2.b] The teeth in your mouth can be summarised in a short code called the dental formula. Knowing how a tooth is built also shows why it is hard-wearing yet sensitive.
[bio27-s2.2.0] The dental formula gives the number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper jaw over one half of the lower jaw, in the order incisors, canines, premolars, molars.
[bio27-s2.2.1] The adult human dental formula is 2.1.2.3 over 2.1.2.3, so there are 16 teeth on each side of the mouth (8 in each quarter) and 32 teeth in total.
[bio27-s2.2.2] Child's milk (deciduous) teeth have the formula 2.1.0.2 over 2.1.0.2, giving 20 teeth in total. They have no premolars and only two molars in each quarter, so the adult set has 12 more teeth.
[bio27-s2.2.3] The third molars are the wisdom teeth. They appear last, often in the late teens or later, or not at all.
[bio27-s2.2.4] A tooth has a crown above the gum and a root fixed in the jawbone. Enamel, the hardest substance in the body, covers the crown, so it can withstand biting and chewing.
[bio27-s2.2.5] Dentine, a bone-like layer, lies beneath the enamel. The pulp cavity in the centre contains nerves and blood vessels, which is why a damaged tooth hurts. Cement holds the root in its socket.
[bio27-s2.3.b] Enamel is very hard, but acid can still dissolve it. Tooth decay is the result of bacteria turning the sugar in the mouth into acid.
[bio27-s2.3.0] Bacteria in plaque (a sticky film on the teeth) feed on sugars and release acid. The acid dissolves enamel and then dentine, forming a cavity.
[bio27-s2.3.1] Eating and drinking sugary food less often gives the bacteria less to feed on, so less acid is made.
[bio27-s2.3.2] Brushing with fluoride toothpaste removes plaque and hardens enamel. Drinking fluoridated water (water with fluoride added) also hardens enamel, so it resists the acid. There is more on fluoride in Minerals.
[bio27-s2.3.3] Regular dental check-ups let decay be found and treated early, before the cavity reaches the pulp cavity.
[bio27-s2.4.b] Food has to travel the whole length of the gut, even though the gut has no hands or pump. Peristalsis does this with waves of muscle, and it also helps break the food up.
[bio27-s2.4.0] Peristalsis is a wave of muscular contraction that passes along the wall of the gut, pushing food along. It happens from the oesophagus (the food pipe) to the rectum, and also in the ureters (the tubes from the kidneys to the bladder).
[bio27-s2.4.1] The gut wall has an inner layer of circular muscle and an outer layer of longitudinal muscle. Both are smooth, involuntary muscle, which means they work without conscious control.
[bio27-s2.4.2] 1. The circular muscle just behind the bolus contracts, narrowing the gut and squeezing the food forwards.
[bio27-s2.4.3] 2. The circular muscle just in front of the bolus relaxes, widening the gut to receive it. The wave then moves along, carrying the food with it.
[bio27-s2.4.4] Peristalsis is automatic (involuntary) once food is swallowed, and it works against gravity: a person can swallow while lying down or upside down, and the food still reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s2.4.5] Peristalsis also mixes food with digestive juices and breaks it up, so it is part of mechanical breakdown. In the small intestine it keeps moving the contents past the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s2.4.6] Dietary fibre adds bulk that the muscles can grip and push against, so a high-fibre diet keeps peristalsis effective and prevents constipation. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio27-s2.4.7] Diagram: a section of the gut showing a bolus with contracted circular muscle (narrow) behind it and relaxed muscle (wide) in front, with an arrow for the direction of movement; label circular muscle, longitudinal muscle, bolus, contraction and relaxation.
[bio27-s2.5.b] The stomach is a muscular bag that holds food for a few hours. Its walls churn the food, which is the third mechanical process after chewing and peristalsis.
[bio27-s2.5.0] The stomach wall has three layers of smooth muscle: longitudinal, circular and an oblique (diagonal) layer. Having muscle running in three directions means their contractions churn the food, breaking it up and mixing it thoroughly with gastric juice (the stomach's digestive secretion).
[bio27-s2.5.1] After a few hours of churning, food becomes chyme, a thick, creamy liquid. The sphincters (rings of muscle that close an opening) at the entrance and exit stay closed during churning, so food is held in the stomach.
[bio27-s2.5.2] The pyloric sphincter, at the exit of the stomach, relaxes now and then to let small amounts of chyme into the duodenum. This means the small intestine is never overloaded and has time to digest and neutralise each portion.
[bio27-s2.5.3] The stomach also has a chemical role, using acid and pepsin. There is more on this in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.6.b] Fat does not mix with water, so it forms large droplets in the watery gut. Bile salts solve this by breaking fat into tiny droplets, a mechanical step that gives the fat-digesting enzyme far more surface to work on.
[bio27-s2.6.0] Fats and oils do not mix with the watery contents of the gut, so they form large droplets. Lipase (the fat-digesting enzyme, which works in water) can therefore reach only the droplet surfaces.
[bio27-s2.6.1] Bile salts are made by the liver and enter the duodenum in bile. Each bile-salt molecule has one end that attracts water (hydrophilic) and one end that attracts fat (hydrophobic).
[bio27-s2.6.2] The bile salts gather on the surface of fat droplets with the fat-loving end inwards. With the churning of the gut they break the large droplets into many tiny droplets and stop them joining together again.
[bio27-s2.6.3] This is emulsification, and the mixture of tiny droplets in water is an emulsion.
[bio27-s2.6.4] Emulsification is mechanical: the fat molecules are unchanged and no bonds are broken. It greatly increases the surface area of fat exposed to lipase, so fat is digested much faster.
[bio27-s2.6.5] Bile contains no enzymes. Its other roles, neutralising stomach acid and excreting bile pigments (waste products), are in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.6.6] Washing-up liquid emulsifies grease in the same way, which is why bile salts are often compared to a detergent.
[bio27-s2.6.7] Diagram: one large fat droplet, then, after bile salts, many small droplets each coated with bile-salt molecules, labelled large surface area for lipase.

CANDIDATES bio27-s3: Chemical breakdown
[bio27-s3.0.b] Food is made of large molecules that are too big to pass into the blood. Chemical digestion cuts them into small units using enzymes (biological catalysts made by the body).
[bio27-s3.0.0] Chemical digestion is the hydrolysis of large food molecules into their small, soluble units by digestive enzymes: each bond is broken by adding a molecule of water, which is what hydrolysis means.
[bio27-s3.0.1] Carbohydrates become simple sugars, proteins become amino acids, and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. These small units are soluble and small enough to be absorbed.
[bio27-s3.0.2] Each digestive enzyme is specific to one type of substrate (the molecule it acts on) and works best at its optimum pH, the pH of the part of the gut where it acts.
[bio27-s3.0.3] The gut changes pH along its length: neutral in the mouth, acid in the stomach and alkaline in the small intestine. This suits the different enzymes working in each place.
[bio27-s3.0.4] Most digestive enzymes are released from glands into the gut cavity, so digestion there is extracellular, meaning it happens outside cells.
[bio27-s3.1.b] Digestion begins in the mouth, where food is mixed with saliva. Saliva moistens the food and also starts the digestion of starch.
[bio27-s3.1.0] Saliva is produced by the three pairs of salivary glands and released into the mouth.
[bio27-s3.1.1] The pH in the mouth is about 7 (neutral to slightly alkaline), which is the optimum for salivary amylase, the enzyme in saliva.
[bio27-s3.1.2] Salivary amylase digests starch to maltose (a sugar made of two glucose units). The product of an amylase is maltose.
[bio27-s3.1.3] Food is in the mouth only briefly, so only a little starch is digested there. Stomach acid denatures (destroys the shape of) the amylase soon after it reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s3.1.4] Saliva also contains water, which dissolves food so that it can be tasted and moistens it, and mucus, which lubricates swallowing.
[bio27-s3.1.5] Saliva also contains lysozyme, an enzyme that kills some bacteria.
[bio27-s3.2.b] In the stomach, food meets a strongly acidic fluid called gastric juice. It begins protein digestion and also protects the body from bacteria that arrive in food.
[bio27-s3.2.0] Gastric juice is produced by gastric glands in the stomach lining and released into the stomach. It contains hydrochloric acid, pepsin (released as inactive pepsinogen) and mucus.
[bio27-s3.2.1] The pH in the stomach is about 2 (strongly acidic), which is the optimum for pepsin.
[bio27-s3.2.2] Hydrochloric acid kills most bacteria in food, which is a defence against infection. It also provides the acid pH that pepsin needs.
[bio27-s3.2.3] Hydrochloric acid converts inactive pepsinogen to active pepsin, and it stops salivary amylase, which cannot work at this pH.
[bio27-s3.2.4] Pepsin is a protease (a protein-digesting enzyme): it hydrolyses peptide bonds inside protein chains, breaking proteins into shorter chains called polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.2.5] The product of a protease is polypeptides. Complete protein digestion needs further enzymes in the small intestine to give amino acids.
[bio27-s3.2.6] Pepsin is made in the inactive form pepsinogen so that it does not digest the gastric gland cells that make it. It becomes active only in the stomach cavity.
[bio27-s3.2.7] A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus barrier fails, the lining is damaged, causing a stomach ulcer.
[bio27-s3.3.b] Fat digestion needs help before enzymes can act, and the acid from the stomach has to be dealt with. Bile does both jobs when food enters the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s3.3.0] Bile is produced by the liver, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, and released through the bile duct into the duodenum when chyme (the partly digested, acidic food mixture leaving the stomach), especially fatty chyme, arrives.
[bio27-s3.3.1] Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments and sodium hydrogencarbonate. It contains no enzymes.
[bio27-s3.3.2] Bile salts emulsify fats, which means they break large fat droplets into tiny droplets. This is a physical change, not a chemical one. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s3.3.3] Sodium hydrogencarbonate is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach. This raises the pH to suit the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes and stops the action of pepsin.
[bio27-s3.3.4] Bile pigments are excretory wastes from the breakdown of haemoglobin from old red blood cells in the liver. They give faeces their brown colour.
[bio27-s3.4.b] The pancreas adds a second fluid to the duodenum, one that carries most of the enzymes needed to digest starch, protein and fat. Because the duodenum is alkaline, these enzymes work best there.
[bio27-s3.4.0] Pancreatic juice is produced by the pancreas and released through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. It contains sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralises acid, and three enzymes: pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.4.1] Pancreatic amylase digests the remaining starch (the starch not digested in the mouth) to maltose.
[bio27-s3.4.2] Trypsin is a protease, released in an inactive form and activated in the duodenum. It digests proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptides.
[bio27-s3.4.3] Lipase digests fats and oils (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. The product of a lipase is fatty acids and glycerol, not fatty acids alone.
[bio27-s3.4.4] Lipase acts on the tiny droplets made by emulsification, because they give it a much larger surface to work on.
[bio27-s3.4.5] The pH in the duodenum and small intestine is about 8 (slightly alkaline), because of the sodium hydrogencarbonate in bile and pancreatic juice. This suits pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.5.b] The last stage of digestion is done by enzymes made by the cells lining the small intestine. They finish breaking down the sugars and peptides that earlier enzymes produced.
[bio27-s3.5.0] The cells lining the villi (the tiny finger-like projections of the small intestine) make enzymes that complete digestion.
[bio27-s3.5.1] Many of these enzymes are held in the membranes of the lining cells, so the last step of digestion happens right at the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s3.5.2] Maltase digests maltose to two glucose molecules. Sucrase digests sucrose to glucose and fructose. Lactase digests lactose to glucose and galactose.
[bio27-s3.5.3] Peptidases digest peptides to amino acids.
[bio27-s3.5.4] These enzymes work at a pH of about 8, the same alkaline conditions as the duodenum.
[bio27-s3.5.5] Lactose intolerance: people who stop making lactase after childhood cannot digest lactose, so bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, causing bloating, wind and diarrhoea. There is more on this in Variation and Diet.
[bio27-s3.6.b] This table pulls together where each enzyme is made, where it acts, its pH and its products. Each line has the same pattern so you can compare them quickly.
[bio27-s3.6.0] Salivary amylase – made in salivary glands – acts in mouth – pH about 7 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.1] Pepsin (protease) – made in gastric glands of stomach wall – acts in stomach – pH about 2 – protein → polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.6.2] Pancreatic amylase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.3] Trypsin (protease) – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – proteins and polypeptides → peptides.
[bio27-s3.6.4] Lipase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.6.5] Maltase – made in the wall of the small intestine – acts there – pH about 8 – maltose → glucose.
[bio27-s3.6.6] Peptidases – made in the wall of the small intestine – act there – pH about 8 – peptides → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.6.7] Bile salts (not an enzyme) – made in liver, stored in gallbladder – act in duodenum – emulsify fats.
[bio27-s3.7.b] Putting the enzymes in order shows how each nutrient is broken down step by step along the gut. You can then use these flow models to predict what goes wrong when part of the system fails.
[bio27-s3.7.0] Carbohydrate model: starch is digested by salivary amylase in the mouth (pH 7) to maltose. The remaining starch is digested by pancreatic amylase in the duodenum (pH 8) to maltose. Maltose is digested by maltase in the small intestine wall to glucose.
[bio27-s3.7.1] Sucrose and lactose are digested by sucrase and lactase in the small intestine wall. Cellulose is not digested, because humans make no enzyme for it, so it passes on as fibre.
[bio27-s3.7.2] Protein model: protein → (pepsin, stomach, pH 2) → polypeptides → (trypsin, duodenum, pH 8) → shorter peptides → (peptidases, small intestine wall) → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.7.3] Fat model: large fat droplets → (bile salts, duodenum: emulsification, a physical change) → tiny droplets → (lipase from the pancreas, duodenum, pH 8) → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.7.4] Fat is digested only in the small intestine, after emulsification.
[bio27-s3.7.5] Using the models to predict: if the bile duct is blocked, for example by gallstones, fats are poorly emulsified and digested, and undigested fat appears in the faeces.
[bio27-s3.7.6] If the pancreas releases too little juice, as in cystic fibrosis, digestion of all three nutrients is reduced, which is why patients take enzyme capsules. There is more on this in Industrial enzymes.
[bio27-s3.7.7] Diagram: a flow chart with three rows (carbohydrate, protein, fat), each running left to right through mouth, stomach, duodenum and small intestine wall, showing enzyme or bile salts, pH and product at each step; or an alimentary canal (whole gut) outline with enzymes and pH at each site.
[bio27-s3.8.b] Visking tubing is a simple physical model of the small intestine. It shows why food must be digested into small molecules before it can pass into the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.0] Visking (dialysis) tubing is a selectively permeable membrane: it lets small molecules (water, glucose and maltose) through but not large ones (starch). It models the wall of the small intestine, and the water around it models the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.1] Method, step 1: tie one end of two lengths of Visking tubing, after soaking them in water.
[bio27-s3.8.2] Method, step 2: fill tube A with starch solution plus amylase solution. Fill tube B, the control (the comparison with no enzyme), with the same volume of starch solution plus water (or boiled amylase).
[bio27-s3.8.3] Method, step 3: tie the open ends, rinse the outsides, and place each tube in a separate boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C.
[bio27-s3.8.4] Method, step 4: at the start, and again after about 30 minutes, test samples of the water outside each tube with iodine solution (for starch) and with Benedict's solution in a hot water bath (for reducing sugar), using the food tests.
[bio27-s3.8.5] Expected results: the water outside both tubes stays yellow-brown with iodine, so no starch passes out.
[bio27-s3.8.6] After 30 minutes the water outside tube A turns brick red with Benedict's solution, showing reducing sugar, while the water outside tube B stays blue. Inside tube A, a test would show less starch and much reducing sugar.
[bio27-s3.8.7] Conclusion: starch molecules are too large to pass through the membrane. Amylase digests starch to maltose, which is small enough to pass through. This models why food must be digested before it can be absorbed into the blood. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio27-s3.8.8] Safety: wear safety glasses; Benedict's solution is an irritant; take care with hot water; heat the tubes in a water bath, never directly.
[bio27-s3.9.b] A model is useful only if you know where it differs from the real gut. The tubing copies the barrier but little else.
[bio27-s3.9.0] The tubing has no villi or microvilli, so its surface area is far smaller than the gut's.
[bio27-s3.9.1] No blood flows past to carry products away and keep the concentration gradient (the difference in concentration that drives diffusion).
[bio27-s3.9.2] The real gut absorbs some products by active transport (using energy to move molecules), which tubing cannot do.
[bio27-s3.9.3] Only one enzyme and one nutrient are modelled, at a constant pH, whereas the gut uses a sequence of enzymes and pH values.
[bio27-s3.9.4] The tubing does not move food by peristalsis (the waves of muscle contraction that push food along the gut).
[bio27-s3.9.5] Diagram: a boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C, with a knotted length of Visking tubing containing starch and amylase hanging in it; label Visking tubing, starch and amylase, distilled water, water bath at 37 °C; show the control tube beside it.

CANDIDATES bio32-s4: Biotic factors and relationships
[bio32-s4.0.b] Biotic factors are the effects that living organisms have on each other. In a grassland they include food supply, predation, grazing, competition, parasites and disease, pollination and decomposition, and human activities such as grazing livestock, mowing and fertilising. Grazing and mowing show how one biotic factor shapes which plants can live in a habitat.
[bio32-s4.0.0] Grazing by rabbits, cattle or sheep and mowing keep grass short. This lets low rosette plants (plants whose leaves lie flat close to the ground) such as daisy, dandelion and plantain get light, and stops shrubs and trees from invading.
[bio32-s4.0.1] If grazing or mowing stops, taller grasses shade out the rosette plants because they grow above them and take the light. In time scrub (a tangle of woody shrubs, e.g. brambles, hawthorn) invades.
[bio32-s4.1.b] Other living things work in the grassland soil and on its flowers. Some recycle dead material back into the ground, and others carry pollen between flowers.
[bio32-s4.1.0] Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) and detritivores (animals that eat dead organic matter, e.g. earthworms, woodlice, and slugs in part) break down dead plants, dung and dead animals, returning minerals to the soil so that plants can take them up again.
[bio32-s4.1.1] Decomposition recycles carbon and nitrogen, and there is more on this in The carbon cycle, The nitrogen cycle and Bacteria and fungi.
[bio32-s4.1.2] Earthworms also drag leaves underground, mix and aerate the soil (let air into it), and improve drainage.
[bio32-s4.1.3] Pollinators such as bumblebees, hoverflies and butterflies feed on the nectar and pollen of clover, dandelion and buttercup flowers, and carry pollen between them, which is how these plants are pollinated.
[bio32-s4.2.b] To work out who eats whom in a grassland, you gather primary data, meaning information you collect yourself in the field. You then check it against what other people have already recorded.
[bio32-s4.2.0] Direct observation of animals feeding shows a feeding relationship at the moment it happens.
[bio32-s4.2.1] Feeding signs are evidence left behind: nibbled grass, holes in leaves, and broken snail shells at a thrush’s stone.
[bio32-s4.2.2] Droppings and pellets show what an animal ate, e.g. undigested fur and bones in the pellets of birds of prey.
[bio32-s4.2.3] The habitat where each animal was caught is a clue, e.g. aphids found on the stems they feed on.
[bio32-s4.2.4] Your findings are then checked against secondary sources (information collected by others), such as field guides, for what each species eats.
[bio32-s4.3.b] Every feeding relationship starts with a green plant that makes food. Animals then feed on the plants, and on each other, in layers called trophic levels (feeding levels).
[bio32-s4.3.0] Producers in the grassland are grasses, clover, daisy, dandelion, buttercup and other green plants, which make food by photosynthesis.
[bio32-s4.3.1] Primary consumers (herbivores, which eat plants) in the grassland: rabbits and hares graze grass and clover; slugs and snails eat leaves; grasshoppers and caterpillars chew leaves; aphids suck sap from stems; bumblebees take nectar and pollen.
[bio32-s4.3.2] Secondary and higher consumers (carnivores, which eat animals, and omnivores, which eat both): ladybirds eat aphids; spiders catch flies, grasshoppers and other insects; ground beetles eat slugs and other invertebrates.
[bio32-s4.3.3] Song thrushes eat snails (smashing their shells on a stone) and earthworms; blackbirds eat earthworms and insects; hedgehogs eat slugs, beetles and earthworms.
[bio32-s4.3.4] Badgers eat mainly earthworms; foxes eat rabbits, mice and birds; kestrels eat wood mice, pygmy shrews and large insects.
[bio32-s4.3.5] Pygmy shrews eat small invertebrates such as beetles, spiders and woodlice.
[bio32-s4.4.b] A food chain follows one line of feeding, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder. Because most animals eat more than one thing, the chains join up into a food web.
[bio32-s4.4.0] Worked example of grassland food chains: grass → rabbit → fox; clover leaves → slug → song thrush; plant sap → aphid → ladybird → (insect-eating bird); grass seeds and invertebrates → wood mouse → kestrel.
[bio32-s4.4.1] These chains connect into a food web. For example, the fox eats rabbits, wood mice and birds, so it is on several chains.
[bio32-s4.4.2] The earthworm feeds on dead leaves and is eaten by thrushes, blackbirds, badgers and hedgehogs, so it links the dead material in the soil to several animals above it.
[bio32-s4.4.3] Diagram: a grassland food web of 8–12 of the species listed above, with arrows pointing from the food to the feeder.
[bio32-s4.5.b] A food web shows that organisms depend on each other, so a change in one population spreads through the whole community.
[bio32-s4.5.0] The web shows interdependence: a change in one population affects others.
[bio32-s4.5.1] For example, if disease kills many rabbits, foxes eat more wood mice and birds, so those populations fall, and the grass grows taller because fewer rabbits graze it, shading out daisies.
[bio32-s4.5.2] An omnivore or a predator with several prey is less affected than one with a single food source, because it can switch to another food when one becomes scarce. There is more on predicting changes from webs in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio32-s4.6.b] Predation is the best-known feeding relationship between animals. Predators have features that help them find, catch and kill their prey.
[bio32-s4.6.0] Predation is a feeding relationship in which one animal, the predator, kills and eats another, the prey.
[bio32-s4.6.1] Grassland examples of predation: fox (predator) and rabbit (prey); kestrel and wood mouse; ladybird and aphid; song thrush and snail.
[bio32-s4.6.2] The fox has acute hearing and smell to find prey, forward-facing eyes for judging distance, speed and sharp teeth, and hunts mostly at night, when rabbits feed.
[bio32-s4.6.3] The kestrel hovers into the wind while scanning the ground with very keen eyesight, then drops onto its prey, gripping it with sharp talons and tearing it with a hooked beak.
[bio32-s4.6.4] Spiders build webs to trap flying insects.
[bio32-s4.7.b] Prey animals need to spot danger early, escape, or avoid being noticed or eaten in the first place.
[bio32-s4.7.0] Rabbits have eyes on the sides of the head, giving an almost all-round field of view, and large mobile ears, so they can detect a predator coming from any direction.
[bio32-s4.7.1] Rabbits feed in groups so that some can watch for danger, thump the ground with a hind foot and flash the white underside of the tail as a warning, run in zigzags, and shelter in burrows.
[bio32-s4.7.2] Snails withdraw into their shells for protection.
[bio32-s4.7.3] Ladybirds have bright warning colours and a bad taste, so predators learn to avoid them.
[bio32-s4.7.4] Many caterpillars and grasshoppers are green, camouflaged against the grass, so predators find them hard to see.
[bio32-s4.8.b] Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together in a repeating cycle, because each population depends on the other.
[bio32-s4.8.0] 1. When prey are plentiful, more predators survive and breed, so predator numbers rise.
[bio32-s4.8.1] 2. More predators eat more prey, so prey numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.2] 3. With less food, predators starve or breed less, so predator numbers fall.
[bio32-s4.8.3] 4. With fewer predators, prey numbers recover, and the cycle repeats.
[bio32-s4.8.4] Predator numbers peak after prey numbers, because predators need time to breed once food is plentiful. There are usually fewer predators than prey.
[bio32-s4.8.5] The classic data are the fur-trading records of the snowshoe hare and the lynx in Canada, which rose and fell in cycles of about ten years.
[bio32-s4.8.6] Diagram: a graph of predator and prey numbers against time: two out-of-step waves, the predator curve lower and peaking after the prey curve.
[bio32-s4.9.b] Predators do more than eat prey. They also help to set how many prey a habitat holds.
[bio32-s4.9.0] Predators usually take the old, sick, young or weak prey, which are the easiest to catch.
[bio32-s4.9.1] The effect of predation increases as prey become more numerous, because prey are easier to find. This helps keep the prey population near the habitat’s carrying capacity (the largest population the habitat can support). There is more on this in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio32-s4.9.2] Removing a predator can let its prey increase until it overgrazes its food.
[bio32-s4.10.b] Organisms compete when they need the same resource and there is not enough for all. Competition can be sorted by who is competing and by how the resource is shared out.
[bio32-s4.10.0] Competition is the struggle between organisms for a resource that is in short supply. Plants compete for light, water, minerals and space; animals compete for food, water, space or territory, shelter and mates.
[bio32-s4.10.1] If the resource is plentiful, there is no competition.
[bio32-s4.10.2] Intraspecific competition is between members of the same species. It is the most intense kind, because they have exactly the same needs, e.g. daisy rosettes crowding each other for space and light, or male blackbirds or robins defending territories that contain food and nest sites.
[bio32-s4.10.3] Interspecific competition is between different species that need the same resource, e.g. grasses and daisies competing for light, or rabbits and sheep grazing the same grass.
[bio32-s4.10.4] Contest competition: there is an active physical contest for the resource and the winner takes it, e.g. birds fighting over territory, or stags fighting over mates.
[bio32-s4.10.5] Scramble competition: each competitor gets as much as it can of a shared resource, so when it is scarce all get too little, e.g. seedlings crowded together all grow weak and thin, or aphids crowded on a stem.
[bio32-s4.11.b] Competition has a cost for the organisms that lose, and this affects the whole population. Organisms also have features that help them win.
[bio32-s4.11.0] Competition reduces the growth, survival and breeding of the losers, and so limits population size. Its effect grows as the population becomes more crowded.
[bio32-s4.11.1] Between species, one may exclude the other from the habitat, or each may survive by using the resources in a slightly different way (different niches, meaning different roles or ways of living in the habitat). There is more on this in The niche.
[bio32-s4.11.2] Adaptations that help organisms compete: fast growth, and tall stems or climbing (e.g. ivy, brambles) in plants competing for light; deep roots for water; rapid breeding; aggressive defence of territory in animals.
[bio32-s4.12.b] Two examples show interspecific competition at work: one in the grassland and one from Ireland.
[bio32-s4.12.0] In short, grazed or mown grass, daisies and other rosette plants get enough light. Where grass is left long, it outgrows and shades them, and they disappear (interspecific competition for light).
[bio32-s4.12.1] Irish example: grey squirrels, introduced to Ireland at Castle Forbes, Co. Longford, in 1911, compete with the native red squirrel for food, especially seeds and nuts.
[bio32-s4.12.2] Grey squirrels are larger, can digest acorns (which red squirrels digest poorly), eat hazelnuts before they ripen, and carry squirrelpox virus, which kills red squirrels but rarely harms grey squirrels. Red squirrels have disappeared from much of the area greys occupy.
[bio32-s4.12.3] Where pine marten numbers have recovered in Ireland, grey squirrels have declined and red squirrels have returned.
[bio32-s4.13.b] Some organisms do not just compete or eat each other but live together. This close partnership is called symbiosis, and it is sorted into three types by who benefits.
[bio32-s4.13.0] Symbiosis is a close, long-term relationship in which two organisms of different species live together.
[bio32-s4.13.1] There are three types, named by who benefits: mutualism (both benefit, +/+), commensalism (one benefits and the other is unaffected, +/0) and parasitism (one benefits and the other is harmed, +/−).
[bio32-s4.13.2] Pollinators and flowers benefit each other (food for the insect, pollination for the plant), so the relationship is mutualistic, but the two don’t live together, so many biologists don’t call it symbiosis.
[bio32-s4.14.b] In mutualism both partners gain. The first example is a bacterium that gives clover a supply of nitrogen, which is why clover can enrich grassland.
[bio32-s4.14.0] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) live in root nodules (small swellings) of clover and other legumes. The bacteria convert nitrogen gas into ammonium, which the plant uses to make amino acids and proteins.
[bio32-s4.14.1] The plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates (from photosynthesis) and a protected home.
[bio32-s4.14.2] Active nodules are pink inside, from a pigment (leghaemoglobin) that controls oxygen levels for the bacteria.
[bio32-s4.14.3] This is why clover grows well in soils poor in nitrogen and enriches grassland. There is more on this in The nitrogen cycle.
[bio32-s4.14.4] To observe: dig up a clover plant, wash the roots and examine the nodules with a hand lens; cut one to see the pink colour.
[bio32-s4.15.b] Mutualism is common wherever one partner can do something the other cannot. Here are three more examples, and in each the partners swap food or shelter for something they lack.
[bio32-s4.15.0] Cellulose-digesting microorganisms (bacteria, protists and fungi) live in the rumen (first stomach) of cattle and sheep, and in the caecum (a pouch of the gut) of rabbits. They digest the cellulose of grass, which the animal has no enzyme to digest.
[bio32-s4.15.1] The microorganisms get a warm, food-rich home, and the animal gets the products (fatty acids) as food. Rabbits also eat some of their own soft droppings to absorb nutrients released by these microorganisms.
[bio32-s4.15.2] Mycorrhizal fungi grow around and into the roots of most grassland plants and trees. The fungal hyphae (thread-like filaments) spread far through the soil, absorbing water and minerals (especially phosphate) and passing them to the plant; the plant supplies the fungus with sugars.
[bio32-s4.15.3] Lichens (e.g. on walls, rocks and tree bark around the grassland) are a fungus and an alga (or cyanobacterium) living together. The alga photosynthesises and supplies sugars; the fungus gives shelter, holds water and absorbs minerals.
[bio32-s4.15.4] Bacteria in the human gut are another example of mutualism. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.b] In commensalism one partner gains and the other is neither helped nor harmed. It is easy to confuse with mutualism, so check whether the second partner really gets nothing.
[bio32-s4.16.0] Commensalism: one partner benefits and the other is neither helped nor harmed.
[bio32-s4.16.1] Mosses and lichens growing on the bark of trees get a place in the light without harming the tree.
[bio32-s4.16.2] Many bacteria living on human skin feed on dead skin cells and sweat without harming the host. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio32-s4.16.3] Seeds with hooks (e.g. burdock) are carried in an animal’s fur, and robins follow a gardener or grazing cattle to catch the invertebrates they disturb; both are commensal relationships, since the animal or gardener is unaffected.
[bio32-s4.17.b] A parasite lives on or in another organism, the host, and feeds on it. Unlike a predator, it usually keeps its host alive.
[bio32-s4.17.0] Parasitism: the parasite lives in or on the host and feeds on it, harming it. A parasite usually weakens its host without killing it quickly, since it depends on the host.
[bio32-s4.17.1] Grassland ectoparasites (parasites living on the outside of the host): fleas on rabbits and hedgehogs; ticks on sheep, deer and humans. Ticks are also vectors of Lyme disease, meaning they carry it from host to host.
[bio32-s4.17.2] The rabbit flea carries the myxoma virus that causes myxomatosis, which has killed very large numbers of rabbits.
[bio32-s4.17.3] Grassland endoparasites (parasites living inside the host): liver fluke in sheep and cattle on wet pasture; roundworms and tapeworms in the gut of grazing animals. There is more on liver fluke and tapeworms in Pathogens.
[bio32-s4.17.4] Plant parasites: rust fungi on grasses and cereals.
[bio32-s4.17.5] Yellow rattle, a meadow plant, is a partial parasite: it photosynthesises but taps the roots of grasses for water and minerals, weakening the grasses so that other wild flowers can grow. For this reason it is sown when restoring wildflower meadows.
[bio32-s4.17.6] Ivy is not a parasite: it climbs trees but makes its own food and takes nothing from them.
[bio32-s4.18.b] A parasite usually weakens its host rather than killing it quickly, but parasites and disease can still change how many hosts there are, because weakened hosts breed less and are caught more easily.
[bio32-s4.18.0] Parasites and disease weaken hosts, making them easier prey and less able to breed.
[bio32-s4.18.1] Parasites spread fastest when hosts are crowded, so their effect increases with population density, like predation.
[bio32-s4.19.b] The signs show the effect on each partner: + for benefits, − for harmed, and 0 for unaffected. Use them to compare the five relationships.
[bio32-s4.19.0] Predation (+/−): a predator kills and eats its prey, e.g. fox and rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.1] Competition (−/−): both organisms lose some of a scarce resource, e.g. grass and daisy for light, or grey and red squirrel.
[bio32-s4.19.2] Mutualism (+/+): both benefit, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules.
[bio32-s4.19.3] Commensalism (+/0): one benefits and the other is unaffected, e.g. mosses on tree bark.
[bio32-s4.19.4] Parasitism (+/−): the host is harmed but usually not killed quickly, e.g. a flea on a rabbit.
[bio32-s4.19.5] Predation and parasitism are both +/−. The difference is that a predator kills its prey at once and eats it, while a parasite lives on a living host over a long time.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.
