MODEL ANSWER: As light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases, because light supplies the energy for the light-dependent stage so more ATP and hydrogen are made per minute. At higher light intensity the curve levels off (plateau) because another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, is now in shortest supply and is the limiting factor, so more light makes no difference.

CANDIDATES bio14-s2: Investigation: rate of photosynthesis
[bio14-s2.0.b] Before measuring anything, you need to know what "rate" means and which conditions can speed photosynthesis up or hold it back. This theory is what every hypothesis in the investigation rests on.
[bio14-s2.0.0] The rate of photosynthesis is the amount of product made, or reactant used, per unit time. It is usually measured as the rate of oxygen production, in bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute, but it can also be measured as the rate of CO₂ uptake.
[bio14-s2.0.1] Oxygen is a good thing to measure because it is a product: it comes from the splitting of water in the light-dependent stage (the first stage, which needs light).
[bio14-s2.0.2] Light intensity: light supplies the energy for the light-dependent stage, so more light means more ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and hydrogen are made per minute and the rate rises, until another factor holds it back.
[bio14-s2.0.3] Carbon dioxide concentration: CO₂ is the raw material of the light-independent stage (the second stage, which builds glucose), so more CO₂ raises the rate, until another factor holds it back.
[bio14-s2.0.4] Temperature: the light-independent stage is controlled by enzymes. Raising the temperature increases the rate, because molecules move faster and collide more often with the enzymes' active sites (the part of the enzyme where the reaction happens), up to an optimum.
[bio14-s2.0.5] Above the optimum the rate falls quickly, because the enzymes are denatured (their shape is permanently changed, so they stop working). There is more on this in Enzymes as catalysts.
[bio14-s2.0.6] A limiting factor is the factor in shortest supply, which holds back the rate of a process even when the other factors are increased. At any moment the rate is limited by whichever of light intensity, CO₂ concentration or temperature is furthest from its best value.
[bio14-s2.0.7] Raising a limiting factor increases the rate. Raising a factor that is not limiting has no effect, because something else is still holding the rate back.
[bio14-s2.1.b] The bubbles you count are not the whole story. A plant is also respiring, and respiration uses oxygen, so what you measure is a net result.
[bio14-s2.1.0] A plant respires all the time, using some of the oxygen it makes, so the oxygen given off is photosynthesis minus respiration. This is called the net (apparent) rate.
[bio14-s2.1.1] In very dim light the two processes are equal and no oxygen is given off, because all the oxygen made is used up. This is the compensation point.
[bio14-s2.1.2] In darkness there is no photosynthesis, so the plant takes in oxygen for respiration.
[bio14-s2.2.b] A good investigation changes one thing and measures one thing. Planning starts with a clear aim, a prediction for each factor, and a plant that lets you see the result.
[bio14-s2.2.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of light intensity (or CO₂ concentration, or temperature) on the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed. Write one investigation per factor, changing only that factor, so you know which one caused any change.
[bio14-s2.2.1] Hypothesis for light: as light intensity increases, the rate of photosynthesis increases until it levels off. This rests on the theory that light drives the light-dependent stage until something else becomes limiting.
[bio14-s2.2.2] Hypothesis for CO₂: as CO₂ concentration increases, the rate increases until it levels off. This rests on CO₂ being the raw material of the light-independent stage.
[bio14-s2.2.3] Hypothesis for temperature: the rate increases with temperature up to an optimum and then decreases, because the enzymes of the light-independent stage work faster until they are denatured.
[bio14-s2.2.4] Elodea (Canadian pondweed) is the standard school plant. It is aquatic, so the oxygen it gives off appears as visible bubbles from the cut stem, which can be counted or collected. It is also easy to obtain, grows quickly and photosynthesises rapidly.
[bio14-s2.3.b] Next you decide what to change, what to measure and what to hold steady, and gather the apparatus. The variables below are for the light-intensity investigation.
[bio14-s2.3.0] The independent variable (the one you change) is light intensity, set by the lamp's distance from the plant or measured with a light meter in lux.
[bio14-s2.3.1] The dependent variable (the one you measure) is the rate of oxygen production, as bubbles per minute or cm³ of gas per minute.
[bio14-s2.3.2] Controlled variables (kept the same, so they cannot affect the result): temperature (water bath), CO₂ concentration (same sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration), the same pondweed (species, length, leaves), the same lamp and bulb, the adjustment time and the counting period.
[bio14-s2.3.3] For the other factors, swap the independent variable for CO₂ concentration or temperature, and keep light intensity constant.
[bio14-s2.3.4] Control: an identical set-up kept in darkness (lamp off, tube covered with foil), or a tube with no plant, gives no bubbles. This shows that the bubbles come from photosynthesis by the plant in the light, not from gas leaving the solution.
[bio14-s2.3.5] Equipment: fresh Elodea, scalpel or scissors, boiling tube or test tube, paper clip to weigh the pondweed down, beaker as a water bath (or an electric water bath), thermometer, bench lamp (an LED bulb gives out less heat), metre stick, stopwatch, and a light meter (optional).
[bio14-s2.3.6] Chemicals: sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) powder, or solutions of known concentration, which supplies the plant with CO₂, in water that has been boiled and cooled.
[bio14-s2.3.7] For measuring volume: a funnel and graduated tube, or a capillary tube attached to a syringe, called a photosynthometer. A glowing splint is optional, for testing the gas.
[bio14-s2.4.b] This basic set-up is used for all three investigations. Only the factor you change differs, so learn it once and adapt it.
[bio14-s2.4.0] 1. Fill a boiling tube with water containing a set concentration of sodium hydrogencarbonate. It dissolves to supply CO₂ to the plant, so that CO₂ is not limiting (except in the carbon dioxide investigation).
[bio14-s2.4.1] 2. Cut a healthy shoot of pondweed about 5 to 8 cm long, cutting the stem cleanly and slantwise under water.
[bio14-s2.4.2] 3. Place it in the tube upside down, with the cut end at the top, weighed down with a paper clip.
[bio14-s2.4.3] 4. Stand the tube in a beaker of water at a constant temperature, for example 25 °C, checked with a thermometer, so the temperature stays steady.
[bio14-s2.4.4] 5. Carry out the investigation in a darkened room, so that the lamp is the only light source.
[bio14-s2.4.5] Counting: let the plant adjust to each new condition for about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles leaving the cut end in one minute. Repeat the count three times at each setting and calculate the mean.
[bio14-s2.4.6] Collecting (more accurate): place an inverted funnel over the pondweed in a beaker, with a water-filled graduated tube over its stem, and record the gas collected in a set time, for example 5 minutes. Alternatively, draw the gas up a capillary tube with a syringe and measure the bubble's length.
[bio14-s2.4.7] Volume per unit time is more accurate than counting, because bubbles vary in size. Optional gas test: it relights a glowing splint, so it is rich in oxygen, but not pure oxygen, since dissolved nitrogen and carbon dioxide also come out.
[bio14-s2.5.b] Here the independent variable is light intensity, which you change by moving the lamp. The challenge is changing the light without also changing the temperature.
[bio14-s2.5.0] 1. Set up as in the basic set-up.
[bio14-s2.5.1] 2. Place the lamp 10 cm from the pondweed, measured with a metre stick from the bulb to the plant.
[bio14-s2.5.2] 3. Allow 5 minutes to adjust, then count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.5.3] 4. Move the lamp to 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, and repeat.
[bio14-s2.5.4] 5. Check the water temperature at each distance and keep it constant, by adding cold or warm water to the bath.
[bio14-s2.5.5] A lamp close to the plant also heats it, which would change two variables at once. Use an LED bulb, or place a beaker or flat-sided tank of water between the lamp and the plant as a heat shield, and check the thermometer.
[bio14-s2.5.6] Moving the lamp further away lowers the light intensity as the inverse square of the distance (light intensity ∝ 1/d²): doubling the distance gives a quarter of the light intensity. It does not fall in simple proportion.
[bio14-s2.5.7] So plot the rate against 1/d² to show the relationship with light intensity, or measure the light intensity at each position with a light meter.
[bio14-s2.6.b] Here the independent variable is CO₂ concentration, which you change by using sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of different strengths. Light and temperature are held constant.
[bio14-s2.6.0] 1. Make up a series of sodium hydrogencarbonate solutions of increasing concentration, for example 0%, 0.25%, 0.5%, 0.75% and 1%, using water that has been boiled and cooled. Boiling removes dissolved CO₂, so the 0% tube has very little.
[bio14-s2.6.1] 2. Keep the lamp at a fixed distance close to the plant, for example 10 cm, and keep the temperature constant, so that CO₂ is the only thing that changes.
[bio14-s2.6.2] 3. Place the same pondweed in each solution in turn, allow 5 minutes to adjust, and count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.6.3] 4. Calculate the mean for each concentration.
[bio14-s2.7.b] Here the independent variable is temperature, changed with water baths. Light and CO₂ are held constant.
[bio14-s2.7.0] 1. Set up as in the basic set-up, with a fixed lamp distance and a fixed sodium hydrogencarbonate concentration.
[bio14-s2.7.1] 2. Place the tube in a water bath at a range of temperatures, for example 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C, using ice or warm water or electric water baths, and check each with a thermometer.
[bio14-s2.7.2] 3. Let the tube reach the bath temperature, about 5 minutes, then count the bubbles per minute three times.
[bio14-s2.7.3] 4. If the first pondweed stops producing bubbles, use a fresh piece for the higher temperatures, and note that you have done so.
[bio14-s2.7.4] Warm water holds less dissolved gas, so some bubbles at high temperatures can come from gas leaving the solution. The darkness control at each temperature checks this.
[bio14-s2.8.b] This investigation mixes electricity, water, heat and sharp tools, so each hazard needs its own precaution.
[bio14-s2.8.0] Electricity and water: keep the lamp, its cable and plug away from the water bath, dry your hands before touching switches, and do not move a lamp while it is on and hot.
[bio14-s2.8.1] Lamp bulbs, other than LEDs, get very hot and can burn, so let them cool before moving them.
[bio14-s2.8.2] Take care with the scalpel: cut on a tile, away from the body. Take care with hot water baths and glassware.
[bio14-s2.8.3] Wash your hands after handling pondweed and pond water.
[bio14-s2.8.4] Elodea canadensis is a non-native invasive species (one that spreads and harms native wildlife) in Ireland. Never put leftover pondweed or its water into ponds, lakes, rivers or drains; dispose of it in compost or general waste.
[bio14-s2.9.b] The results table and graph turn counts into a relationship you can explain. For each factor the shape of the graph tells you what is limiting the rate.
[bio14-s2.9.0] Record the primary data (the results you collect yourself) in a table: the independent variable (distance in cm and 1/d², concentration in %, or temperature in °C), the three counts and the mean number of bubbles per minute.
[bio14-s2.9.1] Plot the mean rate on the y-axis against the independent variable on the x-axis, with units, and join the points with a smooth curve or straight lines.
[bio14-s2.9.2] Light intensity: as the lamp moves closer, the rate increases, so it falls as distance increases. At high intensity the curve levels off in a plateau, because another factor, CO₂ concentration or temperature, has become limiting.
[bio14-s2.9.3] CO₂ concentration: the rate increases with CO₂ concentration and then levels off, because light intensity or temperature becomes limiting. At 0% (boiled water) there are few or no bubbles.
[bio14-s2.9.4] Temperature: the rate rises up to an optimum, then falls steeply above it, as the enzymes of the light-independent stage are denatured. Bubbling may stop at the highest temperatures. The curve is not symmetrical: the fall is steeper than the rise.
[bio14-s2.9.5] Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data and whether it supports the hypothesis. On the levelling-off part of the graph, name the factor that is now limiting.
[bio14-s2.10.b] Examiners often ask you to draw the apparatus or sketch the expected graphs. Learn the labels for each.
[bio14-s2.10.0] Diagram: the set-up. Labels: lamp, metre stick, heat shield, beaker water bath, thermometer, boiling tube of sodium hydrogencarbonate solution, upside-down pondweed with bubbles at the cut end (or the funnel and graduated collecting tube).
[bio14-s2.10.1] Diagram: two sketch graphs. (a) Rate against light intensity, rising then levelling off, plus a higher curve labelled higher CO₂ concentration; label the rise light limiting and the plateau CO₂ or temperature limiting. (b) Rate against temperature, rising to an optimum then falling steeply.
[bio14-s2.11.b] Your own results are only one source. You can also use other people's data to support a conclusion, and apply the idea of limiting factors to growing crops.
[bio14-s2.11.0] Secondary data are results collected by others: other groups' class results, textbook or published graphs, or data-logger results from an oxygen or CO₂ sensor. Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility (whether others get the same result).
[bio14-s2.11.1] Interpreting a graph of rate against light intensity at two CO₂ concentrations (or two temperatures): where the two curves overlap, light is the limiting factor, because raising CO₂ makes no difference.
[bio14-s2.11.2] Where the two curves separate and each levels off, CO₂ (or temperature) is limiting, because raising it raises the plateau.
[bio14-s2.11.3] Applying limiting factors: commercial glasshouse growers raise the yield of crops such as tomatoes and lettuce by supplying extra light (artificial lighting), heat and CO₂. Burning paraffin or propane gives both heat and CO₂, or CO₂ gas can be added.
[bio14-s2.11.4] Each factor is kept just high enough that it is not limiting. Raising a factor that is not limiting wastes fuel and money and adds to emissions.
[bio14-s2.12.b] No school investigation is perfect. Knowing where the errors come from tells you how to reduce them.
[bio14-s2.12.0] Bubbles vary in size and can be missed or merge when the rate is fast, so counting is imprecise. Collecting the volume of gas is more accurate.
[bio14-s2.12.1] Other light in the room, such as daylight or other groups' lamps, changes the light intensity, so work in a darkened room.
[bio14-s2.12.2] Temperature can drift, especially with a hot bulb close to the plant, so check it and adjust it.
[bio14-s2.12.3] The gas is not pure oxygen, and some oxygen dissolves in the water instead of forming bubbles, so the bubbles underestimate the true rate. The measured rate is also a net rate, after respiration.
[bio14-s2.12.4] The carbon dioxide from sodium hydrogencarbonate is used up over time, and the plant's response may change, so use fresh solution, a fresh plant for each run if needed, and repeat the whole series.
[bio14-s2.12.5] Allowing too little time to adjust after each change gives rates that still reflect the previous setting.
[bio14-s2.12.6] Repeat each reading at least three times and calculate a mean, so a single odd count has less effect. Compare your results with other groups' results.

CANDIDATES bio14-s4: The light-independent reactions
[bio14-s4.0.b] The second stage of photosynthesis is where glucose is actually built. It uses the ATP and NADPH made in the first stage as its energy and hydrogen supply, and in return sends the empty carriers back.
[bio14-s4.0.0] Location: the stroma (the fluid that fills the chloroplast), which contains the enzymes for these reactions. The reactions need no light directly, but they depend on the ATP and NADPH made in the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoids.
[bio14-s4.0.1] 1. Carbon dioxide diffuses into the stroma, from the air spaces of the leaf through the cell and the chloroplast's membranes. It is taken up, or fixed, by combining with a carbon dioxide-accepting compound already present in the stroma.
[bio14-s4.0.2] 2. NADPH and ATP from the light-dependent reactions enter the reactions, because they supply the hydrogen and the energy that CO₂ needs to become glucose.
[bio14-s4.0.3] 3. Protons and electrons, that is hydrogen, are transferred from NADPH to the carbon compounds made from CO₂. This adds hydrogen to the carbon, which is reduction, turning CO₂ into carbohydrate. NADPH becomes NADP⁺.
[bio14-s4.0.4] 4. The energy to achieve this conversion comes from ATP, which is broken down to ADP + Pi (phosphate).
[bio14-s4.0.5] 5. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) is the product. Six molecules of CO₂ are fixed for each molecule of glucose, because each supplies one of its six carbon atoms. The glucose is then used, or converted to sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s4.0.6] 6. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return to the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes, where they are reloaded to ATP and NADPH and can be re-used.
[bio14-s4.0.7] The reactions form a cycle, the Calvin cycle. Most of the carbon compounds made regenerate the CO₂-accepting compound, so the cycle can keep fixing CO₂, and the rest is built into glucose.
[bio14-s4.1.b] Once you know the inputs and outputs, you can explain why this stage responds to temperature and CO₂ and why it stops soon after dark, even though it uses no light directly.
[bio14-s4.1.0] Summary in words: carbon dioxide + hydrogen (from NADPH) + energy (from ATP) → glucose, with NADP⁺ and ADP + Pi released, so the carriers are free to be reloaded.
[bio14-s4.1.1] Every step is catalysed by enzymes in the stroma, so the rate rises with temperature up to an optimum, and falls when the enzymes are denatured. This is why temperature affects the rate of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s4.1.2] CO₂ is the raw material of this stage, so its supply directly limits the rate of glucose production.
[bio14-s4.1.3] In darkness the light-dependent reactions stop, so ATP and NADPH run out within a short time. The light-independent reactions then stop too, even though they do not use light directly.
[bio14-s4.2.b] Photosynthesis is best understood as two linked stages joined by recycled carrier molecules. Modelling it means showing what goes in and out of each stage and what the carriers move between them.
[bio14-s4.2.0] Stage 1, the light-dependent reactions on the thylakoid membranes (the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast): light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll, splits water and drives electron transfer. This produces O₂, which is released, and loads the transfer molecules: ADP + Pi → ATP and NADP⁺ → NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.1] Stage 2, the light-independent reactions in the stroma: the transfer molecules are unloaded, which is why this stage needs stage 1. NADPH gives its protons and electrons to CO₂, and ATP gives the energy, producing glucose. NADPH → NADP⁺ and ATP → ADP + Pi.
[bio14-s4.2.2] The transfer molecules link the two stages. They carry energy (ATP) and electrons and hydrogen (NADPH) from stage 1 to stage 2, and return empty, as ADP + Pi and NADP⁺, from stage 2 to stage 1.
[bio14-s4.2.3] Because the transfer molecules are recycled, the chloroplast needs only a small supply of each.
[bio14-s4.2.4] Tracing the atoms: the oxygen gas comes from water (stage 1). The carbon and the oxygen in glucose come from CO₂ (stage 2). The hydrogen in glucose comes from water, carried by NADPH.
[bio14-s4.2.5] Diagram: thylakoid (stage 1) beside stroma (stage 2). Stage 1: in light, H₂O; out O₂. Stage 2: in CO₂; out glucose. Two loops between them: ATP across and ADP + Pi back, NADPH across and NADP⁺ back, each arrow labelled energy, or protons and electrons.

CANDIDATES bio14-s5: The chloroplast's internal structure
[bio14-s5.0.b] Photosynthesis happens inside the chloroplast, and its two stages happen in different parts of it. Each internal part has a structure that suits its job.
[bio14-s5.0.0] Envelope: two membranes, an outer and an inner, surround the chloroplast. The inner membrane controls which substances enter and leave, such as CO₂ going in and the products of photosynthesis going out, so the stroma (the fluid inside the envelope) keeps its enzymes and conditions separate from the cytoplasm.
[bio14-s5.0.1] Thylakoids are flattened membrane sacs inside the chloroplast, separate from the envelope. Each one encloses a fluid-filled space called the thylakoid space, or lumen.
[bio14-s5.0.2] Thylakoids are stacked like piles of coins into grana (singular: granum). Grana are joined by single, unstacked thylakoid membranes called intergranal lamellae, so the whole system is one connected network of membranes.
[bio14-s5.0.3] The thylakoid membranes hold the pigments (molecules that absorb light, such as chlorophyll) in their clusters and reaction centres, the electron transport chains (chains of electron carriers) and the enzyme ATP synthase. This is why they are the site of the light-dependent reactions, the first stage of photosynthesis.
[bio14-s5.0.4] The stroma contains the enzymes of the light-independent reactions, the second stage of photosynthesis, so it is the site of those reactions. It also holds starch grains, which are a temporary store of glucose, lipid droplets, ribosomes (the small structures that make proteins) and a small loop of DNA.
[bio14-s5.0.5] The chloroplast makes some of its own proteins, including some of the proteins of the thylakoid membrane and of the light-independent reactions, using its own DNA and ribosomes. There is more on this in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio14-s5.0.6] Diagram: an electron-microscope-level drawing of a chloroplast in section, labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, stroma, granum, thylakoid, thylakoid space, intergranal lamella, starch grain, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio14-s5.1.b] The light-dependent reactions need a chain that moves electrons along and releases their energy bit by bit. In the chloroplast, this chain is built into the thylakoid membrane.
[bio14-s5.1.0] An electron transport chain is a series of electron carriers that pass electrons from one to the next, each at a slightly lower energy level. It releases energy in small steps that can be used, such as water running downhill in stages, losing height and so energy at each step.
[bio14-s5.1.1] In the thylakoid membrane, the chain is made of a number of large protein complexes (large groups of proteins that work together) embedded in, or spanning, the membrane. Smaller mobile electron carriers that move within or along the membrane link the complexes, carrying electrons from one to the next.
[bio14-s5.1.2] Some protein complexes contain the clusters of pigment molecules and the reaction-centre chlorophyll, the special chlorophyll molecule that loses electrons when light hits it. Others pass electrons on and use their energy.
[bio14-s5.1.3] NADP⁺, a carrier that takes up electrons and hydrogen, is reduced (gains electrons) to NADPH, as NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH, on the stroma side of the membrane. This means NADPH is made right where the light-independent reactions use it.
[bio14-s5.1.4] Embedding the complexes in a membrane holds them in a fixed order and close together, so electrons pass quickly and in the right sequence. The energy released can move protons across the membrane, setting up a proton gradient (a difference in proton concentration across it) that drives ATP making.
[bio14-s5.2.b] The energy released by the electron transport chain is used to move protons (H⁺) into a small enclosed space. The protons then flow back out through an enzyme, and this flow powers the making of ATP.
[bio14-s5.2.0] As electrons pass along the chain, protons (H⁺, hydrogen ions) are moved from the stroma into the thylakoid space. Protons released by the photolysis of water (splitting water using light energy) are also released there, so the small, enclosed thylakoid space builds up a high concentration of protons.
[bio14-s5.2.1] Photolysis happens on the thylakoid-space side of the membrane, and its equation is 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂, so the protons it makes go straight into the thylakoid space.
[bio14-s5.2.2] The protons flow back out of the thylakoid space into the stroma through ATP synthase in the thylakoid membrane. The enzyme uses this flow to make ATP from ADP and phosphate (Pi), as ADP + Pi → ATP, on the stroma side, where the light-independent reactions need it.
[bio14-s5.2.3] The same mechanism makes ATP in mitochondria. There is more on this in The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase.
[bio14-s5.3.b] Exams ask how the structure of the chloroplast suits it to photosynthesis. The key idea is that each feature gives the reactions more room to work, or keeps them in the right place.
[bio14-s5.3.0] Large surface area: the many stacked thylakoids give a huge area of membrane for pigment clusters, electron transport chains and ATP synthase, so more light can be absorbed and more ATP and NADPH can be made.
[bio14-s5.3.1] Organisation of pigments: grouping many pigment molecules around each reaction centre funnels the energy of photons absorbed over a wide area, and over a range of colours, to the reaction centres. This means more of the light energy reaches the reaction centres.
[bio14-s5.3.2] Separate compartments: the thylakoid space is a small, enclosed compartment, so protons can be concentrated in it to drive ATP synthase. The thylakoid membrane is not freely permeable to protons, so the gradient is kept.
[bio14-s5.3.3] Two stages side by side: the light-dependent reactions on the membranes and the light-independent reactions in the stroma are next to each other, so ATP and NADPH are released into the stroma directly where they are used. ADP + Pi and NADP⁺ return only a short distance.
[bio14-s5.3.4] The stroma holds all the enzymes of the light-independent reactions in one compartment, with their substrates (the substances they act on), so they meet easily. It stores its glucose temporarily as starch.
[bio14-s5.3.5] Own DNA and ribosomes let the chloroplast make some of its own proteins on the spot, such as the membrane and enzyme proteins its two stages need.
[bio14-s5.3.6] Related: the palisade mesophyll, the leaf cells that do most photosynthesis, are packed with chloroplasts, which can move within the cell towards or away from the light.
[bio14-s5.3.7] Diagram: a thylakoid membrane, stroma above and thylakoid space below. Draw pigment clusters, protein complexes and electron carriers with electron arrows, water splitting and H⁺ building up inside, and ATP synthase making ATP in the stroma. Label thylakoid membrane, thylakoid space, stroma, protein complex, mobile electron carrier, ATP synthase.
