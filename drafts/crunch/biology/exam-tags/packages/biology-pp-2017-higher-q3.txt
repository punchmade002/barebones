CANDIDATES bio11-s3: Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance
[bio11-s3.0.b] Most of your genes sit on the chromosomes inside the nucleus of each cell, and nuclear inheritance is how those genes pass from parents to offspring. It follows a tidy, predictable route from the parent's nucleus to the child's.
[bio11-s3.0.0] Nuclear inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried on the chromosomes in the nucleus. Nearly all of an organism's genes are nuclear; in humans, about 20,000 protein-coding genes.
[bio11-s3.0.1] 1. The DNA in the nucleus is copied before division, so each new cell can receive a full set of genetic information.
[bio11-s3.0.2] 2. Meiosis (the division that makes gametes, the sex cells) separates each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent). Each gamete therefore receives one chromosome of every pair, and so one allele (version) of each gene.
[bio11-s3.0.3] 3. At fertilisation the nuclei of a male and a female gamete fuse, so the zygote (the first cell of the new individual) receives one set of chromosomes from each parent.
[bio11-s3.0.4] 4. Mitosis (ordinary cell division) then passes the full set of chromosomes to every body cell as the zygote grows.
[bio11-s3.0.5] Both parents contribute equally: the offspring inherits one allele of each autosomal gene (a gene on a non-sex chromosome) from its mother and one from its father. So for nuclear genes it does not matter which parent carries an allele.
[bio11-s3.0.6] For example, a tall male crossed with a short female gives the same results as a short male crossed with a tall female (reciprocal crosses). The exception is genes on the sex chromosomes.
[bio11-s3.0.7] Each gamete gets only one allele of each pair, chosen at random, so nuclear genes are inherited in the predictable ratios described by Mendel's laws, and new combinations of alleles arise in every generation. There is more on this in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s3.1.b] The nucleus is not the only place in a cell that holds DNA. Mitochondria and chloroplasts carry a little of their own, and genes in this DNA are inherited by a different route from nuclear genes.
[bio11-s3.1.0] Non-nuclear (also called extranuclear or cytoplasmic) inheritance is the inheritance of genes carried in DNA outside the nucleus. This DNA is in mitochondria, which are found in all eukaryotes, and in chloroplasts, which are found in plants and algae only.
[bio11-s3.1.1] Each mitochondrion and chloroplast contains several copies of its own small, circular DNA molecule, like the DNA of a bacterium. It is not wound around histones (the proteins that nuclear DNA is wrapped around). Each organelle also has its own small (70S) ribosomes.
[bio11-s3.1.2] A cell contains many such organelles, so it has hundreds to thousands of copies of the organelle DNA, compared with two copies of each nuclear gene.
[bio11-s3.1.3] Organelle genes code for some of the proteins the organelle needs: for mitochondria, some proteins of aerobic respiration (releasing energy from food using oxygen); for chloroplasts, some proteins of photosynthesis. They also code for the organelle's own rRNA and tRNA (the RNA parts of its ribosomes and protein-making machinery).
[bio11-s3.1.4] Most proteins in these organelles are coded by nuclear genes and imported, so the organelle depends on both its own genes and the nuclear genes.
[bio11-s3.1.5] Mitochondria and chloroplasts are thought to be descended from bacteria that came to live inside an ancestral cell, which is why they have their own DNA. Evidence includes their circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, double membrane and division by splitting in two.
[bio11-s3.1.6] New mitochondria and chloroplasts come only from the division of existing ones; the cell does not make them from scratch. This is why the organelle DNA is passed on only along with the cytoplasm that holds the organelles.
[bio11-s3.2.b] Chromosomes are moved around by a precise mechanism, but organelles are not. This difference, together with the way the egg and sperm differ in size, explains why non-nuclear genes usually come from the mother only.
[bio11-s3.2.0] Organelles lie in the cytoplasm (the cell contents outside the nucleus), and they are shared out between daughter cells at random when the cytoplasm divides, not by the spindle (the fibres that pull chromosomes apart) as chromosomes are.
[bio11-s3.2.1] Their genes therefore do not segregate (separate into different gametes) in meiosis, and do not follow Mendel's laws or Mendelian ratios.
[bio11-s3.2.2] In humans and most animals, the egg contributes nearly all the cytoplasm of the zygote, including its mitochondria. The sperm contributes almost only its nucleus; the few sperm mitochondria, in its midpiece, that enter the egg are destroyed.
[bio11-s3.2.3] So mitochondrial genes are inherited from the mother only. In most flowering plants, chloroplasts (and mitochondria) are likewise inherited through the egg cell, from the female parent.
[bio11-s3.2.4] Consequence 1: all the offspring of a mother share her mitochondrial DNA, whatever the father.
[bio11-s3.2.5] Consequence 2: reciprocal crosses give different results, with the offspring resembling the female parent for the trait, unlike nuclear genes where reciprocal crosses give the same results.
[bio11-s3.2.6] Consequence 3: mitochondrial DNA does not mix between two parents, so it changes only by mutation and passes down the female line almost unchanged.
[bio11-s3.3.b] An early experiment on plants showed non-nuclear inheritance in action. It is a worked example of offspring copying the female parent rather than following Mendel's ratios.
[bio11-s3.3.0] In 1909 Carl Correns studied the four-o'clock plant (Mirabilis jalapa), which has branches bearing green, white or variegated (green-and-white) leaves. The white areas lack working chloroplasts, so they cannot photosynthesise.
[bio11-s3.3.1] Seeds from flowers on green branches gave green seedlings, and seeds from flowers on white branches gave white seedlings, which soon died.
[bio11-s3.3.2] Seeds from variegated branches gave a mixture of seedlings, whatever branch the pollen came from.
[bio11-s3.3.3] The offspring took the leaf type of the female parent. Leaf colour was inherited through the chloroplasts in the egg's cytoplasm, which made this one of the first demonstrations of non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio11-s3.4.b] Human mitochondrial DNA is tiny, but because of maternal inheritance it has medical and scientific uses. Faults in it cause disease, and its unmixed female line makes it a useful record.
[bio11-s3.4.0] Human mitochondrial DNA is about 16,600 base pairs long and carries 37 genes. By comparison, there are about 20,000 protein-coding genes on nuclear chromosomes of up to hundreds of millions of base pairs each.
[bio11-s3.4.1] Mutations in mitochondrial DNA can cause diseases that mostly affect tissues with high energy demands, such as muscle, nerves, brain and eyes, because they reduce aerobic respiration, the process that supplies those tissues' energy. Example: Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), which causes loss of central vision, usually in young adults.
[bio11-s3.4.2] An affected mother can pass a mitochondrial disease to all her children, sons and daughters, because every child gets its mitochondria from her egg. An affected father passes it to none, because his mitochondria are not passed on. Sons inherit their mother's mitochondria too, but only daughters pass them on.
[bio11-s3.4.3] Severity varies: a cell may contain a mixture of normal and mutant mitochondria, and the proportion passed to each egg varies, so children of the same mother can be affected to different degrees.
[bio11-s3.4.4] Mitochondrial donation (“three-parent IVF”) replaces a mother's faulty mitochondria with healthy mitochondria from a donor egg, keeping the nuclear DNA of both parents. In 2015 the UK became the first country to allow it by law.
[bio11-s3.4.5] In mitochondrial donation the child's nuclear DNA comes from its two parents and its small amount of mitochondrial DNA from the donor. There is more on the ethics in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio11-s3.4.6] Because mitochondrial DNA passes down the female line without mixing and changes only by mutation, it is used to trace maternal ancestry and human migration and evolution.
[bio11-s3.4.7] It is also used to identify remains when nuclear DNA is too damaged, since there are many copies per cell and it survives better.
[bio11-s3.5.b] Putting the two systems side by side shows how a difference in where the DNA is kept leads to differences in how it is passed on. Each point below pairs nuclear inheritance with non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio11-s3.5.0] Location: nuclear DNA is in chromosomes in the nucleus; non-nuclear DNA is in mitochondria and chloroplasts in the cytoplasm.
[bio11-s3.5.1] Form: nuclear DNA is long, linear molecules wound around histones; non-nuclear DNA is small, circular molecules without histones.
[bio11-s3.5.2] Amount: most of the genes are nuclear (about 20,000 protein-coding genes in humans); few are non-nuclear (37 in human mitochondria), coding mainly for the organelle's own proteins and RNA.
[bio11-s3.5.3] Copies: two copies of each autosomal gene in a diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes); hundreds to thousands of copies of organelle DNA per cell.
[bio11-s3.5.4] Parents contributing: both parents equally for nuclear genes; usually the mother only for non-nuclear genes (maternal inheritance).
[bio11-s3.5.5] Distribution to gametes and daughter cells: chromosomes are separated precisely by the spindle in meiosis and mitosis; organelles are shared out randomly when the cytoplasm divides.
[bio11-s3.5.6] Patterns: nuclear inheritance follows Mendel's laws with predictable ratios, and reciprocal crosses give the same results; non-nuclear inheritance gives no Mendelian ratios, and reciprocal crosses give different results.
[bio11-s3.5.7] Variation between generations: nuclear inheritance gives new combinations of alleles from both parents each generation; non-nuclear DNA does not mix, and changes only by mutation.
[bio11-s3.6.b] Despite their differences, the two kinds of DNA are the same kind of molecule working in the same way. A comparison answer should say what they share as well as how they differ.
[bio11-s3.6.0] Both are DNA, using the same genetic code (with minor differences in mitochondria) and the same base pairing, so the same rules read and copy them.
[bio11-s3.6.1] Both are copied before cell division, so that each daughter cell or organelle receives genetic information.
[bio11-s3.6.2] Both can mutate and so cause inherited disease, such as LHON for mitochondrial DNA.
[bio11-s3.6.3] Diagram: sperm and egg fusing. Egg large, nucleus plus many mitochondria in cytoplasm; sperm nucleus in head, few mitochondria in midpiece. Arrows: nuclear DNA from both parents, mitochondria from egg only. Labels: nucleus (nuclear DNA, both parents), mitochondria (mitochondrial DNA, mother).

CANDIDATES bio15-s5: The mitochondrion's internal structure and ATP synthase
[bio15-s5.0.b] A mitochondrion is an organelle (a specialised compartment inside a cell). Its inside is divided into distinct regions by two membranes, and each region has a job.
[bio15-s5.0.0] Outer membrane: smooth, it surrounds the mitochondrion and is permeable to small molecules such as pyruvate, ADP and ATP, so these can move freely in and out.
[bio15-s5.0.1] Inner membrane: folded into many cristae (singular crista), which greatly increase its surface area. It holds the electron transport chains and ATP synthase (the enzyme that makes ATP). It is impermeable to protons except through ATP synthase.
[bio15-s5.0.2] Intermembrane space: the narrow space between the outer and inner membranes, into which protons are moved. This is the space "outside the inner membrane" in the specification's wording, not outside the mitochondrion.
[bio15-s5.0.3] Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane. It contains the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle (the earlier stages of respiration that deliver electrons to the chain).
[bio15-s5.0.4] The matrix also holds the mitochondrion's own small loop of DNA and its ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), so the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins.
[bio15-s5.0.5] Diagram: electron-microscope-level drawing of a mitochondrion in section. Label outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae, intermembrane space, matrix, DNA, ribosomes, and ATP synthase drawn as small knobs on the matrix side of the cristae.
[bio15-s5.1.b] The electron transport chain is the machinery that passes electrons along a series of steps, releasing energy at each one. It is built into the inner membrane, so the membrane's structure matters.
[bio15-s5.1.0] The chain is made of a number of large protein complexes embedded in, and spanning, the inner membrane (reaching from one side to the other).
[bio15-s5.1.1] Smaller mobile electron carriers link the complexes. They move within or along the membrane and carry electrons from one complex to the next, so the electrons are handed on step by step.
[bio15-s5.1.2] NADH (the carrier that brings electrons from earlier stages of respiration) delivers electrons to the first complex. The electrons then move from complex to complex, each at a lower energy level.
[bio15-s5.1.3] At the last complex the electrons are passed to oxygen, forming water. There is more on this in Second-stage process.
[bio15-s5.2.b] The electrons do not make ATP directly. Their energy is used to build up a store of protons on one side of the inner membrane, and the protons' flow back drives ATP synthase. The sequence runs in seven steps.
[bio15-s5.2.0] 1. Electrons arrive: high-energy electrons from NADH are transferred to a protein complex in the inner membrane. Electrons are negatively charged, so their arrival gives the complex a negative charge, a charge difference.
[bio15-s5.2.1] 2. Protons are attracted: the charge difference attracts positively charged protons (H⁺) to the complex. The protons are given by donor molecules: NADH releases a proton when it gives up its electrons, and more protons come from the solution in the matrix.
[bio15-s5.2.2] 3. Protons are moved out: the protons pass through the complex from the matrix side to the outside of the inner membrane, into the intermembrane space. Energy from the electrons drives this. It happens at several complexes along the chain as the electrons pass from one to the next. Textbooks call this proton pumping.
[bio15-s5.2.3] 4. A gradient builds: protons accumulate in the narrow intermembrane space, giving a high concentration of protons outside the inner membrane and a low concentration in the matrix. This proton (concentration) gradient is a store of energy, like water held behind a dam. It also makes the intermembrane space more positive and more acidic than the matrix.
[bio15-s5.2.4] 5. Protons flow back: the inner membrane lets protons through only at the enzyme ATP synthase. They flow back down their gradient, from the intermembrane space across the inner membrane into the matrix, through a channel in ATP synthase. They do not pass back through the electron transport complexes.
[bio15-s5.2.5] 6. ATP is made: ATP synthase harnesses the flow of protons. It turns part of the enzyme, like a water wheel or turbine, and uses the energy to join ADP and Pi (inorganic phosphate) into ATP on the matrix side.
[bio15-s5.2.6] 7. Oxygen keeps the system running: oxygen accepts the electrons and protons at the end of the chain, forming water, so electrons keep flowing and protons keep being moved out. Without oxygen, the gradient is not maintained and ATP synthase stops.
[bio15-s5.2.7] Diagram: inner membrane, matrix below, intermembrane space above. Show NADH → NAD⁺ + H⁺ + 2e⁻; electron arrow; many H⁺ up; ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O; H⁺ down ATP synthase, ADP + Pi → ATP. Label protein complex, mobile electron carrier, inner membrane, intermembrane space, matrix, ATP synthase, proton gradient.
[bio15-s5.3.b] The process in the last block has a name, and its history shows how a new scientific idea can win acceptance.
[bio15-s5.3.0] Making ATP using a proton gradient across a membrane is called chemiosmosis.
[bio15-s5.3.1] Nature of Science: the idea was proposed by the British biochemist Peter Mitchell in 1961 and was at first widely doubted. He was awarded the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1978, once experiments had supported it.
[bio15-s5.4.b] Each feature of the mitochondrion's structure suits the way the proton gradient is built and used. Linking a feature to its effect is how you answer structure-and-function questions.
[bio15-s5.4.0] Cristae: folding gives a large area of inner membrane, which holds very many electron transport chains and ATP synthase molecules, so more ATP can be made. Mitochondria in very active cells, such as heart and flight muscle, have more densely packed cristae.
[bio15-s5.4.1] Narrow intermembrane space: its small volume means that protons moved into it quickly build up a high concentration and a steep gradient.
[bio15-s5.4.2] Impermeable inner membrane: the gradient is kept, and protons can return only through ATP synthase, so their energy is not wasted.
[bio15-s5.4.3] Complexes embedded in the membrane in a fixed order: electrons pass quickly and in sequence, and protons are moved in one direction only, across the membrane.
[bio15-s5.4.4] Matrix next to the inner membrane: the enzymes of the link step and the citric acid cycle are right beside the chains, so NADH is delivered quickly. ATP is made into the matrix, from where it is exported to the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s5.4.5] Double membrane: separates the matrix's enzymes and conditions from the cytoplasm. The outer and inner membranes together create the intermembrane space.
[bio15-s5.4.6] Own DNA and ribosomes: the mitochondrion makes some of its own proteins, including some of the chain's, and can divide to make more mitochondria when a cell needs more energy, for example in muscles during training.
[bio15-s5.5.b] Chloroplasts (the organelles where photosynthesis happens) make ATP using a very similar mechanism, built into their thylakoid membranes (flattened internal membrane sacs). The two organelles are alike in several ways, then differ in the details.
[bio15-s5.5.0] Both have electron transport chains of protein complexes and mobile electron carriers embedded in a membrane.
[bio15-s5.5.1] Both use the energy of electrons passing along the chain to move protons across the membrane, building a proton gradient, and both make ATP as protons flow back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis).
[bio15-s5.5.2] Both have a large membrane area, from cristae in the mitochondrion or stacked thylakoids in the chloroplast, so each can hold many chains and ATP synthase molecules.
[bio15-s5.5.3] Both organelles have their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio15-s5.6.b] The shared mechanism is fed by different energy and electron sources, and it sits in different membranes and compartments. Compare each feature as a pair.
[bio15-s5.6.0] Source of energy: the mitochondrion uses chemical energy from food (glucose), delivered as electrons by NADH; the chloroplast uses light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll.
[bio15-s5.6.1] Source of electrons: NADH, from glucose, in the mitochondrion; water, via the reaction-centre chlorophyll, in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.2] Final electron acceptor: oxygen, forming water, in the mitochondrion; NADP⁺, forming NADPH, in the chloroplast (non-cyclic pathway).
[bio15-s5.6.3] Oxygen: used at the end of the chain in mitochondria; released by photolysis (the splitting of water) in chloroplasts.
[bio15-s5.6.4] Which membrane: the inner membrane of the envelope (cristae) in the mitochondrion; the thylakoid membrane, a separate internal membrane system, in the chloroplast, not its envelope.
[bio15-s5.6.5] Where protons build up: in the intermembrane space, between the inner and outer membranes, in the mitochondrion; inside the thylakoid space in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.6] Where ATP is made and used: in the matrix, then exported for use all over the cell, in the mitochondrion; in the stroma (the fluid around the thylakoids), where it is used in the light-independent reactions, in the chloroplast.
[bio15-s5.6.7] Diagram: side-by-side sketches of a section of the inner mitochondrial membrane and a section of the thylakoid membrane. Each shows its chain, the direction of proton movement, the high-proton compartment (intermembrane space; thylakoid space) and ATP synthase. Label the electron source and final acceptor on each.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio7-s1: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
[bio7-s1.0.b] Biologists sort all cells into two types, depending on whether the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. The names themselves tell you the difference, and you need the units to talk about how big cells are.
[bio7-s1.0.0] A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments wrapped in their own membrane). Its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. “Pro” means before and “karyon” means kernel or nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.1] A eukaryotic cell has its DNA enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and it has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. “Eu” means true, so eukaryote means “true nucleus”. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.2] Cells are far too small for millimetres to be a handy unit, so their sizes are given in micrometres. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres) and 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.
[bio7-s1.1.b] Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells look very different, but both are living cells that must do the same basic jobs. So they share a core set of features.
[bio7-s1.1.0] Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves the cell, and cytoplasm, the fluid where many of the cell's reactions take place.
[bio7-s1.1.1] Both have ribosomes (the tiny structures that make proteins), and both use DNA as the genetic material, reading it with the same genetic code.
[bio7-s1.1.2] Both respire, releasing energy from food, and both use ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy) as the energy currency.
[bio7-s1.1.3] These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor, because such an unusual set of identical features is unlikely to have arisen separately.
[bio7-s1.2.b] The most important difference between the two cell types is how the DNA is stored. This affects where the DNA sits, what shape it has and what it is packed with.
[bio7-s1.2.0] Nucleus: prokaryotes have none. Their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Eukaryotes have a true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores (small channels through the envelope).
[bio7-s1.2.1] Number and shape of chromosomes: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome (a closed loop of DNA). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes (DNA with two ends).
[bio7-s1.2.2] Packaging: eukaryotic DNA is wound around histone proteins, which help to pack it. In bacteria the circular chromosome is not wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s1.2.3] Prokaryotes often also have plasmids, small extra rings of DNA separate from the main chromosome. Plasmids can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance, which is why resistance can spread between bacteria.
[bio7-s1.2.4] Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast), not in all eukaryotes, so do not say that eukaryotes have none or that all have them.
[bio7-s1.3.b] Eukaryotic cells divide their work between membrane-bound organelles, whereas prokaryotes have to do everything without them. Ribosomes are the one structure both have, but they differ in size.
[bio7-s1.3.0] Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none, so no mitochondria, no chloroplasts and no other membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.3.1] Without organelles, prokaryotes carry out respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it (folds of the membrane that increase its area).
[bio7-s1.3.2] Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. This is one more way these organelles resemble prokaryotic cells.
[bio7-s1.3.3] Ribosomes: both cell types have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). The S number is a measure of size.
[bio7-s1.3.4] Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they stop bacteria making proteins and harm bacteria but not human cells, which use 80S ribosomes in their cytoplasm.
[bio7-s1.4.b] The remaining differences are about the whole cell: how big it is, what surrounds it, and how the organism is built and reproduces.
[bio7-s1.4.0] Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so they are roughly ten times larger in length.
[bio7-s1.4.1] Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one, made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells have chitin walls, and animal cells have none.
[bio7-s1.4.2] Some prokaryotes have extra outer structures: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum, a whip-like tail, for movement. There is more on the full bacterial cell in Microorganisms.
[bio7-s1.4.3] Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular (made of a single cell) and divide by binary fission, in which one cell splits in two. Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis.
[bio7-s1.4.4] Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotes are protists, fungi, plants and animals.
[bio7-s1.5.b] How a cell looks in a micrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope) depends on the microscope, because a light microscope cannot show very small structures but an electron microscope can. Knowing what each shows lets you identify a cell.
[bio7-s1.5.0] Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure. It is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
[bio7-s1.5.1] Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm and the cell membrane as a line. Plant cells also show the cell wall, a large vacuole (a fluid-filled sac) and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.5.2] Electron micrographs show what the light microscope cannot. In prokaryotes they show the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall.
[bio7-s1.5.3] In eukaryotes, electron micrographs show the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
[bio7-s1.5.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule, flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), with the eukaryotic cell drawn much larger.
[bio7-s1.6.b] You may be given a micrograph and asked whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and to justify the answer. Work through the same checks in order.
[bio7-s1.6.0] 1. Check for a nucleus. If one is present, the cell is eukaryotic, because only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nucleus.
[bio7-s1.6.1] 2. Check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size. A cell of about 1–10 µm suggests a prokaryote, since eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm.
[bio7-s1.6.2] 3. Look for membrane-bound organelles, which only eukaryotes have.
[bio7-s1.6.3] 4. For a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts, which mean a plant cell, or their absence, which suggests an animal cell. There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.
