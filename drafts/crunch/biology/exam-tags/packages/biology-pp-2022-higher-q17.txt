CANDIDATES bio11-s2: Key concepts of information transfer
[bio11-s2.0.b] Inheritance starts with a few basic ideas: what counts as one kind of organism, what is passed on, and what carries it. These terms are used throughout genetics.
[bio11-s2.0.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed in natural conditions to produce fertile offspring (offspring that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, the mule, is sterile, so horses and donkeys are different species.
[bio11-s2.0.1] The species definition has limits: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually, because they never interbreed, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding. There is more on how new species form in Evidence for evolution.
[bio11-s2.0.2] Heredity is the passing of characteristics (traits), by means of genes, from parents to offspring, that is, from one generation to the next. A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for a trait.
[bio11-s2.0.3] Genetics is the study of heredity (how traits are passed on) and of variation (the differences between individuals).
[bio11-s2.0.4] A chromosome is the structure that carries genes from one generation to the next. Body cells carry chromosomes in homologous pairs (two matching chromosomes, one from each parent), and each gamete carries one chromosome from each pair.
[bio11-s2.1.b] Parents pass genes on through special sex cells. Following the chromosome number from parent to gametes to zygote shows how each generation gets its genes and why the number stays constant.
[bio11-s2.1.0] A gamete is a sex cell: a sperm or egg in animals, or in flowering plants the male gamete nucleus in the pollen grain and the egg cell in the ovule.
[bio11-s2.1.1] Gametes are haploid, with one set of chromosomes (one from each pair), so a human gamete has 23 chromosomes. They are made by meiosis. Gametes must be haploid because fertilisation adds the two sets together, so otherwise the chromosome number would double every generation.
[bio11-s2.1.2] Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of two gametes, usually from two parents. The offspring receive half their chromosomes from each parent, so they are genetically different from both parents and from each other. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio11-s2.1.3] Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no gametes, so it produces genetically identical offspring, called clones.
[bio11-s2.1.4] Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes), the first cell of the new organism. It restores the diploid number: in humans 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.5] The zygote divides by mitosis, so every body cell carries the same set of chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.6] Diagram: life-cycle flow: parent (2n = 46) → meiosis → gametes (n = 23, sperm and egg) → fertilisation → zygote (2n = 46) → mitosis → offspring; label haploid, diploid, meiosis, fertilisation and mitosis.
[bio11-s2.2.b] A gene is a section of DNA for one trait, such as seed shape. Genes come in slightly different versions, and these versions, called alleles, are what make individuals differ.
[bio11-s2.2.0] An allele is one of the alternative forms of a gene, found at the same locus (position) on homologous chromosomes. Alleles differ slightly in base sequence, as they arise by mutation, so they may produce different versions of the trait.
[bio11-s2.2.1] In peas, the gene for seed shape has an allele for round seeds (R) and one for wrinkled seeds (r).
[bio11-s2.2.2] A diploid organism has two alleles for each autosomal gene (a gene on a non-sex chromosome), one on each homologous chromosome, one inherited from each parent.
[bio11-s2.2.3] A population may have more than two alleles of a gene, but each individual carries only two, because it has only two homologous chromosomes. The human ABO blood-group gene has three alleles (A, B and O) in the population.
[bio11-s2.2.4] Homozygous means having two identical alleles of a gene (RR or rr). A homozygous organism is also called pure-breeding, because it passes on the same allele in every gamete.
[bio11-s2.2.5] Heterozygous means having two different alleles of a gene (Rr). A heterozygous organism is also called a hybrid for that gene.
[bio11-s2.2.6] Diagram: a pair of homologous chromosomes with one gene locus marked, one chromosome carrying R and the other r (heterozygous); a second pair carrying r and r (homozygous); label homologous chromosomes, locus and allele.
[bio11-s2.3.b] Two terms separate what an organism's genes are from what it looks like or does. They are not always the same, because the environment and dominance both play a part.
[bio11-s2.3.0] Genotype is the genetic make-up of an organism: the alleles it has for a particular trait, written with letters, such as RR, Rr or rr.
[bio11-s2.3.1] Phenotype is the observable (physical, chemical or behavioural) characteristics of an organism that result from its genotype, such as round or wrinkled seeds, or blood group A.
[bio11-s2.3.2] Phenotype = genotype + environment. The genotype sets the possible range, and the environment affects how it is expressed.
[bio11-s2.3.3] Identical twins have the same genotype but may differ in mass because of diet and exercise, so the same genes can give different phenotypes.
[bio11-s2.3.4] Hydrangea plants of the same variety have blue flowers in acid soil and pink flowers in alkaline soil, so soil pH changes the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.3.5] A plant with the genes for chlorophyll is pale yellow if grown in the dark, because the environment (no light) stops the pigment forming.
[bio11-s2.3.6] Different genotypes can give the same phenotype: RR and Rr are both round, because one allele is dominant. So the phenotype does not always reveal the genotype. There is more on how to find the genotype (the test cross) in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s2.4.b] When an organism is heterozygous, the two alleles do not always show equally. Dominant and recessive describe which allele is seen in the phenotype and which is hidden.
[bio11-s2.4.0] A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote, masking the effect of the other allele. It is written with a capital letter (R), and a dominant trait shows in both RR and Rr.
[bio11-s2.4.1] A recessive allele is expressed in the phenotype only when no dominant allele is present, that is, only in the homozygous condition (rr). It is written with the lower-case letter (r).
[bio11-s2.4.2] In a heterozygote, a recessive allele is present but hidden, and it can still be passed on to offspring. It does not disappear and is not weaker.
[bio11-s2.4.3] Often the dominant allele codes for a working protein and the recessive allele for a non-working one. One working copy makes enough protein, so Rr looks the same as RR.
[bio11-s2.4.4] In peas, R codes for a working starch-branching enzyme; rr seeds make less starch and wrinkle. There is more on this chain in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio11-s2.4.5] Dominant does not mean common or better. The allele causing Huntington's disease is dominant but rare, and harmful.
[bio11-s2.4.6] Cystic fibrosis and albinism are caused by recessive alleles, so two carriers (heterozygotes) can have an affected child.
[bio11-s2.4.7] A carrier is a heterozygous individual who carries a recessive allele for a condition but does not show it.
[bio11-s2.5.b] Having a gene is not the same as using it. Gene expression is how a cell turns the information in a gene into something that affects the organism.
[bio11-s2.5.0] Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make its product (a protein or a functional RNA), so that the gene affects the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.5.1] A gene that is being used is switched on (expressed); one that is not is switched off.
[bio11-s2.5.2] Every body cell has the same genes, but each cell type expresses only some of them, which is why cells become specialised.
[bio11-s2.5.3] Expression is controlled by regulatory DNA (stretches of DNA that switch genes on or off), by signals such as hormones, and by the environment: some genes are switched on only in response to a stimulus.
[bio11-s2.5.4] There is more on how the environment and behaviour change expression in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s2.6.b] Four more terms extend the basic vocabulary. Each has a short definition and, where there is one, a named example.
[bio11-s2.6.0] Epigenetic expression is a change in gene expression that is not caused by a change in the DNA base sequence, but by chemical tags on the DNA or on the histones (the proteins DNA is wound around) that switch genes on or off.
[bio11-s2.6.1] Epigenetic tags can be caused by the environment and behaviour, can be passed on when cells divide, and some are reversible.
[bio11-s2.6.2] Incomplete dominance means neither allele is dominant over the other, so the heterozygote has a phenotype intermediate between the two homozygotes. For example, red-flowered and white-flowered snapdragons give pink-flowered heterozygotes. This contrasts with complete dominance, where the heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote.
[bio11-s2.6.3] Linkage: genes located on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together, because they do not separate into different gametes. Linked genes do not assort independently.
[bio11-s2.6.4] Sex-linkage: a gene located on a sex chromosome (in humans almost always the X chromosome) is sex-linked, so the trait's inheritance pattern differs between males and females. Examples are red–green colour blindness and haemophilia, which are much more common in males.

CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio12-s3: Evidence for evolution
[bio12-s3.0.b] Fossils are the actual remains or traces of organisms that lived long ago. To use them as evidence you need to know how they form and how old they are.
[bio12-s3.0.0] A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago, for example bones, shells, teeth, footprints or impressions of leaves.
[bio12-s3.0.1] Most fossils form when an organism is quickly buried in sediment (mud or sand) in water. Its soft parts decay, and minerals gradually replace the hard parts as the sediment becomes sedimentary rock (rock formed from compacted sediment).
[bio12-s3.0.2] Whole organisms are occasionally preserved in amber (hardened tree resin), ice or peat, which is why these finds are rare and valuable.
[bio12-s3.0.3] In undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, deeper layers are older, because new sediment settles on top of old. So fossils in deeper layers are older than those above them.
[bio12-s3.0.4] Radiometric dating measures the decay of radioactive elements in rocks, and gives ages in years rather than just older or younger.
[bio12-s3.0.5] The Earth is about 4.6 billion years old. The oldest widely accepted fossils, of simple bacteria-like cells, are about 3.5 billion years old.
[bio12-s3.0.6] Diagram: a cross-section of rock layers with fossils, the deepest layer labelled oldest and the top labelled youngest, showing simpler forms in the lower layers; optionally, three horse feet from Hyracotherium to the modern horse showing toe reduction.
[bio12-s3.1.b] Laid out in order of age, the fossils show a pattern: life changes over time in a consistent direction. This is what evolution predicts and is why the record counts as evidence.
[bio12-s3.1.0] The oldest rocks contain only simple, single-celled life, so complex organisms had not yet appeared.
[bio12-s3.1.1] More complex organisms appear in younger rocks in a consistent order, for example fish before amphibians, and reptiles before birds and mammals.
[bio12-s3.1.2] Most species that ever lived are extinct, so the living world is only a small part of the history of life.
[bio12-s3.1.3] In many groups a series of fossils shows gradual change over time, which is what you would expect if one form slowly gave rise to another.
[bio12-s3.2.b] Some fossils show features of two different groups. These are called transitional fossils, and Archaeopteryx and Tiktaalik are two examples.
[bio12-s3.2.0] Archaeopteryx was found in Germany in 1861, two years after On the Origin of Species, and is about 150 million years old.
[bio12-s3.2.1] It had feathers and wings like a bird, but also teeth, a long bony tail and clawed fingers like a reptile (a dinosaur). So it shows features of both groups.
[bio12-s3.2.2] Tiktaalik was found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic and is about 375 million years old. It was a fish with scales, fins and gills.
[bio12-s3.2.3] It also had a flat head, a neck, and fin bones like the bones of a limb, so it was intermediate between fish and the first four-limbed land animals.
[bio12-s3.2.4] The team searched rocks of that age because the theory predicted such an animal should have lived then, and the prediction was confirmed. There is more on this in The importance of the theory.
[bio12-s3.3.b] A long series of horse fossils shows gradual change in one group. The record is also incomplete, so you need to know why gaps exist and why they do not weaken the case.
[bio12-s3.3.0] The horse series runs over more than 50 million years, from Hyracotherium, a small animal about the size of a dog with four toes on its front feet, to the modern horse.
[bio12-s3.3.1] The modern horse is much larger, stands on a single toe (the hoof), and has teeth suited to grazing. This matches a change from forest to open grassland.
[bio12-s3.3.2] The fossil record is incomplete, because fossils form only in rare conditions and soft-bodied organisms seldom fossilise.
[bio12-s3.3.3] Many fossils have also been destroyed by erosion or movements of the Earth, and many have not yet been found.
[bio12-s3.3.4] The gaps do not contradict evolution, and every new fossil found has fitted the expected order.
[bio12-s3.4.b] Comparing the bodies of different species shows similarities that are hard to explain unless the species share ancestors. Structures with the same basic plan but different jobs are called homologous.
[bio12-s3.4.0] Homologous structures have the same basic structure because they were inherited from a common ancestor, but are adapted for different functions.
[bio12-s3.4.1] The pentadactyl (five-digit) limb is the classic example: the human arm (grasping), bat wing (flying), whale flipper (swimming) and horse leg (running).
[bio12-s3.4.2] All of them have one upper bone (humerus), two lower bones (radius and ulna), wrist bones (carpals), palm bones (metacarpals) and finger bones (phalanges), differently shaped and sized.
[bio12-s3.4.3] This is divergent evolution: one ancestral form adapted in different directions, so the same bones were modified for different uses.
[bio12-s3.4.4] Diagram: the forelimbs of a human, a bat and a whale side by side, each bone group shaded the same way in all three; label humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges.
[bio12-s3.5.b] Not every similarity points to a shared ancestor, and some structures have lost their original job. Analogous and vestigial structures show two different things.
[bio12-s3.5.0] Analogous structures have the same function but different structures and origins, for example the wing of a bird (bones and feathers) and the wing of an insect (a thin membrane of chitin with no bones).
[bio12-s3.5.1] Analogous structures are not evidence of common ancestry. They show that similar environments select similar solutions, which is called convergent evolution.
[bio12-s3.5.2] Vestigial structures are reduced, with little or no function, but were functional in the organism's ancestors.
[bio12-s3.5.3] Examples are the human coccyx (the remains of a tail), the human appendix (a much-reduced part of the gut, larger in plant-eating mammals), the small pelvic and leg bones inside some whales, and the tiny wings of flightless birds such as the kiwi.
[bio12-s3.5.4] Vestigial structures make sense only if the organism descended from ancestors that used them.
[bio12-s3.6.b] Embryos (animals in the earliest stages of development) can reveal ancestry that adult bodies hide. Vertebrates, the animals with backbones, are a good example.
[bio12-s3.6.0] Comparative embryology compares the development of embryos. The early embryos of all vertebrates (fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals) look very similar: all have a tail and a series of pouches in the throat region (pharyngeal pouches).
[bio12-s3.6.1] In fish these pouches develop into gills; in humans they develop into parts of the ear, jaw and throat, and the tail is reduced to the coccyx.
[bio12-s3.6.2] The shared early stages suggest that all vertebrates inherited the same basic plan of development from a common ancestor, which was then modified in each group.
[bio12-s3.6.3] Limitations: the similarity is in early development, not in adult structures, and embryos do not pass through the adult forms of their ancestors.
[bio12-s3.6.4] In the 19th century Ernst Haeckel published drawings that exaggerated the similarity of embryos; modern photographs confirm the shared features but not the exaggeration.
[bio12-s3.6.5] Diagram: early embryos of a fish, a chicken and a human side by side, labelled tail and pharyngeal pouches.
[bio12-s3.7.b] Evolution predicts that the more closely two species are related, the more similar their DNA and proteins should be. Comparing these molecules tests that prediction independently of anatomy.
[bio12-s3.7.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group; phylogenetic trees show which species share the most recent common ancestors. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s3.7.1] Species closely related by anatomy and fossils should also have the most similar DNA and proteins, because they have had less time to accumulate differences since their common ancestor.
[bio12-s3.7.2] All living things use DNA, the same four bases, almost the same genetic code and the same ATP (the energy-carrying molecule of cells). This is evidence that all life shares one origin.
[bio12-s3.7.3] Human and chimpanzee DNA is about 98–99% identical in comparable sequences; human DNA is less similar to that of a mouse, and less again to that of a fish.
[bio12-s3.7.4] Cytochrome c is a protein used in aerobic respiration in nearly all eukaryotes (organisms whose cells have a nucleus). Its amino-acid sequence is identical in humans and chimpanzees.
[bio12-s3.7.5] It differs by one amino acid between humans and rhesus monkeys, and differs more in more distantly related organisms such as yeast.
[bio12-s3.7.6] The trees built from DNA and proteins largely agree with those built from anatomy and fossils. Independent lines of evidence giving the same pattern are strong support for evolution.
[bio12-s3.7.7] Molecular evidence has also corrected some classifications based on appearance, for example the domains of life (the three largest groupings of living things).
[bio12-s3.8.b] Bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics, and the way this happens is natural selection taking place in front of us. Once you can describe the steps, you can explain it in an exam.
[bio12-s3.8.0] An antibiotic is a drug that kills or stops the growth of bacteria; antibiotics have no effect on viruses. Antibiotic resistance is the ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them.
[bio12-s3.8.1] 1. A bacterial population varies: a few bacteria carry a mutation (or a resistance gene on a plasmid, a small ring of DNA) that makes them resistant, for example by producing an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic.
[bio12-s3.8.2] 2. The antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria.
[bio12-s3.8.3] 3. The resistant bacteria survive and, with less competition, reproduce rapidly by binary fission (splitting in two), as often as every 20 minutes in ideal conditions.
[bio12-s3.8.4] 4. They pass the resistance gene to their offspring, and can pass plasmids carrying it to other bacteria, even of other species.
[bio12-s3.8.5] 5. The population soon becomes mostly resistant, and the antibiotic no longer works.
[bio12-s3.8.6] Diagram: four panels of a bacterial population: mostly non-resistant cells with a few resistant ones (shaded), then antibiotic added and non-resistant cells die, then resistant cells multiply, then the population is mostly resistant.
[bio12-s3.9.b] A common mistake is to think antibiotics create resistance. The evidence shows that resistance is already there, and that selection simply favours it.
[bio12-s3.9.0] The resistance mutations are present before the antibiotic is used: the antibiotic selects them but does not cause them.
[bio12-s3.9.1] In 1952 Joshua and Esther Lederberg showed this by growing bacteria that had never met the antibiotic and finding resistant colonies already among them.
[bio12-s3.9.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many common antibiotics and causes infections that are hard to treat, especially in hospitals.
[bio12-s3.9.3] Natural selection is observed directly, within years, because bacteria have huge populations and very short generation times.
[bio12-s3.9.4] Overuse of antibiotics, for example prescribing them for viral infections or in farm animals, speeds up resistance by applying selection more often. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio12-s3.10.b] Natural selection can be seen in wild populations too, and the way species are spread across the world supports common descent.
[bio12-s3.10.0] Peter and Rosemary Grant studied the medium ground finch on the Galápagos island of Daphne Major. In a drought in 1977, small soft seeds became scarce and mainly large, hard seeds were left.
[bio12-s3.10.1] Most finches died, but birds with deeper (larger) beaks, which could crack the hard seeds, survived more, and their offspring had, on average, deeper beaks than the generation before.
[bio12-s3.10.2] Natural selection was measured happening in one generation.
[bio12-s3.10.3] Biogeography (the distribution of species): species on oceanic islands resemble those on the nearest mainland rather than those on islands with similar conditions elsewhere. The Galápagos species resemble South American species.
[bio12-s3.10.4] This is explained by colonisation from the mainland followed by evolution, not by separate creation for each environment.
[bio12-s3.11.b] Speciation is the formation of new species, and it is evidence that evolution can produce the diversity of life. One route to speciation is when a population is split by a physical barrier.
[bio12-s3.11.0] A species is a group that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so a new species has formed when two populations can no longer interbreed: they are reproductively isolated. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio12-s3.11.1] 1. A population is split by a barrier, such as a sea, a mountain range or a river, so the two groups no longer interbreed.
[bio12-s3.11.2] 2. Different mutations arise in each group.
[bio12-s3.11.3] 3. The environments differ, so natural selection favours different characteristics in each.
[bio12-s3.11.4] 4. Over many generations the two populations become so different that they could not interbreed even if they met; they are now separate species.
[bio12-s3.11.5] Diagram: a flow diagram of geographical speciation: one population, then a barrier divides it, then different mutations and selection on each side, then two species that can't interbreed.
[bio12-s3.12.b] The finches of the Galápagos show geographical speciation in a real place. Different islands acted as separate environments.
[bio12-s3.12.0] More than a dozen species of finch on the Galápagos are descended from a common ancestor that arrived from the South American mainland.
[bio12-s3.12.1] Populations on different islands, eating different foods, evolved beaks of different shapes, for example large, deep beaks for cracking seeds and thin, pointed beaks for catching insects.
[bio12-s3.12.2] Why it is evidence: new species have been seen to form, and the pattern of species (groups of similar species on neighbouring islands) is what the theory predicts.
[bio12-s3.13.b] A barrier is not always needed. In plants, a change in the number of chromosomes can make a new species almost at once.
[bio12-s3.13.0] In plants, a new species can form in one or a few generations when a change in chromosome number (polyploidy, having extra sets of chromosomes) makes offspring unable to breed with the parent species. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s3.13.1] The cordgrass Spartina anglica arose in southern England in the late 1800s from a cross between a native European cordgrass and an introduced American one.
[bio12-s3.13.2] The chromosomes then doubled, which made the hybrid fertile. It is now a separate, widespread species, including on Irish coasts.
[bio12-s3.14.b] Each source of evidence has weak points, but together they make a strong case because they are independent of each other.
[bio12-s3.14.0] Each source has limitations: an incomplete fossil record, embryos similar only early on, and analogous structures that can mislead.
[bio12-s3.14.1] The sources are independent of each other and all point to the same conclusion: common descent with change over time, driven largely by natural selection.
[bio12-s3.14.2] Antibiotic resistance, the finch beaks, the peppered moth and new species such as Spartina anglica show evolution happening now. There is more on the peppered moth in The theory of evolution by natural selection.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio23-s5: Pregnancy
[bio23-s5.0.b] Pregnancy starts with a single fertilised cell and, within about a week, a ball of cells that must attach itself to the wall of the uterus. The journey and the attachment (implantation) happen in a set order and in set places.
[bio23-s5.0.0] Fertilisation takes place in the fallopian tube (the tube that carries the egg from the ovary towards the uterus) and gives a diploid zygote (a cell with a full set of chromosomes), the first cell of the new individual. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s5.0.1] The zygote divides repeatedly by mitosis (division that makes identical cells) as cilia (tiny moving hairs lining the tube) and muscle contractions of the tube wall carry it along towards the uterus.
[bio23-s5.0.2] The cells do not grow between divisions, so they become smaller with each division and the ball stays about the size of the egg.
[bio23-s5.0.3] After about five days the ball reaches the uterus, the muscular organ in which the baby will develop, as a small ball of cells.
[bio23-s5.0.4] Implantation: about 6–7 days after fertilisation the ball of cells sinks into the thick, spongy endometrium (the lining of the uterus) and embeds itself there, with enzymes from its outer cells digesting a way in. From implantation the woman is pregnant.
[bio23-s5.1.b] Once the ball of cells is embedded, the endometrium has to stay in place, or it would be shed in a period and the pregnancy would be lost. A hormone from the embryo makes sure it stays.
[bio23-s5.1.0] Implantation needs an endometrium kept thick by progesterone, a hormone made by the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after the egg is released).
[bio23-s5.1.1] After implantation, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so the endometrium is kept and menstruation (the monthly period) stops.
[bio23-s5.1.2] A missed period is often the first sign of pregnancy, and pregnancy tests work by detecting this hormone, hCG, in urine. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s5.1.3] If the ball of cells implants in the fallopian tube instead of the uterus, it is an ectopic pregnancy.
[bio23-s5.2.b] Soon after implantation the embryo needs a way to get food and oxygen from its mother and to get rid of its wastes. The placenta is the organ that does this, and it is built jointly by the embryo and the mother.
[bio23-s5.2.0] After implantation, the outer cells of the embryo grow many finger-like projections (villi) into the endometrium.
[bio23-s5.2.1] The villi, together with the mother's endometrium and the blood spaces around them, form the placenta, a disc-shaped organ attached to the wall of the uterus.
[bio23-s5.2.2] The placenta is therefore made by both the embryo (the villi) and the mother (the endometrium).
[bio23-s5.2.3] The placenta is fully formed by about the end of the third month (12 weeks) and keeps growing until birth.
[bio23-s5.3.b] The foetus (the developing baby) floats in the uterus, away from the placenta. The umbilical cord is the link between them, and it carries blood in two directions.
[bio23-s5.3.0] The umbilical cord connects the foetus to the placenta.
[bio23-s5.3.1] The cord contains two umbilical arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) with wastes from the foetus to the placenta.
[bio23-s5.3.2] The cord contains one umbilical vein, which carries oxygenated blood with nutrients from the placenta to the foetus.
[bio23-s5.3.3] The vein carries oxygenated blood, the reverse of the adult pattern for most arteries and veins, because arteries are defined by carrying blood away from the heart and veins by carrying it towards the heart. There is more on this in Circulation.
[bio23-s5.4.b] Mother and foetus swap materials at the placenta, but their blood never meets. Everything that is exchanged has to cross a very thin barrier, and the placenta's structure is built to make that crossing quick.
[bio23-s5.4.0] The mother's and the foetus's blood do not mix. They are separated by a thin barrier of the villi, only a few cells thick.
[bio23-s5.4.1] This barrier protects the foetus from the mother's high blood pressure and from her immune cells, and it matters because their blood groups may differ. There is more on this in Blood.
[bio23-s5.4.2] The villi give a very large surface area, and the barrier is very thin, so the distance for diffusion is short.
[bio23-s5.4.3] The foetus's capillaries (tiny blood vessels) in the villi lie close to pools of the mother's blood, and blood flows on both sides, which keeps the concentration gradients high so diffusion carries on.
[bio23-s5.4.4] Substances cross by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), and some, such as glucose and amino acids, cross by facilitated diffusion or active transport.
[bio23-s5.5.b] Before birth the foetus's own lungs, gut and kidneys cannot do their jobs, so the placenta does them. It also protects and controls the pregnancy.
[bio23-s5.5.0] It supplies oxygen, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins, minerals and water to the foetus, so it acts as the foetus's lungs and gut.
[bio23-s5.5.1] It removes carbon dioxide and urea, which pass into the mother's blood to be excreted by her lungs and kidneys, so it acts as the foetus's kidneys.
[bio23-s5.5.2] It passes antibodies from mother to foetus, giving passive natural immunity (protection received ready-made, not made by the baby's own body) for the first months after birth. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s5.5.3] It secretes hormones, taking over the production of progesterone and oestrogen from the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s5.5.4] It forms a barrier to many pathogens (disease-causing organisms) and to the mother's blood cells.
[bio23-s5.6.b] The placenta is a barrier to many things, but not to everything. Some harmful substances in the mother's blood can cross to the foetus, which is why what a mother drinks, smokes, takes or catches matters.
[bio23-s5.6.0] Alcohol crosses and can cause foetal alcohol spectrum disorder, with damage to the brain and growth.
[bio23-s5.6.1] Nicotine and carbon monoxide from smoking reduce the oxygen supply to the foetus, and are linked to low birth weight and premature birth.
[bio23-s5.6.2] Many drugs, legal and illegal, cross the placenta; the thalidomide case is a well-known example. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio23-s5.6.3] Some viruses cross the placenta. Rubella (German measles) can cause deafness, blindness and heart defects if caught in early pregnancy, and HIV can also cross.
[bio23-s5.6.4] This is why antenatal advice (advice given to women during pregnancy) matters.
[bio23-s5.7.b] The foetus also needs protection from knocks and from drying out or overheating. A fluid-filled sac around it provides that.
[bio23-s5.7.0] The amnion is a thin membrane that forms a fluid-filled sac, the amniotic sac, around the embryo.
[bio23-s5.7.1] Amniotic fluid cushions the foetus against shocks, keeps its temperature steady, lets it move and so develop its muscles and bones, and prevents it sticking to the membranes.
[bio23-s5.7.2] Foetal cells in the fluid can be sampled for testing by amniocentesis. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s5.8.b] A labelled drawing of the foetus in the uterus pulls together the placenta, cord and amnion in one picture.
[bio23-s5.8.0] Diagram: a foetus in the uterus, showing the uterus wall and endometrium, the placenta attached to the uterus wall, the umbilical cord with its two arteries and one vein, the amnion and amniotic fluid, and the cervix with its mucus plug. Label each.
[bio23-s5.9.b] Over about nine months one cell becomes a baby with every tissue and organ. This follows a timetable, and the early weeks are the most delicate.
[bio23-s5.9.0] The baby develops by repeated mitosis (increasing cell number), cell growth, and cell differentiation (cells becoming specialised for different jobs), so that one zygote gives rise to all the tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.9.1] Length of pregnancy (gestation): about 38 weeks from fertilisation, which in practice is counted as about 40 weeks from the first day of the woman's last period, because the date of fertilisation is not known.
[bio23-s5.9.2] The pregnancy is divided into three trimesters of about three months each.
[bio23-s5.9.3] In the first two months all the main organs form, and the heart begins beating by the end of the first month.
[bio23-s5.9.4] By about 12 weeks the baby is recognisably human with all organs present, though tiny.
[bio23-s5.9.5] During the rest of pregnancy the organs grow and mature, the baby moves (felt by the mother from about 16–20 weeks), and fat is laid down under the skin in the last weeks.
[bio23-s5.9.6] The lungs are among the last organs to mature, which is why babies born very early have difficulty breathing. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s5.9.7] The first two months are when the baby is most at risk from harmful substances, because that is when the organs are forming; damage then can cause structural birth defects.
[bio23-s5.10.b] The ball of cells on its way to the uterus passes through two named stages. The names describe what the ball looks like at each point.
[bio23-s5.10.0] Zygote: the single diploid cell formed at fertilisation.
[bio23-s5.10.1] Morula: after about 3–4 days of division the zygote has become a solid ball of about 16 or more cells, still inside the zona pellucida (the protective coat around the egg), looking like a mulberry, which is the meaning of "morula".
[bio23-s5.10.2] The morula is still in the fallopian tube or just entering the uterus.
[bio23-s5.10.3] Blastocyst: by about day 5 the cells have rearranged into a hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity.
[bio23-s5.10.4] The blastocyst has two parts: an outer layer of cells, the trophoblast, which will form the embryo's part of the placenta and its membranes; and a small cluster inside, the inner cell mass, which will form the embryo itself.
[bio23-s5.10.5] The blastocyst hatches out of the zona pellucida and is the stage that implants, trophoblast first.
[bio23-s5.10.6] The cells of the inner cell mass can develop into any type of body cell, which is why they are embryonic stem cells. Their use and the ethical questions they raise are in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s5.10.7] Diagram: the sequence zygote → 2 cells → 4 cells → morula → blastocyst; the blastocyst in section (trophoblast, inner cell mass, fluid-filled cavity); and a side view of tube and uterus marking fertilisation, morula (both in the tube) and blastocyst implanting in the endometrium.
[bio23-s5.11.b] Soon after implantation the cells of the embryo sort themselves into three layers. Each layer goes on to form a particular set of tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.11.0] Germ layers: during the third week after fertilisation the cells of the inner cell mass move and arrange themselves into three layers, the germ layers, each of which gives rise to particular tissues and organs.
[bio23-s5.11.1] Ectoderm (outer layer) forms the epidermis of the skin, hair and nails, the whole nervous system (brain, spinal cord and nerves), and parts of the eye.
[bio23-s5.11.2] Mesoderm (middle layer) forms muscles, bones and cartilage, the heart, blood and blood vessels, the kidneys and the reproductive organs.
[bio23-s5.11.3] Endoderm (inner layer) forms the lining of the digestive system and of the breathing system (trachea, bronchi and lungs), and the liver and pancreas.
[bio23-s5.11.4] Memory aid: ecto = outer skin and nerves; meso = movement, blood and support; endo = inner linings.
[bio23-s5.12.b] From implantation to birth the developing baby has two names. The boundary between them is when the organs have finished forming.
[bio23-s5.12.0] Embryo: the developing individual from implantation, about the second week after fertilisation, until the end of the eighth week, during which all the main organs form (organ formation).
[bio23-s5.12.1] By the end of week 8 the embryo is about 3 cm long and has all its major organs.
[bio23-s5.12.2] Foetus: from the ninth week until birth. No new organs form, but the existing ones grow, mature and begin to work.
[bio23-s5.12.3] The distinction: embryo means organs forming; foetus means organs present and growing.
[bio23-s5.12.4] Summary sequence: zygote (day 0, fallopian tube) → morula (days 3–4) → blastocyst (about day 5; implants about days 6–7) → germ layers form (week 3) → embryo (to the end of week 8; organs form) → foetus (week 9 to birth; growth and maturation).
[bio23-s5.13.b] Labour is the end of pregnancy. It starts when the uterus, which has held the baby for about nine months, begins to push it out.
[bio23-s5.13.0] Labour is the process by which the baby, then the placenta, is pushed out of the uterus.
[bio23-s5.13.1] Labour usually begins at about 40 weeks of pregnancy, when the baby is normally head down.
[bio23-s5.13.2] Hormonal changes start it, and the hormone oxytocin drives the contractions (tightening of the uterus muscle) by positive feedback (a change that leads to more of the same change). There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s5.14.b] The first stage prepares the way out. The cervix (the narrow neck at the bottom of the uterus), which stayed closed during pregnancy, has to open wide enough for the baby's head.
[bio23-s5.14.0] The muscle of the uterus wall begins to contract regularly, and the contractions become stronger and more frequent.
[bio23-s5.14.1] The contractions pull the cervix open until it is fully dilated, about 10 cm wide.
[bio23-s5.14.2] The mucus plug (the plug of mucus that sealed the cervix) comes away, and the amnion usually bursts, releasing the amniotic fluid; this is what is meant by "the waters break".
[bio23-s5.14.3] Stage 1 is the longest stage, often many hours in a first birth.
[bio23-s5.15.b] In the second stage the baby is pushed out through the birth canal. As soon as it is born it must switch from relying on the placenta to doing things for itself.
[bio23-s5.15.0] Strong contractions of the uterus, helped by the mother pushing with her abdominal muscles, push the baby, usually head first, out through the cervix and the vagina, the birth canal. This stage is also called expulsion.
[bio23-s5.15.1] The umbilical cord is then clamped and cut; the stump later dries and falls off, leaving the navel.
[bio23-s5.15.2] At birth the baby must start breathing for itself: the first breaths inflate the lungs, and its circulation changes so that blood goes to the lungs for oxygen instead of relying on the placenta.
[bio23-s5.16.b] Labour is not over when the baby is born: the placenta still has to come out. Sometimes a vaginal birth is not safe, and the baby is delivered by surgery instead.
[bio23-s5.16.0] Shortly after the birth, further contractions separate the placenta and membranes from the uterus wall and push them out; this is the afterbirth.
[bio23-s5.16.1] Contraction of the uterus also closes the blood vessels where the placenta was attached, which limits bleeding.
[bio23-s5.16.2] A Caesarean section is the delivery of the baby through a surgical cut in the abdomen and uterus, used when a vaginal birth would be unsafe for the mother or baby, for example a baby lying the wrong way, or a placenta blocking the cervix.

CANDIDATES bio23-s6: Hormones in reproduction
[bio23-s6.0.b] Reproduction is run by chemical messengers (hormones) carried in the blood. Three places make nearly all of them, and in pregnancy a fourth organ joins in.
[bio23-s6.0.0] Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain, the testes (the male gonads, which make sperm) and the ovaries (the female gonads, which make eggs).
[bio23-s6.0.1] The pituitary gland makes oxytocin and prolactin, and also the hormones that stimulate the gonads, which is how the brain controls the testes and ovaries.
[bio23-s6.0.2] The testes make testosterone, and the ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone. In pregnancy the placenta (the organ that links mother and foetus) also makes oestrogen and progesterone.
[bio23-s6.0.3] Prolactin is made by the pituitary gland, and its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts. There is more on where each gland sits in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.b] Puberty is the changeover from child to adult body. A hormone signal from the brain switches the reproductive system on.
[bio23-s6.1.0] Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes (sperm or eggs), and the secondary sexual characteristics develop. These are the body changes, apart from the reproductive organs themselves, that mark a person as male or female.
[bio23-s6.1.1] Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls, and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
[bio23-s6.1.2] It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads (the testes and ovaries). There is more on these pituitary hormones in the Higher Level block on the gonad-stimulating hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.3] As a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone, and the ovaries much more oestrogen. It is these sex hormones that cause the changes of puberty.
[bio23-s6.1.4] Testosterone at puberty starts sperm production, causes growth of the penis and testes, and causes the male secondary sexual characteristics. It also increases sex drive.
[bio23-s6.1.5] Oestrogen at puberty causes growth of the uterus (womb) and vagina, preparing the body for pregnancy, and the female secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.1.6] Oestrogen, working with progesterone, starts the menstrual cycles, so a girl becomes able to ovulate and conceive; the first period is called menarche.
[bio23-s6.2.b] Once puberty has started the gametes, hormones keep them coming. The pattern differs between the sexes: continuous in men, monthly in women.
[bio23-s6.2.0] Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes where sperm are made), and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.2.1] Testosterone is made by the interstitial cells between the tubules, not by the tubules themselves, which make sperm.
[bio23-s6.2.2] Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously rather than in a cycle, which is why men stay fertile from day to day.
[bio23-s6.2.3] Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle (a fluid-filled sac holding a developing egg) to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus). There is more on the hormone levels and feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.3.b] After fertilisation the embryo has to bury itself in the uterus lining (implantation) and the lining must then be kept, not shed. Two hormones make this possible.
[bio23-s6.3.0] Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms.
[bio23-s6.3.1] Without high progesterone, implantation cannot succeed, because the embryo would have no suitable lining to attach to or feed from.
[bio23-s6.3.2] hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation.
[bio23-s6.3.3] hCG keeps the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after ovulation) alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine, using a monoclonal-antibody test.
[bio23-s6.4.b] The corpus luteum cannot support a whole pregnancy, so the placenta takes over hormone production. Progesterone from it then keeps the pregnancy going in several ways.
[bio23-s6.4.0] From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
[bio23-s6.4.1] Progesterone during pregnancy maintains the endometrium and so the placenta, which is attached to it.
[bio23-s6.4.2] Progesterone keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it does not contract and expel the foetus early.
[bio23-s6.4.3] Progesterone keeps the cervical mucus plug (the mucus that seals the cervix, the neck of the uterus) thick, and prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts.
[bio23-s6.4.4] Progesterone, with oestrogen, prevents further ovulation and menstruation during pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.5.b] Oestrogen supports the growth of the foetus by building up the mother's tissues, and late in pregnancy it prepares her body for birth.
[bio23-s6.5.0] Oestrogen during pregnancy drives the growth of the foetus: it stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, and increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta, which supplies the foetus.
[bio23-s6.5.1] Oestrogen also develops the milk ducts of the breasts, so they are ready to carry milk after birth.
[bio23-s6.5.2] Late in pregnancy oestrogen makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
[bio23-s6.5.3] Level summary: progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.
[bio23-s6.6.b] Birth needs the powerful uterus muscle to contract strongly. A shift in hormone balance and the hormone oxytocin make this happen.
[bio23-s6.6.0] Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
[bio23-s6.6.1] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus (a part of the brain just above the pituitary) and released by the pituitary into the blood.
[bio23-s6.6.2] Oxytocin causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour, the process of giving birth.
[bio23-s6.7.b] Labour speeds itself up. Each contraction causes more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, a pattern called positive feedback, where a change leads to more of the same change.
[bio23-s6.7.0] 1. The baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix.
[bio23-s6.7.1] 2. Stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain.
[bio23-s6.7.2] 3. The pituitary releases more oxytocin.
[bio23-s6.7.3] 4. Stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more.
[bio23-s6.7.4] 5. The cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus (the stretching), so the loop stops.
[bio23-s6.7.5] A nervous pathway (the impulses) and a hormonal pathway (oxytocin in the blood) work together here. There is more on this in Nervous vs hormonal coordination.
[bio23-s6.7.6] Diagram: the positive feedback loop as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → back to the start; birth ends the loop.
[bio23-s6.8.b] Oxytocin's work does not stop when the baby arrives. It also helps the uterus recover, and doctors use a man-made version of it.
[bio23-s6.8.0] After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding.
[bio23-s6.8.1] Oxytocin also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size, because its contractions squeeze the muscle smaller.
[bio23-s6.8.2] Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour, and to reduce bleeding after birth.
[bio23-s6.9.b] The breasts are prepared during pregnancy, but milk only starts once the placenta has gone. The hormone that actually makes milk is prolactin.
[bio23-s6.9.0] During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises.
[bio23-s6.9.1] The high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced, even though prolactin is already rising.
[bio23-s6.9.2] When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
[bio23-s6.9.3] Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk, which is why it is called the milk-making hormone.
[bio23-s6.9.4] Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made: supply matches demand.
[bio23-s6.9.5] While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often do not return for some months; this is not reliable contraception. There is more on this in Fertility control and infertility.
[bio23-s6.10.b] Making milk is not the same as delivering it. Oxytocin does the second job, so a feed needs both hormones.
[bio23-s6.10.0] Oxytocin causes the milk to be released (called let-down, or milk ejection).
[bio23-s6.10.1] Suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple.
[bio23-s6.10.2] Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
[bio23-s6.11.b] The first milk a baby gets is different from the milk that follows. It is tuned to protect a newborn rather than to fatten it.
[bio23-s6.11.0] Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth.
[bio23-s6.11.1] Colostrum is a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar.
[bio23-s6.11.2] Its antibodies give the newborn passive immunity, which means protection made by the mother rather than by the baby's own immune system. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s6.11.3] Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose (milk sugar) for energy and growth.
[bio23-s6.12.b] A woman's fertility does not last for life. Menopause is the point where the ovaries stop responding, and the fall in hormones has effects across the body.
[bio23-s6.12.0] Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months.
[bio23-s6.12.1] It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
[bio23-s6.12.2] Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone. It is not simply that the eggs are used up by ovulation, because most follicles degenerate without ever ovulating.
[bio23-s6.12.3] Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, so there is nothing to shed and periods stop.
[bio23-s6.12.4] Effects of falling oestrogen in the short term: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, and vaginal dryness.
[bio23-s6.12.5] Effects of falling oestrogen in the longer term: loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease.
[bio23-s6.12.6] Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.
[bio23-s6.12.7] There is more on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.13.b] One bullet per hormone: its gland, its roles and how its level changes through life.
[bio23-s6.13.0] Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth, puberty, sperm production and sex drive. There is more on development before birth in Sex determination.
[bio23-s6.13.1] Testosterone level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, and declines slowly in later life.
[bio23-s6.13.2] Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, and keeping bones strong.
[bio23-s6.13.3] Oestrogen level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, and falls at menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.4] Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares the milk glands and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.13.5] Progesterone level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.6] Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth, and milk let-down. Its level rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
[bio23-s6.13.7] Prolactin (pituitary): milk production. Its level rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed, and falls when breastfeeding stops.
[bio23-s6.14.b] FSH and LH are the two pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads. In males they hold testosterone steady through negative feedback, and in females they drive the monthly cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.0] At puberty the hypothalamus, the part of the brain above the pituitary, signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.14.1] In males these hormones stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
[bio23-s6.14.2] In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, working with testosterone, so without FSH sperm output falls.
[bio23-s6.14.3] In males, LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
[bio23-s6.14.4] Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH and FSH from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again.
[bio23-s6.14.5] This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.6] Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone) have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.14.7] In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s6.15.b] FSH and LH levels follow the state of the ovaries and the other hormones. When oestrogen and progesterone are high they stay low, and when these hormones are lost they rise.
[bio23-s6.15.0] In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens.
[bio23-s6.15.1] While breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.15.2] After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
[bio23-s6.15.3] Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood and rise at puberty.
[bio23-s6.15.4] In men FSH and LH are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle, with an LH surge at ovulation.
[bio23-s6.15.5] In women FSH and LH are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and high after the menopause. The levels and feedback through the month are in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.16.b] A model shows how a control centre, a hormone, a target organ and feedback fit together. You can then use it to predict what happens when one part changes.
[bio23-s6.16.0] A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback, with − for inhibition and + for stimulation.
[bio23-s6.16.1] Use the model to predict effects: blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone, as in the pill, keeps FSH and LH low.
[bio23-s6.16.2] Diagram: two flow charts side by side, male then female. Male: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → testes → sperm and testosterone, with a “−” arrow from testosterone back to the pituitary.
[bio23-s6.16.3] Female flow chart: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with “−” arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a “+” arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge.

CANDIDATES bio23-s8: Fertility control and infertility
[bio23-s8.0.b] Fertility is the ability to produce offspring, but it is not available all the time in everyone. Males and females differ because sperm production runs continuously while egg release happens once per cycle.
[bio23-s8.0.0] A fertile period is the time during which intercourse can lead to pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.0.1] Males are continuously fertile: from puberty, sperm are made every day for the rest of life, declining slowly in old age, and every ejaculation (release of semen) contains millions of sperm. This is why a man is fertile on any day.
[bio23-s8.0.2] Females are cyclically fertile: from puberty to menopause, usually one egg is released per cycle (ovulation), so fertility comes in a short window once each cycle rather than every day.
[bio23-s8.0.3] The egg survives only about 12–24 hours after release, while sperm can survive up to about five days in the female body.
[bio23-s8.0.4] So the fertile window in each cycle is about six days: the five days before ovulation, when sperm already present can wait for the egg, and the day of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.0.5] In a regular 28-day cycle this is roughly days 9 to 14, but because the day of ovulation varies from woman to woman and cycle to cycle, the window cannot be predicted exactly from the calendar alone.
[bio23-s8.1.b] A woman's fertility is not the same at every age. It follows the state of her eggs, which she is born with and which do not renew.
[bio23-s8.1.0] Female fertility is highest in the twenties and falls with age, more steeply after about 35, because the number and quality of the remaining eggs decline.
[bio23-s8.1.1] Female fertility ends at the menopause, the time of life when ovulation and periods stop for good. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s8.2.b] Contraception means stopping a pregnancy from starting. Every method blocks one step on the route from egg release to implantation, and methods differ in how reliable they are and in what else they protect against.
[bio23-s8.2.0] Contraception is the deliberate prevention of pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.2.1] Methods work in one of three ways: by preventing ovulation, by preventing sperm from reaching the egg (preventing fertilisation), or by preventing implantation, the embedding of the embryo in the lining of the uterus.
[bio23-s8.2.2] Methods are grouped as natural, mechanical (barrier), surgical and chemical (hormonal and spermicidal).
[bio23-s8.2.3] Effectiveness with perfect use: the male condom is about 98% effective; the pill, the implant, the coils and sterilisation are each more than 99% effective.
[bio23-s8.2.4] With typical, real-life use, methods that depend on the user remembering or using them correctly every time, namely natural methods, condoms and the pill, fail more often, because people forget or make mistakes.
[bio23-s8.2.5] Long-acting methods, namely the implant, coils and sterilisation, stay above 99% effective with typical use, because there is nothing to remember.
[bio23-s8.2.6] Only condoms, male and female, protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs), because they stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.2.7] People using another method need condoms as well to reduce the risk of STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.b] Natural methods use no drugs or devices. They rely on behaviour: avoiding intercourse altogether, or avoiding it on the days when pregnancy is possible.
[bio23-s8.3.0] Abstinence, not having intercourse, is the only method that is completely effective against pregnancy and STIs.
[bio23-s8.3.1] Fertility awareness (natural family planning): the couple avoid intercourse during the fertile window, which they find by combining several signs.
[bio23-s8.3.2] Sign 1, the calendar (rhythm) method: recording cycle lengths over time to estimate when ovulation happens.
[bio23-s8.3.3] Sign 2, body temperature: measured each morning before getting up, it rises by a small amount, a few tenths of a degree Celsius, just after ovulation and stays up for the rest of the cycle, because of the hormone progesterone.
[bio23-s8.3.4] Sign 3, cervical mucus (the fluid made by the cervix, the neck of the uterus): it becomes thin, clear, stretchy and slippery around ovulation.
[bio23-s8.3.5] Home kits that detect the hormone surge before ovulation in urine can also be used, giving a warning that ovulation is near. There is more on this in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.4.b] Natural methods appeal to some people but are demanding to use and fail more often than other methods in real life. Knowing both sides lets you discuss them fairly.
[bio23-s8.4.0] Advantages of natural methods: no drugs, devices or side effects, and they are acceptable to people whose religious beliefs rule out artificial contraception, for example the teaching of the Catholic Church, which accepts only natural methods.
[bio23-s8.4.1] Limits of natural methods: they need training, daily records and regular cycles, and are much less reliable with typical use.
[bio23-s8.4.2] Natural methods are hard to use after childbirth, when breastfeeding or near the menopause, because cycles are then irregular. They also give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.4.3] Withdrawal of the penis before ejaculation is unreliable, because some sperm may be released before ejaculation.
[bio23-s8.4.4] Breastfeeding delays ovulation but is not reliable contraception, because ovulation can return without warning.
[bio23-s8.5.b] Barrier methods put a physical block between sperm and egg. Of the methods used during intercourse, only the condoms in this group protect against STIs, because they also stop body fluids passing between partners.
[bio23-s8.5.0] Male condom: a thin latex, or non-latex, sheath placed over the erect penis that collects the semen, so sperm cannot enter the vagina.
[bio23-s8.5.1] The male condom also blocks the exchange of body fluids, so it reduces the spread of STIs, including HIV.
[bio23-s8.5.2] The male condom is cheap, widely available and has no hormones; it can tear or slip, and must be put on before any contact.
[bio23-s8.5.3] Female condom: a soft pouch placed in the vagina that lines it and collects semen; it also protects against STIs.
[bio23-s8.5.4] Diaphragm or cap: a dome of soft silicone placed over the cervix before intercourse, used with a spermicide (a sperm-killing chemical) and left in for several hours afterwards; it does not protect well against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.b] The copper coil is a device rather than a barrier, but it is grouped with mechanical methods because it is a physical object placed in the body. It works without hormones.
[bio23-s8.6.0] Copper coil (copper intrauterine device, IUD): a small T-shaped device with copper, placed in the uterus by a doctor or nurse.
[bio23-s8.6.1] Copper is toxic to sperm and eggs, preventing fertilisation, and it changes the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) so an embryo is unlikely to implant.
[bio23-s8.6.2] The copper coil lasts for 5–10 years, contains no hormones, and can be removed at any time with a quick return to fertility.
[bio23-s8.6.3] The copper coil can also be used as emergency contraception if fitted within five days.
[bio23-s8.6.4] The copper coil can make periods heavier, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.6.5] It is grouped as mechanical because it is a device; the hormonal coil is in the chemical methods.
[bio23-s8.7.b] Sterilisation is a permanent operation that blocks the tubes gametes travel along. In men this is vasectomy, which stops sperm joining the semen without changing anything else about the man.
[bio23-s8.7.0] Vasectomy (male sterilisation): under local anaesthetic, each vas deferens (the tube carrying sperm from the testis) is cut and tied or sealed, so sperm cannot leave the testes. There is more on this in The male reproductive system.
[bio23-s8.7.1] After a vasectomy the man still ejaculates semen, because the fluids come from glands that are untouched; his testosterone and sex drive are unaffected.
[bio23-s8.7.2] The sperm that are still made are broken down and reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.7.3] A vasectomy is not effective at once, because sperm remain in the ducts beyond the cut; semen tests are needed after some weeks to confirm there are no sperm.
[bio23-s8.8.b] Female sterilisation works on the same principle as vasectomy: the tubes are blocked so egg and sperm cannot meet. Unlike vasectomy, it works straight away.
[bio23-s8.8.0] Tubal ligation (female sterilisation): each fallopian tube, the tube that carries the egg from the ovary to the uterus, is cut, tied or blocked with a clip, usually by keyhole surgery under general anaesthetic, so the egg and sperm cannot meet.
[bio23-s8.8.1] After tubal ligation ovulation and the menstrual cycle continue as before, because the egg is reabsorbed.
[bio23-s8.8.2] Tubal ligation is effective at once.
[bio23-s8.9.b] Sterilisation suits some people very well and others badly. The key issue is that it is meant to be permanent.
[bio23-s8.9.0] Sterilisation is very effective and permanent, with nothing to remember, but it should be treated as irreversible: reversal is difficult and often fails.
[bio23-s8.9.1] Sterilisation carries the small risks of any surgery, and gives no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.9.2] It suits people who are sure they want no more children.
[bio23-s8.9.3] Diagram: outline of the male system with the cut in each vas deferens marked, and of the female system with the cut or clip in each fallopian tube marked; label the vas deferens, testis, fallopian tube, ovary, uterus and “cut and tied”.
[bio23-s8.10.b] Chemical methods use hormones or sperm-killing chemicals. The pill is the best known: it is taken by mouth every day and uses synthetic versions of the body's own reproductive hormones.
[bio23-s8.10.0] The combined pill contains synthetic oestrogen and progesterone and prevents ovulation; the detail of how is in Hormonal manipulation. It also thickens the cervical mucus so sperm cannot pass.
[bio23-s8.10.1] The combined pill is taken daily, usually with a break or dummy pills each month, during which a withdrawal bleed (a bleed like a period) happens.
[bio23-s8.10.2] The progestogen-only pill (synthetic progesterone only) works mainly by thickening the cervical mucus, and in some types also stops ovulation; it suits women who cannot take oestrogen.
[bio23-s8.11.b] Some hormonal methods release their hormone slowly over months or years, so the user has nothing to remember daily. This is why the implant and the coils, which are long-acting, stay above 99% effective in real life.
[bio23-s8.11.0] Long-acting hormonal methods release a synthetic progesterone continuously.
[bio23-s8.11.1] The implant is a small flexible rod placed under the skin of the upper arm, lasting about 3 years.
[bio23-s8.11.2] The injection is given every few months.
[bio23-s8.11.3] The hormonal coil (intrauterine system, IUS) is placed in the uterus and lasts several years; it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the endometrium.
[bio23-s8.11.4] The contraceptive patch and vaginal ring release oestrogen and progesterone, like the combined pill.
[bio23-s8.12.b] Some chemical methods are used after the event or alongside another method rather than as a regular choice.
[bio23-s8.12.0] Emergency hormonal pills, taken as soon as possible after unprotected intercourse, work mainly by delaying ovulation, so there is no egg for sperm to meet; one type works within 3 days and another within 5 days.
[bio23-s8.12.1] The copper coil fitted within 5 days is the most effective emergency method. Emergency pills are not for regular use.
[bio23-s8.12.2] Spermicides are chemicals in a gel or cream that kill sperm; they are not reliable alone and are used with a diaphragm or cap.
[bio23-s8.13.b] Hormonal methods are very effective and have extra health benefits, but they act on the whole body, so they also carry side effects and risks.
[bio23-s8.13.0] Hormonal methods are very effective; periods often become lighter, more regular and less painful.
[bio23-s8.13.1] The combined pill lowers the risk of cancers of the ovary and of the endometrium (womb).
[bio23-s8.13.2] Hormonal methods can cause side effects, for example headaches, mood changes and irregular bleeding with progesterone-only methods.
[bio23-s8.13.3] The combined pill slightly raises the risk of blood clots (deep vein thrombosis) and stroke, especially in smokers and women over 35, so it is not prescribed to everyone.
[bio23-s8.13.4] Hormonal methods give no protection against STIs.
[bio23-s8.13.5] The pill must be taken correctly every day; vomiting or some medicines can stop it working.
[bio23-s8.14.b] Some infections spread mainly through sexual contact, and some of them damage the reproductive system. This links contraception to infertility.
[bio23-s8.14.0] An STI (sexually transmitted infection) is an infection passed on mainly by sexual contact.
[bio23-s8.14.1] Bacterial STIs are chlamydia, gonorrhoea and syphilis, which are treated with antibiotics.
[bio23-s8.14.2] Viral STIs are HIV, genital herpes, human papillomavirus (HPV, which causes genital warts and most cervical cancers) and hepatitis B, which can be controlled but is not usually cured. There is more on pathogens in Pathogens.
[bio23-s8.14.3] Chlamydia and gonorrhoea often cause no symptoms, especially in women, but can spread to the uterus and fallopian tubes, causing pelvic inflammatory disease, which scars and blocks the tubes.
[bio23-s8.14.4] Blocked tubes from pelvic inflammatory disease are a major preventable cause of female infertility, because the egg and sperm can no longer meet, and they raise the risk of ectopic pregnancy (a pregnancy that starts outside the uterus).
[bio23-s8.14.5] In men chlamydia and gonorrhoea can inflame the epididymis, the coiled tube on the testis where sperm mature and are stored.
[bio23-s8.15.b] Because many STIs cause no symptoms and can damage fertility, prevention combines protection during sex, testing and treatment, and vaccination.
[bio23-s8.15.0] Prevention: correct use of condoms every time, and fewer sexual partners.
[bio23-s8.15.1] Prevention: regular testing and treatment of infected people and their partners breaks the chain of transmission, because many infected people have no symptoms and would otherwise pass the infection on without knowing.
[bio23-s8.15.2] Prevention: vaccination against HPV and hepatitis B. There is more on HPV in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio23-s8.15.3] For HIV, anti-HIV drugs taken before possible exposure (pre-exposure prophylaxis, PrEP) lower the risk of infection.
[bio23-s8.15.4] Treatment of people with HIV reduces their virus to levels at which it is not passed on sexually.
[bio23-s8.15.5] Gonorrhoea is becoming resistant to antibiotics (the drugs no longer kill it), which makes prevention more important.
[bio23-s8.16.b] Egg freezing (oocyte cryopreservation) lets a woman store her eggs while they are still young and healthy, to be used later. It uses a series of steps similar to the start of IVF.
[bio23-s8.16.0] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs at once, rather than the usual one.
[bio23-s8.16.1] 2. The eggs are collected with a fine needle through the vaginal wall, guided by ultrasound, under sedation.
[bio23-s8.16.2] 3. The eggs are frozen very rapidly (vitrification), so that ice crystals, which would damage the cells, do not form, and they are stored in liquid nitrogen at −196 °C.
[bio23-s8.16.3] 4. Later the eggs are thawed and fertilised by IVF (fertilisation in a dish) or by ICSI (one sperm injected into an egg).
[bio23-s8.17.b] Egg freezing preserves fertility but cannot promise a baby. Who uses it, and why it is not a guarantee, are the points to know.
[bio23-s8.17.0] Uses: before cancer treatment, because chemotherapy or radiotherapy can destroy the eggs in the ovaries, or by women who wish to delay having children.
[bio23-s8.17.1] Sperm, and embryos made in IVF, can also be frozen; sperm freezing is offered to men before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.17.2] Limits: success depends strongly on the woman's age when the eggs are frozen, since younger eggs have fewer chromosome errors and survive freezing better.
[bio23-s8.17.3] Freezing gives no guarantee of a baby, and not every egg survives thawing.
[bio23-s8.17.4] The process involves hormone treatment and a procedure, and it is expensive.
[bio23-s8.18.b] Infertility is common, and its causes can lie with the man, the woman, both, or neither. Finding the cause decides which treatment is suitable.
[bio23-s8.18.0] Infertility is the failure to become pregnant after 12 months of regular unprotected intercourse.
[bio23-s8.18.1] The World Health Organization estimated in 2023 that about 1 in 6 people worldwide experience infertility at some point in their lives.
[bio23-s8.18.2] Causes are found in the man, the woman, both, or neither, which is called unexplained infertility.
[bio23-s8.19.b] Male infertility usually comes down to too few sperm, sperm that cannot do their job, or sperm that cannot get out. Lifestyle can also reduce sperm production.
[bio23-s8.19.0] Low sperm count (too few sperm in the semen), and sperm that swim poorly or are abnormally shaped, for example with a faulty tail, reduce the chance that one reaches the egg.
[bio23-s8.19.1] A blockage of the epididymis or vas deferens, for example after infection or vasectomy, stops sperm leaving the testes.
[bio23-s8.19.2] Undescended testes (testes that have not moved down into the scrotum) and low testosterone or low pituitary hormones are also male causes.
[bio23-s8.19.3] Lifestyle and environmental factors reduce sperm production, such as smoking, heavy drinking, obesity, anabolic steroids and overheating of the testes. There is more on steroids in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s8.19.4] Male infertility is diagnosed by semen analysis, which tests sperm count, movement and shape under the microscope.
[bio23-s8.20.b] Female infertility arises when no egg is released, when egg and sperm cannot meet, or when the uterus cannot support an embryo.
[bio23-s8.20.0] Failure to ovulate, caused by a hormone imbalance, for example polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), or by being very underweight or overweight, means no egg is released.
[bio23-s8.20.1] Blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, for example after pelvic inflammatory disease from an STI, stop egg and sperm meeting.
[bio23-s8.20.2] Endometriosis is a condition in which tissue like the endometrium grows outside the uterus and can cause scarring around the tubes and ovaries.
[bio23-s8.20.3] Female causes also include problems of the uterus or cervical mucus, and age, because egg number and quality fall.
[bio23-s8.20.4] Female infertility is diagnosed by blood hormone tests, ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus, and tests that show whether the tubes are open.
[bio23-s8.21.b] If the problem is that a woman does not release eggs regularly, the treatment is to make her ovaries release them. This is a first step before more complex techniques.
[bio23-s8.21.0] Stimulating ovulation is for women who do not ovulate regularly, for example because of PCOS.
[bio23-s8.21.1] Fertility drugs, as tablets or injections, cause the ovaries to mature and release eggs.
[bio23-s8.21.2] The woman is monitored by ultrasound to see how many follicles (the fluid-filled sacs in the ovary that each contain an egg) develop, and intercourse or insemination is timed to ovulation.
[bio23-s8.21.3] Risk: more than one egg may be released, so twins or triplets are more likely.
[bio23-s8.21.4] Risk: the ovaries can over-respond (ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome), becoming swollen and painful, which is occasionally serious.
[bio23-s8.21.5] Lifestyle change, for example weight loss, can restore ovulation in some women.
[bio23-s8.22.b] Fertility drugs work by acting on the same hormone system that controls the menstrual cycle, either by boosting the brain's signals to the ovaries or by supplying them directly.
[bio23-s8.22.0] Some fertility tablets block oestrogen's negative feedback on the brain and pituitary, so the pituitary releases more FSH and LH, which stimulate follicle development and ovulation. There is more on feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s8.22.1] Injected fertility drugs contain FSH, often with LH, and act directly on the ovaries to make follicles develop.
[bio23-s8.22.2] A final injection of a hormone that acts like LH triggers the eggs' final maturation and ovulation, imitating the LH surge.
[bio23-s8.22.3] The same injections are used to stimulate the ovaries in IVF and egg freezing.
[bio23-s8.23.b] Artificial insemination helps when the sperm have trouble reaching the egg. It puts sperm into the uterus by hand, so more of them start closer to the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.0] Artificial insemination, or intrauterine insemination (IUI): semen from the partner or a donor is collected, the sperm are washed and concentrated, and they are placed directly into the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube, at the time of ovulation.
[bio23-s8.23.1] This places many active sperm close to the fallopian tubes, where fertilisation happens.
[bio23-s8.23.2] It is used for a mildly low sperm count or poor motility (swimming), cervical mucus that blocks sperm, difficulty with intercourse, and single women or female couples using donor sperm.
[bio23-s8.23.3] The woman's fallopian tubes must be open, because the sperm still have to travel along them to meet the egg.
[bio23-s8.23.4] The same technique is widely used in farming, for example to breed cattle from selected bulls.
[bio23-s8.24.b] IVF takes fertilisation out of the body. Eggs and sperm are brought together in the laboratory, and the resulting embryo is then placed in the uterus.
[bio23-s8.24.0] IVF means “in glass”, that is, fertilisation outside the body.
[bio23-s8.24.1] 1. Hormone injections stimulate the ovaries to mature several eggs.
[bio23-s8.24.2] 2. The eggs are collected with a needle guided by ultrasound.
[bio23-s8.24.3] 3. The eggs are mixed with sperm in a dish in the laboratory, where fertilisation takes place.
[bio23-s8.24.4] 4. The embryos are grown in an incubator for 2–5 days, up to the blastocyst stage (a ball of cells ready to implant). There is more on this in Pregnancy.
[bio23-s8.24.5] 5. Usually one, sometimes two, embryos are placed in the uterus through the cervix with a thin tube.
[bio23-s8.24.6] 6. A pregnancy test about two weeks later shows whether an embryo has implanted.
[bio23-s8.24.7] Spare good-quality embryos can be frozen for later attempts.
[bio23-s8.25.b] IVF is used when simpler treatments will not work or are unsuitable. It was developed in the twentieth century and is now widely used.
[bio23-s8.25.0] IVF is used for blocked or damaged fallopian tubes, unexplained infertility, endometriosis, failure of other treatments, and couples needing preimplantation genetic testing (checking embryos for a genetic condition before transfer). There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio23-s8.25.1] History: the first baby conceived by IVF, Louise Brown, was born in England on 25 July 1978, after work by the scientist Robert Edwards and the gynaecologist Patrick Steptoe.
[bio23-s8.25.2] Edwards was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2010.
[bio23-s8.25.3] Millions of babies have since been born by IVF worldwide.
[bio23-s8.26.b] ICSI is a version of IVF designed for severe male infertility. Instead of leaving sperm to fertilise the egg in the dish, the laboratory places one sperm inside the egg.
[bio23-s8.26.0] ICSI is the same as IVF except that in step 3 a single sperm is chosen and injected directly into the cytoplasm of each egg with a very fine glass needle, under a microscope.
[bio23-s8.26.1] Because the sperm does not have to swim to or enter the egg itself, ICSI overcomes severe male infertility.
[bio23-s8.26.2] ICSI is used for very low sperm count, sperm that cannot swim or cannot penetrate the egg, and previous failure of fertilisation in IVF.
[bio23-s8.26.3] ICSI is also used when sperm are taken directly from the testis or epididymis by a needle, for example after a vasectomy or a blockage.
[bio23-s8.26.4] ICSI is also used with frozen eggs, which are thawed before the sperm is injected.
[bio23-s8.27.b] A flow chart shows IVF and ICSI together, because they share every step except how the egg is fertilised.
[bio23-s8.27.0] Diagram: a flow chart of IVF with ICSI as a branch: stimulate ovaries → collect eggs → (IVF: mix eggs and sperm in a dish | ICSI: inject one sperm into each egg) → grow embryos for 2–5 days → transfer an embryo to the uterus → pregnancy test.
[bio23-s8.28.b] Discussing contraception means weighing benefits against drawbacks. The benefits range from the individual to the whole planet.
[bio23-s8.28.0] Contraception lets people choose whether and when to have children and how many, and to space pregnancies, which improves the health of mothers and babies.
[bio23-s8.28.1] It prevents unintended pregnancy, including in teenagers.
[bio23-s8.28.2] Condoms reduce the spread of STIs, including HIV, a major public-health benefit.
[bio23-s8.28.3] Hormonal methods can treat heavy or painful periods, and the combined pill lowers the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer.
[bio23-s8.28.4] At a global scale, access to contraception slows population growth, reducing pressure on food, water and habitats. This is a sustainability link; there is more on this in The cross-cutting themes.
[bio23-s8.29.b] Contraception also has drawbacks, some medical, some personal, and one environmental.
[bio23-s8.29.0] Hormonal methods have side effects and, for some women, a small increased risk of blood clots.
[bio23-s8.29.1] No method except condoms prevents STIs, so a person relying on the pill may still catch one.
[bio23-s8.29.2] User-dependent methods often fail in real life, because they must be used correctly every time.
[bio23-s8.29.3] Sterilisation is effectively irreversible and may be regretted.
[bio23-s8.29.4] Some people object to artificial contraception on religious or moral grounds.
[bio23-s8.29.5] Synthetic oestrogen from the pill, along with natural oestrogens, passes through sewage treatment into rivers, where it has been linked to the feminisation of male fish, an environmental effect.
[bio23-s8.30.b] Treatments for infertility bring benefits that are both personal and medical. Each argument rests on the biology of the technique.
[bio23-s8.30.0] It allows people with infertility to have a child who is genetically their own, relieving much distress.
[bio23-s8.30.1] ICSI lets many men with very few or poorly swimming sperm become fathers.
[bio23-s8.30.2] Egg and sperm freezing preserve fertility for people having cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.30.3] Preimplantation testing in IVF lets couples who carry a serious genetic condition avoid passing it on.
[bio23-s8.30.4] Donor eggs or sperm allow people without their own gametes to have children.
[bio23-s8.30.5] Research in IVF has increased understanding of fertilisation and early development.
[bio23-s8.31.b] Infertility treatments also raise health, cost and ethical concerns. Some are risks to mother and baby, others are questions of fairness and identity.
[bio23-s8.31.0] Stimulating ovulation and transferring more than one embryo raise the chance of twins and triplets, which are more often born premature and underweight and carry more risk for the mother; this is why single-embryo transfer is now favoured.
[bio23-s8.31.1] Ovarian hyperstimulation can be dangerous.
[bio23-s8.31.2] Success rates per cycle are limited and fall sharply with the woman's age, so treatment can mean repeated cycles with much stress and cost, and unequal access for those who cannot pay.
[bio23-s8.31.3] ICSI bypasses the natural selection of sperm, so a genetic cause of the father's infertility, for example some changes on the Y chromosome, can be passed to a son.
[bio23-s8.31.4] Spare frozen embryos raise questions about how long they are stored and whether they may be used in research or discarded. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio23-s8.31.5] Children conceived with donor gametes may want to know their genetic origins, which raises questions about donor anonymity.
[bio23-s8.31.6] Treatment allows parenthood at older ages, when pregnancy carries more risk, for example a higher chance of chromosome conditions.
[bio23-s8.32.b] Egg freezing gives women more control over timing but is not a guarantee. Weigh the benefits against the limits.
[bio23-s8.32.0] For: it preserves a woman's own eggs before cancer treatment.
[bio23-s8.32.1] For: it gives women more choice over when to have children.
[bio23-s8.32.2] Against: success is uncertain, and lower when eggs are frozen at older ages.
[bio23-s8.32.3] Against: it involves hormone treatment, egg collection and high costs.
[bio23-s8.32.4] Against: it may give a false sense of security that leads to delayed attempts at pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio24-s4: Fruit and seed dispersal
[bio24-s4.0.b] Dispersal is the carrying of fruits and seeds away from the parent plant. Plants cannot move, so dispersal is how they spread their offspring, and it gives the young plants a much better chance of surviving.
[bio24-s4.0.0] Dispersal reduces competition between the young plants and the parent, and among the young plants themselves, for light, water, minerals and space, because seeds spread out are not all drawing on the same patch of soil.
[bio24-s4.0.1] Dispersal lets the species colonise new areas and new habitats, so it can grow beyond the place where the parent happens to live.
[bio24-s4.0.2] Dispersal spreads the population out, so a local disaster, such as disease, fire or flooding, is less likely to destroy all of it.
[bio24-s4.0.3] Seeds that fall directly under the parent are usually shaded and short of water and minerals, so few survive. Plants produce many seeds because most are lost during dispersal or eaten.
[bio24-s4.0.4] Sustainability link: the ability of some plants to spread their seeds widely makes them invasive (they take over) when introduced to new countries, e.g. Himalayan balsam, an invasive plant along Irish rivers with exploding seed pods.
[bio24-s4.1.b] Dispersal works through fruits, so it helps to know what a fruit is. The type of fruit a plant makes decides which dispersal method suits it.
[bio24-s4.1.0] A fruit is a mature ovary (the part of the flower that holds the ovules) containing seeds; its wall is the pericarp. It protects the seeds and is adapted to disperse them. Botanically, many foods called vegetables are fruits, e.g. tomatoes, cucumbers and pea pods.
[bio24-s4.1.1] Fleshy (succulent) fruits have a soft, juicy pericarp, often brightly coloured and sweet when ripe, e.g. blackberry, tomato and cherry; they are mostly dispersed by animals that eat them.
[bio24-s4.1.2] Dry fruits have a hard or papery pericarp, e.g. pea pod, hazelnut and sycamore; they are dispersed by wind, water, animals carrying them, or by splitting open.
[bio24-s4.1.3] A false fruit is one in which other parts of the flower, not only the ovary, form the fruit, e.g. strawberry, where the fleshy part is the receptacle (the swollen top of the flower stalk).
[bio24-s4.2.b] Wind carries a fruit or seed further the more slowly it falls. Wind-dispersed plants therefore make fruits or seeds that are light and have a large surface area compared with their mass.
[bio24-s4.2.0] Adaptations: fruits or seeds are small and light, or have wings or parachutes that increase their surface area, so they fall slowly and are carried further by the wind.
[bio24-s4.2.1] Parachutes: dandelion and thistle fruits each carry a parachute of fine hairs that lets them float long distances.
[bio24-s4.2.2] Wings: sycamore has paired winged fruits and ash has single winged fruits. The wing makes the fruit spin like a helicopter blade as it falls, slowing it so the wind carries it away from the parent.
[bio24-s4.2.3] Censer (pepper-pot) mechanism: the poppy's dry fruit, a capsule, has a ring of pores near the top. When the wind sways the stalk, the tiny seeds are shaken out a few at a time, like pepper from a shaker.
[bio24-s4.2.4] Dust-like seeds: orchid seeds are so tiny and light that they are blown like dust.
[bio24-s4.3.b] Plants growing beside or on water use it to carry their offspring. The key adaptation is the ability to float, so the fruit is carried rather than sinking.
[bio24-s4.3.0] Adaptations: fruits or seeds that float, because they contain air spaces or have a light, waterproof covering, so they can be carried by streams, rivers or the sea.
[bio24-s4.3.1] Coconut: the fruit has a thick, fibrous, air-filled husk and a waterproof outer layer, so it floats for long periods and is carried by ocean currents to other shores and islands.
[bio24-s4.3.2] Alder: a tree of riverbanks whose small fruits contain air and float, and are carried downstream to settle on new banks.
[bio24-s4.4.b] Some plants use animals as couriers by offering them food. The animal eats the fruit, and the seeds travel inside it before being left somewhere new.
[bio24-s4.4.0] Fleshy fruits are eaten by birds and mammals. The animal digests the fleshy pericarp, but the seeds have a tough testa (the hard seed coat) or a hard stone that resists digestion. 
[bio24-s4.4.1] The seeds pass out in the faeces, often far from the parent, and the droppings act as a supply of minerals.
[bio24-s4.4.2] Adaptations: bright colours (often red, black or purple) when ripe, attracting birds; sweet, juicy, nutritious flesh as a reward for the animal.
[bio24-s4.4.3] Unripe fruit is green and sour while the seeds are unripe, so the fruit isn't eaten too early, before the seeds can survive and grow.
[bio24-s4.4.4] Examples: blackberry, holly berries and rowan berries (eaten by birds), and tomato.
[bio24-s4.4.5] Animal caching: squirrels and jays bury acorns and hazelnuts as winter stores and fail to recover some, which then grow into new trees.
[bio24-s4.5.b] Other plants do not offer food. Instead their fruits hitch a ride on the outside of an animal.
[bio24-s4.5.0] Fruits have hooks or sticky surfaces that catch on the fur or feathers of passing animals (or on human clothing). They fall or are brushed off later, away from the parent.
[bio24-s4.5.1] Examples: burdock has hooked burs; goosegrass (cleavers) has small fruits covered in hooked hairs.
[bio24-s4.5.2] Technology link: the hooked burs of burdock inspired Velcro, invented by the Swiss engineer George de Mestral after he found burs stuck to his dog's coat.
[bio24-s4.6.b] A few plants need no outside carrier. The fruit itself throws the seeds out.
[bio24-s4.6.0] Some dry fruits split open forcefully as they dry, flinging the seeds out: gorse and pea pods twist and burst in dry weather, and Himalayan balsam pods explode when touched.
[bio24-s4.7.b] Each method of dispersal goes with a matching set of adaptations and named examples. Learn them as method, adaptation and example together.
[bio24-s4.7.0] Wind: wings, parachutes and light seeds (sycamore, ash, dandelion, poppy).
[bio24-s4.7.1] Water: floating, air-filled fruits (coconut, alder).
[bio24-s4.7.2] Animal, internal: fleshy, coloured, edible fruits with indigestible seeds (blackberry, holly).
[bio24-s4.7.3] Animal, external: hooks (burdock, goosegrass).
[bio24-s4.7.4] Self-dispersal: exploding pods (gorse, pea).
[bio24-s4.7.5] Diagram: labelled drawings of one fruit for each method: sycamore (wing and seed), dandelion (parachute of hairs and the fruit containing a seed), burdock bur (hooks) and blackberry (fleshy fruit and seeds). A coconut in section (fibrous, air-filled husk and seed) is optional.

CANDIDATES bio29-s1: Investigation: the heart
[bio29-s1.0.b] The heart is a muscular pump that never stops, and its structure is built around one job: moving blood. It is divided into four chambers, two upper and two lower, split into a left and a right side by a central wall.
[bio29-s1.0.0] Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax (the chest) between the two lungs and behind the sternum (breastbone), with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left.
[bio29-s1.0.1] The heart is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers. The fluid reduces friction as the heart beats, and the membrane prevents the heart from over-stretching.
[bio29-s1.0.2] Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
[bio29-s1.0.3] Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries (the vessels that carry blood away from the heart), so their walls are thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.0.4] The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance. The right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels).
[bio29-s1.0.5] Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.
[bio29-s1.0.6] In other words, the heart is two separate pumps side by side: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.b] Blood has to arrive at the heart and leave it by separate routes. Veins bring blood in to the atria, and arteries carry it out from the ventricles.
[bio29-s1.1.0] Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it towards it; this is not about oxygen, which is why one artery and one set of veins break the usual pattern below.
[bio29-s1.1.1] The venae cavae bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium: the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms, and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body.
[bio29-s1.1.2] The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
[bio29-s1.1.3] Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs.
[bio29-s1.1.4] The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.5] The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself with blood. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.2.b] Blood pressure inside the heart changes sharply as the muscle squeezes, and blood would simply flow back where it came from without help. Valves are flaps of tissue that keep blood flowing in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.2.0] Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. A reminder is tRIcuspid on the RIght.
[bio29-s1.2.1] AV valves let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
[bio29-s1.2.2] The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (heart strings), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
[bio29-s1.2.3] Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets.
[bio29-s1.2.4] Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
[bio29-s1.3.b] The heart wall is made of a special muscle found nowhere else in the body. Its features suit it to beating without rest for a whole lifetime.
[bio29-s1.3.0] Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped) and branched, and are joined end to end by intercalated discs.
[bio29-s1.3.1] Intercalated discs let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell, so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit.
[bio29-s1.3.2] Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue).
[bio29-s1.3.3] Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. There is more on the pacemaker that starts each beat in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.3.4] Diagram: a vertical section through the heart viewed from the front, so that the heart's left side is on the right of the page. Label the right and left atria, the right and left ventricles (left wall thicker), the septum, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves, the chordae tendineae, the papillary muscles, the semilunar valves, the superior and inferior venae cavae, the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary veins and the aorta. Add arrows for the direction of blood flow, and shade or label the oxygenated (left) and deoxygenated (right) sides.
[bio29-s1.3.5] Diagram: cardiac muscle as seen under the microscope, from a prepared slide or micrograph, showing branched, striated cells with a central nucleus and intercalated discs. Label the striations, nucleus, intercalated disc and branch.
[bio29-s1.4.b] A dissection is an investigation, so it needs an aim, hypotheses to test, controlled variables and both kinds of data. The heart you cut open is a model for the human heart.
[bio29-s1.4.0] Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection, and to relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs and images of the human heart).
[bio29-s1.4.1] Hypothesis to test: the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body.
[bio29-s1.4.2] Hypothesis to test: arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it.
[bio29-s1.4.3] Hypothesis to test: the valves allow blood to flow in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.4.4] Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): the independent variable (the one you change) is the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle or atrium); the dependent variable (the one you measure) is the wall thickness in mm.
[bio29-s1.4.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): measure at the same level, about halfway between the base and the apex, with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall.
[bio29-s1.4.6] Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean. Pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
[bio29-s1.4.7] Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle; published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart, because mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
[bio29-s1.4.8] Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep's, pig's or ox's heart from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long; dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; a camera or phone to record observations.
[bio29-s1.5.b] Start with protection and with the outside of the heart. Identifying the front surface and the vessels first tells you which side is which before you cut anything.
[bio29-s1.5.0] 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
[bio29-s1.5.1] 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it, and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart's left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
[bio29-s1.5.2] 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top), the ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top.
[bio29-s1.5.3] Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae and pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see, and photograph it.
[bio29-s1.5.4] 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters.
[bio29-s1.5.5] The vena cava leads to the right atrium; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the pulmonary veins lead to the left atrium; the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
[bio29-s1.5.6] Butchers often trim the vessels short, so use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
[bio29-s1.6.b] Now cut the heart open to look at the chambers, valves and wall thickness. Work on the right side first, then the left, then compare the walls in cross-section.
[bio29-s1.6.0] 5. Open the right side. Using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers.
[bio29-s1.6.1] Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles. Pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap. 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
[bio29-s1.6.2] 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve. Look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
[bio29-s1.6.3] 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing that the valves allow one-way flow.
[bio29-s1.6.4] Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
[bio29-s1.6.5] 9. Cut a transverse section (straight across) across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them.
[bio29-s1.6.6] Measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler, following the variables and repeats above. Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
[bio29-s1.6.7] 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope, or a micrograph, and draw it. 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings. Dispose of the heart and clean up as described under Safety and ethics.
[bio29-s1.7.b] A dissection uses sharp blades and raw animal tissue, so each hazard has a specific precaution. There is also an ethical side to using animal material.
[bio29-s1.7.0] Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person. Hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade, carry sharp instruments pointing down, and report any cut at once.
[bio29-s1.7.1] Raw meat can carry bacteria, such as Salmonella, that cause food poisoning. Wear gloves, do not touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
[bio29-s1.7.2] Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection. Wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher; it is not to be left in an open bin.
[bio29-s1.7.3] Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or when pouring water through the vessels.
[bio29-s1.7.4] Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food, a by-product of the food industry, so that no animal is killed for the dissection, and treat the tissue with respect.
[bio29-s1.7.5] A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos as secondary data, in line with the 3Rs of animal use. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio29-s1.8.b] Your results come from two sources: primary data, which you collect yourself from the dissected heart, and secondary data, which comes from diagrams, models and micrographs. Together they support the conclusions.
[bio29-s1.8.0] Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right, typically several times thicker in measurements; the septum thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.8.1] Valves: the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s1.8.2] Vessels: the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface, with their openings just above the aortic valve.
[bio29-s1.8.3] Results table to record: the structure; where it is found; its description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); the measured wall thickness in mm, with three readings and the mean; and its function.
[bio29-s1.8.4] Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels. Differences seen are due to the butcher's trimming, which may remove the atria or vessels, or to species size.
[bio29-s1.8.5] Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.
[bio29-s1.9.b] The conclusion links each structure you found to its function, and states whether each hypothesis was supported by the evidence.
[bio29-s1.9.0] The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
[bio29-s1.9.1] Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
[bio29-s1.9.2] The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
[bio29-s1.9.3] The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
[bio29-s1.9.4] Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls. There is more on this in Blood vessels.
[bio29-s1.9.5] For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.
[bio29-s1.10.b] A dissected animal heart is not a living human heart, and the measurements are rough. Knowing the limits shows how far the conclusions can be trusted and how to improve them.
[bio29-s1.10.0] Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
[bio29-s1.10.1] Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
[bio29-s1.10.2] Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
[bio29-s1.10.3] A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
[bio29-s1.10.4] One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio29-s2: Circulation
[bio29-s2.0.b] Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to get rid of wastes. The circulatory system is the transport network that does this for all of them.
[bio29-s2.0.0] The human circulatory system has three parts: the heart, a pump; the blood vessels, a closed network of tubes made up of arteries, capillaries and veins; and the blood, the transport medium that carries the materials.
[bio29-s2.0.1] It is a closed system: blood stays inside the vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across the thin walls of the capillaries (the tiniest vessels, which pass right alongside the cells).
[bio29-s2.0.2] In a large, many-celled animal, diffusion (the slow, random spreading of molecules) alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut and kidneys) and cells deep in the body.
[bio29-s2.0.3] The circulatory system solves this by carrying materials quickly by mass flow (the whole liquid moving together), so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
[bio29-s2.0.4] Materials transported: oxygen (lungs to all cells); carbon dioxide (cells to lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine to liver to cells); urea (liver to kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands to target organs); water and salts.
[bio29-s2.0.5] Also transported: antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets (which seal wounds); and heat, carried from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin.
[bio29-s2.1.b] Humans have two circuits joined at the heart rather than one loop. Blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and is then sent out to the rest of the body.
[bio29-s2.1.0] Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. This does not mean two hearts; it is one heart with two sides.
[bio29-s2.1.1] The pulmonary circuit (pulmonary means 'of the lungs') runs heart to lungs to heart. The systemic circuit runs heart to body organs to heart.
[bio29-s2.1.2] The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit. There is more on the chambers in Investigation: the heart.
[bio29-s2.1.3] Full route of a red blood cell: vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, semilunar valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valve, aorta, artery, arteriole, capillaries of an organ, venule, vein, vena cava.
[bio29-s2.1.4] Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation. Heart as four boxes, lungs above, body organs below. Add arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae.
[bio29-s2.1.5] Diagram labels: right and left atrium and ventricle, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta, venae cavae, pulmonary circuit, systemic circuit. Shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue.
[bio29-s2.2.b] The pulmonary circuit has one job: to send blood that has given up its oxygen to the lungs to collect more. It starts at the right side of the heart.
[bio29-s2.2.0] 1. Deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) is pumped by the right ventricle (the lower right chamber, which pumps blood out of the heart) into the pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s2.2.1] 2. The pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung, so both lungs receive blood.
[bio29-s2.2.2] 3. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂, because oxygen diffuses in from the air in the alveoli (air sacs) and CO₂ diffuses out. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio29-s2.2.3] 4. Oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium (the upper left chamber, which receives blood coming into the heart).
[bio29-s2.3.b] The systemic circuit takes oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to every organ and brings it back. Its steps follow on from the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.3.0] 1. Oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve (the one-way valve between the left atrium and left ventricle) to the left ventricle.
[bio29-s2.3.1] 2. The left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, the body's largest artery.
[bio29-s2.3.2] 3. Arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ, where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes.
[bio29-s2.3.3] 4. Deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae (the large veins bringing blood from the upper and lower body) and into the right atrium.
[bio29-s2.3.4] 5. It passes through the tricuspid valve (the one-way valve between the right atrium and right ventricle) to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
[bio29-s2.4.b] A double circulation is better than a single loop for an active, warm-blooded animal. Passing the blood through the heart twice solves two problems: lost pressure and the risk of damage to the lungs.
[bio29-s2.4.0] (a) Blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all the body organs quickly.
[bio29-s2.4.1] (b) The two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit, to drive blood through the many organs, and lower in the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.4.2] The lower pulmonary pressure matters because the lung capillaries are thin-walled, so they are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs).
[bio29-s2.4.3] (c) The septum (the wall dividing the two sides of the heart) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated.
[bio29-s2.4.4] Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
[bio29-s2.5.b] Blood reaches each organ along its own named artery and leaves along its own named vein. Knowing these names lets you describe the route to any organ.
[bio29-s2.5.0] A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and is drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and each is usually named after the organ.
[bio29-s2.5.1] Lungs (pulmonary pathway): the pulmonary artery, from the right ventricle, carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins, to the left atrium, carry oxygenated blood. In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s2.5.2] Head and brain: the carotid arteries, in the neck, supply it, and the jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava. Arms: the subclavian arteries and veins. Legs: the iliac arteries and veins.
[bio29-s2.5.3] Kidneys: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered, and the renal veins return filtered blood, low in urea, to the inferior vena cava. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio29-s2.5.4] Heart muscle: the coronary arteries and cardiac veins, which supply the heart's own muscle with blood.
[bio29-s2.5.5] Liver and gut: the liver receives blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut). Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
[bio29-s2.5.6] Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels showing the heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
[bio29-s2.6.b] Most veins take blood straight back towards the heart. The vein from the gut is unusual because it detours through the liver first, so the liver gets the first chance to deal with what has just been absorbed.
[bio29-s2.6.0] A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. Blood therefore passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
[bio29-s2.6.1] The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the capillaries in the villi (the tiny finger-like folds of the small intestine wall), and from the walls of the stomach and large intestine.
[bio29-s2.6.2] It carries this blood to a second set of capillaries, the sinusoids, in the liver. After a meal this blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
[bio29-s2.6.3] Absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio29-s2.6.4] The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood, because it has already passed through the gut capillaries, so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery.
[bio29-s2.7.b] The circulatory system works as the delivery service for every other system. Each system either puts materials into the blood or takes materials out of it, and you can model these exchanges.
[bio29-s2.7.0] Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂. The circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and carries CO₂ back, so without circulation the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
[bio29-s2.7.1] Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals, small vessels in the villi) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells. The blood also supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
[bio29-s2.7.2] Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it. The kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume.
[bio29-s2.7.3] Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs, for example insulin from the pancreas to the liver and ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys. Adrenaline, carried in the blood, increases heart rate.
[bio29-s2.7.4] Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds.
[bio29-s2.7.5] Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells. Active muscles receive extra blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, and contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping to return blood to the heart.
[bio29-s2.7.6] Nervous system: the medulla oblongata (the part of the brain that controls automatic functions) adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles (small arteries), so blood is sent where it is needed. The brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
[bio29-s2.7.7] Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs, and widening or narrowing of the skin arterioles controls heat loss.
[bio29-s2.8.b] A model is a simplified picture that shows how the systems connect. A flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre makes it clear which materials travel where.
[bio29-s2.8.0] Diagram: a labelled flow diagram modelling these interactions, with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain. Label each arrow with the materials transferred: O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones and heat.
[bio29-s2.8.1] One limitation of the model is that it shows what moves but not the structure of the vessels or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
[bio29-s2.8.2] Worked example: during exercise, muscles respire faster, so breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s2.8.3] In the same example, heart rate rises and the arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them, while the arterioles to the gut narrow. Heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

CANDIDATES bio29-s3: Heartbeat
[bio29-s3.0.b] The heart does not wait for instructions to beat. A small patch of muscle in its own wall sets the pace and sends a wave of electrical signals that makes the chambers contract in the right order.
[bio29-s3.0.0] A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles (the two lower chambers) into the arteries.
[bio29-s3.0.1] An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
[bio29-s3.0.2] Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid.
[bio29-s3.0.3] The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium (an upper chamber), near where the superior vena cava (the large vein from the upper body) enters.
[bio29-s3.0.4] The SA node produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
[bio29-s3.1.b] The impulse from the SA node does not reach every part at once. It travels in a set route with a built-in pause, which makes the atria contract first and the ventricles second.
[bio29-s3.1.0] 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.1] 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles, so the ventricles cannot contract too early.
[bio29-s3.1.2] 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum (the wall between the two sides of the heart). The AV node delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.3] 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex (the pointed lower tip of the heart), and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
[bio29-s3.1.4] 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery, the two arteries leaving the heart. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.
[bio29-s3.1.5] Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.
[bio29-s3.2.b] The SA node sets a basic rhythm, but the body's needs change from moment to moment. Nerves and a hormone adjust the rate the SA node sets, so the heart can speed up or slow down.
[bio29-s3.2.0] The rate is changed to match the body's needs. It rises during exercise, because the muscles then need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂, which the faster-flowing blood delivers and carries away.
[bio29-s3.2.1] Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata (part of the brainstem) sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down.
[bio29-s3.2.2] The medulla responds to information from receptors, which detect changes in the body. For example, a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate, and a rise in blood pressure slows it.
[bio29-s3.2.3] Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction.
[bio29-s3.2.4] Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
[bio29-s3.2.5] Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart.
[bio29-s3.2.6] The artificial pacemaker detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.
[bio29-s3.3.b] Each time the ventricles contract, they force a surge of blood into the arteries. You can feel this surge as the pulse, which gives an easy way to measure heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.0] The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.1] The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe.
[bio29-s3.3.2] Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries (the tiny vessels between arteries and veins) before the blood reaches them.
[bio29-s3.3.3] Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes, so the reading is a resting one.
[bio29-s3.3.4] 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
[bio29-s3.3.5] 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
[bio29-s3.3.6] 4. Repeat three times and take the mean, which reduces the effect of any one miscount.
[bio29-s3.3.7] If asked what the pulse is, say it is a wave in the artery wall, not blood flowing past your finger.
[bio29-s3.4.b] Exercise makes the muscles need more oxygen, so the heart speeds up. Measuring the pulse before and after exercise shows this, and how quickly it recovers shows how fit someone is.
[bio29-s3.4.0] This is a standard class investigation with primary data (data you collect yourself). Measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value.
[bio29-s3.4.1] The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people.
[bio29-s3.4.2] Fitter people also tend to have a lower resting pulse, because each beat pumps more blood.
[bio29-s3.4.3] Independent variable (the one you change): activity level (rest, or after exercise). Dependent variable (the one you measure): pulse rate in beats per minute.
[bio29-s3.4.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.
[bio29-s3.5.b] Blood only flows round the body because the ventricles push it with force. That force, pressing on the walls of the vessels, is blood pressure, and when it stays too high it harms the body.
[bio29-s3.5.0] Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.5.1] Pressure is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles (small arteries) and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae.
[bio29-s3.5.2] Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
[bio29-s3.5.3] Pressure is given as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.4] Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.5] Hypertension usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
[bio29-s3.5.6] Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet, being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.5.7] High blood pressure is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.
[bio29-s3.6.b] The heart works non-stop, so its muscle needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose. It cannot get these from the blood inside its chambers, so it has a separate circulation of its own.
[bio29-s3.6.0] The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
[bio29-s3.6.1] The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve (the valve at the exit of the left ventricle). They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle.
[bio29-s3.6.2] These small arteries supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration (energy release using oxygen) that keeps it working continuously.
[bio29-s3.6.3] Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.
[bio29-s3.6.4] Diagram: a front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins. Label each.
[bio29-s3.7.b] The coronary arteries are narrow, so damage to them has serious effects. When they narrow or block, the heart muscle is starved of oxygen.
[bio29-s3.7.0] Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
[bio29-s3.7.1] If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction.
[bio29-s3.7.2] Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after a heart attack can break down the clot and restore blood flow to the starved muscle, so they limit the damage (see 'Industrial enzymes').
[bio29-s3.7.3] Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol, high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.7.4] Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight, which each remove a risk factor.
[bio29-s3.7.5] Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter (a thin tube) is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open.
[bio29-s3.7.6] Alternatively, the narrowed artery can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.

CANDIDATES bio6-s3: Classification
[bio6-s3.0.b] There are so many kinds of living thing that biologists sort them into groups, the way a library sorts books into sections, shelves and titles. The groups sit inside one another in a fixed set of levels.
[bio6-s3.0.0] Classification is the grouping of organisms according to their shared characteristics. Taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
[bio6-s3.0.1] Organisms are placed in a hierarchy of ranks (taxa, the levels of grouping), from broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
[bio6-s3.0.2] Moving down the ranks, each group has fewer members, which share more features and are more closely related. This is why two species in the same genus are far more alike than two in the same kingdom.
[bio6-s3.0.3] Worked example, human: domain Eukarya, kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Primates, family Hominidae, genus Homo, species Homo sapiens.
[bio6-s3.1.b] The species is the smallest rank, so it needs a clear definition and a universal name. Every species gets a two-part scientific name, which is called binomial nomenclature.
[bio6-s3.1.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (young that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, a mule, is infertile, so they are different species.
[bio6-s3.1.1] Limitation of the species definition: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually (e.g. bacteria), because they do not interbreed at all, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding.
[bio6-s3.1.2] Binomial nomenclature: every species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name (capital first letter) followed by the species name (lower case), printed in italics or underlined when handwritten.
[bio6-s3.1.3] Examples of binomial names: Homo sapiens (human), Escherichia coli (a gut bacterium), Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould).
[bio6-s3.1.4] The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature; his Species Plantarum (1753) used it for all plants.
[bio6-s3.1.5] Species in the same genus are closely related, which is why they share the first part of their name. The lion (Panthera leo) and tiger (Panthera tigris) share the genus Panthera.
[bio6-s3.2.b] Before sorting living things into kingdoms, biologists ask one basic question about their cells: is there a nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) or not? The answer splits cellular life into two big groups.
[bio6-s3.2.0] The first division of cellular life is by cell type. Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (the small working parts of a cell). Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, and membrane-bound organelles. There is more on this in Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
[bio6-s3.2.1] Two kingdoms are prokaryotic, Bacteria and Archaea, each forming its own domain. Four kingdoms are eukaryotic, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, all in the domain Eukarya.
[bio6-s3.2.2] The distinction became clear only once the electron microscope showed cell interiors, because the details inside prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are too small to see with a light microscope.
[bio6-s3.3.b] Both prokaryotic kingdoms are single-celled with no nucleus, so cell structure alone does not separate them. One way of telling them apart is the chemistry of their cell walls, and another is rRNA. Each is described by cell type, number of cells, cell wall, nutrition and examples.
[bio6-s3.3.0] Bacteria: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls of peptidoglycan (a mesh of sugars and amino acids). They reproduce by binary fission (one cell splits into two).
[bio6-s3.3.1] Bacterial nutrition varies. Bacteria are autotrophic (make their own food), either photosynthetic or chemosynthetic, or heterotrophic (take in food made by others), either saprophytic (feeding on dead matter) or parasitic (feeding on a living host).
[bio6-s3.3.2] Bacteria examples: Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus (used to make yoghurt) and Streptococcus.
[bio6-s3.3.3] Archaea: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls that have no peptidoglycan. This difference in cell wall is one way of telling them apart from bacteria. Many live in extreme environments.
[bio6-s3.3.4] Archaea examples: methanogens (methane-producing archaea) in cattle digestive systems, and Halobacterium in salt lakes.
[bio6-s3.4.b] The four eukaryotic kingdoms all have a nucleus. They are separated by how many cells they have, what their cell walls are made of, how they get food and how they store carbohydrate.
[bio6-s3.4.0] Protista: eukaryotic and mostly unicellular, though some are multicellular algae such as seaweeds (kelp). They have no tissues (groups of similar cells doing one job) of the kind found in plants and animals. Some are photosynthetic (algae) and some are heterotrophic.
[bio6-s3.4.1] Protista includes eukaryotes that do not belong in the other three eukaryotic kingdoms. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (causes malaria) and Phytophthora infestans (causes potato blight, which caused the Great Famine in Ireland, 1845–1849).
[bio6-s3.4.2] Fungi: eukaryotic and mostly multicellular, made of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium (a tangled mass of threads), though yeasts are unicellular. They have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise, and their cell walls are made of chitin.
[bio6-s3.4.3] Fungi are heterotrophic: they secrete enzymes onto food and absorb the digested products, living as saprophytes or parasites. They store carbohydrate as glycogen and reproduce by spores. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), mushrooms and Penicillium.
[bio6-s3.4.4] Plantae: eukaryotic and multicellular, with cell walls of cellulose and chloroplasts (where photosynthesis happens). They are autotrophic by photosynthesis, store carbohydrate as starch, and mostly do not move. Examples: mosses, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants such as the daisy and oak.
[bio6-s3.4.5] Animalia: eukaryotic and multicellular, with no cell walls. They are heterotrophic, taking in (ingesting) food, and store carbohydrate as glycogen. Most can move and have nerves and muscles. Examples: sponges, earthworms, insects, fish and mammals.
[bio6-s3.5.b] Classification by kingdom and domain only works for things made of cells. Viruses fall outside it.
[bio6-s3.5.0] Viruses are not in any kingdom or domain, because they are not cells. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio6-s3.6.b] Grouping by appearance can mislead, so modern classification also asks which organisms share a recent ancestor. This evolutionary family-tree approach is called phylogeny.
[bio6-s3.6.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms. Phylogenetic classification groups organisms by how closely related they are by descent from common ancestors, rather than by appearance alone.
[bio6-s3.6.1] A phylogenetic tree shows these relationships. Each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor, and the closer two species' branches join, the more recent their common ancestor and the more closely related they are.
[bio6-s3.6.2] Species in the same genus share a very recent common ancestor; genera in the same family share an older one. This is how the ranks of the hierarchy reflect evolutionary history.
[bio6-s3.6.3] Example: the lion and tiger (genus Panthera) are more closely related to each other than either is to the domestic cat (Felis catus), but all three are in the cat family (Felidae).
[bio6-s3.6.4] The dog (Canis familiaris, also written Canis lupus familiaris) is in a different family, Canidae, and all four animals are in the order Carnivora.
[bio6-s3.6.5] Evidence used to work out phylogeny includes shared anatomical structures with the same basic plan, e.g. the five-digit limb of mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians; embryology (the study of early development); and fossils. There is more on this in Evolution: The Origins of Life.
[bio6-s3.6.6] The most powerful evidence is comparison of DNA base sequences and protein amino-acid sequences: the fewer the differences, the more closely related the organisms, because less time has passed for differences to build up.
[bio6-s3.6.7] Appearance can mislead. Whales and dolphins look like fish but are mammals: they breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young on milk.
[bio6-s3.6.8] Diagram: a phylogenetic tree for lion, tiger, domestic cat and dog, with the nodes for genus Panthera, family Felidae and order Carnivora labelled.
[bio6-s3.7.b] Classification is not fixed. Each time scientists gained a new tool for looking at living things, the groupings were redrawn, which is why you will meet different numbers of kingdoms in the history of the subject.
[bio6-s3.7.0] Early systems had two kingdoms, plants and animals. Linnaeus, in the 18th century, classified organisms by visible features.
[bio6-s3.7.1] Microscopes revealed single-celled organisms that fitted neither kingdom. In 1866 Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista.
[bio6-s3.7.2] In 1969 Robert Whittaker proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. Monera held all the prokaryotes.
[bio6-s3.7.3] Fungi were separated from plants in Whittaker's system because they have chitin walls, no chlorophyll and absorb their food.
[bio6-s3.7.4] rRNA sequencing (reading the sequence of a molecule found in ribosomes) by Carl Woese showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups. This gave three domains (1990).
[bio6-s3.7.5] The six kingdoms used in this course followed, with Monera split into Bacteria and Archaea. There is more on this in Domains of life.
[bio6-s3.7.6] Systems change because new technology provides new evidence, e.g. the light microscope, the electron microscope, biochemistry and DNA sequencing. Classification is revised to reflect evolutionary relationships more accurately. It is a model that scientists keep testing and improving.
[bio6-s3.8.b] DNA sequencing, reading the order of bases in an organism's DNA, is now the strongest tool for classification. It is still moving organisms between groups and adding new ones.
[bio6-s3.8.0] DNA sequencing and whole-genome comparisons are now the main evidence for classification, and have moved many organisms. Phytophthora (potato blight) was long classed as a fungus because it grows as threads. Its DNA and its cellulose cell walls show that it belongs with the protists (water moulds).
[bio6-s3.8.1] DNA evidence placed the giant panda in the bear family.
[bio6-s3.8.2] Environmental DNA sequencing, which sequences all the DNA in a soil or water sample, has revealed huge numbers of microorganisms that have never been grown in a laboratory. These add new branches to the tree of life.
[bio6-s3.8.3] Asgard archaea were first reported in 2015 from DNA in sediment near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vents in the Arctic Ocean. They share many genes with eukaryotes.
[bio6-s3.8.4] Some scientists argue that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, which would mean only two primary domains. This is still debated, so classification remains a work in progress.
[bio6-s3.8.5] DNA barcoding uses a short standard gene sequence to identify species quickly, e.g. to identify species from tiny samples or to detect mislabelled food.
[bio6-s3.9.b] A dichotomous key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by answering a series of either/or questions about what you can see. The worked key below identifies six invertebrates (animals without backbones).
[bio6-s3.9.0] A dichotomous key identifies an organism by a series of steps. Each step gives a choice between two contrasting statements (a couplet) about a visible feature. Each statement leads either to a name or to the number of the next couplet.
[bio6-s3.9.1] Worked example: a key to six invertebrates.
[bio6-s3.9.2] 1. Legs present → go to 2; legs absent → go to 4.
[bio6-s3.9.3] 2. Three pairs of legs → ladybird (an insect); more than three pairs of legs → go to 3.
[bio6-s3.9.4] 3. Four pairs of legs → spider; seven pairs of legs → woodlouse.
[bio6-s3.9.5] 4. Shell present → snail; no visible shell → go to 5.
[bio6-s3.9.6] 5. Body divided into rings (segments) → earthworm; body not divided into rings → slug.
[bio6-s3.10.b] To use a key, start at couplet 1 and follow the numbers until you reach a name. Practise tracing a route for each organism so you can do it in the exam.
[bio6-s3.10.0] Using the key: an animal with eight legs goes 1 (legs present) → 2 (more than three pairs) → 3 (four pairs) → spider. Eight legs means four pairs, which is why it ends at spider.
[bio6-s3.10.1] Write the route for one more organism: the slug goes 1 → 4 → 5 → slug, because it has no legs, no visible shell and a body that is not divided into rings.
[bio6-s3.11.b] You may have to build a key of your own, or use the features of the kingdoms to place an unknown organism. Both rely on picking features that give a clear yes or no.
[bio6-s3.11.0] To construct a key, list the organisms, choose a feature that splits them into two groups, and keep splitting each group in two until each organism is on its own.
[bio6-s3.11.1] Use features that are easy to observe and do not vary between individuals or seasons, e.g. number of legs, not size or colour, because size and colour change with age and season and would give unreliable answers.
[bio6-s3.11.2] A key for n organisms needs n − 1 couplets. For example, the six-invertebrate key above has five couplets.
[bio6-s3.11.3] To classify an unknown organism to a kingdom, use the features in order. Does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a bacterium or an archaeon, separated by cell-wall chemistry or rRNA.
[bio6-s3.11.4] If it has a nucleus, ask further questions. Is it multicellular with tissues? What is its cell wall made of? Is it autotrophic or heterotrophic?
[bio6-s3.11.5] Worked example: a newly found organism is eukaryotic, is made of hyphae with chitin walls, and absorbs food from dead leaves. It is in the kingdom Fungi.
[bio6-s3.12.b] Classification is more than tidy labelling. It makes biology easier to study, communicate and apply to health, farming and conservation.
[bio6-s3.12.0] It organises the enormous diversity of life into manageable groups, making it easier to study and to find information.
[bio6-s3.12.1] Scientific names are universal and unique, which avoids the confusion of common names. The robin of Europe (Erithacus rubecula) and the robin of North America (Turdus migratorius) are different species in different genera.
[bio6-s3.12.2] It shows evolutionary relationships, so features of a new organism can be predicted from its relatives. For example, relatives of a plant that produces a useful drug may produce similar compounds.
[bio6-s3.12.3] Health: correctly identifying a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) decides the treatment. A bacterial infection may respond to an antibiotic, but a fungal or viral one will not.
[bio6-s3.12.4] Agriculture and the environment: identifying pests, invasive species and endangered species is needed for control and conservation, and for measuring biodiversity (the variety of living things in an area).
