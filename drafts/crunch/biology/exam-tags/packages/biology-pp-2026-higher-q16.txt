CANDIDATES bio16-s1: The cell cycle
[bio16-s1.0.b] Before you can follow the cell cycle you need to know what is being copied and shared out: the chromosomes. How many a cell has, and how they are arranged, decides whether it is diploid or haploid.
[bio16-s1.0.0] A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (the units of inheritance). There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio16-s1.0.1] Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin (DNA with its proteins). Before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio16-s1.0.2] Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent; the alleles (versions of a gene) on them may differ. Human body cells contain 23 such pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.3] Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, so that its chromosomes lie in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46. The 46 counts chromosomes, not pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.4] Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23. Haploid means one complete set, not half a chromosome.
[bio16-s1.0.5] When two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.
[bio16-s1.0.6] Haploid cells are made by meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number; mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s1.0.7] Each species has its own chromosome number: human 2n = 46, the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8, the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.
[bio16-s1.1.b] Cells don't just divide and stop: each one lives through a repeating sequence of events called the cell cycle. It shows how a cell prepares for division and then carries it out.
[bio16-s1.1.0] The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
[bio16-s1.1.1] It has three main activities, in this order. First, growth: the cell makes new proteins and organelles (the working parts of a cell) and gets bigger.
[bio16-s1.1.2] Second, DNA synthesis: every chromosome is copied, so the cell has two copies of its genetic information to hand out.
[bio16-s1.1.3] Third, division: the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells.
[bio16-s1.1.4] The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, about 90% of the cycle, so division is short.
[bio16-s1.1.5] In many human cells grown in the laboratory, one cycle takes about 24 hours.
[bio16-s1.1.6] Interphase is not a resting stage. The cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
[bio16-s1.1.7] One cycle, including division, produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s1.2.b] Not every cell divides at the same pace, and no cell divides without being told it is safe to. Whether a cell cycles depends on its job and on control checks built into the cycle.
[bio16-s1.2.0] Some cells leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, for example most mature nerve cells. Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide.
[bio16-s1.2.1] Other cells divide often throughout life, for example cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems, regions where growth happens).
[bio16-s1.2.2] The cycle is controlled. Proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on.
[bio16-s1.2.3] Signals from other cells (growth factors, chemical messages) tell a cell whether to divide. There is more on how loss of this control leads to cancer in Cancer.
[bio16-s1.2.4] Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle. Large sector: interphase (cell growth and DNA replication). Small sector: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis). Arrow for direction. Higher Level: also label G1, S, G2 and the four stages of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.3.b] Interphase is long and busy, and it has three parts: G1, S and G2. Together they prepare the cell so that division can be quick and accurate.
[bio16-s1.3.0] G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including the enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job.
[bio16-s1.3.1] S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere (the narrow point where they are held together).
[bio16-s1.3.2] G2 (second growth phase): further growth and more organelles, for example mitochondria. The proteins of the spindle (the fibres that later pull chromosomes apart) are made, and ATP is stored for division.
[bio16-s1.3.3] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), for example mature nerve cells.
[bio16-s1.3.4] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.
[bio16-s1.4.b] Mitosis is the division of the nucleus. It runs as one smooth process, but it is split into four stages to make it easier to describe, and each stage has a characteristic look and job.
[bio16-s1.4.0] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process, divided for convenience into four stages in this order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. It is followed by cytokinesis, which is division of the cytoplasm and not a stage of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.4.1] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear membrane breaks down, and spindle fibres (protein tubes called microtubules) begin to form.
[bio16-s1.4.2] In animal cells, two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them. Plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
[bio16-s1.4.3] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
[bio16-s1.4.4] Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first, which gives a V shape.
[bio16-s1.4.5] Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome, so each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio16-s1.4.6] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
[bio16-s1.4.7] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell, 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, shaded to show the two parents). Labels: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow (end of telophase).
[bio16-s1.5.b] You can tell which stage a cell is in just by looking at how its chromosomes are arranged under the microscope. Counting dividing cells then tells you how fast a tissue is dividing.
[bio16-s1.5.0] In stained cells from a growing root tip (for example onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase, seen as a nucleus with no visible chromosomes, because interphase is the longest part of the cycle.
[bio16-s1.5.1] Dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes: condensed and scattered for prophase; in a line across the middle for metaphase.
[bio16-s1.5.2] Two groups moving apart is anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, with new nuclei forming, is telophase.
[bio16-s1.5.3] The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing. For example, 24 dividing cells out of 200 gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%.
[bio16-s1.5.4] A high mitotic index shows a tissue that is dividing rapidly, such as a root tip or a tumour.
[bio16-s1.6.b] After the nucleus has divided, the cell still has to split in two. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and plant and animal cells do it differently because plant cells have a rigid wall.
[bio16-s1.6.0] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.1] In animal cells the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
[bio16-s1.6.2] In plant cells the rigid cell wall can't pinch in. Instead, vesicles (small membrane sacs) from the Golgi apparatus collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate.
[bio16-s1.6.3] The cell plate grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.4] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio16-s4: Cancer
[bio16-s4.0.b] Your body replaces and repairs tissue by mitosis (cell division that makes two identical cells), and it keeps this tightly controlled. Cancer is what happens when that control fails.
[bio16-s4.0.0] Cancer is a group of diseases in which some body cells lose control of mitosis. They divide too often (the rate of division is abnormal) and too many times (the number of divisions is not limited).
[bio16-s4.0.1] Cancer cells can also invade nearby tissues and spread to other parts of the body, which is what makes cancer more than just extra cells.
[bio16-s4.0.2] There are over 200 kinds of cancer, named after the organ or tissue where they start, e.g. lung, breast, skin, bowel and blood.
[bio16-s4.0.3] Carcinomas are the group that start in epithelial tissues (the tissues that line or cover organs), e.g. most lung, breast and bowel cancers.
[bio16-s4.0.4] Sarcomas start in bone, muscle or connective tissue (the supporting tissue that holds other tissues together).
[bio16-s4.0.5] Leukaemias start in the blood-forming cells of the bone marrow, and lymphomas start in the lymphatic system (the network of vessels and nodes that drains fluid and carries immune cells).
[bio16-s4.0.6] Cancer starts from a single cell whose DNA has changed (mutated). All the cancer cells in a tumour are descended from that one cell by mitosis, so they all carry its mutation.
[bio16-s4.1.b] To see how cancer starts, you first need to know how healthy cells stay in check. Normal cells divide only when told to, and have built-in ways to stop damaged cells from dividing.
[bio16-s4.1.0] Normal cells divide only when they receive signals to divide (growth factors, chemical messengers from other cells), and stop when signals tell them to stop.
[bio16-s4.1.1] Checkpoints in the cell cycle (the ordered series of stages a cell goes through to divide) halt the cycle if the DNA is damaged or not copied correctly, so errors are not passed on.
[bio16-s4.1.2] Two groups of genes control this. Proto-oncogenes code for proteins that stimulate the cell cycle, so they are the accelerator, e.g. growth factor receptors (proteins that detect the growth signal).
[bio16-s4.1.3] Tumour suppressor genes code for proteins that stop the cycle at checkpoints, so they are the brakes. They also repair damaged DNA or make a badly damaged cell destroy itself.
[bio16-s4.1.4] Apoptosis is programmed cell death: a damaged or unwanted cell breaks itself down in a controlled way, without harming neighbouring cells. It removes cells with damaged DNA before they can divide.
[bio16-s4.1.5] TP53 is an example of a tumour suppressor gene. It codes for the protein p53, which stops the cell cycle when DNA is damaged so that the DNA can be repaired, or triggers apoptosis if the damage is too great.
[bio16-s4.1.6] p53 is called the guardian of the genome because it protects the DNA. TP53 is mutated in about half of all human cancers, which shows how important its protection is.
[bio16-s4.1.7] Contact inhibition: normal cells in a tissue, and in a culture dish, stop dividing when they touch neighbouring cells, so they form a single layer.
[bio16-s4.1.8] Most normal human cells can divide only a limited number of times before they stop, because the protective caps at the ends of their chromosomes (telomeres) shorten with each division.
[bio16-s4.2.b] Cancer develops when the genes that control division are damaged by mutation (a change in the DNA). The damage affects the accelerator, the brakes, or both, and it builds up step by step.
[bio16-s4.2.0] 1. A mutation in a proto-oncogene can turn it into an oncogene, which is permanently switched on and keeps telling the cell to divide even with no signal, like an accelerator stuck down.
[bio16-s4.2.1] 2. A mutation that inactivates a tumour suppressor gene removes the brakes: damaged cells keep dividing instead of stopping, being repaired or dying by apoptosis.
[bio16-s4.2.2] Usually both copies (alleles) of a tumour suppressor gene must be inactivated before control is lost, because one working copy can still make enough of the protein.
[bio16-s4.2.3] 3. One mutation is usually not enough. Several mutations in different control genes must build up in the same cell, often over many years.
[bio16-s4.2.4] This is why the risk of most cancers rises with age, and why anything that increases the rate of mutation raises the risk.
[bio16-s4.2.5] 4. Once one cell has lost control, every daughter cell inherits the same mutations. As they divide faster and faster, further mutations arise, and the cells that divide fastest or survive best take over, so the cancer cells become more abnormal over time.
[bio16-s4.2.6] 5. The immune system (the body's defence against disease) destroys many abnormal cells before they form a tumour. People whose immune system is weakened have a higher risk of some cancers.
[bio16-s4.3.b] Cancer cells differ from normal cells in many ways. Most of the differences come straight from the lost controls on division, and they are best learned as pairs.
[bio16-s4.3.0] Division: normal cells divide only when signalled, at a controlled rate. Cancer cells divide rapidly and continuously without signals, and ignore signals to stop.
[bio16-s4.3.1] Lifespan: normal cells divide a limited number of times and die by apoptosis when damaged. Cancer cells avoid apoptosis, and many keep their telomeres long by using the enzyme telomerase, so they can divide indefinitely (immortal cells).
[bio16-s4.3.2] Contact inhibition is lost: cancer cells ignore contact with neighbours and pile up into a mass, whereas normal cells stop at contact.
[bio16-s4.3.3] Specialisation: normal cells are specialised (differentiated) for a job. Cancer cells are unspecialised (poorly differentiated), so they don't carry out the tissue's function.
[bio16-s4.3.4] Appearance under the microscope: cancer cells vary in size and shape and have large, irregular, darkly staining nuclei, often with abnormal numbers of chromosomes, and many are seen dividing. This is how cancer is diagnosed from a biopsy (tissue sample) or smear.
[bio16-s4.3.5] Blood supply: cancer cells can release signals that make new blood vessels grow into the tumour (angiogenesis), supplying the oxygen and nutrients needed for further growth.
[bio16-s4.3.6] Attachment: normal cells stick to their neighbours and stay in place. Cancer cells can lose this adhesion (stickiness), break away and move.
[bio16-s4.3.7] HeLa cells are a famous example of immortal cancer cells. They were taken in 1951 from a cervical cancer of Henrietta Lacks at Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, USA, without her knowledge or consent.
[bio16-s4.3.8] They were the first human cells to keep dividing indefinitely in the laboratory, and they are still used in research worldwide, including in developing the polio vaccine. Her case is a well-known example of the ethics of consent in research.
[bio16-s4.4.b] Not every lump of abnormal cells is cancer. What decides it is whether the cells stay put or invade and spread.
[bio16-s4.4.0] A tumour is an abnormal mass of cells produced by uncontrolled division.
[bio16-s4.4.1] A benign tumour grows slowly, is usually enclosed in a capsule (a layer of tissue around it), does not invade surrounding tissue or spread, and its cells look fairly normal.
[bio16-s4.4.2] A benign tumour is usually removable by surgery and not life-threatening, though it can be dangerous if it presses on a vital organ such as the brain. Examples are most moles and warts. A benign tumour is not cancer.
[bio16-s4.4.3] A malignant tumour, which is a cancer, grows quickly, has no capsule, invades and destroys surrounding tissue, can spread around the body, and has abnormal cells.
[bio16-s4.4.4] Benign and malignant tumours differ in rate of growth (slow or quick), capsule (present or absent), invasion (none or destructive), spread (none or around the body) and appearance of cells (fairly normal or abnormal).
[bio16-s4.4.5] They also differ in danger: a benign tumour is usually not life-threatening, while a malignant tumour is a cancer and can be life-threatening because it invades and spreads.
[bio16-s4.5.b] A cancer becomes dangerous when it spreads beyond where it began. These are the steps from one mutated cell to tumours in other organs.
[bio16-s4.5.0] 1. A cell mutates and divides without control, producing a clone (a group of identical cells) of abnormal cells.
[bio16-s4.5.1] 2. Further mutations and division form a small tumour in the tissue of origin, the primary tumour.
[bio16-s4.5.2] 3. Angiogenesis (the growth of new blood vessels) supplies the tumour with blood, so it grows larger.
[bio16-s4.5.3] 4. Cancer cells invade the surrounding tissue.
[bio16-s4.5.4] 5. Some cells break away, enter blood vessels or lymph vessels, and are carried around the body.
[bio16-s4.5.5] 6. They leave the vessels in another organ and divide there to form secondary tumours, called metastases.
[bio16-s4.5.6] Diagram: draw normal tissue (a neat single layer), then one mutated cell, a primary tumour with a vessel growing in, cells entering a vessel, a secondary tumour elsewhere. Labels: normal cells, cancer cell with large nucleus, primary tumour, blood vessel, invasion, metastasis, secondary tumour.
[bio16-s4.6.b] Spread is what makes cancer so serious, and the harm tumours do comes from their size, position and appetite for nutrients.
[bio16-s4.6.0] Metastasis is the spread of cancer from the primary tumour to form secondary tumours elsewhere.
[bio16-s4.6.1] Secondary tumours contain the same type of cells as the primary tumour: breast cancer that spreads to the bones is still breast cancer cells, not bone cancer.
[bio16-s4.6.2] Metastasis makes cancer much harder to treat, because the cells are in many places, which is why early detection matters. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio16-s4.6.3] Tumours harm the body by pressing on and damaging organs, and by blocking tubes such as the airways, gut or blood vessels.
[bio16-s4.6.4] They also destroy normal tissue and stop it working, e.g. leukaemia cells crowd out normal blood-forming cells in the bone marrow, causing anaemia (too few red blood cells) and infections.
[bio16-s4.6.5] Tumours use up the body's nutrients, causing weight loss.

CANDIDATES bio22-s1: Pathogens
[bio22-s1.0.b] Some organisms and particles make us ill, and the biology of disease starts with naming them. A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host, the organism it lives in or on.
[bio22-s1.0.0] An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, which is why such diseases can spread through a population.
[bio22-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful, so bacteria and fungi should not be lumped together as 'germs'. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s1.0.2] Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: they invade and destroy host cells or tissues, they release toxins, or they take nutrients from the host.
[bio22-s1.0.3] Invasion means the pathogen destroys cells: viruses burst the cells they reproduce in, and the malaria parasite bursts red blood cells.
[bio22-s1.0.4] Toxins are poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work; the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms.
[bio22-s1.0.5] In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage, for example inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia.
[bio22-s1.1.b] A pathogen only survives if it can keep moving from host to host. So each one is adapted to its host and, often, to a carrier that helps it spread.
[bio22-s1.1.0] Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host.
[bio22-s1.1.1] Many pathogens need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease: the mosquito for malaria and aphids (small sap-sucking insects) for many plant viruses.
[bio22-s1.1.2] The routes by which pathogens pass between hosts are called routes of transmission. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.2.b] A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is neither a cell nor a virus. It is the odd one out among pathogens, because a protein alone causes the disease.
[bio22-s1.2.0] The name comes from proteinaceous infectious particle, a term coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
[bio22-s1.2.1] A prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein, the prion protein, which is found on nerve cells, especially in the brain.
[bio22-s1.2.2] When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction.
[bio22-s1.2.3] Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so that the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope. This is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathy.
[bio22-s1.2.4] Prion diseases take years to appear, are always fatal and have no cure.
[bio22-s1.3.b] Prion diseases have been found in cattle, sheep and humans. Because prions are very hard to destroy, prevention depends on keeping infected material out of the food chain.
[bio22-s1.3.0] Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, mad cow disease) in cattle spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals.
[bio22-s1.3.1] Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans is linked to eating BSE-infected beef and was first described in the UK in 1996.
[bio22-s1.3.2] Scrapie affects sheep. Kuru affected the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
[bio22-s1.3.3] Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants.
[bio22-s1.3.4] The immune system does not attack prions, because they are the body's own protein in a different shape, so the body has no defence against them.
[bio22-s1.3.5] Prevention therefore keeps prions out of the food chain: meat-and-bone meal is banned from the feed of farm animals, and the brain and spinal cord of cattle are removed from food as specified risk material.
[bio22-s1.4.b] A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat. It is an obligate intracellular parasite, meaning it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s1.4.0] Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work.
[bio22-s1.4.1] Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins (molecules on the cell surface that it fits onto), so it infects only certain hosts and tissues: HIV infects helper T cells and influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways.
[bio22-s1.4.2] Human viral diseases include influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) and AIDS (HIV).
[bio22-s1.4.3] Animal viral diseases include foot-and-mouth disease, which affects cattle, sheep and pigs, and avian influenza, which affects birds.
[bio22-s1.4.4] Plant viral disease: tobacco mosaic virus infects tobacco and tomatoes.
[bio22-s1.4.5] Antibiotics don't affect viruses, because viruses have no cell structures for the drugs to target and reproduce inside host cells.
[bio22-s1.5.b] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes with no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, about 1–10 µm long. Only some bacteria are pathogens, but those that are can multiply very quickly inside the body.
[bio22-s1.5.0] Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission (one cell splitting into two) in host tissues, so numbers rise fast.
[bio22-s1.5.1] Most bacteria cause harm by releasing toxins; some invade and destroy tissue.
[bio22-s1.5.2] Most bacterial diseases can be treated with antibiotics, which target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s1.5.3] Antibiotic-resistant strains (bacteria no longer killed by the drug) are increasing, for example MRSA.
[bio22-s1.6.b] Each bacterial disease has a named bacterium and a usual route of spread. Learn them as pairs, and note the plant and cattle examples as well as the human ones.
[bio22-s1.6.0] Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spreads in airborne droplets and mainly affects the lungs.
[bio22-s1.6.1] Food poisoning is caused by Salmonella, which spreads through contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs.
[bio22-s1.6.2] Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae in water contaminated with faeces, and causes severe diarrhoea.
[bio22-s1.6.3] Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani. Its spores in soil enter a wound, and its toxin causes muscle spasms, known as lockjaw.
[bio22-s1.6.4] Lyme disease is caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and is passed on by tick bites, so the tick is the vector.
[bio22-s1.6.5] In plants, fire blight of apple and pear trees is caused by Erwinia amylovora.
[bio22-s1.6.6] In cattle, bovine TB is caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
[bio22-s1.7.b] Fungi are eukaryotes (their cells have a nucleus) with cell walls of chitin. Most are made of thread-like hyphae, and some, such as yeasts, are single cells. Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts.
[bio22-s1.7.0] Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, and the fungus absorbs the products.
[bio22-s1.7.1] Fungi spread by spores, which are often carried by wind, water or contact.
[bio22-s1.7.2] Athlete's foot and ringworm are skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (the protein in skin); ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
[bio22-s1.7.3] Thrush is caused by the yeast Candida albicans in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check.
[bio22-s1.7.4] Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
[bio22-s1.7.5] Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.
[bio22-s1.8.b] Fungi infect plants and animals as well as people. The examples below affect trees, cereal crops and amphibians.
[bio22-s1.8.0] Ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, is killing ash trees across Europe.
[bio22-s1.8.1] Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, is spread by elm bark beetles, which act as vectors.
[bio22-s1.8.2] Cereal rusts such as wheat rust are caused by Puccinia species.
[bio22-s1.8.3] Chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.9.b] Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are neither animals, plants nor fungi (kingdom Protista). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host. Malaria is one example.
[bio22-s1.9.0] Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, which is passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector.
[bio22-s1.9.1] The parasite reproduces first in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia (too few red blood cells).
[bio22-s1.9.2] Malaria is controlled with insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining of standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs such as artemisinin. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.10.b] Other protists cause gut and blood diseases in people, and one protist-like organism caused a famine in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.10.0] Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, which spreads in water or food contaminated with faeces.
[bio22-s1.10.1] African sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei, which is spread by tsetse flies, the vector.
[bio22-s1.10.2] Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus, being thread-like and spread by spores, but it is classed with the protists.
[bio22-s1.10.3] Its spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather, and it rots leaves and tubers.
[bio22-s1.10.4] Potato late blight caused the Irish Great Famine from 1845, because potatoes were the main crop and the disease destroyed them. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.11.b] A parasite lives in or on another organism, its host, feeding on it and harming it. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods. Endoparasites live inside the host.
[bio22-s1.11.0] Tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers.
[bio22-s1.11.1] Humans become infected with tapeworms by eating undercooked infected pork or beef.
[bio22-s1.11.2] Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) is a flatworm of sheep and cattle that damages the liver.
[bio22-s1.11.3] The liver fluke needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.11.4] Onchocerca volvulus is a roundworm spread by blackflies that causes river blindness. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.12.b] Ectoparasites live on the outside of the host. Worms inside the body are treated with drugs, and prevention targets how the parasite reaches us.
[bio22-s1.12.0] Head lice are ectoparasites that feed on blood from the scalp.
[bio22-s1.12.1] Ticks feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio22-s1.12.2] Parasitic worms are treated with anthelmintic drugs that kill them, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s1.12.3] Prevention: cook meat thoroughly, maintain sanitation, and control the second host or vector, because breaking the life cycle stops the parasite spreading.
[bio22-s1.13.b] The six types of pathogen differ in whether they are cells, what genetic material they carry, their size and how they are treated. The treatment follows from the structure, which is why antibiotics work only on bacteria.
[bio22-s1.13.0] Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment.
[bio22-s1.13.1] Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; treated with antivirals, not antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.2] Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; treated with antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.3] Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; from single cells to large networks of hyphae; treated with antifungals.
[bio22-s1.13.4] Protist: eukaryotic and mostly single-celled; DNA; treated with antiprotozoal drugs such as antimalarials.
[bio22-s1.13.5] Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; from millimetres to metres in size; treated with anthelmintics.

CANDIDATES bio22-s4: Viruses: replication
[bio22-s4.0.b] A virus is not a cell, so it lacks the machinery that every living cell uses to build new copies of itself. This is why a virus can only reproduce by taking over a host cell (the living cell it infects).
[bio22-s4.0.0] A virus has no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), almost no enzymes and no energy supply, so it can't make anything by itself.
[bio22-s4.0.1] It reproduces by delivering its nucleic acid (its DNA or RNA) into a host cell. The host cell then makes new virus particles using its own ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier), nucleotides (the building blocks of nucleic acids) and amino acids (the building blocks of proteins).
[bio22-s4.1.b] Every virus follows the same basic cycle of six stages, which ends with new viruses leaving the cell. The first three get the virus's nucleic acid inside the right kind of cell and free to act.
[bio22-s4.1.0] Stage 1, attachment: proteins on the capsid (the protein coat) or the envelope (the outer membrane some viruses have) bind to specific receptor proteins on the host cell membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.1] HIV binds to the CD4 receptor on helper T cells, and the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds to the ACE2 receptor on cells lining the airways. Receptor binding decides which cells a virus can infect, because only cells with the matching receptor can be attached to.
[bio22-s4.1.2] Stage 2, entry: a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria) injects only its nucleic acid through the bacterial wall, leaving the empty coat outside.
[bio22-s4.1.3] Animal viruses enter whole, either by the host membrane folding in around them (endocytosis) or, for enveloped viruses, by the envelope fusing with the host membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.4] Stage 3, uncoating: inside the cell, the capsid breaks down and releases the viral nucleic acid, so its genes can be read.
[bio22-s4.2.b] Once the viral nucleic acid is free inside the cell, the virus uses the cell's machinery to make its parts, put them together and escape. The new viruses then repeat the cycle in other cells.
[bio22-s4.2.0] Stage 4, replication and synthesis: the viral genes take over the cell. Viral mRNA (the message copied from a gene) is made, and the host's ribosomes translate it into viral proteins, which are capsid proteins and viral enzymes.
[bio22-s4.2.1] In stage 4 the viral nucleic acid is copied many times, and host-cell activities are often shut down so that the cell's resources go into making viruses.
[bio22-s4.2.2] Stage 5, assembly: new nucleic acid and capsid proteins come together to form many new virus particles, often hundreds per cell. Viruses are assembled from parts, not grown or divided.
[bio22-s4.2.3] Stage 6, release: either the cell bursts (lysis), releasing all the viruses at once and killing the cell, or, for enveloped viruses, each new virus pushes out through the membrane and takes a piece of it as its envelope (budding), so the cell can survive for a time.
[bio22-s4.2.4] The new viruses infect neighbouring cells, and the cycle repeats.
[bio22-s4.2.5] Diagram: the lytic cycle of a bacteriophage in five or six sketches in a circle: phage attached by tail fibres; DNA injected; phage DNA copied and proteins made; new phages assembled; cell bursting. Label phage, bacterial cell wall, phage DNA, bacterial chromosome, new phages.
[bio22-s4.2.6] Diagram: an enveloped animal virus, such as influenza, entering a cell by endocytosis or fusion and leaving by budding, with the host membrane forming the new envelope.
[bio22-s4.3.b] A virus does not always burst its host straight away. Some bacteriophages (and some human viruses) can instead hide inside the host for a time, so there are two possible routes after infection.
[bio22-s4.3.0] Lytic cycle: the virus replicates immediately, following the stages above, and the host cell bursts. Example: bacteriophage T4 infecting the bacterium E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.1] Lysogenic cycle: some viruses insert their DNA into the host cell's chromosome, where it stays inactive and is copied every time the cell divides, so all daughter cells carry it.
[bio22-s4.3.2] In bacteria the inserted DNA is called a prophage. Example: bacteriophage lambda in E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.3] A trigger, such as stress like UV light, can cut the viral DNA out of the chromosome and start the lytic cycle.
[bio22-s4.3.4] Latency in human viruses: some viruses hide in cells in an inactive state for years and reactivate later.
[bio22-s4.3.5] Herpes simplex virus stays in nerve cells after the first infection and reactivates to cause repeated cold sores.
[bio22-s4.3.6] Varicella-zoster virus causes chickenpox, stays in nerve cells, and can reactivate decades later as shingles.
[bio22-s4.3.7] A latent virus is hidden from the immune system and from antiviral drugs, because it is not making viral proteins or new viruses that they could target.
[bio22-s4.4.b] Viruses cause disease in several different ways, from simply destroying cells to turning the body's own defences against it. Each mode of action follows from what a virus does to the cells it infects.
[bio22-s4.4.0] Killing cells: lysis destroys infected cells. Influenza destroys cells lining the airways, causing a sore throat and cough, and the cold virus damages cells of the nose and throat.
[bio22-s4.4.1] Disrupting cell function: the virus diverts the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and energy to making viruses, so the cell stops its normal work.
[bio22-s4.4.2] Damaging the immune system: HIV infects and destroys helper T cells, the white blood cells that coordinate the immune response.
[bio22-s4.4.3] As helper T cell numbers fall over years, the immune system can no longer coordinate its responses, and the person develops AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome).
[bio22-s4.4.4] People with AIDS die of opportunistic infections and cancers, which a healthy immune system would control but which take hold when the immune system is weakened.
[bio22-s4.4.5] Causing cancer: some viruses insert genes that interfere with the control of cell division, e.g. human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio22-s4.4.6] Triggering harmful immune responses: much of the fever, aches and tissue damage in viral illness is caused by the body's own immune response (inflammation) to infected cells.
[bio22-s4.5.b] Viruses are grouped by the type of nucleic acid they carry, and this affects where they replicate, which enzymes they need and how fast they change. Comparing the two groups is a likely exam question.
[bio22-s4.5.0] Genetic material: a DNA virus has DNA as its genetic material; an RNA virus has RNA.
[bio22-s4.5.1] DNA viruses include herpes simplex and varicella-zoster (chickenpox) viruses, human papillomavirus (HPV), adenoviruses, the smallpox virus, and bacteriophages T4 and lambda.
[bio22-s4.5.2] RNA viruses include influenza, measles, mumps, polio, rhinoviruses (the common cold), SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Ebola and tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s4.5.3] Where they replicate: most DNA viruses enter the host nucleus and use the host's own enzymes, such as DNA polymerase and RNA polymerase, to copy their DNA and make mRNA.
[bio22-s4.5.4] Poxviruses such as the smallpox virus are an exception: they replicate in the cytoplasm with their own enzymes.
[bio22-s4.5.5] Most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm and must carry or code for their own enzyme, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, to copy RNA from RNA, because host cells have no enzyme that does this.
[bio22-s4.5.6] Mutation rate: the enzyme that copies RNA has no proofreading (no checking for mistakes), so RNA viruses make many more copying errors and mutate much faster than DNA viruses, whose DNA is copied by host polymerases that proofread.
[bio22-s4.5.7] RNA viruses such as influenza and HIV change their surface antigens quickly, so new strains escape existing immunity. This is why flu vaccines are updated every year, and drug resistance appears quickly.
[bio22-s4.6.b] Once inside the cell, an RNA virus's genes still have to be turned into proteins. RNA viruses do this in one of two ways, depending on the virus.
[bio22-s4.6.0] In some RNA viruses, such as poliovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the RNA acts directly as mRNA when it enters the cell and is translated at once by the host's ribosomes.
[bio22-s4.6.1] In others, such as influenza and measles, the RNA must first be copied into a complementary strand that acts as mRNA.
[bio22-s4.6.2] DNA is a more stable molecule than RNA, which fits with DNA viruses mutating more slowly.
[bio22-s4.6.3] Mutation as a factor in disease emergence is covered further in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s4.7.b] Retroviruses break the usual rule that information flows from DNA to RNA. HIV is the key example, and its cycle has a few extra steps compared with the general cycle.
[bio22-s4.7.0] A retrovirus is an RNA virus that makes a DNA copy of its RNA inside the host cell, using its own enzyme, reverse transcriptase, which reverses the usual direction of DNA to RNA. HIV is an example.
[bio22-s4.7.1] HIV replication, step 1: its envelope protein binds the CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
[bio22-s4.7.2] Step 2: the envelope fuses with the cell membrane, and the capsid enters.
[bio22-s4.7.3] Step 3: reverse transcriptase makes double-stranded DNA from the viral RNA.
[bio22-s4.7.4] Step 4: a second viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this DNA into the host's chromosome, where it is called a provirus and can stay hidden for years.
[bio22-s4.7.5] Step 5: when the cell is activated, the provirus is transcribed into viral mRNA and new viral RNA, and viral proteins are made.
[bio22-s4.7.6] Step 6: new viruses assemble and bud from the cell, taking an envelope of host membrane.
[bio22-s4.7.7] Reverse transcriptase makes many errors, so HIV mutates very quickly, which is one reason there is still no effective HIV vaccine.
[bio22-s4.7.8] Diagram: an HIV particle (envelope with surface proteins, capsid, two RNA strands and reverse transcriptase), and a flow of the six steps into and out of a helper T cell. Label receptor, viral RNA, viral DNA, host chromosome (provirus) and budding virus.
[bio22-s4.8.b] Antibiotics don't work on viruses, so viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs that interrupt one stage of the replication cycle. Each drug targets a step that depends on a viral protein.
[bio22-s4.8.0] Antiviral drugs block a step in the cycle that uses a viral protein, so the host cell isn't harmed.
[bio22-s4.8.1] Aciclovir blocks the DNA polymerase of herpes viruses, which treats cold sores and shingles.
[bio22-s4.8.2] Oseltamivir blocks neuraminidase, the influenza enzyme that releases new viruses from the cell surface.
[bio22-s4.8.3] HIV drugs include reverse transcriptase inhibitors, of which zidovudine (AZT), approved in 1987, was the first.
[bio22-s4.8.4] HIV drugs also include protease inhibitors, which stop new viral proteins being cut into their working form.
[bio22-s4.8.5] HIV is treated with a combination of several antiretroviral drugs at once (antiretroviral therapy).
[bio22-s4.8.6] Antiretroviral therapy keeps the virus at very low levels and lets the immune system recover, but it doesn't cure the infection, because the provirus remains in the host DNA.
[bio22-s4.8.7] Using several drugs together makes resistance much less likely, since the virus would need several mutations at once.
[bio22-s4.9.b] The syllabus asks you to model how viruses replicate within cells. A model turns the six stages into something you can draw, handle and use to make predictions, while leaving out some detail.
[bio22-s4.9.0] To model the cycle, first list the stages in order: attachment, entry, uncoating, replication and synthesis, assembly and release. Then draw each as a labelled sketch or box, showing the host-cell part used at that stage (receptor, membrane, ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides).
[bio22-s4.9.1] Join the stages with arrows into a cycle, because the new viruses go on to infect other cells.
[bio22-s4.9.2] Mark how a DNA virus, an RNA virus and a retrovirus differ at stage 4.
[bio22-s4.9.3] Mark where an antiviral drug would act.
[bio22-s4.9.4] A physical model can use card or modelling clay to represent viruses and cells, to show attachment and budding.
[bio22-s4.9.5] The model shows the order of events, the dependence on host machinery, and why one infected cell releases many viruses. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s4.9.6] The model simplifies the timing (a cycle can take minutes to hours), the numbers released, the molecular steps, and the differences between viruses.
[bio22-s4.9.7] Worked prediction: if each infected cell releases 100 viruses and each of those infects a new cell, two cycles from one virus give 100 × 100 = 10,000 viruses. This is an illustrative calculation, not a figure for a real virus.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio25-s2: Osmosis in food preservation and plant health
[bio25-s2.0.b] Food goes off because microorganisms feed on it, and microorganisms need water to live. Adding a lot of salt or sugar turns osmosis against them: it pulls water out of their cells.
[bio25-s2.0.0] Food spoils mainly because bacteria and fungi (moulds and yeasts) grow on it, digesting it and making it unsafe or unpleasant to eat. Like all cells, they need water for their enzymes to work and to grow and reproduce.
[bio25-s2.0.1] Mechanism, step 1: a high concentration of salt or sugar is added to the food.
[bio25-s2.0.2] 2. The food's water is now a concentrated (hypertonic) solution compared with the cytoplasm (the watery contents) of any microorganism on it.
[bio25-s2.0.3] 3. Water leaves the microbial cells by osmosis, moving from the more dilute cytoplasm to the more concentrated solution outside.
[bio25-s2.0.4] 4. The cells are dehydrated and plasmolysed (the cell membrane shrinks away from the cell wall; bacteria and fungi have cell walls), so their enzymes can't work and they can't grow or reproduce; many die.
[bio25-s2.0.5] The salt or sugar also binds water in the food, so less free water is available to any microbe.
[bio25-s2.0.6] Preserving by drying (e.g. dried fruit and dried fish) works by removing water directly, which has the same effect on microbes. It is related to osmosis but is not osmosis itself.
[bio25-s2.1.b] Three traditional methods preserve food with salt, sugar or acid. Salt and sugar work by osmosis, but acid works in a different way, by its low pH.
[bio25-s2.1.0] Salting: meat and fish are rubbed with dry salt or soaked in strong brine (salt solution), e.g. bacon, ham and salted fish. Salt also draws water out of the food itself by osmosis, drying it further, so even less water is left for microbes.
[bio25-s2.1.1] Sugaring: fruit is preserved in a high concentration of sugar, e.g. jam, marmalade and candied fruit; fruit is also stored in heavy syrup.
[bio25-s2.1.2] Honey keeps for a very long time because its sugar concentration is so high that microbes lose water to it instead of growing.
[bio25-s2.1.3] Pickling: vegetables kept in vinegar are preserved mainly by the low pH of the acid (ethanoic acid), which stops enzymes and microbes working, not by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.1.4] Brine pickles (salt solutions) combine osmosis with acid made by fermentation (microbes breaking down sugars in the vegetable), so both effects protect the food.
[bio25-s2.2.b] Osmotic preservation is useful but not perfect. To evaluate it, weigh what it does well against where it fails and what it does to our health.
[bio25-s2.2.0] Advantages: it is cheap, simple and traditional; it needs no refrigeration, so it was used for long journeys and winter stores; and it works against both bacteria and fungi.
[bio25-s2.2.1] Limits: some microorganisms tolerate high salt or sugar concentrations, especially moulds and yeasts, so jam can still grow mould on its surface once opened and should be refrigerated.
[bio25-s2.2.2] Preservation fails if the salt or sugar concentration is too low, because there is then not enough of a concentration difference to draw water out of microbes. For example, reduced-sugar jams spoil faster.
[bio25-s2.2.3] Osmotic preservation slows microbial growth but doesn't sterilise the food (kill all microbes). It is usually combined with other methods, such as cooking, sealing, refrigeration, smoking and acids.
[bio25-s2.2.4] Health link: salted foods add much salt to the diet, and high salt intake is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.
[bio25-s2.2.5] Sugary preserves add sugar, which is linked to tooth decay and obesity. So salting and sugaring are used less today, and refrigeration and freezing more. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio25-s2.2.6] Osmosis also affects food preparation: salting sliced vegetables, e.g. cucumber and aubergine, draws water out of them.
[bio25-s2.2.7] Limp vegetables, e.g. celery and lettuce, become crisp again when placed in cold water, because their cells take in water by osmosis and become turgid (swollen and firm with water).
[bio25-s2.3.b] Plant cells stay firm because they are filled with water taken in by osmosis, and this water pressure does several jobs for the plant.
[bio25-s2.3.0] Healthy plants keep their cells turgid (full of water and pushing outwards) by taking in water by osmosis into the vacuole (the large sap-filled space) of each cell.
[bio25-s2.3.1] The turgor pressure of cells pressing against their walls supports non-woody plants, leaves and flowers, holding them upright and spreading leaves to the light for photosynthesis.
[bio25-s2.3.2] Turgor pressure drives cell enlargement: young cells expand as water enters their vacuoles, so plant growth depends on water uptake by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.3.3] Guard cells (the pair of cells around each stoma, the tiny pore in a leaf) open the stomata when they become turgid and close them when they become flaccid (soft and limp from losing water), controlling gas exchange and water loss. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.3.4] Wilting: when water is lost from the leaves by transpiration (evaporation from the leaf) faster than the roots take it up, the cells lose water, become flaccid, and the plant wilts.
[bio25-s2.3.5] Wilting closes the stomata, so less carbon dioxide gets in and it reduces photosynthesis and growth. It is reversed if water is supplied soon enough; prolonged wilting kills the plant.
[bio25-s2.4.b] Roots rely on the soil water being more dilute than their own cells. If that balance flips, osmosis runs the wrong way and the plant loses water even though the soil is wet.
[bio25-s2.4.0] Roots take in water by osmosis because the soil water is normally more dilute than the cell sap (the solution in the vacuole) of the root cells. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s2.4.1] If the soil water becomes more concentrated than the contents of the root cells, water stops entering the roots and can leave them by osmosis. The root cells become plasmolysed, the plant cannot take up enough water, and it wilts and may die, even in moist soil.
[bio25-s2.4.2] Causes of salty soil water: flooding of coastal land by seawater (e.g. storm surges), and salt spread on roads in icy weather, which damages roadside trees and hedges.
[bio25-s2.4.3] Irrigation (watering crops artificially) in hot, dry regions is another cause: water evaporates and leaves salts behind in the soil (salinisation), lowering crop yields.
[bio25-s2.4.4] Fertiliser burn: applying too much fertiliser, especially concentrated fertiliser close to roots or on dry soil, makes the soil solution too concentrated, so water leaves the roots and the leaves turn brown and scorched.
[bio25-s2.4.5] Advice to avoid fertiliser burn: apply the recommended amount, spread it evenly and water it in. Salt was used in the past as a weedkiller for the same reason.
[bio25-s2.4.6] Some plants of salty habitats, such as salt marshes and seashores, survive because their root cells hold a higher solute concentration than the salty soil water, so water still enters by osmosis.
[bio25-s2.4.7] Sustainability link: soil salinisation from irrigation, and seawater flooding linked to rising sea levels and stronger storms, reduce the land available for crops. Careful irrigation and drainage reduce salinisation.

CANDIDATES bio29-s4: Blood
[bio29-s4.0.b] Blood is a tissue, like muscle or bone, but a liquid one. It is made of cells and cell fragments carried in a fluid, and each part has its own job.
[bio29-s4.0.0] Blood is a liquid tissue made of cells and cell fragments suspended in a liquid, the plasma. An adult has about 5 litres of blood.
[bio29-s4.0.1] If blood, with a chemical added to stop it clotting, is spun in a centrifuge (a machine that spins samples so the heaviest parts sink), it separates into about 55% plasma, a straw-coloured liquid on top, and about 45% cells.
[bio29-s4.0.2] The cells form a thin pale layer of white blood cells and platelets above a dark red layer of red blood cells at the bottom. Red blood cells make up nearly all of the cell volume.
[bio29-s4.0.3] Numbers in a healthy adult: red blood cells about 5 million per mm³ (cubic millimetre); white blood cells about 4,000 to 11,000 per mm³; platelets about 150,000 to 400,000 per mm³. Red cells therefore greatly outnumber white cells, by several hundred to one.
[bio29-s4.0.4] All blood cells and platelets are made in the red bone marrow, found in spongy bone such as the sternum, ribs, vertebrae, pelvis and the ends of long bones. They come from stem cells (unspecialised cells that can become other cell types) that divide by mitosis.
[bio29-s4.0.5] Functions of blood: transport (oxygen, carbon dioxide, nutrients, wastes, hormones and heat), defence against infection, clotting to seal wounds, and keeping the internal environment steady (pH, temperature and water balance).
[bio29-s4.0.6] Diagram: a stained blood smear under a light microscope. Draw many red blood cells (pale centres, no nucleus), one or two white blood cells (larger, with a stained nucleus: a phagocyte with a lobed nucleus and a lymphocyte with a large round nucleus) and small platelets. Label each.
[bio29-s4.0.7] Diagram: draw a centrifuged tube labelled plasma, white cells and platelets layer, and red cells layer, with percentages.
[bio29-s4.1.b] Plasma is the liquid part of blood, and everything else in blood is carried in it or by it. Because it is mostly water, it can dissolve and transport a huge range of substances around the body.
[bio29-s4.1.0] Plasma is about 90% water, with substances dissolved or suspended in it. Water is a good solvent, so many substances dissolve in it, and it also carries heat.
[bio29-s4.1.1] Dissolved substances transported in plasma: nutrients (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol, vitamins and minerals); wastes (urea, from the liver to the kidneys, and most of the carbon dioxide); hormones; and salts, such as sodium, chloride, calcium and hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio29-s4.1.2] Carbon dioxide is carried mostly in the plasma as hydrogencarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻). They form in red blood cells, by the enzyme carbonic anhydrase (CO₂ + H₂O → carbonic acid → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻).
[bio29-s4.1.3] A small amount of carbon dioxide is simply dissolved in plasma, and some is carried by haemoglobin. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s4.1.4] Plasma proteins: fibrinogen and other clotting factors, for clotting; antibodies, for defence against pathogens; and albumin, which holds water in the blood by osmosis and carries some substances. Serum is plasma with the clotting proteins removed.
[bio29-s4.1.5] Plasma distributes heat from active organs, such as working muscles, around the body. Its hydrogencarbonate and protein buffers (chemicals that resist changes in acidity) keep the blood pH near 7.4.
[bio29-s4.2.b] Red blood cells carry oxygen from the lungs to every cell in the body. Almost every feature of their structure helps them do that one job well.
[bio29-s4.2.0] Red blood cells, or erythrocytes, are biconcave discs (round and thinner in the centre than at the edge), about 7 to 8 µm across. They carry oxygen from the lungs to all body cells.
[bio29-s4.2.1] The biconcave shape gives a large surface area for their volume, so oxygen diffuses in and out quickly, and every part of the cell is close to the surface.
[bio29-s4.2.2] There is no nucleus, and no mitochondria, in the mature cell, which leaves more room for haemoglobin. The cell is also packed with haemoglobin, so each cell can carry a lot of oxygen.
[bio29-s4.2.3] The cell is small and flexible, so it squeezes through capillaries in single file, close to the capillary wall. This shortens the distance oxygen must diffuse.
[bio29-s4.2.4] Because they have no mitochondria, red blood cells respire anaerobically (without oxygen), so they do not use up the oxygen they carry.
[bio29-s4.2.5] Haemoglobin is a red protein made of four polypeptide chains, each with a haem group containing iron, so each haemoglobin molecule can carry four oxygen molecules. In the lungs, where oxygen is plentiful, haemoglobin combines with oxygen to form oxyhaemoglobin, which is bright red.
[bio29-s4.2.6] In respiring tissues, where oxygen is low, oxyhaemoglobin releases its oxygen. The reaction is reversible. Haemoglobin + oxygen ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
[bio29-s4.3.b] Red blood cells wear out and must be constantly replaced. Problems with their number or with haemoglobin affect how much oxygen reaches the tissues.
[bio29-s4.3.0] Red blood cells are made in the red bone marrow and live about 120 days. With no nucleus they cannot repair themselves, so they wear out.
[bio29-s4.3.1] Old cells are broken down in the liver and spleen. The iron is recycled to make new haemoglobin, and the rest of the haem is converted into bile pigments, which are excreted in bile.
[bio29-s4.3.2] The kidney hormone EPO stimulates the production of red blood cells. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio29-s4.3.3] Anaemia is a shortage of red blood cells or haemoglobin, so less oxygen is carried, causing tiredness and pale skin. A common cause is iron deficiency, because iron is needed to make haem.
[bio29-s4.3.4] Carbon monoxide, in cigarette smoke and from faulty gas appliances, combines with haemoglobin far more tightly than oxygen does, so less oxygen is carried and it can kill.
[bio29-s4.3.5] Sickle-cell anaemia is a further disorder of red blood cells, named here only.
[bio29-s4.4.b] White blood cells are the body's defenders against infection. They differ from red cells in several ways, which makes a good comparison to learn.
[bio29-s4.4.0] White blood cells, or leucocytes, defend the body against infection. Unlike red blood cells, they have a nucleus, are larger and colourless (they have no haemoglobin), and are far fewer in number.
[bio29-s4.4.1] They are made in the red bone marrow, and many can squeeze out through capillary walls into the tissues, where infections often are.
[bio29-s4.4.2] There are two broad groups: phagocytes, which engulf and digest pathogens (phagocytosis), and lymphocytes, which produce antibodies or kill infected cells.
[bio29-s4.4.3] The number of white cells rises during an infection, so a white cell count is used in diagnosing infections. Leukaemia is a cancer of the white-cell-forming cells.
[bio29-s4.5.b] Platelets are not whole cells but fragments, and they are the first responders when a blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.5.0] Platelets, or thrombocytes, are small fragments of cytoplasm enclosed in membrane, with no nucleus. They break off from large cells in the red bone marrow.
[bio29-s4.5.1] Platelets live about 7 to 10 days and start the clotting of blood.
[bio29-s4.5.2] Clotting matters because it stops blood loss from a damaged vessel and seals the wound so that pathogens cannot enter. It is part of the first line of defence against infection.
[bio29-s4.6.b] Clotting is a chain of events in which one change triggers the next, ending in a mesh of protein threads that plugs the wound. Learn the stages in order.
[bio29-s4.6.0] 1. A blood vessel is damaged.
[bio29-s4.6.1] 2. Platelets stick to the exposed damaged wall and to each other, forming a temporary plug.
[bio29-s4.6.2] 3. The platelets and damaged tissue release clotting factors.
[bio29-s4.6.3] 4. A series of reactions, needing calcium ions and clotting proteins made in the liver with the help of vitamin K, converts the inactive plasma protein prothrombin into the active enzyme thrombin.
[bio29-s4.6.4] 5. Thrombin converts the soluble plasma protein fibrinogen into insoluble threads of fibrin.
[bio29-s4.6.5] 6. The fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells and platelets, forming a clot.
[bio29-s4.6.6] 7. At the surface the clot dries and hardens into a scab, under which the tissue repairs. Calcium and vitamin K are both needed for clotting. Haemophilia is an inherited disorder in which a clotting factor is missing.
[bio29-s4.6.7] An unwanted clot inside a vessel, a thrombosis, can block an artery and cause a heart attack or stroke. Diagram: a flow chart of damage, platelets, clotting factors (with Ca²⁺ and vitamin K), prothrombin, thrombin, fibrinogen, fibrin mesh and clot. Add fibrin threads trapping red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.7.b] Your ABO blood group depends on molecules on the surface of your red blood cells. The plasma then carries antibodies matched to whichever molecules your own cells lack.
[bio29-s4.7.0] A blood group is set by the antigens on the surface membrane of a person's red blood cells. An antigen is a molecule that can trigger an immune response. In the ABO system there are two antigens, A and B.
[bio29-s4.7.1] The plasma naturally contains antibodies against the ABO antigen or antigens that a person's own red cells lack. This stops the body attacking its own cells.
[bio29-s4.7.2] Group A has antigen A on the red cells and anti-B antibodies in the plasma. Group B has antigen B and anti-A antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.3] Group AB has both antigens and no antibodies. Group O has no antigens and both anti-A and anti-B antibodies.
[bio29-s4.7.4] Agglutination: if red cells carrying an antigen meet the matching antibody, for example A cells meeting anti-A, the antibodies bind to the antigens and stick the cells together in clumps.
[bio29-s4.7.5] In a transfusion the clumps can block small blood vessels, and the donor cells are destroyed, or burst. This can cause kidney failure and death.
[bio29-s4.7.6] Diagram: four red blood cells, one for each group, showing the antigens on the membrane (A, B, both, none), with the antibodies in the plasma written beside each. Add a sketch of agglutination, with antibodies linking red cells into a clump.
[bio29-s4.8.b] Transfusion works only if the donor's red cells do not meet an antibody in the recipient's plasma that matches their antigens. The rule follows from the ABO antigens and antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.0] What matters is the donor's red-cell antigens and the recipient's antibodies. A recipient can safely receive red cells that carry no antigen against which their own plasma has antibodies.
[bio29-s4.8.1] Group O red cells carry no antigens, so they can be given to anyone, and group O is called the universal donor. This applies to red cells: group O plasma contains anti-A and anti-B.
[bio29-s4.8.2] Group AB people have no antibodies, so they can receive red cells of any ABO group, and group AB is the universal recipient. In practice, hospitals give the same group whenever possible.
[bio29-s4.8.3] Compatibility: group O can receive O only; group A can receive A or O; group B can receive B or O; group AB can receive A, B, AB or O.
[bio29-s4.8.4] Blood-group testing: a drop of blood is mixed separately with anti-A serum and with anti-B serum. Clumping with anti-A only means group A; clumping with anti-B only means group B; clumping with both means AB; clumping with neither means O.
[bio29-s4.8.5] A third test, with anti-D, tests the Rhesus group.
[bio29-s4.9.b] The Rhesus factor is a second red-cell antigen system, on top of ABO. Unlike the ABO antibodies, the Rhesus antibody has to be learned by the immune system first.
[bio29-s4.9.0] The Rhesus (Rh) factor is another red-cell antigen, the D antigen. People whose red cells carry it are Rhesus positive (Rh+), and those without it are Rhesus negative (Rh−).
[bio29-s4.9.1] A person's full blood group combines both systems, for example O negative or A positive.
[bio29-s4.9.2] Unlike anti-A and anti-B, anti-D antibodies are not naturally present. A Rh− person makes them only after Rh+ red cells enter their blood, for example through a transfusion of Rh+ blood or during pregnancy.
[bio29-s4.9.3] A first exposure therefore does little harm, but a second exposure triggers a rapid, strong attack on Rh+ cells. So Rh− patients must receive Rh− blood.
[bio29-s4.10.b] A Rhesus negative mother carrying a Rhesus positive baby can become sensitised to her baby's blood, and that may endanger a later baby. The problem is called haemolytic disease of the newborn, and it can be prevented.
[bio29-s4.10.0] 1. A Rh− mother carries a Rh+ baby, inherited from a Rh+ father.
[bio29-s4.10.1] 2. Normally the blood of mother and fetus does not mix, but at birth, or after a miscarriage, abortion or injury, some fetal red cells enter the mother's blood.
[bio29-s4.10.2] 3. Her immune system makes anti-D antibodies and memory cells, and she is now sensitised. The first baby is usually unaffected, because it is born before many antibodies are made.
[bio29-s4.10.3] 4. In a later pregnancy with a Rh+ baby, her anti-D antibodies cross the placenta and destroy the fetus's red blood cells.
[bio29-s4.10.4] 5. The baby can suffer severe anaemia and jaundice, brain damage or death.
[bio29-s4.10.5] Prevention: a Rh− mother is given an injection of anti-D antibodies, anti-D immunoglobulin, during pregnancy and within 72 hours after the birth of a Rh+ baby, or after a miscarriage or injury.
[bio29-s4.10.6] These antibodies destroy any fetal Rh+ cells in her blood before her own immune system can respond, so she does not make her own anti-D or memory cells. The injected antibodies are broken down over a few weeks.
[bio29-s4.10.7] This is artificial passive immunity (antibodies given ready-made, not made by the body). Every pregnant woman's blood group is tested early in pregnancy for this reason.
[bio29-s4.11.b] Knowing a person's blood group saves lives in several ways, from transfusions to pregnancy care. Each use below comes from knowing the groups.
[bio29-s4.11.0] Safe blood transfusion: before a transfusion, the patient's ABO and Rhesus groups are tested, and the donor blood is cross-matched, meaning it is mixed with the patient's plasma to check that no clumping occurs.
[bio29-s4.11.1] This prevents fatal transfusion reactions and allows transfusions in surgery, after accidents, in childbirth, for anaemia and in cancer treatment.
[bio29-s4.11.2] Emergencies: group O Rh− red cells are given when there is no time to test the patient, because they carry no A, B or D antigens. Only a minority of people are O negative, so their donations are especially valuable.
[bio29-s4.11.3] Pregnancy: identifying Rh− mothers and giving anti-D has made haemolytic disease of the newborn rare.
[bio29-s4.11.4] Blood donation: knowing which groups are needed lets the blood service plan donor sessions and stocks. Donors of all groups are needed, because blood can be stored only for a limited time.
[bio29-s4.11.5] In Ireland, donations are collected and tested by the Irish Blood Transfusion Service, and donated blood is screened for infections.
[bio29-s4.11.6] Organ and bone-marrow transplants: donors and recipients are matched for ABO group and tissue type to reduce the chance of rejection.
[bio29-s4.11.7] Nature of Science: before blood groups were known, transfusions often killed patients. Karl Landsteiner discovered the ABO blood groups in 1901, for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930, which made safe transfusion possible.
[bio29-s4.11.8] In 1940 Landsteiner and Alexander Wiener described the Rhesus factor, named after the rhesus monkeys whose blood was used in the experiments.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio35-s1: Genetic engineering
[bio35-s1.0.b] Genetic engineering lets scientists give an organism a new trait by changing its DNA directly, rather than waiting for the trait to arise by breeding. Three terms name the technique, its products and the DNA it creates.
[bio35-s1.0.0] Genetic engineering (genetic modification, GM) is the deliberate change of an organism’s DNA by artificial means, usually by cutting a gene out of one organism and inserting it into the DNA of another, often a different species.
[bio35-s1.0.1] The organism produced is a genetically modified organism (GMO). One carrying a gene from another species is transgenic, which means it holds DNA that did not come from its own species.
[bio35-s1.0.2] DNA made by joining DNA from two different sources is recombinant DNA, because the pieces have been recombined into a new combination that did not exist before.
[bio35-s1.1.b] A gene from one species can work in another, and that is what separates genetic engineering from older methods of improving organisms. The technique is also quite recent.
[bio35-s1.1.0] It works because the genetic code is universal: every organism uses the same code to read DNA, so a host cell reads a gene from any species and makes the same protein.
[bio35-s1.1.1] The inserted gene must include the instructions the host needs to switch it on, so that it is transcribed (copied into mRNA) and translated (built into a protein) in the host.
[bio35-s1.1.2] Selective breeding (artificial selection, where humans choose which organisms reproduce) mixes whole sets of genes between members of the same or closely related species, and takes many generations.
[bio35-s1.1.3] Genetic engineering instead moves one chosen gene, even between unrelated species (for example bacterium to plant, or human to bacterium), in one generation. This is why selective breeding is not genetic engineering.
[bio35-s1.1.4] In 1973 Stanley Cohen and Herbert Boyer in the USA inserted foreign DNA into a plasmid (a small ring of DNA found in bacteria) and put it into bacteria, which copied it: the first genetically engineered organism.
[bio35-s1.1.5] Bacterial restriction enzymes (enzymes that cut DNA), discovered in the late 1960s and early 1970s, made this possible.
[bio35-s1.2.b] A gene cannot get into a host cell on its own, so it is carried in. The carrier is called a vector, and several kinds are used depending on the host.
[bio35-s1.2.0] A vector is a carrier that takes the gene into the host cell.
[bio35-s1.2.1] A plasmid is a small circle of double-stranded DNA found in bacteria, separate from the main chromosome. It copies itself independently and is passed on when the bacterium divides, so the new gene is copied too.
[bio35-s1.2.2] Plasmids are the most common vector for bacteria and yeast.
[bio35-s1.2.3] Plasmids used as vectors usually carry a marker gene (for example for resistance to an antibiotic) so that cells which have taken them up can be identified.
[bio35-s1.2.4] Modified viruses are also vectors, because viruses naturally inject their DNA into cells. Bacteriophages (viruses that infect bacteria) are used for bacteria. Harmless modified viruses are used for human and animal cells in gene therapy and some vaccines.
[bio35-s1.2.5] Agrobacterium tumefaciens, a soil bacterium, naturally transfers part of its plasmid (the Ti plasmid) into plant cells, so it is used to modify plants.
[bio35-s1.2.6] Some methods use no vector. In microinjection, DNA is injected into a fertilised animal egg with a fine needle. A “gene gun” fires tiny metal particles coated with DNA into plant cells.
[bio35-s1.3.b] To move a gene, scientists first need a way to cut DNA at exact places. Restriction enzymes do this, and the way they cut makes it easy to join pieces back together.
[bio35-s1.3.0] Restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases) cut DNA at a specific short base sequence, the recognition site, usually 4 to 8 base pairs long.
[bio35-s1.3.1] In nature restriction enzymes protect bacteria by cutting up the DNA of invading viruses.
[bio35-s1.3.2] Many restriction enzymes cut the two strands at slightly different points, leaving short single-stranded overhangs called sticky ends. Others cut straight across, leaving blunt ends.
[bio35-s1.3.3] Worked example: the restriction enzyme EcoRI (from the bacterium Escherichia coli) recognises GAATTC and cuts between G and A on each strand: …G | AATTC… on one strand and …CTTAA | G… on the other, leaving the four-base sticky ends AATT.
[bio35-s1.3.4] Any two pieces of DNA cut with the same enzyme have complementary sticky ends, so they can base-pair (A with T, C with G). This is why the gene and the plasmid are cut with the same enzyme.
[bio35-s1.3.5] Diagram: a double strand with GAATTC/CTTAAG, the staggered cut shown, and the two fragments with their AATT overhangs labelled “sticky end”.
[bio35-s1.4.b] Sticky ends hold two fragments together only weakly. A second enzyme is needed to make the join permanent.
[bio35-s1.4.0] DNA ligase is the enzyme that joins DNA fragments by re-forming the sugar–phosphate backbone (the long chain on the outside of each DNA strand) where the sticky ends have paired.
[bio35-s1.4.1] The sticky ends pair by hydrogen bonds between bases, which are weak. DNA ligase makes the strong backbone bonds, so it is sometimes called “molecular glue”.
[bio35-s1.5.b] Making a recombinant plasmid has three stages: get the DNA, cut it, then join the gene into the plasmid. Human insulin is the standard example.
[bio35-s1.5.0] 1. DNA isolation: the donor DNA containing the gene is extracted from the donor cells (for example human cells for the insulin gene). Separately, plasmids are extracted from bacteria by breaking the cells open.
[bio35-s1.5.1] 2. Cutting: the donor DNA and the plasmid are cut with the same restriction enzyme.
[bio35-s1.5.2] This cuts the gene out of the donor DNA and opens the plasmid ring once, so both have matching sticky ends.
[bio35-s1.5.3] 3. Ligation: the gene and the opened plasmids are mixed. Their complementary sticky ends base-pair, and DNA ligase seals the backbone, giving a recombinant plasmid that contains the new gene.
[bio35-s1.6.b] The recombinant plasmid now has to get into bacteria and be put to work. The bacteria then become small factories for the protein.
[bio35-s1.6.0] 4. Transformation: bacteria take up the recombinant plasmid from their surroundings. Transformation means a cell taking up and keeping foreign DNA.
[bio35-s1.6.1] In the laboratory, uptake is increased by treating the bacteria with calcium chloride and a brief heat shock.
[bio35-s1.6.2] Only a small fraction of bacteria take up a plasmid, so they are selected. They are grown on agar containing the antibiotic, and only bacteria carrying the plasmid’s resistance marker gene survive.
[bio35-s1.6.3] 5. Expression: the transformed bacteria are cloned, each dividing repeatedly by binary fission (splitting in two) to produce millions of identical copies, each with the plasmid.
[bio35-s1.6.4] The bacteria transcribe and translate the inserted gene and make the protein; the gene is “expressed”.
[bio35-s1.6.5] They are grown in large numbers in a fermenter (bioreactor, a large vessel for growing microorganisms), and the protein is extracted and purified.
[bio35-s1.6.6] Diagram: flow chart: donor cell → gene cut out by restriction enzyme; bacterium → plasmid cut by the same enzyme; gene + plasmid joined by DNA ligase → recombinant plasmid; recombinant plasmid taken up by a bacterium (transformation); bacteria multiply and make the protein (expression).
[bio35-s1.6.7] Diagram labels: donor DNA, gene, restriction enzyme, sticky ends, plasmid, DNA ligase, recombinant plasmid, host bacterium, marker gene.
[bio35-s1.7.b] Bacteria, yeast and other cells that carry a human gene can make human proteins cheaply and in large amounts.
[bio35-s1.7.0] Human insulin: the human insulin gene is inserted into bacteria (or yeast), which make human insulin for people with type 1 diabetes.
[bio35-s1.7.1] In 1982 human insulin became the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use.
[bio35-s1.7.2] Advantages over insulin from cattle and pig pancreases: it is identical to human insulin, so there are fewer allergic reactions; the supply is unlimited and consistent; and no animal slaughter is needed, avoiding religious and vegetarian objections.
[bio35-s1.7.3] Other medicines are made by genetically engineered cells, such as human growth hormone, for children who lack it.
[bio35-s1.7.4] Clotting factor VIII for people with haemophilia A (a condition where blood does not clot properly) is made by genetically engineered mammalian cells grown in culture, which removes the risk of infection from donated blood.
[bio35-s1.7.5] The hepatitis B vaccine is made from a viral protein produced by genetically modified yeast.
[bio35-s1.7.6] Many industrial and food enzymes are made by genetically modified microorganisms carrying the gene for the enzyme, for example chymosin for cheese-making, replacing rennet from calves’ stomachs, and enzymes in biological detergents.
[bio35-s1.8.b] Plants are modified to protect themselves from pests, to tolerate weed-killers and to carry more nutrients. Each example below inserts a gene from another organism.
[bio35-s1.8.0] Insect-resistant crops: a gene from the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) for a protein toxic to certain insect larvae (for example caterpillars and corn borers) is inserted into maize or cotton, giving Bt maize and Bt cotton.
[bio35-s1.8.1] Larvae that eat the plant die, so farmers spray less insecticide. The protein is harmless to mammals, birds and most other insects.
[bio35-s1.8.2] Herbicide-tolerant crops: a gene for resistance to a herbicide (weed-killer) such as glyphosate is inserted into crops such as soya and maize, so the field can be sprayed to kill weeds without harming the crop.
[bio35-s1.8.3] Improved nutrition: Golden Rice has genes from other organisms inserted so that its grain makes beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A. The beta-carotene gives the rice its yellow colour.
[bio35-s1.8.4] Golden Rice was developed to reduce vitamin A deficiency, a cause of childhood blindness and death where rice is the main food. It was approved for growing in the Philippines in 2021.
[bio35-s1.8.5] The EU regulates GM crops strictly. The only GM crop grown commercially in the EU is a Bt maize, grown mostly in Spain.
[bio35-s1.8.6] No GM crops are grown commercially in Ireland, but GM soya and maize are imported, mainly for animal feed.
[bio35-s1.9.b] Genes can also be added to animals, and to human cells to treat disease. The effect depends on which cells are changed.
[bio35-s1.9.0] GM animals: AquAdvantage salmon carries a growth-hormone gene from another salmon species (the Chinook salmon), so it grows to market size faster.
[bio35-s1.9.1] AquAdvantage salmon was approved for food in the USA in 2015, the first GM animal approved for food there.
[bio35-s1.9.2] GM animals, especially mice, are also used in medical research as models of human diseases.
[bio35-s1.9.3] Gene therapy: a working copy of a gene is inserted into a patient’s own cells to treat a genetic disease caused by a faulty allele (version of a gene), usually using a harmless modified virus as the vector.
[bio35-s1.9.4] Somatic gene therapy changes body cells only, so the change is not inherited.
[bio35-s1.9.5] Germline changes (in eggs, sperm or embryos) would be inherited by the patient’s children, and are not allowed in most countries.
[bio35-s1.10.b] Plasmid methods add a new gene. Gene editing is a newer approach that changes a gene exactly where it already sits in the genome (all of an organism’s DNA).
[bio35-s1.10.0] CRISPR-Cas9 is a gene-editing tool adapted from a system that bacteria use to defend themselves against viruses.
[bio35-s1.10.1] A short guide RNA, made to be complementary to the target DNA sequence, leads the enzyme Cas9 to that exact place in the genome, where Cas9 cuts both DNA strands.
[bio35-s1.10.2] When the cell repairs the cut, the gene can be switched off, or a corrected sequence supplied by scientists can be built in.
[bio35-s1.10.3] Unlike the earlier plasmid method, which adds a new gene carried in a vector, CRISPR changes a gene in its own place in the organism’s DNA (editing). It is faster, cheaper and more precise.
[bio35-s1.10.4] Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier shared the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for developing it.
[bio35-s1.10.5] In 2023 the UK became the first country to approve a CRISPR-based treatment, for sickle-cell disease and beta thalassaemia. The patient’s own blood stem cells are edited outside the body and returned.
[bio35-s1.11.b] The uses of genetic engineering fall into three themes, which is a useful way to organise an answer.
[bio35-s1.11.0] Health: medicines (insulin, growth hormone, clotting factors, vaccines), gene therapy and gene editing.
[bio35-s1.11.1] Sustainability: crops needing less insecticide, nutrition-enhanced crops, and enzymes replacing harsh chemical processes.
[bio35-s1.11.2] Technology: genetic engineering is a core biotechnology (the use of living organisms to make useful products).

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.

CANDIDATES bio6-s3: Classification
[bio6-s3.0.b] There are so many kinds of living thing that biologists sort them into groups, the way a library sorts books into sections, shelves and titles. The groups sit inside one another in a fixed set of levels.
[bio6-s3.0.0] Classification is the grouping of organisms according to their shared characteristics. Taxonomy is the science of naming, describing and classifying organisms.
[bio6-s3.0.1] Organisms are placed in a hierarchy of ranks (taxa, the levels of grouping), from broadest to narrowest: domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, species.
[bio6-s3.0.2] Moving down the ranks, each group has fewer members, which share more features and are more closely related. This is why two species in the same genus are far more alike than two in the same kingdom.
[bio6-s3.0.3] Worked example, human: domain Eukarya, kingdom Animalia, phylum Chordata, class Mammalia, order Primates, family Hominidae, genus Homo, species Homo sapiens.
[bio6-s3.1.b] The species is the smallest rank, so it needs a clear definition and a universal name. Every species gets a two-part scientific name, which is called binomial nomenclature.
[bio6-s3.1.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring (young that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, a mule, is infertile, so they are different species.
[bio6-s3.1.1] Limitation of the species definition: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually (e.g. bacteria), because they do not interbreed at all, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding.
[bio6-s3.1.2] Binomial nomenclature: every species has a two-part scientific name, the genus name (capital first letter) followed by the species name (lower case), printed in italics or underlined when handwritten.
[bio6-s3.1.3] Examples of binomial names: Homo sapiens (human), Escherichia coli (a gut bacterium), Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould).
[bio6-s3.1.4] The Swedish botanist Carl Linnaeus introduced binomial nomenclature; his Species Plantarum (1753) used it for all plants.
[bio6-s3.1.5] Species in the same genus are closely related, which is why they share the first part of their name. The lion (Panthera leo) and tiger (Panthera tigris) share the genus Panthera.
[bio6-s3.2.b] Before sorting living things into kingdoms, biologists ask one basic question about their cells: is there a nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) or not? The answer splits cellular life into two big groups.
[bio6-s3.2.0] The first division of cellular life is by cell type. Prokaryotic cells have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (the small working parts of a cell). Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus surrounded by a membrane, and membrane-bound organelles. There is more on this in Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells.
[bio6-s3.2.1] Two kingdoms are prokaryotic, Bacteria and Archaea, each forming its own domain. Four kingdoms are eukaryotic, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, all in the domain Eukarya.
[bio6-s3.2.2] The distinction became clear only once the electron microscope showed cell interiors, because the details inside prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells are too small to see with a light microscope.
[bio6-s3.3.b] Both prokaryotic kingdoms are single-celled with no nucleus, so cell structure alone does not separate them. One way of telling them apart is the chemistry of their cell walls, and another is rRNA. Each is described by cell type, number of cells, cell wall, nutrition and examples.
[bio6-s3.3.0] Bacteria: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls of peptidoglycan (a mesh of sugars and amino acids). They reproduce by binary fission (one cell splits into two).
[bio6-s3.3.1] Bacterial nutrition varies. Bacteria are autotrophic (make their own food), either photosynthetic or chemosynthetic, or heterotrophic (take in food made by others), either saprophytic (feeding on dead matter) or parasitic (feeding on a living host).
[bio6-s3.3.2] Bacteria examples: Escherichia coli, Lactobacillus (used to make yoghurt) and Streptococcus.
[bio6-s3.3.3] Archaea: prokaryotic and unicellular, with cell walls that have no peptidoglycan. This difference in cell wall is one way of telling them apart from bacteria. Many live in extreme environments.
[bio6-s3.3.4] Archaea examples: methanogens (methane-producing archaea) in cattle digestive systems, and Halobacterium in salt lakes.
[bio6-s3.4.b] The four eukaryotic kingdoms all have a nucleus. They are separated by how many cells they have, what their cell walls are made of, how they get food and how they store carbohydrate.
[bio6-s3.4.0] Protista: eukaryotic and mostly unicellular, though some are multicellular algae such as seaweeds (kelp). They have no tissues (groups of similar cells doing one job) of the kind found in plants and animals. Some are photosynthetic (algae) and some are heterotrophic.
[bio6-s3.4.1] Protista includes eukaryotes that do not belong in the other three eukaryotic kingdoms. Examples: Amoeba, Paramecium, Plasmodium (causes malaria) and Phytophthora infestans (causes potato blight, which caused the Great Famine in Ireland, 1845–1849).
[bio6-s3.4.2] Fungi: eukaryotic and mostly multicellular, made of thread-like hyphae forming a mycelium (a tangled mass of threads), though yeasts are unicellular. They have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise, and their cell walls are made of chitin.
[bio6-s3.4.3] Fungi are heterotrophic: they secrete enzymes onto food and absorb the digested products, living as saprophytes or parasites. They store carbohydrate as glycogen and reproduce by spores. Examples: Rhizopus (bread mould), yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae), mushrooms and Penicillium.
[bio6-s3.4.4] Plantae: eukaryotic and multicellular, with cell walls of cellulose and chloroplasts (where photosynthesis happens). They are autotrophic by photosynthesis, store carbohydrate as starch, and mostly do not move. Examples: mosses, ferns, conifers, and flowering plants such as the daisy and oak.
[bio6-s3.4.5] Animalia: eukaryotic and multicellular, with no cell walls. They are heterotrophic, taking in (ingesting) food, and store carbohydrate as glycogen. Most can move and have nerves and muscles. Examples: sponges, earthworms, insects, fish and mammals.
[bio6-s3.5.b] Classification by kingdom and domain only works for things made of cells. Viruses fall outside it.
[bio6-s3.5.0] Viruses are not in any kingdom or domain, because they are not cells. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio6-s3.6.b] Grouping by appearance can mislead, so modern classification also asks which organisms share a recent ancestor. This evolutionary family-tree approach is called phylogeny.
[bio6-s3.6.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group of organisms. Phylogenetic classification groups organisms by how closely related they are by descent from common ancestors, rather than by appearance alone.
[bio6-s3.6.1] A phylogenetic tree shows these relationships. Each branch point (node) represents a common ancestor, and the closer two species' branches join, the more recent their common ancestor and the more closely related they are.
[bio6-s3.6.2] Species in the same genus share a very recent common ancestor; genera in the same family share an older one. This is how the ranks of the hierarchy reflect evolutionary history.
[bio6-s3.6.3] Example: the lion and tiger (genus Panthera) are more closely related to each other than either is to the domestic cat (Felis catus), but all three are in the cat family (Felidae).
[bio6-s3.6.4] The dog (Canis familiaris, also written Canis lupus familiaris) is in a different family, Canidae, and all four animals are in the order Carnivora.
[bio6-s3.6.5] Evidence used to work out phylogeny includes shared anatomical structures with the same basic plan, e.g. the five-digit limb of mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians; embryology (the study of early development); and fossils. There is more on this in Evolution: The Origins of Life.
[bio6-s3.6.6] The most powerful evidence is comparison of DNA base sequences and protein amino-acid sequences: the fewer the differences, the more closely related the organisms, because less time has passed for differences to build up.
[bio6-s3.6.7] Appearance can mislead. Whales and dolphins look like fish but are mammals: they breathe air with lungs, are warm-blooded and feed their young on milk.
[bio6-s3.6.8] Diagram: a phylogenetic tree for lion, tiger, domestic cat and dog, with the nodes for genus Panthera, family Felidae and order Carnivora labelled.
[bio6-s3.7.b] Classification is not fixed. Each time scientists gained a new tool for looking at living things, the groupings were redrawn, which is why you will meet different numbers of kingdoms in the history of the subject.
[bio6-s3.7.0] Early systems had two kingdoms, plants and animals. Linnaeus, in the 18th century, classified organisms by visible features.
[bio6-s3.7.1] Microscopes revealed single-celled organisms that fitted neither kingdom. In 1866 Ernst Haeckel proposed a third kingdom, Protista.
[bio6-s3.7.2] In 1969 Robert Whittaker proposed five kingdoms: Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia. Monera held all the prokaryotes.
[bio6-s3.7.3] Fungi were separated from plants in Whittaker's system because they have chitin walls, no chlorophyll and absorb their food.
[bio6-s3.7.4] rRNA sequencing (reading the sequence of a molecule found in ribosomes) by Carl Woese showed that prokaryotes form two very different groups. This gave three domains (1990).
[bio6-s3.7.5] The six kingdoms used in this course followed, with Monera split into Bacteria and Archaea. There is more on this in Domains of life.
[bio6-s3.7.6] Systems change because new technology provides new evidence, e.g. the light microscope, the electron microscope, biochemistry and DNA sequencing. Classification is revised to reflect evolutionary relationships more accurately. It is a model that scientists keep testing and improving.
[bio6-s3.8.b] DNA sequencing, reading the order of bases in an organism's DNA, is now the strongest tool for classification. It is still moving organisms between groups and adding new ones.
[bio6-s3.8.0] DNA sequencing and whole-genome comparisons are now the main evidence for classification, and have moved many organisms. Phytophthora (potato blight) was long classed as a fungus because it grows as threads. Its DNA and its cellulose cell walls show that it belongs with the protists (water moulds).
[bio6-s3.8.1] DNA evidence placed the giant panda in the bear family.
[bio6-s3.8.2] Environmental DNA sequencing, which sequences all the DNA in a soil or water sample, has revealed huge numbers of microorganisms that have never been grown in a laboratory. These add new branches to the tree of life.
[bio6-s3.8.3] Asgard archaea were first reported in 2015 from DNA in sediment near the Loki's Castle hydrothermal vents in the Arctic Ocean. They share many genes with eukaryotes.
[bio6-s3.8.4] Some scientists argue that eukaryotes arose from within the archaea, which would mean only two primary domains. This is still debated, so classification remains a work in progress.
[bio6-s3.8.5] DNA barcoding uses a short standard gene sequence to identify species quickly, e.g. to identify species from tiny samples or to detect mislabelled food.
[bio6-s3.9.b] A dichotomous key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by answering a series of either/or questions about what you can see. The worked key below identifies six invertebrates (animals without backbones).
[bio6-s3.9.0] A dichotomous key identifies an organism by a series of steps. Each step gives a choice between two contrasting statements (a couplet) about a visible feature. Each statement leads either to a name or to the number of the next couplet.
[bio6-s3.9.1] Worked example: a key to six invertebrates.
[bio6-s3.9.2] 1. Legs present → go to 2; legs absent → go to 4.
[bio6-s3.9.3] 2. Three pairs of legs → ladybird (an insect); more than three pairs of legs → go to 3.
[bio6-s3.9.4] 3. Four pairs of legs → spider; seven pairs of legs → woodlouse.
[bio6-s3.9.5] 4. Shell present → snail; no visible shell → go to 5.
[bio6-s3.9.6] 5. Body divided into rings (segments) → earthworm; body not divided into rings → slug.
[bio6-s3.10.b] To use a key, start at couplet 1 and follow the numbers until you reach a name. Practise tracing a route for each organism so you can do it in the exam.
[bio6-s3.10.0] Using the key: an animal with eight legs goes 1 (legs present) → 2 (more than three pairs) → 3 (four pairs) → spider. Eight legs means four pairs, which is why it ends at spider.
[bio6-s3.10.1] Write the route for one more organism: the slug goes 1 → 4 → 5 → slug, because it has no legs, no visible shell and a body that is not divided into rings.
[bio6-s3.11.b] You may have to build a key of your own, or use the features of the kingdoms to place an unknown organism. Both rely on picking features that give a clear yes or no.
[bio6-s3.11.0] To construct a key, list the organisms, choose a feature that splits them into two groups, and keep splitting each group in two until each organism is on its own.
[bio6-s3.11.1] Use features that are easy to observe and do not vary between individuals or seasons, e.g. number of legs, not size or colour, because size and colour change with age and season and would give unreliable answers.
[bio6-s3.11.2] A key for n organisms needs n − 1 couplets. For example, the six-invertebrate key above has five couplets.
[bio6-s3.11.3] To classify an unknown organism to a kingdom, use the features in order. Does it have a nucleus? If not, it is a bacterium or an archaeon, separated by cell-wall chemistry or rRNA.
[bio6-s3.11.4] If it has a nucleus, ask further questions. Is it multicellular with tissues? What is its cell wall made of? Is it autotrophic or heterotrophic?
[bio6-s3.11.5] Worked example: a newly found organism is eukaryotic, is made of hyphae with chitin walls, and absorbs food from dead leaves. It is in the kingdom Fungi.
[bio6-s3.12.b] Classification is more than tidy labelling. It makes biology easier to study, communicate and apply to health, farming and conservation.
[bio6-s3.12.0] It organises the enormous diversity of life into manageable groups, making it easier to study and to find information.
[bio6-s3.12.1] Scientific names are universal and unique, which avoids the confusion of common names. The robin of Europe (Erithacus rubecula) and the robin of North America (Turdus migratorius) are different species in different genera.
[bio6-s3.12.2] It shows evolutionary relationships, so features of a new organism can be predicted from its relatives. For example, relatives of a plant that produces a useful drug may produce similar compounds.
[bio6-s3.12.3] Health: correctly identifying a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) decides the treatment. A bacterial infection may respond to an antibiotic, but a fungal or viral one will not.
[bio6-s3.12.4] Agriculture and the environment: identifying pests, invasive species and endangered species is needed for control and conservation, and for measuring biodiversity (the variety of living things in an area).

CANDIDATES bio7-s3: Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells
[bio7-s3.0.b] Every cell needs a boundary that holds it together and controls what gets in and out. Plant cells have two layers of boundary: a tough cell wall outside and a thin cell membrane just inside it.
[bio7-s3.0.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the cell membrane, made mainly of cellulose fibres in plants. It is fully permeable, so water and dissolved substances pass through it freely.
[bio7-s3.0.1] The cell wall gives strength, support and a fixed shape. It stops the cell bursting when it takes in water by osmosis (water moving in across the membrane), so the cell becomes firm (turgid), which supports non-woody plants.
[bio7-s3.0.2] Cell walls are found in plant cells, in fungi (where they are made of chitin) and in bacteria, but not in animal cells. The cell wall is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.0.3] Cell membrane (plasma membrane): a very thin, flexible boundary around every cell, made of a double layer of phospholipids (fatty molecules) with proteins in it. It is selectively permeable, so it controls which substances enter and leave, and it holds the cell contents together.
[bio7-s3.0.4] Proteins in the cell membrane act as receptors that recognise hormones and other signals, and as markers that let cells recognise each other.
[bio7-s3.0.5] The cell membrane is found in all cells, and lies inside the wall in plant cells. With the light microscope it is seen only as a line. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio7-s3.1.b] Inside the membrane, the cell's contents sit in a watery fluid, and plant cells also have a large fluid-filled sac that helps hold them firm. Together these provide the setting for the cell's chemistry and its support.
[bio7-s3.1.0] Cytoplasm: everything inside the cell membrane except the nucleus. It is a watery, jelly-like fluid (cytosol) containing dissolved nutrients, salts and enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions), in which the organelles (the cell's specialised working parts) are suspended.
[bio7-s3.1.1] The cytoplasm is the site of many metabolic reactions (the chemical reactions of the cell), for example the first stage of respiration (glucose broken down to pyruvate) and anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen). It is also where proteins are made at free ribosomes. It is found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.1.2] Vacuole: a fluid-filled sac surrounded by a single membrane. A mature plant cell has one large permanent central vacuole filled with cell sap, which is water containing sugars, salts and sometimes pigments, for example the red of beetroot.
[bio7-s3.1.3] The plant vacuole stores water, food and waste. It takes in water by osmosis and pushes the cytoplasm against the wall, which keeps the cell turgid and the plant upright. When water is lost, plants wilt.
[bio7-s3.1.4] Animal cells have only small, temporary vacuoles, for example food vacuoles. Some single-celled protists such as Amoeba have contractile vacuoles that pump out excess water.
[bio7-s3.1.5] The plant vacuole is visible with the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.b] Cells need a constant supply of usable energy. Mitochondria are the organelles that release it from food, so cells with high energy demands are packed with them.
[bio7-s3.2.0] Mitochondrion (plural mitochondria): a rod- or sausage-shaped organelle with a double membrane. The outer membrane is smooth, and the inner membrane is folded into cristae.
[bio7-s3.2.1] The folds of the cristae give a large surface area for the enzymes of the later stages of aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen). The fluid inside the inner membrane is the matrix.
[bio7-s3.2.2] Function: the site of most of aerobic respiration, releasing energy from food to make most of the cell's ATP (the molecule that carries energy for cell activities).
[bio7-s3.2.3] Mitochondria are found in almost all eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), in plants as well as animals. Plant cells respire as well as photosynthesise, so they need mitochondria too.
[bio7-s3.2.4] Cells that need a lot of energy have many mitochondria, for example muscle cells, liver cells, nerve cells and the middle section of sperm.
[bio7-s3.2.5] Mitochondria contain their own DNA and ribosomes. They are too small to show detail with the light microscope, so their structure is seen with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.2.6] Diagram: a mitochondrion in section labelled outer membrane, inner membrane, cristae and matrix.
[bio7-s3.3.b] Plants make their own food by photosynthesis, and the organelle that does this is the chloroplast. Its green pigment captures light, and its fluid interior builds sugar.
[bio7-s3.3.0] Chloroplast: a disc-shaped organelle with a double membrane. It contains stacks of flattened membrane sacs (thylakoids; a stack is a granum) that carry the green pigment chlorophyll.
[bio7-s3.3.1] The chloroplast is surrounded by a fluid called the stroma, and starch grains may be stored in the stroma.
[bio7-s3.3.2] Function: the site of photosynthesis. Chlorophyll in the thylakoid membranes absorbs light energy (the light-dependent stage). The stroma contains the enzymes that use this energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide (the light-independent stage).
[bio7-s3.3.3] The many stacked membranes give a large surface area for absorbing light. There is more on this in Photosynthesis.
[bio7-s3.3.4] Chloroplasts are found only in plant cells (and algae), in parts exposed to light, especially the palisade cells of leaves and guard cells.
[bio7-s3.3.5] Not all plant cells have chloroplasts. They are not found in root cells or in onion bulb cells, which are not exposed to light, and they are never found in animal cells.
[bio7-s3.3.6] Chloroplasts are visible as green discs with the light microscope. They contain their own DNA and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.3.7] Diagram: a chloroplast labelled double membrane, thylakoids, granum, stroma and starch grain.
[bio7-s3.4.b] The nucleus is the cell's control centre. It holds the instructions for making proteins, and nuclear pores are the gateways that let the right materials move between the nucleus and the rest of the cell.
[bio7-s3.4.0] Nucleus: usually the largest organelle, round or oval, surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope. It contains the cell's DNA as chromatin, which is DNA wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s3.4.1] Function: the nucleus controls the cell's activities, because its DNA carries the genes that code for the cell's proteins, including the enzymes that control every reaction.
[bio7-s3.4.2] The nucleus is where DNA is copied before cell division, and where genes are copied into messenger RNA (mRNA, the working copy of a gene that carries the code out to the ribosomes).
[bio7-s3.4.3] The nucleolus is a dense region inside the nucleus where ribosomes are made.
[bio7-s3.4.4] The nucleus is found in all eukaryotic cells except a few that lose it, for example mature human red blood cells. It is visible with the light microscope, especially when stained.
[bio7-s3.4.5] Nuclear pore: one of many openings through the nuclear envelope, each lined with proteins that control what passes through it.
[bio7-s3.4.6] mRNA and ribosome parts pass out to the cytoplasm. Proteins such as enzymes and histones, nucleotides (the building blocks of DNA and RNA) and ATP pass in. The DNA is too large to leave, so it stays protected in the nucleus.
[bio7-s3.4.7] Nuclear pores are seen only with the electron microscope, because they are too small for the light microscope.
[bio7-s3.5.b] The DNA in the nucleus is not a loose tangle. It is organised into chromosomes, which carry the genes and are passed on when cells divide and when organisms reproduce.
[bio7-s3.5.0] Chromosome: a long thread of DNA wound around proteins (histones), carrying many genes (sections of DNA that code for proteins).
[bio7-s3.5.1] For most of the cell's life the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin, which is why they cannot be seen.
[bio7-s3.5.2] Before division the chromosomes coil up tightly, becoming short and thick, and can then be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio7-s3.5.3] Human body cells have 46 chromosomes (23 pairs).
[bio7-s3.5.4] Function: chromosomes carry genetic information and pass it to new cells and offspring. There is more on this in Genetic Inheritance, and division is in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio7-s3.6.b] Proteins are made on tiny organelles called ribosomes, which read the code carried by mRNA. Every cell needs them, so they are one of the few organelles found in all cells.
[bio7-s3.6.0] Ribosome: a very small organelle with no membrane, made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein, in two subunits.
[bio7-s3.6.1] Function: the ribosome is the site of protein synthesis, where amino acids (the building blocks of proteins) are joined in the order coded for by mRNA.
[bio7-s3.6.2] Ribosomes are found in all cells, prokaryotic (without a nucleus) and eukaryotic, free in the cytoplasm. There are many in cells making a lot of protein.
[bio7-s3.6.3] Ribosomes are seen only with the electron microscope, as dots, because they are very small. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio7-s3.6.4] RNA but no DNA is found in ribosomes and in the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells. DNA is in the nucleus, mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s3.7.b] Some proteins are not used inside the cell but are exported, such as digestive enzymes. The endoplasmic reticulum is the first stage of the assembly line that folds and transports them, and it also makes lipids.
[bio7-s3.7.0] Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane-bound flattened sacs and tubes running through the cytoplasm, continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It forms channels that transport materials through the cell.
[bio7-s3.7.1] Rough ER is covered with ribosomes. Proteins made at these ribosomes enter the ER, where they are folded and transported. These are mainly proteins that will be secreted from the cell (for example digestive enzymes made by pancreas cells, and antibodies) or built into membranes.
[bio7-s3.7.2] Proteins made at free ribosomes stay in the cytoplasm. Cells that secrete a lot of protein have a lot of rough ER, as in pancreas cells secreting digestive enzymes.
[bio7-s3.7.3] Smooth ER has no ribosomes. It makes lipids, including phospholipids for membranes and steroid hormones. In liver cells it helps to break down (detoxify) drugs and alcohol.
[bio7-s3.7.4] The ER is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.b] The Golgi apparatus is the second stage of the assembly line: it modifies, sorts and packages proteins from the ER and ships them out of the cell.
[bio7-s3.8.0] Golgi apparatus (Golgi body): a stack of flattened, curved membrane sacs, usually near the nucleus, with small membrane bubbles (vesicles) budding off it.
[bio7-s3.8.1] The Golgi apparatus receives proteins from the ER in vesicles, modifies them (for example adding carbohydrate to make glycoproteins), and sorts and packages them into vesicles that carry them to where they are needed.
[bio7-s3.8.2] Secretory vesicles move to the cell membrane, fuse with it and release their contents outside the cell (exocytosis). In plant cells the Golgi apparatus also makes materials for the cell wall.
[bio7-s3.8.3] The Golgi apparatus is found in plant and animal cells, and is seen only with the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.8.4] Secretory pathway, in order: gene in the nucleus, then mRNA leaves through a nuclear pore, then protein is made at a ribosome on the rough ER, then folded and carried in the ER.
[bio7-s3.8.5] The protein then moves in a transport vesicle to the Golgi apparatus (modified and packaged), then in a secretory vesicle, which fuses with the cell membrane, and the protein is released, for example a digestive enzyme.
[bio7-s3.8.6] Diagram: add rough ER (with ribosomes), smooth ER and the Golgi apparatus (with vesicles) to the animal-cell diagram, with arrows showing the secretory pathway.
[bio7-s3.9.b] Plant and animal cells share the same core set of organelles, but plant cells have three extra structures that suit a life of making food and standing upright without a skeleton.
[bio7-s3.9.0] Both have a cell membrane, cytoplasm, a nucleus with nuclear pores and chromosomes, mitochondria and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.1] Only plant cells have a cellulose cell wall, a large permanent central vacuole, and chloroplasts in green parts.
[bio7-s3.9.2] Plant cells usually have a fixed, regular (often rectangular) shape, because the rigid wall holds them in place, and they store carbohydrate as starch.
[bio7-s3.9.3] Animal cells have a flexible, irregular or rounded shape, no wall, only small temporary vacuoles, and store carbohydrate as glycogen.
[bio7-s3.9.4] Seen with the light microscope: the cell wall, vacuole, chloroplasts and nucleus, with the cell membrane seen only as a line. Mitochondria, nuclear pores and ribosomes need the electron microscope.
[bio7-s3.9.5] Diagram: a typical animal cell as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, nucleolus, chromatin, mitochondrion and ribosomes.
[bio7-s3.9.6] Diagram: a typical plant cell (for example a palisade cell) as seen with the electron microscope, labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, large vacuole, chloroplast, mitochondrion, nucleus, nuclear pore and ribosomes.
