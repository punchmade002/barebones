CANDIDATES bio11-s11: Sex-linked traits
[bio11-s11.0.b] Some genes sit on the sex chromosomes rather than on the other chromosomes, and this changes how they are inherited. Because males and females carry different sex chromosomes, the same allele can have a different effect in each sex.
[bio11-s11.0.0] Sex-linked genes are almost all on the X chromosome (X-linked), because the Y carries very few genes.
[bio11-s11.0.1] A sex-linked trait is a trait controlled by such a gene. The Y has no matching allele for most X-linked genes, so in males the X-linked gene has no second copy to pair with.
[bio11-s11.0.2] Females (XX) have two copies of each X-linked gene, so they can be homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous (two different alleles).
[bio11-s11.0.3] A heterozygous female with one recessive allele is a carrier (she carries the allele but does not show the trait), because her dominant allele masks it.
[bio11-s11.0.4] Males (XY) have only one copy, so whatever allele their single X carries is expressed, with no second allele to mask it.
[bio11-s11.0.5] This is why a male cannot be a carrier, and why one recessive allele is enough for him to show the trait.
[bio11-s11.0.6] X-linked recessive conditions are therefore much more common in males than in females.
[bio11-s11.0.7] A female shows the condition only if she inherits the recessive allele from both parents, which means an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
[bio11-s11.1.b] To predict who inherits an X-linked allele, follow the sex chromosomes. A child's sex is decided by the sex chromosome the father contributes, and that also decides where the X-linked allele comes from.
[bio11-s11.1.0] A father passes his X to all his daughters and his Y to all his sons.
[bio11-s11.1.1] So an affected father never passes an X-linked condition to his sons, because sons get his Y, which carries no allele for the gene.
[bio11-s11.1.2] All his daughters are at least carriers, because each receives his X with the recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.1.3] A son's X-linked allele always comes from his mother, since his only X is the one she gave him.
[bio11-s11.2.b] Genetics questions use a fixed way of writing X-linked genotypes so that the sex and the alleles are both clear at a glance.
[bio11-s11.2.0] Write the allele as a superscript on the X, and the Y with no allele, because the Y has no matching allele for these genes.
[bio11-s11.2.1] Colour blindness: Xᴮ = normal colour vision, Xᵇ = colour blind. Haemophilia: Xᴴ = normal clotting, Xʰ = haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.2.2] Colour-blindness genotypes: female XᴮXᴮ (normal), XᴮXᵇ (carrier), XᵇXᵇ (colour blind); male XᴮY (normal), XᵇY (colour blind).
[bio11-s11.2.3] Use the letters a question gives.
[bio11-s11.3.b] Red-green colour blindness is the standard example of an X-linked recessive condition, and the one most often used in crosses.
[bio11-s11.3.0] Red-green colour blindness is an X-linked recessive condition in which a person has difficulty telling red and green apart.
[bio11-s11.3.1] The genes for the red- and green-sensitive light-absorbing pigments of the cone cells of the retina (the colour-detecting cells at the back of the eye) are on the X chromosome. The recessive allele gives a pigment that is missing or works abnormally.
[bio11-s11.3.2] It affects about 1 in 12 men (8%) and about 1 in 200 women (0.5%) of northern European ancestry. The difference fits the pattern that males need only one recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.3.3] It is detected with coloured-dot test charts (Ishihara plates).
[bio11-s11.3.4] It is not treatable, but most people adapt to it.
[bio11-s11.4.b] Haemophilia is the second named X-linked recessive condition. It shows how a single faulty gene can affect the whole body, because it removes one step in blood clotting.
[bio11-s11.4.0] Haemophilia is an X-linked recessive condition in which blood does not clot properly because a clotting protein is missing or faulty.
[bio11-s11.4.1] Haemophilia A lacks clotting factor VIII; haemophilia B lacks factor IX.
[bio11-s11.4.2] Bleeding after injury lasts much longer, and bleeding can occur into joints and muscles.
[bio11-s11.4.3] Haemophilia A affects about 1 in 5,000 male births.
[bio11-s11.4.4] It is treated by injections of the missing clotting factor, now mostly produced by genetically engineered cells. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio11-s11.5.b] Haemophilia in European royal families is a well-known real case of an X-linked allele passing through carrier daughters to affected grandsons.
[bio11-s11.5.0] Queen Victoria (1819–1901) was a carrier of haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.5.1] Her son Leopold had haemophilia. Through two of her daughters, who were carriers, it passed into the royal families of Russia (Tsarevich Alexei, son of Tsar Nicholas II) and Spain.
[bio11-s11.6.b] Haemophilia and colour blindness are not the only X-linked recessive conditions. A third example is useful when a question asks for one other than haemophilia.
[bio11-s11.6.0] Duchenne muscular dystrophy is another X-linked recessive condition, caused by a lack of the muscle protein dystrophin, which causes progressive muscle weakness (muscles get steadily weaker over time).
[bio11-s11.6.1] It is almost always in boys, which fits the pattern for X-linked recessive conditions.
[bio11-s11.7.b] A mother who is a carrier of colour blindness has a partner with normal vision, so we can work out what their children could inherit.
[bio11-s11.7.0] Parents' phenotypes: normal-vision (carrier) female × normal-vision male.
[bio11-s11.7.1] Parents' genotypes: carrier female XᴮXᵇ × normal male XᴮY.
[bio11-s11.7.2] Gametes (each carries one sex chromosome with its allele): carrier female Xᴮ, Xᵇ × normal male Xᴮ, Y.
[bio11-s11.7.3] Punnett square: XᴮXᴮ, XᴮY / XᴮXᵇ, XᵇY.
[bio11-s11.7.4] Offspring genotypes and phenotypes: 1 normal female (XᴮXᴮ) : 1 carrier female (XᴮXᵇ) : 1 normal male (XᴮY) : 1 colour-blind male (XᵇY).
[bio11-s11.7.5] Half the sons are expected to be colour blind and half the daughters carriers; no daughters are colour blind, because every daughter gets the father's normal Xᴮ. 25% of all children are colour blind.
[bio11-s11.7.6] Diagram: the Punnett square for this cross, with the mother's gametes Xᴮ and Xᵇ on one side and the father's Xᴮ and Y on the other, each box labelled with genotype and phenotype (normal female, carrier female, normal male, colour-blind male).
[bio11-s11.8.b] Here the father has haemophilia and the mother has two normal alleles. This cross shows what an affected father passes on.
[bio11-s11.8.0] Parents' phenotypes: normal female × affected male.
[bio11-s11.8.1] Parents' genotypes: normal female XᴴXᴴ × affected male XʰY.
[bio11-s11.8.2] Gametes: normal female Xᴴ × affected male Xʰ, Y.
[bio11-s11.8.3] Offspring: XᴴXʰ (carrier daughters) and XᴴY (normal sons), 1 : 1.
[bio11-s11.8.4] All daughters are carriers, because each gets the father's Xʰ. No children have haemophilia, because the sons get his Y and the mother's normal Xᴴ.
[bio11-s11.9.b] When both parents contribute a recessive allele, a daughter can be affected. This cross shows how that happens.
[bio11-s11.9.0] Parents' phenotypes: carrier female × colour-blind male.
[bio11-s11.9.1] Parents' genotypes: carrier female XᴮXᵇ × colour-blind male XᵇY.
[bio11-s11.9.2] Gametes: carrier female Xᴮ, Xᵇ × colour-blind male Xᵇ, Y.
[bio11-s11.9.3] Offspring: XᴮXᵇ (carrier female), XᵇXᵇ (colour-blind female), XᴮY (normal male), XᵇY (colour-blind male), 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
[bio11-s11.9.4] Half the daughters and half the sons are colour blind. This is how a colour-blind daughter can arise: she inherits Xᵇ from both her mother and her father.
[bio11-s11.10.b] This cross reverses the sexes of the affected parent. It shows that an affected mother passes the condition to every son.
[bio11-s11.10.0] Parents' phenotypes: affected female × normal male.
[bio11-s11.10.1] Parents' genotypes: affected female XʰXʰ × normal male XᴴY.
[bio11-s11.10.2] Gametes: affected female Xʰ × normal male Xᴴ, Y.
[bio11-s11.10.3] Offspring: XᴴXʰ (carrier daughters) and XʰY (sons with haemophilia).
[bio11-s11.10.4] All sons are affected, because their only X comes from the mother; all daughters are carriers, because they also get the father's normal Xᴴ.
[bio11-s11.10.5] Females with haemophilia are very rare; this cross is used to show the pattern.
[bio11-s11.11.b] A pedigree shows how a trait passes through several generations of a family. Before you can use one, you need to know what its symbols and lines mean.
[bio11-s11.11.0] A pedigree is a family-tree diagram showing which members of a family have a trait.
[bio11-s11.11.1] Symbols: square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected; half-shaded (or a dot in the centre) = carrier.
[bio11-s11.11.2] A horizontal line joining a male and a female = parents; a vertical line down to a horizontal line = their children, in birth order from left to right.
[bio11-s11.11.3] Generations are numbered I, II, III and individuals 1, 2, 3 within each, so every person can be named, for example II-2.
[bio11-s11.12.b] Four features of a pedigree point to an X-linked recessive trait. Each follows from the rules on how X chromosomes are passed on.
[bio11-s11.12.0] Many more affected males than females, because a male needs only one recessive allele.
[bio11-s11.12.1] Affected males usually have unaffected parents, because the mother is a carrier.
[bio11-s11.12.2] The trait can skip a generation, passing from an affected man through his carrier daughters to his grandsons.
[bio11-s11.12.3] It is never passed from father to son, because a son gets his father's Y, not his X.
[bio11-s11.13.b] To give the genotype of a named person, or the chance that a child is affected or a carrier, work through the pedigree in a fixed order. This starts with the people whose genotype is certain.
[bio11-s11.13.0] 1. Write XY for every male and XX for every female.
[bio11-s11.13.1] 2. Fill in the alleles of affected males (XʰY) and unaffected males (XᴴY), which follow directly from their phenotype.
[bio11-s11.13.2] 3. Any daughter of an affected male is at least a carrier; any mother of an affected son is a carrier or affected.
[bio11-s11.13.3] 4. Use these to fill in the remaining females.
[bio11-s11.13.4] 5. Then work any further cross with a Punnett square.
[bio11-s11.13.5] Diagram: a three-generation X-linked recessive pedigree. Generation I: normal man, carrier woman. Generation II: affected son, normal son, carrier daughter, normal daughter. Generation III: the carrier daughter's children with a normal man, including an affected grandson. Label symbols and generations.
[bio11-s11.14.b] Genes on the Y chromosome follow a different pattern from X-linked genes, because only males have a Y.
[bio11-s11.14.0] Genes on the Y chromosome (for example SRY) are passed only from father to son and are found only in males. There is more on SRY in Sex determination.

CANDIDATES bio18-s1: The musculoskeletal system as a system for response
[bio18-s1.0.b] The musculoskeletal system is the framework of bones and joints together with the muscles that move it. Between them they let the body respond to a stimulus (a change in the surroundings) by moving.
[bio18-s1.0.0] The skeleton is the bones, together with the cartilage and ligaments that join them at joints (the places where two bones meet).
[bio18-s1.0.1] The skeletal muscles are attached to bones by tendons, which are the other part of the system.
[bio18-s1.0.2] Muscles provide the force, bones act as rigid levers, and joints are the pivots where movement happens, so a muscle pulling on a bone moves it at a joint.
[bio18-s1.0.3] The parts are only named here. There is more on the bones in "The axial skeleton" and "The appendicular skeleton", and on the joints and muscle pairs in "Muscles and joints".
[bio18-s1.0.4] The adult human skeleton has 206 bones, which are divided into two groups.
[bio18-s1.0.5] 80 of these form the axial skeleton: the skull, vertebral column (backbone), ribs and sternum (breastbone), which make up the central axis of the body.
[bio18-s1.0.6] The other 126 form the appendicular skeleton: the pectoral girdle (shoulders), the pelvic girdle (hips) and the limbs attached to them.
[bio18-s1.1.b] Bones look like lifeless sticks, but bone is a living tissue made of cells. Its structure explains why it is both hard and tough.
[bio18-s1.1.0] Bone is a living connective tissue, which means a tissue that supports and holds the body together.
[bio18-s1.1.1] Bone cells lie in a hard matrix (the material around the cells) of calcium phosphate, which gives bone its hardness and resists compression (being squashed).
[bio18-s1.1.2] Collagen fibres in the matrix give bone some flexibility and resist bending and snapping, so bone is not brittle like chalk.
[bio18-s1.1.3] Bone has a blood supply and nerves, can grow and repair itself, and changes shape slowly in response to the forces put on it.
[bio18-s1.2.b] The skeleton does much more than hold us up. It has five functions, and each one can be illustrated with an example.
[bio18-s1.2.0] Support: the skeleton is a rigid framework that holds the body up, gives it its shape and supports soft organs. The vertebral column supports the head and trunk, and the leg bones carry the body's weight.
[bio18-s1.2.1] Protection: bones surround delicate organs so that knocks do not damage them. The skull protects the brain, the ribs and sternum protect the heart and lungs, the vertebrae protect the spinal cord, and the pelvic girdle protects the bladder and reproductive organs.
[bio18-s1.2.2] Movement: bones provide attachment points for muscles and act as levers that the muscles pull on, moving the body at joints.
[bio18-s1.2.3] Making blood cells: red bone marrow, found in spongy bone (the light, honeycomb-like bone), makes red blood cells, white blood cells and platelets.
[bio18-s1.2.4] In adults, red marrow is mainly in the flat bones (skull, ribs, sternum, vertebrae and pelvis) and in the ends of long bones such as the femur (thigh bone) and humerus (upper arm bone).
[bio18-s1.2.5] Yellow marrow fills the hollow shafts of long bones and stores fat.
[bio18-s1.2.6] Storing minerals: bone stores calcium and phosphate.
[bio18-s1.2.7] When blood calcium falls, calcium is released from bone under the control of parathyroid hormone, which helps keep blood calcium constant. This matters because nerves and muscles need calcium to work. There is more on this in "Glands, locations and hormones".
[bio18-s1.3.b] The body has three types of muscle, and they differ in where they are, whether you can control them, how they look and how they contract. Only one of them belongs to the musculoskeletal system.
[bio18-s1.3.0] Skeletal muscle is attached to bones by tendons and is the only muscle type in the musculoskeletal system. It is under voluntary (conscious) control as well as reflex control, and its cells look striped (striated) under the microscope.
[bio18-s1.3.1] Skeletal muscle contracts quickly and powerfully but tires (fatigues), which is why you cannot keep sprinting indefinitely.
[bio18-s1.3.2] Smooth muscle is found in the walls of the gut, blood vessels, bladder and uterus. It is involuntary (not under conscious control) and not striped.
[bio18-s1.3.3] Smooth muscle contracts slowly and can stay contracted for a long time, as in peristalsis (the waves of squeezing that push food along the gut).
[bio18-s1.3.4] Cardiac muscle is found only in the wall of the heart. It is involuntary and striped, and it contracts rhythmically throughout life without tiring. There is more on the heart in "The Circulatory System and Blood".
[bio18-s1.4.b] A skeletal muscle is the effector that carries out a response, in the same way as the other effectors (the parts that act on a nerve message) such as glands. There is more on this in "Structures and systems for response". To see how it works, start with what it is made of.
[bio18-s1.4.0] A skeletal muscle is made of bundles of long cells called muscle fibres.
[bio18-s1.4.1] Each fibre contains many nuclei and many mitochondria, so it has plenty of control and plenty of energy supply for contraction.
[bio18-s1.4.2] Each fibre is packed with two kinds of protein filament: thin filaments of actin and thick filaments of myosin.
[bio18-s1.4.3] The filaments are arranged in repeating units that give the striped appearance of skeletal muscle.
[bio18-s1.5.b] A skeletal muscle never contracts on its own. It contracts only when it is stimulated by a motor neuron (a nerve cell that carries impulses to a muscle), and the contraction follows a fixed sequence.
[bio18-s1.5.0] 1. Skeletal muscle contracts only when it is stimulated by a motor neuron. A voluntary movement starts in the brain; a reflex movement starts in the spinal cord. There is more on this in "The reflex action".
[bio18-s1.5.1] 2. Impulses travel along the motor neuron to its ending on the muscle fibre, a synapse (a gap between nerve and target) called the neuromuscular junction.
[bio18-s1.5.2] 3. At the neuromuscular junction the neurotransmitter (chemical messenger) acetylcholine is released, crosses the gap and binds to receptors on the muscle fibre, starting an impulse in the fibre.
[bio18-s1.5.3] 4. The impulse causes calcium ions (Ca²⁺) to be released from stores inside the fibre.
[bio18-s1.5.4] 5. The calcium allows myosin filaments to attach to actin filaments and pull them inwards, so the filaments slide past each other and the fibre shortens.
[bio18-s1.5.5] 6. Energy for this comes from ATP, the energy carrier of the cell, which is made in the many mitochondria by respiration.
[bio18-s1.6.b] Contraction is only half the story. The muscle must also relax, and the nervous system must be able to vary how hard the muscle pulls.
[bio18-s1.6.0] When the impulses stop, calcium is pumped back into its stores, the filaments detach and the muscle relaxes.
[bio18-s1.6.1] A relaxed muscle does not lengthen by itself: it is pulled back to its full length by an opposing muscle, which is why muscles work in pairs. There is more on this in "Muscles and joints".
[bio18-s1.6.2] A muscle can only pull by contracting; it can never push.
[bio18-s1.6.3] The force is passed through a tendon to a bone, which moves at a joint. This movement is the response.
[bio18-s1.6.4] Strength of response: the more motor neurons that fire and the more muscle fibres that contract, the stronger the contraction, so movements can be gentle or forceful.
[bio18-s1.6.5] Muscle tone: a slight, continuous partial contraction of muscles holds the body's posture without conscious effort.
[bio18-s1.6.6] Diagram: a flow diagram of a voluntary response: brain, spinal cord, motor neuron, neuromuscular junction, skeletal muscle (contracts), tendon, bone moves at joint. Also a sketch of a skeletal muscle cut open to show bundles of muscle fibres, with one fibre enlarged to show actin and myosin filaments, all labelled.
[bio18-s1.7.b] Because skeletal muscle is attached to bones, the system produces a wide range of responses, from obvious movements to constant small adjustments you never notice.
[bio18-s1.7.0] Movement of the whole body (locomotion: walking, running, swimming) to find food, escape danger or move to better conditions.
[bio18-s1.7.1] Movement of parts of the body, such as grasping or turning the head towards a sound.
[bio18-s1.7.2] Maintaining posture and balance: muscles of the back, neck and legs make constant small adjustments, in response to information from receptors, to keep the body upright.
[bio18-s1.7.3] Breathing: the diaphragm and intercostal muscles (the muscles between the ribs) move the ribs. There is more on this in "Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism".
[bio18-s1.7.4] Speech, facial expression, chewing and swallowing all depend on skeletal muscle.
[bio18-s1.7.5] Shivering: rapid involuntary contractions that release heat when the body is cold.
[bio18-s1.8.b] Muscles and bones cannot respond alone. They depend on other body systems to start the contraction, to supply fuel and to prepare them for action.
[bio18-s1.8.0] Nervous system: every contraction of a skeletal muscle is started by a motor neuron.
[bio18-s1.8.1] Receptors in muscles and joints send information back to the brain about the position of the limbs, so that movements can be coordinated.
[bio18-s1.8.2] Circulatory and breathing systems: active muscles need more oxygen and glucose for respiration, and must remove more carbon dioxide and heat, so heart rate and breathing rate rise during exercise.
[bio18-s1.8.3] When oxygen cannot be supplied fast enough, muscle fibres respire anaerobically (without oxygen), producing lactic acid and much less ATP, and soon tire (fatigue).
[bio18-s1.8.4] The lactic acid is removed afterwards, when oxygen is available again; this is one reason breathing stays fast after exercise. There is more on this in "Respiration".
[bio18-s1.8.5] Endocrine system: adrenaline (a hormone) increases blood flow to skeletal muscles and raises blood glucose, preparing them for action.
[bio18-s1.8.6] Health: regular weight-bearing exercise, such as walking or running, strengthens bones and muscles.
[bio18-s1.8.7] A diet with enough protein (for muscle), calcium and vitamin D (for bone) supports the system.

CANDIDATES bio18-s3: The appendicular skeleton
[bio18-s3.0.b] The appendicular skeleton is the skeleton of the limbs and of the two girdles that attach them to the axial skeleton (the skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum). It is the part of the skeleton that lets you reach, walk and run.
[bio18-s3.0.0] A girdle is a ring or half-ring of bones that anchors a pair of limbs to the trunk. The pectoral (shoulder) girdle attaches the arms, and the pelvic (hip) girdle attaches the legs.
[bio18-s3.0.1] Its main job is movement, because the limb bones act as levers for the muscles that pull on them. The legs and pelvic girdle also bear the body's weight, so they are built for strength as well as movement.
[bio18-s3.1.b] Most of the limb bones are long bones, and their structure shows how a bone can be both strong and light. Knowing the layers of a long bone also lets you label a section through one.
[bio18-s3.1.0] The limb bones named in the specification, except the patella, are long bones: a long shaft with a wider end at each joint. The patella is a small flat bone, so it is the exception.
[bio18-s3.1.1] The shaft is a hollow tube of dense compact bone. A tube is nearly as strong as a solid rod of the same width but much lighter, so the limb is strong but light enough to move quickly.
[bio18-s3.1.2] The wider ends are made of spongy bone, a lattice of thin bony struts that is light but strong along the lines of force, and that contains red bone marrow.
[bio18-s3.1.3] The central cavity of the shaft (the marrow cavity) contains yellow marrow.
[bio18-s3.1.4] The ends are covered with smooth articular cartilage (cartilage covering the joint surface) where they form joints. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.1.5] Apart from the joint surfaces, the whole bone is covered by a tough membrane, the periosteum, to which tendons (which join muscle to bone) and ligaments (which join bone to bone) attach.
[bio18-s3.1.6] Diagram: a long bone (the femur) in longitudinal section, labelling compact bone, spongy bone, marrow cavity with yellow marrow, red marrow in the spongy bone, articular cartilage, periosteum and shaft.
[bio18-s3.2.b] The pectoral girdle is the shoulder girdle that hangs the arm from the trunk. Its design favours freedom of movement over strength, which is the opposite of the pelvic girdle.
[bio18-s3.2.0] The pectoral girdle is formed on each side by a clavicle and a scapula. It attaches the arm to the axial skeleton and is built for a wide range of movement rather than for bearing weight.
[bio18-s3.2.1] The clavicle (collarbone) is a slender, S-shaped bone running across the top of the chest from the sternum (breastbone) to the scapula.
[bio18-s3.2.2] The clavicle acts as a strut, holding the shoulder out to the side so the arm can swing freely, and passes forces from the arm to the axial skeleton.
[bio18-s3.2.3] Its joint with the sternum is the only bony connection between the arm and the axial skeleton, which is why the arm is so mobile.
[bio18-s3.2.4] The scapula (shoulder blade) is a large, flat, triangular bone lying on the back of the rib cage.
[bio18-s3.2.5] The scapula is not joined to the ribs or vertebrae but is held in place by muscles, so it can slide and rotate, increasing the range of movement of the arm.
[bio18-s3.2.6] Its large flat surface and ridges give a large area for attachment of shoulder and arm muscles, including the biceps and triceps. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.2.7] The scapula has a shallow socket into which the rounded head of the humerus (the upper arm bone) fits, forming the shoulder joint, a ball-and-socket joint. The shallow socket allows the arm to move in almost every direction, including rotation, but makes the joint less stable than the hip.
[bio18-s3.3.b] The pelvic girdle is the hip girdle that joins the legs to the trunk. Because the legs carry the whole body, it is built for stability and strength rather than range of movement.
[bio18-s3.3.0] The pelvic girdle (pelvis) is a strong, bowl-shaped ring formed by the two hip bones, joined firmly to the sacrum (the base of the vertebral column) at the back and to each other at the front.
[bio18-s3.3.1] Each hip bone is made of several bones fused together; the names of these bones are not required.
[bio18-s3.3.2] The pelvic girdle is joined firmly to the vertebral column through the sacrum, so it is rigid. It carries the weight of the upper body and passes it to the legs, which makes it much stronger and less movable than the pectoral girdle.
[bio18-s3.3.3] It has a deep, cup-shaped socket on each side into which the head of the femur (the thigh bone) fits, forming the hip joint, a ball-and-socket joint.
[bio18-s3.3.4] The deep socket makes the hip very stable and suited to bearing weight, but it gives a smaller range of movement than the shoulder.
[bio18-s3.3.5] The pelvic girdle protects the organs of the lower abdomen: the bladder, lower intestine and reproductive organs, including the uterus.
[bio18-s3.3.6] Its broad surfaces anchor the large muscles of the legs and trunk.
[bio18-s3.3.7] The female pelvis is wider and shallower, with a larger, rounder central opening than the male pelvis, so that a baby's head can pass through during birth.
[bio18-s3.4.b] The two girdles do different jobs, and each difference in structure explains a difference in function. Set them side by side to answer comparison questions.
[bio18-s3.4.0] Attachment: the pectoral girdle is loosely attached to the axial skeleton by muscles, so the arm is free to move; the pelvic girdle is fused to the sacrum, so it is rigid.
[bio18-s3.4.1] Socket: the pectoral girdle has a shallow socket, which allows a wide range of movement; the pelvic girdle has a deep socket, which holds the femur firmly.
[bio18-s3.4.2] Function: the pectoral girdle gives mobility; the pelvic girdle gives stability and carries weight.
[bio18-s3.5.b] The arm has one bone in the upper arm and two in the forearm. Each joint between them is a different type, which gives the arm both stability and mobility.
[bio18-s3.5.0] The humerus is the long bone of the upper arm. Its rounded head fits into the socket of the scapula at the shoulder, a ball-and-socket joint.
[bio18-s3.5.1] The lower end of the humerus meets the radius and ulna at the elbow, a hinge joint (a joint that bends in one direction only). Its long shaft gives attachment to the muscles that move the shoulder and elbow.
[bio18-s3.5.2] The ulna is the longer forearm bone, on the little-finger side.
[bio18-s3.5.3] The upper end of the ulna hooks around the end of the humerus, forming the main part of the elbow hinge and the point of the elbow, which stops the arm bending backwards beyond straight.
[bio18-s3.5.4] The triceps (the muscle at the back of the upper arm) is attached to the ulna. There is more on this in Muscles and joints.
[bio18-s3.5.5] The radius is the shorter forearm bone, on the thumb side. It forms most of the joint with the wrist.
[bio18-s3.5.6] At the elbow, the rounded head of the radius turns against the ulna in a pivot joint, so the radius can rotate around the ulna to turn the palm up or down. The biceps (the muscle at the front of the upper arm) is attached to the radius.
[bio18-s3.5.7] The two forearm bones make the hand both strong and highly mobile: the ulna gives a stable hinge at the elbow, and the radius allows rotation of the hand.
[bio18-s3.6.b] The leg has one bone in the thigh and two in the lower leg, plus the patella at the knee. These bones carry the body's weight and drive walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s3.6.0] The femur (thigh bone) is the longest and strongest bone in the body.
[bio18-s3.6.1] The rounded head of the femur fits into the deep socket of the pelvic girdle at the hip. Its lower end forms the knee joint, a hinge joint, with the tibia.
[bio18-s3.6.2] The thick shaft of compact bone carries the body's weight, and it gives attachment to the large thigh muscles used in walking, running and jumping.
[bio18-s3.6.3] The patella (kneecap) is a small, flat, roughly triangular bone at the front of the knee, embedded in the tendon of the quadriceps, the large muscle at the front of the thigh.
[bio18-s3.6.4] The patella protects the front of the knee joint and holds the tendon away from the joint, improving the leverage of the quadriceps when it straightens the leg.
[bio18-s3.6.5] The tibia (shinbone) is the larger, inner bone of the lower leg, on the big-toe side. It forms the knee joint with the femur and the ankle joint below, and carries almost all the weight passed down from the femur.
[bio18-s3.6.6] The fibula is the thin outer bone of the lower leg. It is not part of the knee joint and carries little weight, but it provides attachment for muscles of the lower leg and helps form and stabilise the outer side of the ankle.
[bio18-s3.6.7] The wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones are not named in the specification; label them on diagrams as wrist bones, hand bones, ankle bones and foot bones. Many small bones give the hands their fine, flexible movements and give the feet a flexible, weight-spreading base.
[bio18-s3.7.b] The arm and the leg look different, but they follow the same basic plan. This similarity is a useful pattern to spot on a skeleton diagram.
[bio18-s3.7.0] The arm and the leg are built to the same plan: one bone in the upper part (humerus; femur), two in the lower part (radius and ulna; tibia and fibula), then many small bones in the wrist or ankle and five digits.
[bio18-s3.7.1] The same plan in the forelimbs of other vertebrates is evidence of common ancestry, because it suggests the animals inherited the pattern from a shared ancestor. There is more on this in Explaining biological phenomena.
[bio18-s3.8.b] Putting the axial and appendicular parts together on one diagram shows how the girdles and limbs attach to the central skeleton.
[bio18-s3.8.0] Diagram: front view of the whole human skeleton, labelling the axial parts (skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum) and the appendicular parts named in the specification (clavicle, scapula, pelvic girdle, humerus, radius, ulna, femur, patella, tibia and fibula), with the wrist, hand, ankle and foot bones labelled as groups.

CANDIDATES bio20-s1: Glands, locations and hormones
[bio20-s1.0.b] Your body coordinates itself partly with chemical messengers called hormones, which travel in the blood. The glands that release them directly into the blood make up the endocrine system.
[bio20-s1.0.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood, that acts on specific target cells or organs. Only cells with receptors (molecules that the hormone fits onto) for that hormone respond, so a hormone reaches the whole body but affects only its targets.
[bio20-s1.0.1] Endocrine glands are ductless: they have no tubes, so they secrete hormones directly into the blood, which carries them round the body.
[bio20-s1.0.2] Exocrine glands secrete through ducts (tubes) to the place where the secretion is used, for example the salivary glands and sweat glands.
[bio20-s1.0.3] The pancreas is both: an exocrine gland making digestive enzymes, and an endocrine gland making insulin.
[bio20-s1.0.4] Diagram: outline of the human body showing the position of the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenals, pancreas, ovaries and testes.
[bio20-s1.1.b] The pituitary is at the base of the brain, below and attached to the hypothalamus (the part of the brain that controls it). It is called the master gland because several of its hormones control other glands.
[bio20-s1.1.0] TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) stimulates the thyroid gland to release thyroxine, which is how the pituitary controls another gland.
[bio20-s1.1.1] Oxytocin, for example, causes the uterus (womb) to contract during labour, and causes milk to be released from the breasts during breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.1.2] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, so the pituitary acts as its release point rather than its maker.
[bio20-s1.2.b] Three more pituitary hormones control the reproductive organs and the kidney. Each has a different job in females and males, or in a different organ.
[bio20-s1.2.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone): in females, stimulates a follicle (a fluid-filled sac in the ovary containing an egg) to develop an egg and to produce oestrogen; in males, stimulates sperm production in the testes.
[bio20-s1.2.1] LH (luteinising hormone): in females, a surge of LH causes ovulation (release of the egg) and the empty follicle to become the corpus luteum, a temporary structure left in the ovary after ovulation.
[bio20-s1.2.2] LH in males stimulates the testes to produce testosterone, so it acts on the testes as FSH does.
[bio20-s1.2.3] ADH (antidiuretic hormone): makes the collecting ducts of the kidney more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed into the blood and a smaller volume of more concentrated urine is produced.
[bio20-s1.2.4] ADH is made in the hypothalamus and released from the pituitary, like oxytocin. There is more on its role in osmoregulation (controlling the body's water balance) in The Urinary System.
[bio20-s1.3.b] The thyroid controls how fast the body's cells use energy, and it is needed for normal growth and development.
[bio20-s1.3.0] Location: in the neck, in front of the trachea (windpipe), just below the larynx (voice box).
[bio20-s1.3.1] Thyroxine controls the metabolic rate, which is the rate of respiration in cells, so it sets how quickly the body releases energy.
[bio20-s1.3.2] Thyroxine is needed for normal growth and development, so a child lacking it grows abnormally.
[bio20-s1.3.3] Thyroxine contains iodine, so the thyroid needs iodine to make it.
[bio20-s1.4.b] The parathyroids are small glands that keep the calcium level of the blood from dropping too low.
[bio20-s1.4.0] Location: four small glands on the back of the thyroid, which is why they are named parathyroid (beside the thyroid).
[bio20-s1.4.1] Parathyroid hormone raises the calcium level of the blood when it falls.
[bio20-s1.4.2] It does this in three ways: by releasing calcium from bone, by increasing calcium absorption from the gut, and by increasing calcium reabsorption in the kidneys, so less calcium is lost in the urine.
[bio20-s1.5.b] The adrenal glands release adrenaline when you face stress or danger. It prepares the body for fight or flight, the choice between confronting a threat and running from it.
[bio20-s1.5.0] Location: one on top of each kidney, which is why they are called adrenal (next to the renal, or kidney, region).
[bio20-s1.5.1] Adrenaline prepares the body for fight or flight in stress or danger, so you can fight the threat or flee from it.
[bio20-s1.5.2] It increases heart rate and breathing rate, so more oxygen reaches the muscles.
[bio20-s1.5.3] It raises blood glucose by causing the liver to convert glycogen (the stored form of glucose) to glucose, giving the muscles more fuel for respiration.
[bio20-s1.5.4] It diverts blood to the muscles, where it is most needed for action, and dilates (widens) the pupils.
[bio20-s1.6.b] The pancreas has an endocrine part that makes insulin, the hormone that brings blood glucose down after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.0] Location: in the abdomen, behind the stomach, so it sits just behind it.
[bio20-s1.6.1] Clusters of cells called the islets of Langerhans secrete insulin directly into the blood.
[bio20-s1.6.2] Insulin lowers blood glucose by making cells take up glucose from the blood, so the level falls after a meal.
[bio20-s1.6.3] Insulin makes the liver and muscles convert glucose to glycogen for storage, which also removes glucose from the blood.
[bio20-s1.7.b] The testes make sperm and also act as endocrine glands, producing the male sex hormone testosterone.
[bio20-s1.7.0] Location: in the scrotum, outside the abdomen, so they hang in a pouch of skin.
[bio20-s1.7.1] Testosterone causes sperm production to begin at puberty (the stage of life when the body becomes sexually mature).
[bio20-s1.7.2] It causes the male secondary sexual characteristics (features that develop at puberty but aren't needed for reproduction itself): growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice and increased muscle growth.
[bio20-s1.8.b] The ovaries release eggs and also act as endocrine glands. They make two hormones, oestrogen and progesterone, which shape female development and prepare the uterus.
[bio20-s1.8.0] Location: in the lower abdomen, one on each side of the uterus (womb), so each ovary sits beside it.
[bio20-s1.8.1] Oestrogen causes the female secondary sexual characteristics, such as breast development and widening of the hips.
[bio20-s1.8.2] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) after menstruation.
[bio20-s1.8.3] Progesterone maintains the endometrium and maintains pregnancy, so the lining stays in place to support a developing embryo.
[bio20-s1.9.b] The pituitary is the one gland with five hormones on the syllabus. Each is listed here with its main function, so you can see how a single location serves several jobs.
[bio20-s1.9.0] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; FSH (Higher Level only); in females stimulates the follicle to develop an egg and produce oestrogen, in males stimulates sperm production.
[bio20-s1.9.1] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; LH (Higher Level only); in females causes ovulation and the corpus luteum, in males stimulates testosterone production.
[bio20-s1.9.2] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; oxytocin; causes uterine contraction in labour and milk release in breastfeeding.
[bio20-s1.9.3] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; TSH; stimulates the thyroid to release thyroxine.
[bio20-s1.9.4] Pituitary gland: base of the brain; ADH (Higher Level only); makes the collecting ducts more permeable, so more water is reabsorbed.
[bio20-s1.10.b] The remaining glands each make one or two hormones. This list gives the gland, location, hormone and main function, in the order of the syllabus list.
[bio20-s1.10.0] Adrenal glands: on top of each kidney; adrenaline; fight or flight, raising heart rate and blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.1] Thyroid gland: in the neck; thyroxine; controls metabolic rate.
[bio20-s1.10.2] Parathyroid glands: on the back of the thyroid; parathyroid hormone; raises blood calcium.
[bio20-s1.10.3] Pancreas: behind the stomach; insulin; lowers blood glucose.
[bio20-s1.10.4] Testes: in the scrotum; testosterone; sperm production and male secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio20-s1.10.5] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; oestrogen; female secondary sexual characteristics and thickening of the endometrium.
[bio20-s1.10.6] Ovaries: in the lower abdomen; progesterone; maintains the endometrium and pregnancy.

CANDIDATES bio22-s1: Pathogens
[bio22-s1.0.b] Some organisms and particles make us ill, and the biology of disease starts with naming them. A pathogen is an organism or agent that causes disease in its host, the organism it lives in or on.
[bio22-s1.0.0] An infectious (communicable) disease is one caused by a pathogen that can be passed from one host to another, which is why such diseases can spread through a population.
[bio22-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are not pathogens: many are harmless or useful, so bacteria and fungi should not be lumped together as 'germs'. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s1.0.2] Pathogens cause harm in three main ways: they invade and destroy host cells or tissues, they release toxins, or they take nutrients from the host.
[bio22-s1.0.3] Invasion means the pathogen destroys cells: viruses burst the cells they reproduce in, and the malaria parasite bursts red blood cells.
[bio22-s1.0.4] Toxins are poisonous chemicals that damage cells or disturb how they work; the tetanus toxin made by bacteria in a wound causes muscle spasms.
[bio22-s1.0.5] In some diseases the host's own immune response causes much of the damage, for example inflammation of the lungs in pneumonia.
[bio22-s1.1.b] A pathogen only survives if it can keep moving from host to host. So each one is adapted to its host and, often, to a carrier that helps it spread.
[bio22-s1.1.0] Each pathogen is adapted to its host: it must enter the host, survive its defences, reproduce, and leave to infect a new host.
[bio22-s1.1.1] Many pathogens need a vector, an organism that carries a pathogen from one host to another without itself being the disease: the mosquito for malaria and aphids (small sap-sucking insects) for many plant viruses.
[bio22-s1.1.2] The routes by which pathogens pass between hosts are called routes of transmission. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.2.b] A prion is an infectious protein particle. It contains no nucleic acid (no DNA or RNA), so it is neither a cell nor a virus. It is the odd one out among pathogens, because a protein alone causes the disease.
[bio22-s1.2.0] The name comes from proteinaceous infectious particle, a term coined by Stanley Prusiner in 1982 (Nobel Prize 1997).
[bio22-s1.2.1] A prion is a misfolded form of a normal protein, the prion protein, which is found on nerve cells, especially in the brain.
[bio22-s1.2.2] When a prion meets a normal prion protein, it causes the normal one to fold into the abnormal shape too, so the misfolded protein builds up in a chain reaction.
[bio22-s1.2.3] Clumps of misfolded protein kill nerve cells, leaving tiny holes so that the brain looks like a sponge under the microscope. This is why the diseases are called spongiform encephalopathy.
[bio22-s1.2.4] Prion diseases take years to appear, are always fatal and have no cure.
[bio22-s1.3.b] Prion diseases have been found in cattle, sheep and humans. Because prions are very hard to destroy, prevention depends on keeping infected material out of the food chain.
[bio22-s1.3.0] Bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, mad cow disease) in cattle spread when cattle were fed meat-and-bone meal made from infected animals.
[bio22-s1.3.1] Variant Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans is linked to eating BSE-infected beef and was first described in the UK in 1996.
[bio22-s1.3.2] Scrapie affects sheep. Kuru affected the Fore people of Papua New Guinea and spread by eating the brains of dead relatives at funeral rites.
[bio22-s1.3.3] Prions are not destroyed by normal cooking, by the usual sterilisation temperatures or by many disinfectants.
[bio22-s1.3.4] The immune system does not attack prions, because they are the body's own protein in a different shape, so the body has no defence against them.
[bio22-s1.3.5] Prevention therefore keeps prions out of the food chain: meat-and-bone meal is banned from the feed of farm animals, and the brain and spinal cord of cattle are removed from food as specified risk material.
[bio22-s1.4.b] A virus is a non-cellular particle of nucleic acid (DNA or RNA) in a protein coat. It is an obligate intracellular parasite, meaning it can reproduce only inside a living host cell. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s1.4.0] Viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in, or by changing how those cells work.
[bio22-s1.4.1] Each virus attaches to particular receptor proteins (molecules on the cell surface that it fits onto), so it infects only certain hosts and tissues: HIV infects helper T cells and influenza viruses infect cells lining the airways.
[bio22-s1.4.2] Human viral diseases include influenza, the common cold, measles, chickenpox, COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2) and AIDS (HIV).
[bio22-s1.4.3] Animal viral diseases include foot-and-mouth disease, which affects cattle, sheep and pigs, and avian influenza, which affects birds.
[bio22-s1.4.4] Plant viral disease: tobacco mosaic virus infects tobacco and tomatoes.
[bio22-s1.4.5] Antibiotics don't affect viruses, because viruses have no cell structures for the drugs to target and reproduce inside host cells.
[bio22-s1.5.b] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes with no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, about 1–10 µm long. Only some bacteria are pathogens, but those that are can multiply very quickly inside the body.
[bio22-s1.5.0] Pathogenic bacteria reproduce rapidly by binary fission (one cell splitting into two) in host tissues, so numbers rise fast.
[bio22-s1.5.1] Most bacteria cause harm by releasing toxins; some invade and destroy tissue.
[bio22-s1.5.2] Most bacterial diseases can be treated with antibiotics, which target structures bacteria have and human cells lack. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s1.5.3] Antibiotic-resistant strains (bacteria no longer killed by the drug) are increasing, for example MRSA.
[bio22-s1.6.b] Each bacterial disease has a named bacterium and a usual route of spread. Learn them as pairs, and note the plant and cattle examples as well as the human ones.
[bio22-s1.6.0] Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, spreads in airborne droplets and mainly affects the lungs.
[bio22-s1.6.1] Food poisoning is caused by Salmonella, which spreads through contaminated food such as undercooked chicken or eggs.
[bio22-s1.6.2] Cholera is caused by Vibrio cholerae in water contaminated with faeces, and causes severe diarrhoea.
[bio22-s1.6.3] Tetanus is caused by Clostridium tetani. Its spores in soil enter a wound, and its toxin causes muscle spasms, known as lockjaw.
[bio22-s1.6.4] Lyme disease is caused by Borrelia burgdorferi and is passed on by tick bites, so the tick is the vector.
[bio22-s1.6.5] In plants, fire blight of apple and pear trees is caused by Erwinia amylovora.
[bio22-s1.6.6] In cattle, bovine TB is caused by Mycobacterium bovis.
[bio22-s1.7.b] Fungi are eukaryotes (their cells have a nucleus) with cell walls of chitin. Most are made of thread-like hyphae, and some, such as yeasts, are single cells. Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts.
[bio22-s1.7.0] Parasitic fungi grow hyphae into or over host tissue and secrete enzymes that digest it, and the fungus absorbs the products.
[bio22-s1.7.1] Fungi spread by spores, which are often carried by wind, water or contact.
[bio22-s1.7.2] Athlete's foot and ringworm are skin infections caused by fungi that feed on keratin (the protein in skin); ringworm is a fungus, not a worm.
[bio22-s1.7.3] Thrush is caused by the yeast Candida albicans in the mouth or vagina, often after antibiotics have killed the bacteria that normally keep it in check.
[bio22-s1.7.4] Fungal infections are most dangerous in people with weakened immune systems.
[bio22-s1.7.5] Antibiotics don't work on fungi, because fungi are eukaryotic and have no peptidoglycan cell wall or 70S ribosomes; antifungal drugs are used instead.
[bio22-s1.8.b] Fungi infect plants and animals as well as people. The examples below affect trees, cereal crops and amphibians.
[bio22-s1.8.0] Ash dieback, caused by the fungus Hymenoscyphus fraxineus, is killing ash trees across Europe.
[bio22-s1.8.1] Dutch elm disease, caused by the fungus Ophiostoma novo-ulmi, is spread by elm bark beetles, which act as vectors.
[bio22-s1.8.2] Cereal rusts such as wheat rust are caused by Puccinia species.
[bio22-s1.8.3] Chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) infects the skin of frogs and other amphibians. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.9.b] Protists are eukaryotes, mostly single-celled, that are neither animals, plants nor fungi (kingdom Protista). Parasitic protists often have complex life cycles with more than one host. Malaria is one example.
[bio22-s1.9.0] Malaria is caused by Plasmodium, which is passed to humans by the bite of an infected female Anopheles mosquito, the vector.
[bio22-s1.9.1] The parasite reproduces first in the liver and then in red blood cells, which burst together, causing repeated fevers and anaemia (too few red blood cells).
[bio22-s1.9.2] Malaria is controlled with insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticides, draining of standing water where mosquitoes breed, and antimalarial drugs such as artemisinin. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.10.b] Other protists cause gut and blood diseases in people, and one protist-like organism caused a famine in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.10.0] Amoebic dysentery is caused by Entamoeba histolytica, which spreads in water or food contaminated with faeces.
[bio22-s1.10.1] African sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei, which is spread by tsetse flies, the vector.
[bio22-s1.10.2] Potato late blight is caused by Phytophthora infestans, a water mould (oomycete). It looks like a fungus, being thread-like and spread by spores, but it is classed with the protists.
[bio22-s1.10.3] Its spores spread on wind and rain splash in mild, wet weather, and it rots leaves and tubers.
[bio22-s1.10.4] Potato late blight caused the Irish Great Famine from 1845, because potatoes were the main crop and the disease destroyed them. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s1.11.b] A parasite lives in or on another organism, its host, feeding on it and harming it. Parasitic animals are multicellular and much larger than microorganisms; most are worms or arthropods. Endoparasites live inside the host.
[bio22-s1.11.0] Tapeworms (e.g. Taenia) live in the small intestine, absorbing digested food through their body surface and attaching by hooks and suckers.
[bio22-s1.11.1] Humans become infected with tapeworms by eating undercooked infected pork or beef.
[bio22-s1.11.2] Liver fluke (Fasciola hepatica) is a flatworm of sheep and cattle that damages the liver.
[bio22-s1.11.3] The liver fluke needs a freshwater mud snail as a second host, so it is common on wet pasture in Ireland.
[bio22-s1.11.4] Onchocerca volvulus is a roundworm spread by blackflies that causes river blindness. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s1.12.b] Ectoparasites live on the outside of the host. Worms inside the body are treated with drugs, and prevention targets how the parasite reaches us.
[bio22-s1.12.0] Head lice are ectoparasites that feed on blood from the scalp.
[bio22-s1.12.1] Ticks feed on blood and can also act as vectors, carrying the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio22-s1.12.2] Parasitic worms are treated with anthelmintic drugs that kill them, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s1.12.3] Prevention: cook meat thoroughly, maintain sanitation, and control the second host or vector, because breaking the life cycle stops the parasite spreading.
[bio22-s1.13.b] The six types of pathogen differ in whether they are cells, what genetic material they carry, their size and how they are treated. The treatment follows from the structure, which is why antibiotics work only on bacteria.
[bio22-s1.13.0] Prion: not cellular; no nucleic acid; a single protein molecule; no treatment.
[bio22-s1.13.1] Virus: not cellular; DNA or RNA; about 20–300 nm; treated with antivirals, not antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.2] Bacterium: prokaryotic cell; DNA; about 1–10 µm; treated with antibiotics.
[bio22-s1.13.3] Fungus: eukaryotic; DNA; from single cells to large networks of hyphae; treated with antifungals.
[bio22-s1.13.4] Protist: eukaryotic and mostly single-celled; DNA; treated with antiprotozoal drugs such as antimalarials.
[bio22-s1.13.5] Parasitic animal: multicellular eukaryote; DNA; from millimetres to metres in size; treated with anthelmintics.

CANDIDATES bio22-s2: Innate and adaptive immunity
[bio22-s2.0.b] Immunity is the body's ability to resist infection by pathogens (disease-causing organisms such as bacteria and viruses). The immune system does this by telling self from non-self, and it has two parts that work at different speeds.
[bio22-s2.0.0] The immune system consists of physical and chemical barriers, white blood cells (made from stem cells in the bone marrow), the lymphatic system (lymph nodes, spleen and thymus) and defence proteins in the blood, such as antibodies and complement.
[bio22-s2.0.1] The body's own cells carry their own marker molecules on their surface, which the immune system normally ignores, so foreign cells and molecules, which lack these markers, are attacked.
[bio22-s2.0.2] Innate immunity, also called non-specific immunity, is present from birth, acts against any pathogen in the same way and acts within minutes to hours.
[bio22-s2.0.3] Adaptive immunity, also called acquired or specific immunity, develops during life after exposure to a particular pathogen and acts against that pathogen only.
[bio22-s2.0.4] Adaptive immunity takes days to develop the first time, but it remembers the pathogen, which is why a later infection is dealt with much faster.
[bio22-s2.0.5] Innate defences are found in all animals and in plants; adaptive immunity, with antibodies and lymphocytes (the white blood cells that run it), is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.1.b] The best way to deal with a pathogen is to stop it getting in. Skin and the linings of the body's openings form physical barriers that block or trap microorganisms before they can cause infection.
[bio22-s2.1.0] Skin is a tough outer layer of dead, keratin-filled cells (keratin is a tough protein) that pathogens can't easily cross.
[bio22-s2.1.1] Oil (sebum) from skin glands makes the surface slightly acidic, which slows bacterial growth.
[bio22-s2.1.2] Breaks in the skin are sealed by blood clotting, which stops blood loss and blocks pathogen entry.
[bio22-s2.1.3] Mucous membranes line the breathing, digestive, urinary and reproductive tracts. Their sticky mucus traps microorganisms and dust, so these don't reach the cells beneath.
[bio22-s2.1.4] In the trachea and bronchi (the main airways to the lungs), cilia (tiny hair-like projections) beat the mucus up to the throat, where it is swallowed or coughed out.
[bio22-s2.1.5] Coughing and sneezing are reflexes (automatic responses) that expel irritants, so trapped microorganisms are blown out of the airways.
[bio22-s2.2.b] Some barriers work by chemistry rather than by physically blocking entry. Acids and enzymes kill or inhibit microorganisms, and harmless resident microorganisms add a living defence of their own.
[bio22-s2.2.0] Hydrochloric acid in the stomach, at about pH 2, kills most microorganisms swallowed with food.
[bio22-s2.2.1] Lysozyme, an enzyme in tears, saliva and mucus, breaks down bacterial cell walls, so the bacteria burst or die.
[bio22-s2.2.2] The acidic conditions in the vagina inhibit many pathogens.
[bio22-s2.2.3] Harmless microorganisms living on the skin and in the gut and vagina, the normal microbiota, compete with pathogens for space and nutrients, which leaves pathogens less room to establish an infection. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s2.3.b] If a pathogen gets past the barriers, the innate response moves on to cells that destroy it. Phagocytes find the invader, swallow it and digest it in a process called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.0] Phagocytes are white blood cells that engulf and digest pathogens, and the process is called phagocytosis.
[bio22-s2.3.1] Neutrophils are the most numerous white blood cells and the first to arrive at an infection. Macrophages, formed from monocytes (another type of white blood cell), are larger and longer-lived.
[bio22-s2.3.2] 1. The phagocyte is attracted to the pathogen by chemicals released by pathogens and damaged cells.
[bio22-s2.3.3] 2. It binds the pathogen.
[bio22-s2.3.4] 3. It surrounds the pathogen with extensions of its membrane and takes it into a vesicle (a small membrane-bound sac inside the cell).
[bio22-s2.3.5] 4. Digestive enzymes are released into the vesicle and break the pathogen down.
[bio22-s2.3.6] 5. The harmless products are absorbed or released.
[bio22-s2.3.7] Diagram: the stages of phagocytosis in four sketches: a phagocyte approaching a bacterium; membrane extensions surrounding it; the bacterium enclosed in a vesicle; the bacterium being digested. Label phagocyte, nucleus, bacterium, vesicle and digestive enzymes.
[bio22-s2.4.b] Inflammation and fever are whole-body or whole-area responses that bring more defences to an infection and make conditions harder for the pathogen. The signs you notice when you are ill are the immune system at work.
[bio22-s2.4.0] Inflammation: damaged cells and mast cells (cells in tissues that release signals when damaged) release histamine and other chemicals, which widen local blood vessels and make capillaries leakier.
[bio22-s2.4.1] More blood flow brings phagocytes and defence proteins to the site, and fluid leaks into the tissue, giving redness, heat, swelling and pain at the site.
[bio22-s2.4.2] Pus is a mixture of dead phagocytes, pathogens and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.4.3] Fever: chemicals released by white blood cells reset the temperature set point in the hypothalamus (the brain region that controls body temperature) upwards, raising body temperature. There is more on this in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio22-s2.4.4] A higher temperature slows the reproduction of many pathogens and speeds up the reactions of the immune system, so fever helps the body fight infection.
[bio22-s2.5.b] Besides phagocytes, the innate response uses proteins in the blood and released by infected cells. Plants have no adaptive immunity, so their innate defences have to do all the work.
[bio22-s2.5.0] Complement, a group of blood proteins, coats pathogens so that phagocytes engulf them more easily, and it can punch holes in bacterial membranes, bursting them.
[bio22-s2.5.1] Interferons are proteins released by virus-infected cells that make neighbouring cells resist virus replication, so the infection is slowed before it spreads.
[bio22-s2.5.2] Natural killer cells also act in the innate response, in addition to phagocytes and defence proteins.
[bio22-s2.5.3] Plants have innate defences only: physical barriers (cuticle, bark and cell walls), protective chemicals, and the death of infected cells to wall off a pathogen. There is more on this in Structures and systems for response.
[bio22-s2.6.b] Adaptive immunity depends on recognising a particular pathogen. The molecules it recognises are called antigens, and the immune system's ability to tell them apart from the body's own molecules is what makes the response specific.
[bio22-s2.6.0] An antigen is a molecule, usually a protein or polysaccharide on the surface of a pathogen or on a toxin it releases, that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s2.6.1] Antigens trigger a specific immune response, including antibody production.
[bio22-s2.6.2] Each pathogen carries several antigens, so more than one kind of antibody can be made against it. The body's own cell markers are self antigens, which are normally tolerated, so the body doesn't attack itself.
[bio22-s2.7.b] An antibody is the body's protein that matches one antigen. Its Y shape and the shape of its tips explain why each antibody works against only one antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.0] An antibody, also called an immunoglobulin, is a Y-shaped protein made by plasma cells, which are derived from B lymphocytes. It binds to one specific antigen.
[bio22-s2.7.1] An antibody has four polypeptide chains (chains of amino acids), two long heavy chains and two short light chains, held together by disulfide bonds.
[bio22-s2.7.2] The tips of the two arms are the variable regions, which differ between antibodies and form two identical antigen-binding sites. The rest of the molecule is the constant region, which is the same in all antibodies of a class.
[bio22-s2.7.3] Specificity: the shape of the antigen-binding site is complementary to the shape of one antigen, so each antibody binds only that antigen, like a key in a lock. There is more on this in Enzyme structure and specificity.
[bio22-s2.7.4] Diagram: an antibody as a Y shape, labelled heavy chain, light chain, variable region, antigen-binding site (two), constant region and disulfide bonds, with an antigen fitting one binding site.
[bio22-s2.8.b] Once antibodies have bound to an antigen, they can stop the pathogen in four different ways. The Y shape matters again here, because the two arms bind the antigen while the stem is recognised by other defences.
[bio22-s2.8.0] Agglutination: because each antibody has two binding sites, antibodies link pathogens together into clumps that can't spread and are easily engulfed.
[bio22-s2.8.1] Neutralisation: antibodies cover the parts of viruses that attach to cells, or bind to toxins (antitoxins), so that they can't act.
[bio22-s2.8.2] Opsonisation: antibodies coat pathogens, marking them for phagocytes, which have receptors for the antibody's constant region.
[bio22-s2.8.3] Complement activation: antibodies activate complement (the blood proteins of the innate response), which bursts bacterial cells.
[bio22-s2.9.b] The body can't know in advance which pathogen it will meet, so it keeps a huge variety of B cells ready. Only the ones that fit the invader are chosen to multiply, a process called clonal selection.
[bio22-s2.9.0] Lymphocytes are the white blood cells of adaptive immunity, and there are two main kinds.
[bio22-s2.9.1] B lymphocytes (B cells) produce antibodies. This is the antibody-mediated or humoral response, acting on pathogens and toxins in blood and tissue fluid.
[bio22-s2.9.2] T lymphocytes (T cells) act directly on infected body cells in the cell-mediated response (Higher Level).
[bio22-s2.9.3] Clonal selection: the body has millions of different B cells, each with one type of antibody fixed in its membrane as a receptor, made before any infection.
[bio22-s2.9.4] When a pathogen enters, only the B cells whose receptors fit its antigen bind to it. These are selected and, with chemical signals from helper T cells, divide rapidly by mitosis to form a clone of identical cells.
[bio22-s2.9.5] The clone differentiates (becomes specialised) into plasma cells and memory B cells.
[bio22-s2.9.6] Plasma cells secrete large amounts of the matching antibody into the blood, thousands of molecules per second, for a few days, and then die.
[bio22-s2.9.7] Memory cells stay in the body for years, ready to respond to the same antigen.
[bio22-s2.10.b] The first time you meet a pathogen, the response is slow. A second meeting is much faster, because memory cells are already there. This difference is the reason you rarely get the same infection twice.
[bio22-s2.10.0] Primary response, on first exposure: there is a delay of several days, often about a week, while matching B cells are selected and cloned. Antibody levels rise slowly and stay fairly low.
[bio22-s2.10.1] During the primary response the person may become ill, because the pathogen multiplies before enough antibodies are made.
[bio22-s2.10.2] Secondary response, on later exposure to the same antigen: memory cells recognise it at once and quickly produce many plasma cells, so antibodies appear within a few days, reach a much higher level and last longer.
[bio22-s2.10.3] The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms appear, so the person is immune. This is the basis of vaccination. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s2.10.4] Diagram: a graph of antibody concentration (y-axis) against time in days (x-axis), with a first exposure giving a small, slow primary peak and a second exposure giving a faster, much taller, longer secondary peak. Label both exposures, the lag phase, and the primary and secondary responses.
[bio22-s2.11.b] Antibodies work in blood and tissue fluid, but some pathogens hide inside body cells. The cell-mediated response uses T cells to find and deal with those infected cells, and it begins with the cell showing that it is infected.
[bio22-s2.11.0] The cell-mediated response is the part of adaptive immunity carried out by T lymphocytes acting directly on cells, not by antibodies.
[bio22-s2.11.1] It is needed because antibodies can't reach pathogens inside body cells, such as viruses and some bacteria, for example the TB bacterium, which lives inside macrophages.
[bio22-s2.11.2] It also destroys cancer cells and cells of transplanted organs, which is why transplant patients take drugs that suppress the immune system.
[bio22-s2.11.3] Antigen presentation: an infected body cell displays fragments of the pathogen's antigens on its surface, held in its self-marker proteins (MHC proteins).
[bio22-s2.11.4] Macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells that have engulfed a pathogen display its antigens in the same way.
[bio22-s2.11.5] T cells can't recognise free antigens, so this presentation is how they detect infection.
[bio22-s2.12.b] Helper T cells act as the managers of adaptive immunity. Once one is activated, it signals to the other cells that need to act, so it links the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.12.0] Activation of helper T cells: a helper T cell whose receptor fits the displayed antigen binds to the antigen-presenting cell and is activated. It divides to form a clone of helper T cells, as B cells do.
[bio22-s2.12.1] Helper T cells coordinate the response by releasing chemical signals called cytokines.
[bio22-s2.12.2] Cytokines stimulate the matching killer T cells to divide.
[bio22-s2.12.3] Cytokines also stimulate the matching B cells to divide into plasma cells and memory cells, because B cells need this help to respond fully.
[bio22-s2.12.4] Cytokines also attract and activate macrophages, which increases phagocytosis at the infection.
[bio22-s2.12.5] Helper T cells therefore link the cell-mediated and antibody responses.
[bio22-s2.13.b] After helper T cells give the signal, the killer T cells destroy infected cells, the B cells add antibodies, and suppressor T cells end the response. Memory cells are left behind for next time.
[bio22-s2.13.0] Killer T cells (cytotoxic T cells) of the clone bind to body cells displaying the antigen and kill them, destroying the pathogens inside before they can multiply. There is more on this in White blood cells.
[bio22-s2.13.1] When the infection is cleared, suppressor T cells switch off the response by stopping the activity of killer T cells and B cells, so that healthy tissue isn't damaged.
[bio22-s2.13.2] Memory T cells remain, so a second infection triggers a faster, larger cell-mediated response.
[bio22-s2.13.3] Role of B lymphocytes: B cells act as antigen-presenting cells, taking in the antigen they bind and displaying it to helper T cells.
[bio22-s2.13.4] The antibodies released by the plasma cells of B cells mark infected cells and pathogens for destruction.
[bio22-s2.13.5] Diagram: draw a flow diagram in this order: pathogen; antigen presented by macrophage or infected cell; helper T cell activated and cloned; cytokines; killer T cells cloned, infected cells killed; B cells cloned into plasma cells, antibodies; memory T and B cells formed; suppressor T cells end response.
[bio22-s2.14.b] The two systems differ in speed, specificity and memory, but they are not separate. A comparison of the two is a standard exam question, so learn each as a pair.
[bio22-s2.14.0] Presence: innate immunity is present from birth, while adaptive immunity develops after exposure to a pathogen.
[bio22-s2.14.1] Specificity: innate immunity is non-specific, giving the same response to every pathogen, while adaptive immunity is specific, with each response against one antigen.
[bio22-s2.14.2] Memory: innate immunity gives the same response each time, while adaptive immunity has memory, so a second response is faster and stronger.
[bio22-s2.14.3] Speed: innate immunity acts within minutes to hours, while adaptive immunity takes days on first exposure.
[bio22-s2.14.4] Mechanisms: innate immunity uses barriers, phagocytes (neutrophils and macrophages), inflammation, fever, complement, interferons and natural killer cells; adaptive immunity uses B and T lymphocytes and antibodies.
[bio22-s2.14.5] Distribution: innate defences are found in all animals and in plants, while adaptive immunity is found only in vertebrates.
[bio22-s2.14.6] The two systems work together: macrophages of the innate system present antigens to start the adaptive response, and antibodies of the adaptive system make phagocytosis and complement more effective.

CANDIDATES bio22-s3: Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment
[bio22-s3.0.b] Acquired immunity is the specific (adaptive) protection a person has against one particular pathogen. It can be obtained in two basic ways, depending on who makes the antibodies, and each way can be natural or artificial.
[bio22-s3.0.0] Active immunity: the person's own immune system makes antibodies and memory cells (long-lived cells that remember the pathogen) in response to an antigen, the molecule on a pathogen that the immune system recognises as foreign.
[bio22-s3.0.1] Active immunity takes days to weeks to develop, because the body has to build its response from scratch. It is long-lasting, often for years or for life, because memory cells remain.
[bio22-s3.0.2] Passive immunity: ready-made antibodies from another person or animal are given to the person, so their own immune system does none of the work.
[bio22-s3.0.3] Passive immunity gives immediate protection but is short-lived, lasting weeks to a few months, because the antibodies are gradually broken down and no memory cells are made.
[bio22-s3.0.4] Because passive immunity leaves no memory cells, a later infection with the same pathogen triggers a primary response (the slow first-time response), as if for the first time.
[bio22-s3.0.5] Natural immunity comes from normal life events such as infection, pregnancy and breastfeeding.
[bio22-s3.0.6] Artificial immunity comes from deliberate medical intervention, such as vaccines and antibody injections.
[bio22-s3.1.b] Combining active or passive with natural or artificial gives four routes to acquired immunity. Each has a typical example, and comparing active with passive is a standard exam question.
[bio22-s3.1.0] Active natural immunity, by infection: catching a disease and recovering leaves memory cells against that pathogen. For example, most people who have had chickenpox or measles don't get them again.
[bio22-s3.1.1] Active artificial immunity, by vaccination: a vaccine stimulates the immune system to make antibodies and memory cells without causing the disease, for example the MMR vaccine against measles, mumps and rubella.
[bio22-s3.1.2] Passive natural immunity, from the mother: antibodies (IgG, the main antibody type in blood) cross the placenta into the fetus during pregnancy, so the baby is born with protection it did not make.
[bio22-s3.1.3] After birth, breast milk, especially the first milk (colostrum), contains antibodies, mainly IgA, that protect the baby's gut and breathing passages, where many infections enter.
[bio22-s3.1.4] This passive protection lasts for the newborn's first few months, while its own immune system develops. The baby does not make its own memory cells from breast milk.
[bio22-s3.1.5] Passive artificial immunity, by donor transfer of antibodies: an injection of antibodies collected from donors (human immunoglobulin) or from immunised animals gives instant protection when there is no time for active immunity to develop.
[bio22-s3.1.6] Examples of passive artificial immunity: tetanus immunoglobulin for an unvaccinated person with a dirty wound; rabies immunoglobulin after a bite from a possibly rabid animal; hepatitis B immunoglobulin for babies born to infected mothers; antivenom, made in horses or sheep, for snake bites.
[bio22-s3.1.7] Active immunity uses the person's own antibodies and memory cells, starts slowly and lasts long. Passive immunity uses donor antibodies, starts immediately, forms no memory and is short-lived. Active immunity is used for prevention in advance; passive immunity is used for emergency protection or to protect newborns.
[bio22-s3.2.b] Vaccination gives active immunity without the danger of catching the disease. It works by letting the immune system practise on a harmless version of the pathogen.
[bio22-s3.2.0] A vaccine contains antigens of a pathogen in a form that can't cause disease. Vaccines contain antigens, not antibodies, which is why they produce active immunity.
[bio22-s3.2.1] Given by injection, or by mouth or nasal spray, a vaccine triggers a primary response, so memory cells form.
[bio22-s3.2.2] A later infection meets a fast, strong secondary response (the quicker, bigger response of memory cells), and the disease doesn't develop. There is more on this in Innate and adaptive immunity.
[bio22-s3.3.b] Vaccines differ in what form of the pathogen they contain. Each type is a different way of presenting an antigen safely, and each has typical examples.
[bio22-s3.3.0] Live attenuated vaccines contain a weakened pathogen that reproduces only slightly, so the immune system sees a realistic infection that causes little or no illness, e.g. MMR, and BCG against TB.
[bio22-s3.3.1] Inactivated vaccines contain a killed pathogen, which cannot reproduce at all, e.g. the injected polio vaccine and most injected flu vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.2] Toxoid vaccines contain an inactivated toxin (a harmful chemical made by a bacterium), so the body learns to neutralise the toxin, e.g. tetanus and diphtheria vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.3] Subunit vaccines contain only a purified antigen, not the whole pathogen, often made by genetically modified cells, e.g. the hepatitis B and HPV vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.4] mRNA vaccines contain the genetic instructions (messenger RNA) for a pathogen protein, which the body's cells use to make the antigen themselves, e.g. some COVID-19 vaccines.
[bio22-s3.3.5] Viral vector vaccines use a harmless virus that carries an antigen gene into body cells, which then make the antigen. There is more on this in Viruses.
[bio22-s3.4.b] A single dose may not give lasting protection, and a vaccinated person is not the only one who benefits. Boosters strengthen the individual's immunity, and herd immunity protects the whole community.
[bio22-s3.4.0] Booster doses: a second or later dose of the same vaccine produces a secondary response, raising antibody levels and the number of memory cells, so immunity lasts longer, e.g. tetanus boosters.
[bio22-s3.4.1] A vaccine booster is active immunity, because the person makes their own antibodies; an injection of donor antibodies is passive.
[bio22-s3.4.2] Herd immunity: when a high enough proportion of a population is immune, a pathogen can't find enough susceptible hosts to keep spreading, so people who can't be vaccinated are protected indirectly.
[bio22-s3.4.3] People who can't be vaccinated include newborns, people with weakened immune systems and people allergic to a vaccine component.
[bio22-s3.4.4] The proportion needed depends on how infectious the disease is. The calculation is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.5.b] Vaccination began as an observation tested in an experiment, and it has since removed a human disease from the world. Like any medical measure it has benefits and some concerns.
[bio22-s3.5.0] In 1796 Edward Jenner showed that infecting a boy, James Phipps, with cowpox, a mild disease, protected him from smallpox. The word vaccine comes from vacca, Latin for cow.
[bio22-s3.5.1] Worldwide vaccination led the World Health Organization to declare smallpox eradicated in 1980, the first human disease to be eradicated.
[bio22-s3.5.2] Benefits: vaccines prevent illness, disability and death, are cheaper than treatment, and protect others through herd immunity.
[bio22-s3.5.3] Concerns: rare side effects, most of them mild, e.g. a sore arm or a low fever.
[bio22-s3.5.4] Live vaccines are not given to people with weakened immune systems, because even a weakened pathogen could cause disease in them.
[bio22-s3.5.5] Misinformation lowers uptake, and outbreaks follow when coverage falls, because herd immunity is lost. The MMR case is in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s3.6.b] Prevention is better than cure, so many strategies work by breaking the chain of transmission before a pathogen reaches a person. These three cut off the routes of contact, water and food.
[bio22-s3.6.0] Hygiene: handwashing with soap removes and kills microorganisms on the hands, breaking transmission by contact. The Semmelweis case is in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio22-s3.6.1] Covering coughs and sneezes and cleaning surfaces also reduce transmission, because they stop droplets and contaminated surfaces passing pathogens on.
[bio22-s3.6.2] Clean water and sanitation: treating drinking water by filtering and chlorination, and safely disposing of sewage, stops the spread of waterborne diseases such as cholera.
[bio22-s3.6.3] Food safety: cooking food thoroughly kills pathogens, e.g. Salmonella in chicken.
[bio22-s3.6.4] Food safety: refrigeration slows the growth of pathogens.
[bio22-s3.6.5] Food safety: pasteurisation heats milk to about 72 °C for 15 seconds and then cools it quickly, killing pathogens without spoiling the milk.
[bio22-s3.6.6] Food safety: avoiding cross-contamination between raw and cooked food stops pathogens moving from raw food, which may carry them, to food that will not be cooked again.
[bio22-s3.7.b] Other strategies target the carrier of a disease or the route of entry into the body. Each one is matched to how a particular disease spreads.
[bio22-s3.7.0] Vaccination: vaccines given before exposure protect the vaccinated person and, through herd immunity, others. The vaccine types are in the vaccination blocks above.
[bio22-s3.7.1] Vector control (a vector is an organism that carries a pathogen between hosts): insecticide-treated bed nets, insecticide spraying and draining standing water reduce the mosquitoes that carry malaria; controlling ticks reduces Lyme disease.
[bio22-s3.7.2] Barrier methods: condoms reduce the spread of sexually transmitted infections such as HIV by stopping body fluids passing between partners.
[bio22-s3.7.3] Blood safety: donated blood is screened for HIV and hepatitis viruses, and needles are sterile and used once, so blood-borne pathogens are not passed from one person to another.
[bio22-s3.8.b] Pathogens also spread through equipment, between sick and healthy people, and among crops and livestock. These strategies deal with each of those.
[bio22-s3.8.0] Sterilisation: autoclaving, which uses steam under pressure at 121 °C, kills microorganisms and spores (tough resting forms of bacteria) on equipment.
[bio22-s3.8.1] Disinfectants and antiseptics are used on surfaces and skin, to kill or inhibit microorganisms there.
[bio22-s3.8.2] Aseptic technique (working so that no microorganisms are introduced) in surgery prevents wound infection.
[bio22-s3.8.3] Isolation separates people who are ill. Quarantine separates people, or animals and plants, who may have been exposed until it is clear they are not infected.
[bio22-s3.8.4] Contact tracing finds people who have been exposed to a case. Population-level use of isolation and quarantine is in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s3.8.5] In plants and farm animals: resistant varieties and breeds, certified disease-free seed and stock, crop rotation, fungicide sprays (e.g. against potato blight), and good animal housing and hygiene.
[bio22-s3.8.6] Prion diseases (diseases caused by infectious misfolded proteins) are prevented by keeping prions out of feed and food. There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio22-s3.9.b] Once an infection is established, drugs can treat it. Antibiotics are the main treatment for bacterial disease because bacterial cells differ from human cells in ways a drug can exploit.
[bio22-s3.9.0] Antibiotics are chemicals, originally made by microorganisms, that kill bacteria or stop them growing, without harming human cells.
[bio22-s3.9.1] Antibiotics work because they target structures that bacteria have and human cells lack.
[bio22-s3.9.2] Penicillin stops bacteria building their peptidoglycan cell wall (the strong mesh around a bacterial cell), so growing bacteria burst.
[bio22-s3.9.3] Tetracycline and streptomycin bind to bacterial 70S ribosomes and stop protein synthesis; human cytoplasmic ribosomes are 80S, so human cells are not harmed.
[bio22-s3.9.4] Antibiotics have no effect on viruses, because viruses have no cell wall or ribosomes of their own for the drugs to target.
[bio22-s3.9.5] Responsible antibiotic use: take antibiotics only when prescribed for a bacterial infection, take the full course as directed, and never share them.
[bio22-s3.9.6] Unnecessary use selects for resistant bacteria, because those that survive the drug go on to reproduce. The detail is in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s3.10.b] Different pathogens need different drugs, and some treatments simply help the body fight on. The drug type must match the type of pathogen.
[bio22-s3.10.0] Antiviral drugs interfere with a stage of virus replication inside the host cell, so each is specific to one virus or a few. Examples and how they act are in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s3.10.1] There are far fewer antivirals than antibiotics, because viruses use the host cell's own machinery, which a drug must not damage.
[bio22-s3.10.2] Antifungal drugs target structures of fungal cells, e.g. clotrimazole cream for athlete's foot and thrush.
[bio22-s3.10.3] Antiprotozoal drugs treat protist infections, e.g. artemisinin-based drugs for malaria.
[bio22-s3.10.4] Anthelmintics kill parasitic worms, e.g. ivermectin.
[bio22-s3.10.5] Monoclonal antibodies, which are identical antibodies made in the laboratory against one antigen, are used to treat some infections and cancers.
[bio22-s3.10.6] Supportive treatment: rest, fluids and rehydration, e.g. oral rehydration solution of water, salts and sugar for cholera, and reducing fever, while the immune system clears the infection.

CANDIDATES bio22-s4: Viruses: replication
[bio22-s4.0.b] A virus is not a cell, so it lacks the machinery that every living cell uses to build new copies of itself. This is why a virus can only reproduce by taking over a host cell (the living cell it infects).
[bio22-s4.0.0] A virus has no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), almost no enzymes and no energy supply, so it can't make anything by itself.
[bio22-s4.0.1] It reproduces by delivering its nucleic acid (its DNA or RNA) into a host cell. The host cell then makes new virus particles using its own ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier), nucleotides (the building blocks of nucleic acids) and amino acids (the building blocks of proteins).
[bio22-s4.1.b] Every virus follows the same basic cycle of six stages, which ends with new viruses leaving the cell. The first three get the virus's nucleic acid inside the right kind of cell and free to act.
[bio22-s4.1.0] Stage 1, attachment: proteins on the capsid (the protein coat) or the envelope (the outer membrane some viruses have) bind to specific receptor proteins on the host cell membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.1] HIV binds to the CD4 receptor on helper T cells, and the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 binds to the ACE2 receptor on cells lining the airways. Receptor binding decides which cells a virus can infect, because only cells with the matching receptor can be attached to.
[bio22-s4.1.2] Stage 2, entry: a bacteriophage (a virus that infects bacteria) injects only its nucleic acid through the bacterial wall, leaving the empty coat outside.
[bio22-s4.1.3] Animal viruses enter whole, either by the host membrane folding in around them (endocytosis) or, for enveloped viruses, by the envelope fusing with the host membrane.
[bio22-s4.1.4] Stage 3, uncoating: inside the cell, the capsid breaks down and releases the viral nucleic acid, so its genes can be read.
[bio22-s4.2.b] Once the viral nucleic acid is free inside the cell, the virus uses the cell's machinery to make its parts, put them together and escape. The new viruses then repeat the cycle in other cells.
[bio22-s4.2.0] Stage 4, replication and synthesis: the viral genes take over the cell. Viral mRNA (the message copied from a gene) is made, and the host's ribosomes translate it into viral proteins, which are capsid proteins and viral enzymes.
[bio22-s4.2.1] In stage 4 the viral nucleic acid is copied many times, and host-cell activities are often shut down so that the cell's resources go into making viruses.
[bio22-s4.2.2] Stage 5, assembly: new nucleic acid and capsid proteins come together to form many new virus particles, often hundreds per cell. Viruses are assembled from parts, not grown or divided.
[bio22-s4.2.3] Stage 6, release: either the cell bursts (lysis), releasing all the viruses at once and killing the cell, or, for enveloped viruses, each new virus pushes out through the membrane and takes a piece of it as its envelope (budding), so the cell can survive for a time.
[bio22-s4.2.4] The new viruses infect neighbouring cells, and the cycle repeats.
[bio22-s4.2.5] Diagram: the lytic cycle of a bacteriophage in five or six sketches in a circle: phage attached by tail fibres; DNA injected; phage DNA copied and proteins made; new phages assembled; cell bursting. Label phage, bacterial cell wall, phage DNA, bacterial chromosome, new phages.
[bio22-s4.2.6] Diagram: an enveloped animal virus, such as influenza, entering a cell by endocytosis or fusion and leaving by budding, with the host membrane forming the new envelope.
[bio22-s4.3.b] A virus does not always burst its host straight away. Some bacteriophages (and some human viruses) can instead hide inside the host for a time, so there are two possible routes after infection.
[bio22-s4.3.0] Lytic cycle: the virus replicates immediately, following the stages above, and the host cell bursts. Example: bacteriophage T4 infecting the bacterium E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.1] Lysogenic cycle: some viruses insert their DNA into the host cell's chromosome, where it stays inactive and is copied every time the cell divides, so all daughter cells carry it.
[bio22-s4.3.2] In bacteria the inserted DNA is called a prophage. Example: bacteriophage lambda in E. coli.
[bio22-s4.3.3] A trigger, such as stress like UV light, can cut the viral DNA out of the chromosome and start the lytic cycle.
[bio22-s4.3.4] Latency in human viruses: some viruses hide in cells in an inactive state for years and reactivate later.
[bio22-s4.3.5] Herpes simplex virus stays in nerve cells after the first infection and reactivates to cause repeated cold sores.
[bio22-s4.3.6] Varicella-zoster virus causes chickenpox, stays in nerve cells, and can reactivate decades later as shingles.
[bio22-s4.3.7] A latent virus is hidden from the immune system and from antiviral drugs, because it is not making viral proteins or new viruses that they could target.
[bio22-s4.4.b] Viruses cause disease in several different ways, from simply destroying cells to turning the body's own defences against it. Each mode of action follows from what a virus does to the cells it infects.
[bio22-s4.4.0] Killing cells: lysis destroys infected cells. Influenza destroys cells lining the airways, causing a sore throat and cough, and the cold virus damages cells of the nose and throat.
[bio22-s4.4.1] Disrupting cell function: the virus diverts the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and energy to making viruses, so the cell stops its normal work.
[bio22-s4.4.2] Damaging the immune system: HIV infects and destroys helper T cells, the white blood cells that coordinate the immune response.
[bio22-s4.4.3] As helper T cell numbers fall over years, the immune system can no longer coordinate its responses, and the person develops AIDS (acquired immune deficiency syndrome).
[bio22-s4.4.4] People with AIDS die of opportunistic infections and cancers, which a healthy immune system would control but which take hold when the immune system is weakened.
[bio22-s4.4.5] Causing cancer: some viruses insert genes that interfere with the control of cell division, e.g. human papillomavirus (HPV) and cervical cancer. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio22-s4.4.6] Triggering harmful immune responses: much of the fever, aches and tissue damage in viral illness is caused by the body's own immune response (inflammation) to infected cells.
[bio22-s4.5.b] Viruses are grouped by the type of nucleic acid they carry, and this affects where they replicate, which enzymes they need and how fast they change. Comparing the two groups is a likely exam question.
[bio22-s4.5.0] Genetic material: a DNA virus has DNA as its genetic material; an RNA virus has RNA.
[bio22-s4.5.1] DNA viruses include herpes simplex and varicella-zoster (chickenpox) viruses, human papillomavirus (HPV), adenoviruses, the smallpox virus, and bacteriophages T4 and lambda.
[bio22-s4.5.2] RNA viruses include influenza, measles, mumps, polio, rhinoviruses (the common cold), SARS-CoV-2, HIV, Ebola and tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s4.5.3] Where they replicate: most DNA viruses enter the host nucleus and use the host's own enzymes, such as DNA polymerase and RNA polymerase, to copy their DNA and make mRNA.
[bio22-s4.5.4] Poxviruses such as the smallpox virus are an exception: they replicate in the cytoplasm with their own enzymes.
[bio22-s4.5.5] Most RNA viruses replicate in the cytoplasm and must carry or code for their own enzyme, an RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, to copy RNA from RNA, because host cells have no enzyme that does this.
[bio22-s4.5.6] Mutation rate: the enzyme that copies RNA has no proofreading (no checking for mistakes), so RNA viruses make many more copying errors and mutate much faster than DNA viruses, whose DNA is copied by host polymerases that proofread.
[bio22-s4.5.7] RNA viruses such as influenza and HIV change their surface antigens quickly, so new strains escape existing immunity. This is why flu vaccines are updated every year, and drug resistance appears quickly.
[bio22-s4.6.b] Once inside the cell, an RNA virus's genes still have to be turned into proteins. RNA viruses do this in one of two ways, depending on the virus.
[bio22-s4.6.0] In some RNA viruses, such as poliovirus and SARS-CoV-2, the RNA acts directly as mRNA when it enters the cell and is translated at once by the host's ribosomes.
[bio22-s4.6.1] In others, such as influenza and measles, the RNA must first be copied into a complementary strand that acts as mRNA.
[bio22-s4.6.2] DNA is a more stable molecule than RNA, which fits with DNA viruses mutating more slowly.
[bio22-s4.6.3] Mutation as a factor in disease emergence is covered further in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s4.7.b] Retroviruses break the usual rule that information flows from DNA to RNA. HIV is the key example, and its cycle has a few extra steps compared with the general cycle.
[bio22-s4.7.0] A retrovirus is an RNA virus that makes a DNA copy of its RNA inside the host cell, using its own enzyme, reverse transcriptase, which reverses the usual direction of DNA to RNA. HIV is an example.
[bio22-s4.7.1] HIV replication, step 1: its envelope protein binds the CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
[bio22-s4.7.2] Step 2: the envelope fuses with the cell membrane, and the capsid enters.
[bio22-s4.7.3] Step 3: reverse transcriptase makes double-stranded DNA from the viral RNA.
[bio22-s4.7.4] Step 4: a second viral enzyme, integrase, inserts this DNA into the host's chromosome, where it is called a provirus and can stay hidden for years.
[bio22-s4.7.5] Step 5: when the cell is activated, the provirus is transcribed into viral mRNA and new viral RNA, and viral proteins are made.
[bio22-s4.7.6] Step 6: new viruses assemble and bud from the cell, taking an envelope of host membrane.
[bio22-s4.7.7] Reverse transcriptase makes many errors, so HIV mutates very quickly, which is one reason there is still no effective HIV vaccine.
[bio22-s4.7.8] Diagram: an HIV particle (envelope with surface proteins, capsid, two RNA strands and reverse transcriptase), and a flow of the six steps into and out of a helper T cell. Label receptor, viral RNA, viral DNA, host chromosome (provirus) and budding virus.
[bio22-s4.8.b] Antibiotics don't work on viruses, so viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs that interrupt one stage of the replication cycle. Each drug targets a step that depends on a viral protein.
[bio22-s4.8.0] Antiviral drugs block a step in the cycle that uses a viral protein, so the host cell isn't harmed.
[bio22-s4.8.1] Aciclovir blocks the DNA polymerase of herpes viruses, which treats cold sores and shingles.
[bio22-s4.8.2] Oseltamivir blocks neuraminidase, the influenza enzyme that releases new viruses from the cell surface.
[bio22-s4.8.3] HIV drugs include reverse transcriptase inhibitors, of which zidovudine (AZT), approved in 1987, was the first.
[bio22-s4.8.4] HIV drugs also include protease inhibitors, which stop new viral proteins being cut into their working form.
[bio22-s4.8.5] HIV is treated with a combination of several antiretroviral drugs at once (antiretroviral therapy).
[bio22-s4.8.6] Antiretroviral therapy keeps the virus at very low levels and lets the immune system recover, but it doesn't cure the infection, because the provirus remains in the host DNA.
[bio22-s4.8.7] Using several drugs together makes resistance much less likely, since the virus would need several mutations at once.
[bio22-s4.9.b] The syllabus asks you to model how viruses replicate within cells. A model turns the six stages into something you can draw, handle and use to make predictions, while leaving out some detail.
[bio22-s4.9.0] To model the cycle, first list the stages in order: attachment, entry, uncoating, replication and synthesis, assembly and release. Then draw each as a labelled sketch or box, showing the host-cell part used at that stage (receptor, membrane, ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides).
[bio22-s4.9.1] Join the stages with arrows into a cycle, because the new viruses go on to infect other cells.
[bio22-s4.9.2] Mark how a DNA virus, an RNA virus and a retrovirus differ at stage 4.
[bio22-s4.9.3] Mark where an antiviral drug would act.
[bio22-s4.9.4] A physical model can use card or modelling clay to represent viruses and cells, to show attachment and budding.
[bio22-s4.9.5] The model shows the order of events, the dependence on host machinery, and why one infected cell releases many viruses. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s4.9.6] The model simplifies the timing (a cycle can take minutes to hours), the numbers released, the molecular steps, and the differences between viruses.
[bio22-s4.9.7] Worked prediction: if each infected cell releases 100 viruses and each of those infects a new cell, two cycles from one virus give 100 × 100 = 10,000 viruses. This is an illustrative calculation, not a figure for a real virus.
[bio22-s4.9.8] Diagram: a flow chart of the cycle, with stages as labelled boxes joined by arrows into a loop, and the host-cell part used at each stage marked on it.

CANDIDATES bio23-s4: The menstrual cycle
[bio23-s4.0.b] Once a month, the female reproductive system gets ready for a possible pregnancy and, if none begins, starts over. This repeating pattern is the menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s4.0.0] The menstrual cycle is the series of changes in the ovary (where eggs are stored) and the uterus (the womb) that happens about once a month in a woman from puberty to menopause, except during pregnancy.
[bio23-s4.0.1] Its purpose is to release an egg and to prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus) to receive an embryo, so a fertilised egg has somewhere to implant and grow.
[bio23-s4.0.2] The cycle lasts about 28 days on average, but a cycle of about 21 to 35 days is normal, and its length varies between women and from month to month.
[bio23-s4.0.3] Day 1 is the first day of bleeding, so every day number in the cycle is counted from the start of a period.
[bio23-s4.0.4] Two sets of changes run side by side: the ovarian cycle, in which a follicle (a small fluid-filled sac holding an egg) develops, releases its egg and becomes the corpus luteum, and the uterine cycle, in which the endometrium is shed, rebuilt and thickened.
[bio23-s4.0.5] Hormones (chemical messengers carried in the blood) coordinate the two sets of changes, which is why the ovary and uterus stay in step with each other.
[bio23-s4.0.6] The first period (menarche) occurs during puberty, and the cycles end at the menopause. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.1.b] The cycle starts with the period, the phase where the lining built up in the last cycle is lost. Counting from day 1, it is the first of the four phases.
[bio23-s4.1.0] Menstruation lasts about days 1–5 of a typical 28-day cycle, although the typical days given for each phase are only approximate.
[bio23-s4.1.1] If no egg was fertilised in the previous cycle, the thickened outer layer of the endometrium is no longer needed, so it breaks down and is shed through the cervix (the neck of the uterus) and vagina.
[bio23-s4.1.2] The shed lining leaves with a small amount of blood. This is a period, and the shedding lasts about 3–7 days.
[bio23-s4.2.b] After the old lining is lost, the body prepares a new egg and rebuilds the lining. This phase is named after the follicles in the ovary that grow during it.
[bio23-s4.2.0] The follicular phase runs from day 1 to about day 13, so it overlaps with menstruation.
[bio23-s4.2.1] In the ovary, several follicles start to develop, and usually one grows into a large, mature, fluid-filled follicle containing the egg, called a Graafian follicle.
[bio23-s4.2.2] In the uterus, after menstruation ends, the endometrium is repaired and grows thicker from about day 6, rebuilding the lining that was lost.
[bio23-s4.2.3] Near the end of this phase the cervical mucus (the fluid made at the cervix) becomes thin, clear and slippery, which lets sperm through.
[bio23-s4.3.b] Ovulation is the high point of the ovarian cycle: the mature egg leaves the ovary.
[bio23-s4.3.0] Ovulation happens at about day 14: the Graafian follicle bursts at the surface of the ovary and releases its egg, which is swept into the fallopian tube (the tube that carries the egg towards the uterus).
[bio23-s4.3.1] This is the most fertile time, because the egg survives only about 12–24 hours, so sperm must already be present or arrive within that short window. There is more on this in Gametes and fertilisation.
[bio23-s4.4.b] After the egg is released, the ovary and the uterus prepare for a possible embryo. What happens next depends on whether an embryo implants.
[bio23-s4.4.0] The luteal phase lasts about days 15–28. The empty follicle turns into a yellow mass of cells, the corpus luteum (Latin for “yellow body”).
[bio23-s4.4.1] The endometrium thickens further, becoming spongy, with more blood vessels and glands, so that it is ready for an embryo to implant (embed in the lining).
[bio23-s4.4.2] If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down after about 10–14 days and the endometrium is shed, so the next menstruation begins on day 1 of a new cycle.
[bio23-s4.4.3] If an embryo implants, the corpus luteum stays active, so the lining is kept and there is no period. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.5.b] Not every woman ovulates on day 14. The timing depends on a fixed gap before the next period, not a fixed day number.
[bio23-s4.5.0] Ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period starts, because the luteal phase is fairly constant at about 14 days.
[bio23-s4.5.1] Differences in cycle length come mostly from the follicular phase, since that is the phase that varies in length.
[bio23-s4.5.2] So in a 35-day cycle ovulation is about day 21, not day 14, because 35 minus 14 gives day 21.
[bio23-s4.6.b] Here is each phase with its days, what the ovary is doing and what the endometrium is doing, for a typical 28-day cycle.
[bio23-s4.6.0] Menstruation: days 1–5; ovary: the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down; endometrium: shed.
[bio23-s4.6.1] Follicular phase: days 1–13; ovary: a follicle develops into the Graafian follicle; endometrium: repaired and growing thicker from about day 6.
[bio23-s4.6.2] Ovulation: about day 14; ovary: the Graafian follicle bursts and releases the egg; endometrium: at the thickness built up in the follicular phase.
[bio23-s4.6.3] Luteal phase: days 15–28; ovary: the corpus luteum forms and then breaks down if there is no pregnancy; endometrium: thickens further, becoming spongy, and is shed if no embryo implants.
[bio23-s4.7.b] A timeline puts the ovary and the uterus side by side, so you can see how the two cycles line up day by day.
[bio23-s4.7.0] Diagram: a timeline, day 0 to day 28. Above: the ovary (follicle growing, Graafian follicle, ovulation at day 14, corpus luteum forming then breaking down). Below: endometrium thickness (shed days 1–5, thickening to day 28). Label the four phases along the timeline.
[bio23-s4.8.b] Four hormones coordinate the cycle. Knowing which organ makes each one is the key to explaining the rest of this topic.
[bio23-s4.8.0] FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone) are made by the pituitary gland (a small gland at the base of the brain). Oestrogen is made mainly by the developing follicle, and in smaller amounts by the corpus luteum. Progesterone is made by the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s4.9.b] The cycle restarts when progesterone falls, and the follicular phase is then driven by FSH and oestrogen. Each hormone's level explains what happens in the ovary and uterus.
[bio23-s4.9.0] Menstruation: oestrogen and progesterone are both low, because the corpus luteum of the last cycle has broken down.
[bio23-s4.9.1] The fall in progesterone is what causes the endometrium to break down. Low oestrogen alone is not the cause.
[bio23-s4.9.2] Low progesterone no longer holds back the pituitary, so FSH begins to rise.
[bio23-s4.9.3] Follicular phase: FSH stimulates follicles to develop, and one becomes the Graafian follicle. The growing follicle secretes oestrogen, so oestrogen rises steadily.
[bio23-s4.9.4] Oestrogen repairs and thickens the endometrium and makes the cervical mucus thin.
[bio23-s4.9.5] Rising oestrogen at first inhibits (holds back) FSH, so FSH falls back and usually only one follicle completes development.
[bio23-s4.10.b] Ovulation is triggered by a sudden surge of LH, which is itself set off by high oestrogen.
[bio23-s4.10.0] When oestrogen reaches a high peak (around day 12–13), it stimulates the pituitary to release a large surge of LH, with a smaller rise in FSH.
[bio23-s4.10.1] The LH surge causes the Graafian follicle to burst and release its egg about a day later (ovulation, about day 14).
[bio23-s4.10.2] Oestrogen falls just after ovulation.
[bio23-s4.11.b] After ovulation the corpus luteum takes over, and progesterone rises. Whether the cycle ends or a pregnancy continues depends on whether the corpus luteum survives.
[bio23-s4.11.0] Luteal phase: LH causes the empty Graafian follicle to develop into the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s4.11.1] The corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and some oestrogen), so progesterone rises to a peak around day 21.
[bio23-s4.11.2] Progesterone maintains and further thickens the endometrium, making it ready for implantation, and makes the cervical mucus thick again.
[bio23-s4.11.3] High progesterone, with oestrogen, inhibits FSH and LH, so no new follicle develops and no further egg is released while the corpus luteum is active. FSH and LH stay low.
[bio23-s4.11.4] End of the cycle: without pregnancy, low LH means the corpus luteum is no longer maintained. It breaks down after about 10–14 days, so progesterone and oestrogen fall.
[bio23-s4.11.5] The endometrium can no longer be maintained and is shed (menstruation). The fall removes the inhibition of FSH, which rises and starts a new cycle.
[bio23-s4.11.6] If an embryo implants, a hormone from the embryo keeps the corpus luteum alive, so progesterone stays high and menstruation does not happen. There is more on this in Hormones in reproduction.
[bio23-s4.12.b] This summary gives the level of each hormone in each phase, with the state of the ovary.
[bio23-s4.12.0] Menstruation: FSH rising, LH low, oestrogen low, progesterone low; a new follicle begins to grow.
[bio23-s4.12.1] Follicular phase: FSH high then falling, LH low, oestrogen rising, progesterone low; a follicle grows to the Graafian follicle.
[bio23-s4.12.2] Ovulation: LH surge, a small FSH peak, oestrogen at its peak then falling, progesterone low; the Graafian follicle bursts.
[bio23-s4.12.3] Luteal phase: FSH and LH low, progesterone high with a peak about day 21, oestrogen at a moderate second, smaller peak, both falling at the end; the corpus luteum active, then breaking down.
[bio23-s4.13.b] The ovary and the pituitary signal each other in a loop, and that feedback is what keeps the cycle going from one month to the next.
[bio23-s4.13.0] The cycle is controlled by feedback, meaning each hormone's level changes the release of the next.
[bio23-s4.13.1] Negative feedback: progesterone, and moderate oestrogen, inhibits the pituitary's release of FSH and LH. This keeps FSH and LH low in the luteal phase, and their fall lets the corpus luteum break down. There is more on negative feedback in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio23-s4.13.2] Positive feedback: high oestrogen just before ovulation causes more LH to be released, giving the LH surge.
[bio23-s4.13.3] Each hormone controls the next: FSH → follicle grows → oestrogen → LH surge → ovulation and corpus luteum → progesterone → FSH and LH inhibited → corpus luteum breaks down → progesterone falls → menstruation and FSH rises.
[bio23-s4.13.4] Technology link: home ovulation test kits detect the LH surge in urine, showing that ovulation is likely within about a day or two.
[bio23-s4.13.5] Technology link: the combined contraceptive pill gives steady oestrogen and progesterone, which by negative feedback keep FSH and LH low, so no follicle matures and no LH surge occurs.
[bio23-s4.13.6] Diagram: this sequence as a loop (a flow chart) linking the pituitary, the ovary and the endometrium, with “+” on the oestrogen-to-LH arrow and “−” on the progesterone-to-FSH/LH arrow.
[bio23-s4.14.b] Exams show graphs of the four hormones across a 28-day cycle. You need to recognise each curve and explain every change by its cause.
[bio23-s4.14.0] How to read the standard graphs of a 28-day cycle: FSH has a rise early in the cycle and a small peak at day 14.
[bio23-s4.14.1] LH is low except for a sharp, tall peak just before day 14.
[bio23-s4.14.2] Oestrogen rises through the follicular phase to a peak just before the LH surge, drops after ovulation, then has a lower second rise in the luteal phase.
[bio23-s4.14.3] Progesterone is low until ovulation, rises to a peak around day 21, then falls to day 28.
[bio23-s4.14.4] Exam answers explain each change by its cause, for example: progesterone rises after day 14 because the corpus luteum secretes it.
[bio23-s4.14.5] Diagram: two graphs on one 28-day time axis, one for FSH and LH (pituitary), one for oestrogen and progesterone (ovarian), lined up with the ovary events and endometrium thickness from the timeline. Label the LH surge, oestrogen peak, progesterone peak and ovulation.

CANDIDATES bio23-s6: Hormones in reproduction
[bio23-s6.0.b] Reproduction is run by chemical messengers (hormones) carried in the blood. Three places make nearly all of them, and in pregnancy a fourth organ joins in.
[bio23-s6.0.0] Reproduction is controlled by hormones from three places: the pituitary gland at the base of the brain, the testes (the male gonads, which make sperm) and the ovaries (the female gonads, which make eggs).
[bio23-s6.0.1] The pituitary gland makes oxytocin and prolactin, and also the hormones that stimulate the gonads, which is how the brain controls the testes and ovaries.
[bio23-s6.0.2] The testes make testosterone, and the ovaries make oestrogen and progesterone. In pregnancy the placenta (the organ that links mother and foetus) also makes oestrogen and progesterone.
[bio23-s6.0.3] Prolactin is made by the pituitary gland, and its main role is to stimulate milk production in the breasts. There is more on where each gland sits in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.b] Puberty is the changeover from child to adult body. A hormone signal from the brain switches the reproductive system on.
[bio23-s6.1.0] Puberty is the stage, usually in the early teens, when the reproductive organs mature and become able to produce gametes (sperm or eggs), and the secondary sexual characteristics develop. These are the body changes, apart from the reproductive organs themselves, that mark a person as male or female.
[bio23-s6.1.1] Puberty typically begins between about 8 and 13 in girls, and between about 9 and 14 in boys.
[bio23-s6.1.2] It begins when the brain signals the pituitary to increase its release of the hormones that stimulate the gonads (the testes and ovaries). There is more on these pituitary hormones in the Higher Level block on the gonad-stimulating hormones.
[bio23-s6.1.3] As a result the testes begin to make much more testosterone, and the ovaries much more oestrogen. It is these sex hormones that cause the changes of puberty.
[bio23-s6.1.4] Testosterone at puberty starts sperm production, causes growth of the penis and testes, and causes the male secondary sexual characteristics. It also increases sex drive.
[bio23-s6.1.5] Oestrogen at puberty causes growth of the uterus (womb) and vagina, preparing the body for pregnancy, and the female secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.1.6] Oestrogen, working with progesterone, starts the menstrual cycles, so a girl becomes able to ovulate and conceive; the first period is called menarche.
[bio23-s6.2.b] Once puberty has started the gametes, hormones keep them coming. The pattern differs between the sexes: continuous in men, monthly in women.
[bio23-s6.2.0] Testosterone is needed throughout adult life for sperm production in the seminiferous tubules (the coiled tubes inside the testes where sperm are made), and to maintain the male reproductive organs and secondary sexual characteristics.
[bio23-s6.2.1] Testosterone is made by the interstitial cells between the tubules, not by the tubules themselves, which make sperm.
[bio23-s6.2.2] Its level stays fairly steady from day to day, so sperm are made continuously rather than in a cycle, which is why men stay fertile from day to day.
[bio23-s6.2.3] Egg production: each month hormones cause one follicle (a fluid-filled sac holding a developing egg) to mature and release its egg, and prepare the endometrium (the lining of the uterus). There is more on the hormone levels and feedback in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.3.b] After fertilisation the embryo has to bury itself in the uterus lining (implantation) and the lining must then be kept, not shed. Two hormones make this possible.
[bio23-s6.3.0] Progesterone prepares the endometrium for implantation: it keeps it thick, spongy and well supplied with blood vessels, and makes its glands secrete nutrients for the early embryo before the placenta forms.
[bio23-s6.3.1] Without high progesterone, implantation cannot succeed, because the embryo would have no suitable lining to attach to or feed from.
[bio23-s6.3.2] hCG (human chorionic gonadotrophin) is a hormone made by the outer cells of the embryo from implantation.
[bio23-s6.3.3] hCG keeps the corpus luteum (the structure left in the ovary after ovulation) alive and secreting progesterone, so the endometrium is not shed and menstruation stops. Home pregnancy tests detect hCG in urine, using a monoclonal-antibody test.
[bio23-s6.4.b] The corpus luteum cannot support a whole pregnancy, so the placenta takes over hormone production. Progesterone from it then keeps the pregnancy going in several ways.
[bio23-s6.4.0] From about the end of the third month the placenta takes over from the corpus luteum, making large amounts of progesterone and oestrogen for the rest of pregnancy; the corpus luteum then breaks down.
[bio23-s6.4.1] Progesterone during pregnancy maintains the endometrium and so the placenta, which is attached to it.
[bio23-s6.4.2] Progesterone keeps the muscle of the uterus relaxed, so it does not contract and expel the foetus early.
[bio23-s6.4.3] Progesterone keeps the cervical mucus plug (the mucus that seals the cervix, the neck of the uterus) thick, and prepares the milk-producing glands of the breasts.
[bio23-s6.4.4] Progesterone, with oestrogen, prevents further ovulation and menstruation during pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.5.b] Oestrogen supports the growth of the foetus by building up the mother's tissues, and late in pregnancy it prepares her body for birth.
[bio23-s6.5.0] Oestrogen during pregnancy drives the growth of the foetus: it stimulates growth of the muscle of the uterus so it enlarges with the growing foetus, and increases blood flow to the uterus and placenta, which supplies the foetus.
[bio23-s6.5.1] Oestrogen also develops the milk ducts of the breasts, so they are ready to carry milk after birth.
[bio23-s6.5.2] Late in pregnancy oestrogen makes the uterus muscle more sensitive to oxytocin, preparing it for labour.
[bio23-s6.5.3] Level summary: progesterone and oestrogen rise steadily to very high levels by late pregnancy, far above their levels in the menstrual cycle, and fall sharply after the placenta is delivered.
[bio23-s6.6.b] Birth needs the powerful uterus muscle to contract strongly. A shift in hormone balance and the hormone oxytocin make this happen.
[bio23-s6.6.0] Near the end of pregnancy the balance of hormones changes: oestrogen's effects increase relative to progesterone's, so the uterus muscle becomes more responsive to oxytocin and begins to contract.
[bio23-s6.6.1] Oxytocin is made in the hypothalamus (a part of the brain just above the pituitary) and released by the pituitary into the blood.
[bio23-s6.6.2] Oxytocin causes the muscle of the uterus to contract strongly in labour, the process of giving birth.
[bio23-s6.7.b] Labour speeds itself up. Each contraction causes more oxytocin, which causes stronger contractions, a pattern called positive feedback, where a change leads to more of the same change.
[bio23-s6.7.0] 1. The baby's head presses on and stretches the cervix.
[bio23-s6.7.1] 2. Stretch receptors in the cervix send nerve impulses to the brain.
[bio23-s6.7.2] 3. The pituitary releases more oxytocin.
[bio23-s6.7.3] 4. Stronger contractions push the head harder against the cervix, stretching it more.
[bio23-s6.7.4] 5. The cycle repeats, building up until the baby is born, which removes the stimulus (the stretching), so the loop stops.
[bio23-s6.7.5] A nervous pathway (the impulses) and a hormonal pathway (oxytocin in the blood) work together here. There is more on this in Nervous vs hormonal coordination.
[bio23-s6.7.6] Diagram: the positive feedback loop as a cycle: baby's head stretches cervix → nerve impulses to brain → pituitary releases oxytocin → uterus contracts more strongly → back to the start; birth ends the loop.
[bio23-s6.8.b] Oxytocin's work does not stop when the baby arrives. It also helps the uterus recover, and doctors use a man-made version of it.
[bio23-s6.8.0] After the birth, oxytocin keeps the uterus contracting, which expels the placenta and closes the blood vessels at its attachment site, reducing bleeding.
[bio23-s6.8.1] Oxytocin also helps the uterus shrink back towards its normal size, because its contractions squeeze the muscle smaller.
[bio23-s6.8.2] Synthetic oxytocin is given by drip in hospital to start (induce) or speed up labour, and to reduce bleeding after birth.
[bio23-s6.9.b] The breasts are prepared during pregnancy, but milk only starts once the placenta has gone. The hormone that actually makes milk is prolactin.
[bio23-s6.9.0] During pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone develop the breasts' ducts and milk glands, and prolactin rises.
[bio23-s6.9.1] The high levels of oestrogen and progesterone stop milk being produced, even though prolactin is already rising.
[bio23-s6.9.2] When the placenta is delivered, oestrogen and progesterone fall sharply, so prolactin can act and milk production begins.
[bio23-s6.9.3] Prolactin stimulates the milk glands to produce milk, which is why it is called the milk-making hormone.
[bio23-s6.9.4] Suckling by the baby stimulates nerve endings in the nipple, causing the pituitary to release more prolactin, so the more the baby feeds, the more milk is made: supply matches demand.
[bio23-s6.9.5] While a mother breastfeeds frequently, high prolactin suppresses ovulation, so periods often do not return for some months; this is not reliable contraception. There is more on this in Fertility control and infertility.
[bio23-s6.10.b] Making milk is not the same as delivering it. Oxytocin does the second job, so a feed needs both hormones.
[bio23-s6.10.0] Oxytocin causes the milk to be released (called let-down, or milk ejection).
[bio23-s6.10.1] Suckling triggers oxytocin release, which makes muscle cells around the milk glands contract and squeeze milk into the ducts and out of the nipple.
[bio23-s6.10.2] Prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it.
[bio23-s6.11.b] The first milk a baby gets is different from the milk that follows. It is tuned to protect a newborn rather than to fatten it.
[bio23-s6.11.0] Colostrum is the first milk, produced for the first few days after birth.
[bio23-s6.11.1] Colostrum is a small volume of thick, yellowish fluid, richer in protein and antibodies than later milk and lower in fat and sugar.
[bio23-s6.11.2] Its antibodies give the newborn passive immunity, which means protection made by the mother rather than by the baby's own immune system. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio23-s6.11.3] Over the following days and weeks it changes to mature milk, with more fat and lactose (milk sugar) for energy and growth.
[bio23-s6.12.b] A woman's fertility does not last for life. Menopause is the point where the ovaries stop responding, and the fall in hormones has effects across the body.
[bio23-s6.12.0] Menopause is the end of a woman's menstrual cycles and of her fertility, confirmed when she has had no period for 12 months.
[bio23-s6.12.1] It usually happens between about 45 and 55 years of age.
[bio23-s6.12.2] Cause: the ovaries run out of follicles that can respond, so ovulation stops and the ovaries make much less oestrogen and progesterone. It is not simply that the eggs are used up by ovulation, because most follicles degenerate without ever ovulating.
[bio23-s6.12.3] Without these hormones the endometrium is no longer built up, so there is nothing to shed and periods stop.
[bio23-s6.12.4] Effects of falling oestrogen in the short term: hot flushes and night sweats, mood changes and poorer sleep, and vaginal dryness.
[bio23-s6.12.5] Effects of falling oestrogen in the longer term: loss of bone density, raising the risk of osteoporosis (brittle bones), and a rise in the risk of heart disease.
[bio23-s6.12.6] Men have no equivalent of the menopause: testosterone falls only slowly with age, and many men can produce sperm into old age, although sperm numbers and quality decline.
[bio23-s6.12.7] There is more on hormone replacement therapy (HRT) in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.13.b] One bullet per hormone: its gland, its roles and how its level changes through life.
[bio23-s6.13.0] Testosterone (testes; small amounts also in females): male development before birth, puberty, sperm production and sex drive. There is more on development before birth in Sex determination.
[bio23-s6.13.1] Testosterone level: low in childhood, rises sharply at puberty, high and fairly steady in adult men, and declines slowly in later life.
[bio23-s6.13.2] Oestrogen (ovaries; placenta in pregnancy): female puberty, repair and thickening of the endometrium each cycle, growth of the uterus and breast ducts in pregnancy, preparing the uterus for oxytocin, and keeping bones strong.
[bio23-s6.13.3] Oestrogen level: low in childhood, rises at puberty, rises and falls each cycle, very high in pregnancy, and falls at menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.4] Progesterone (corpus luteum; placenta in pregnancy): maintains the endometrium for implantation and pregnancy, relaxes the uterus muscle, prepares the milk glands and prevents ovulation in pregnancy.
[bio23-s6.13.5] Progesterone level: high only in the second half of each cycle; very high in pregnancy; falls sharply at birth; very low after menopause.
[bio23-s6.13.6] Oxytocin (made in the hypothalamus, released by the pituitary): contractions of the uterus in labour and after birth, and milk let-down. Its level rises sharply during labour and with each feed.
[bio23-s6.13.7] Prolactin (pituitary): milk production. Its level rises during pregnancy, stays high while breastfeeding, rising further with each feed, and falls when breastfeeding stops.
[bio23-s6.14.b] FSH and LH are the two pituitary hormones that stimulate the gonads. In males they hold testosterone steady through negative feedback, and in females they drive the monthly cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.0] At puberty the hypothalamus, the part of the brain above the pituitary, signals the pituitary to increase its release of FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.14.1] In males these hormones stimulate the testes to make sperm and testosterone; in females they stimulate follicles to develop and the ovaries to make oestrogen, starting the cycles.
[bio23-s6.14.2] In males, FSH stimulates sperm production in the seminiferous tubules, working with testosterone, so without FSH sperm output falls.
[bio23-s6.14.3] In males, LH (sometimes called interstitial-cell-stimulating hormone in males) stimulates the interstitial cells of the testes to secrete testosterone.
[bio23-s6.14.4] Male negative feedback: when testosterone in the blood rises, it inhibits the release of LH and FSH from the pituitary, so less testosterone is made; when testosterone falls, LH rises again.
[bio23-s6.14.5] This keeps testosterone and sperm production steady and continuous, unlike the female cycle.
[bio23-s6.14.6] Example: men who take anabolic steroids (synthetic testosterone) have high testosterone-like levels, which switch off their own LH and FSH, so their testes shrink and sperm production falls. There is more on this in Hormonal manipulation.
[bio23-s6.14.7] In females, FSH stimulates follicle development and oestrogen production, and the LH surge causes ovulation and forms the corpus luteum.
[bio23-s6.15.b] FSH and LH levels follow the state of the ovaries and the other hormones. When oestrogen and progesterone are high they stay low, and when these hormones are lost they rise.
[bio23-s6.15.0] In pregnancy, high progesterone and oestrogen keep FSH and LH low, so no follicles develop and no ovulation happens.
[bio23-s6.15.1] While breastfeeding, high prolactin also suppresses FSH and LH.
[bio23-s6.15.2] After the menopause, FSH and LH rise to high levels, because the ovaries no longer make enough oestrogen and progesterone to inhibit them; a high FSH level in the blood is a sign of the menopause.
[bio23-s6.15.3] Levels summary: FSH and LH are low in childhood and rise at puberty.
[bio23-s6.15.4] In men FSH and LH are fairly steady; in women they vary through each cycle, with an LH surge at ovulation.
[bio23-s6.15.5] In women FSH and LH are low in pregnancy and while breastfeeding, and high after the menopause. The levels and feedback through the month are in The menstrual cycle.
[bio23-s6.16.b] A model shows how a control centre, a hormone, a target organ and feedback fit together. You can then use it to predict what happens when one part changes.
[bio23-s6.16.0] A model of hormonal control shows, in order: the control centre (hypothalamus and pituitary), the hormone released, the target organ, the hormone or effect the target produces, and the feedback, with − for inhibition and + for stimulation.
[bio23-s6.16.1] Use the model to predict effects: blocking testosterone's feedback raises LH; giving steady oestrogen and progesterone, as in the pill, keeps FSH and LH low.
[bio23-s6.16.2] Diagram: two flow charts side by side, male then female. Male: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → testes → sperm and testosterone, with a “−” arrow from testosterone back to the pituitary.
[bio23-s6.16.3] Female flow chart: hypothalamus → pituitary → FSH and LH → ovary → follicle, oestrogen, corpus luteum, progesterone → endometrium, with “−” arrows from progesterone (and oestrogen) to the pituitary and a “+” arrow from high oestrogen to the LH surge.

CANDIDATES bio26-s1: The urinary system
[bio26-s1.0.b] Every cell produces waste as it carries out its chemical reactions, and some of that waste is harmful if it stays in the body. The kidneys are the main organs that clear it away, and they do a second job at the same time: keeping the body's fluids balanced.
[bio26-s1.0.0] Excretion is the removal of the waste products of metabolism (the chemical reactions in cells). Faeces are not excreted: they are undigested food that was never absorbed, so passing them out is egestion.
[bio26-s1.0.1] The kidneys excrete urea, excess water, excess salts and excess hydrogen ions in urine. Other organs help: the lungs excrete carbon dioxide, and sweat removes some water, salts and a little urea.
[bio26-s1.0.2] Urea is made in the liver from excess amino acids (the units of proteins) by deamination, the removal of the nitrogen-containing part of an amino acid. It is carried dissolved in the blood plasma to the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.0.3] Urea is toxic if it builds up, so it must be removed continuously rather than in occasional bursts.
[bio26-s1.0.4] Transport between environments: blood (the internal environment) carries wastes to the kidneys, the kidneys transfer them from the blood into urine, and urine is passed out of the body (the external environment).
[bio26-s1.0.5] At the same time the kidneys return useful substances (glucose, amino acids, most of the water and most of the salts) to the blood, so nutrients are not lost in the urine.
[bio26-s1.0.6] The kidneys have two roles: excretion (removing urea and other wastes), and osmoregulation and homeostasis, which regulate the volume, water content, salt concentration and pH of the body fluids.
[bio26-s1.0.7] Both roles use the same two processes: filtration of the blood (squeezing small molecules out of it), then reabsorption (taking back what the body needs). What is not reabsorbed is urine.
[bio26-s1.1.b] The kidneys sit well protected at the back of the body and are supplied directly by a large artery. That position and supply let them filter a large volume of blood at high pressure.
[bio26-s1.1.0] The kidneys are a pair of bean-shaped organs at the back of the abdomen, one on each side of the vertebral column (backbone), just below the diaphragm. The lowest ribs partly protect them.
[bio26-s1.1.1] The right kidney is slightly lower than the left because the liver lies above it and takes up space.
[bio26-s1.1.2] Each kidney is embedded in a layer of fat that cushions it against physical damage and helps hold it in place, and is covered by a tough outer membrane, the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.1.3] The adrenal glands (small hormone-producing glands) sit on top of the kidneys.
[bio26-s1.1.4] Each kidney receives blood from a renal artery, a short wide branch of the aorta (the body's main artery), so blood arrives at high pressure and in large volume, which suits filtration.
[bio26-s1.1.5] Filtered blood leaves by the renal vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart).
[bio26-s1.1.6] Blood in the renal vein has less urea, less oxygen and less glucose than blood in the renal artery, and more carbon dioxide because the kidney cells respire. It is the blood vessel with the lowest urea concentration in the body.
[bio26-s1.1.7] Diagram: front view of the urinary system. Label two kidneys, renal arteries from the aorta, renal veins to the vena cava, ureters, bladder, sphincter muscles and urethra. The adrenal glands on top of the kidneys may be shown.
[bio26-s1.2.b] Once urine has formed it must be carried away, stored and released at a suitable moment. Three structures do this in turn: the ureters carry it, the bladder stores it and the urethra lets it out.
[bio26-s1.2.0] There are two ureters: narrow muscular tubes, one from the renal pelvis (the funnel-shaped collecting space inside each kidney) down to the bladder.
[bio26-s1.2.1] The ureter walls contain smooth muscle that moves urine by peristalsis (waves of contraction), so urine reaches the bladder whatever the body position, not just by gravity.
[bio26-s1.2.2] The urinary bladder is a hollow muscular bag in the lower abdomen (pelvis) that stores urine.
[bio26-s1.2.3] The bladder wall is elastic and folded, so it can stretch to hold a large volume and contract to empty.
[bio26-s1.2.4] Two rings of sphincter muscle (muscle that closes an opening) at the bladder outlet keep it closed: an inner one that works automatically (involuntary), and an outer one under conscious (voluntary) control, which young children learn to control.
[bio26-s1.2.5] The urethra is the single tube that carries urine from the bladder out of the body.
[bio26-s1.2.6] In males the urethra is longer, runs through the penis and also carries semen. In females it is short and opens just in front of the vagina.
[bio26-s1.3.b] Cut a kidney lengthways and you can see that it is not uniform inside. The three regions each have a different role in making and collecting urine.
[bio26-s1.3.0] A kidney cut lengthways shows three regions: cortex, medulla and renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.3.1] Cortex: the dark outer layer, under the renal capsule.
[bio26-s1.3.2] Medulla: the paler inner layer, divided into cone-shaped renal pyramids.
[bio26-s1.3.3] Renal pelvis: a funnel-shaped space on the inner (concave) side that collects urine and leads into the ureter. No filtration happens in the pelvis and urine is not changed there, so it is only a collecting space.
[bio26-s1.4.b] The real work of the kidney is done by microscopic tubes called nephrons. Each one first filters the blood, then takes back what the body needs, and the leftover liquid becomes urine.
[bio26-s1.4.0] Each kidney contains about one million nephrons, microscopic tubules that are the kidney's filtering units, so together they give a huge filtering surface. Each runs from the cortex down into the medulla and back.
[bio26-s1.4.1] The nephron tubules drain into collecting ducts that run through the pyramids and open into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.4.2] Filtration takes place in the cortex: blood is forced at high pressure through knots of capillaries (glomeruli) into the cup-shaped tops of the nephrons (Bowman's capsules).
[bio26-s1.4.3] Small molecules (water, glucose, amino acids, salts and urea) are filtered out of the blood. Blood cells and plasma proteins are too large, so they stay in the blood. The filtered liquid is called the filtrate.
[bio26-s1.4.4] Reabsorption (taking useful substances back into the blood) takes place in the cortex, in the coiled tubules, and in the medulla, in the loops of Henle and the collecting ducts.
[bio26-s1.4.5] All the glucose and amino acids, most of the salts and most of the water are taken back into the blood. The remaining liquid, carrying urea and excess salts and water, is urine.
[bio26-s1.4.6] Diagram: longitudinal section of a kidney. Label renal capsule, cortex, medulla, renal pyramid, renal pelvis, ureter, renal artery and renal vein. Draw one nephron from cortex into medulla to show where filtration and reabsorption happen.
[bio26-s1.5.b] Urine is what is left of the filtrate once the body has taken back what it needs. Its volume and concentration change from hour to hour, which is how the kidneys keep the water content of the blood steady.
[bio26-s1.5.0] Urine is mostly water, with urea, salts (mainly sodium chloride) and other wastes dissolved in it.
[bio26-s1.5.1] Healthy urine contains no protein and no glucose, because protein is not filtered out of the blood and all the glucose is reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.5.2] After drinking a lot of water, the kidneys produce a large volume of dilute (pale) urine, which gets rid of the extra water.
[bio26-s1.5.3] When water is short (little drinking, heavy sweating in exercise or hot weather, or diarrhoea), a small volume of concentrated (dark yellow) urine is produced, so the body saves water.
[bio26-s1.5.4] The control is by the hormone ADH. There is more on this in ADH and osmoregulation; here you only need the effect on urine.
[bio26-s1.6.b] Water is not the only thing the kidneys balance. They also adjust salts and acidity, and together this keeps the body's internal conditions steady.
[bio26-s1.6.0] Salt balance: after a salty meal the kidneys excrete more salt, and when salt is short they reabsorb more.
[bio26-s1.6.1] Keeping the salt and water concentration of the blood steady stops body cells from gaining or losing water by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio26-s1.6.2] pH: the kidneys remove excess hydrogen ions from the blood in urine, helping to keep blood pH at about 7.4. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio26-s1.6.3] This is homeostasis: the kidneys keep the internal environment constant whatever is eaten or drunk, much as a thermostat holds a room at one temperature. This works by negative feedback, which is taught in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio26-s1.7.b] Urine is made all the time, but it is passed out only now and then. This sequence shows how it travels and why release can be delayed.
[bio26-s1.7.0] 1. Urine forms continuously in the nephrons and drains from the collecting ducts into the renal pelvis.
[bio26-s1.7.1] 2. Peristalsis in the ureter carries it to the bladder in small spurts.
[bio26-s1.7.2] 3. The bladder fills and its wall stretches. Stretch receptors in the wall send nerve impulses that give the urge to urinate.
[bio26-s1.7.3] 4. To urinate, the bladder's muscular wall contracts and the sphincters relax. The outer sphincter relaxes under voluntary control, which is learned after early childhood.
[bio26-s1.7.4] 5. Urine leaves the body through the urethra.
[bio26-s1.8.b] Each part of the urinary system has features that suit its job. Examiners ask you to link structure to function, so learn these as pairs.
[bio26-s1.8.0] Fat layer around each kidney: protects it from physical damage.
[bio26-s1.8.1] Renal artery branching directly from the aorta: delivers high-pressure blood for filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.2] About one million nephrons: a huge filtering surface.
[bio26-s1.8.3] Cortex full of capillary knots: the site of filtration.
[bio26-s1.8.4] Long tubules and loops in the medulla: the site of reabsorption.
[bio26-s1.8.5] Funnel-shaped renal pelvis: collects urine.
[bio26-s1.8.6] Muscular ureters: move urine to the bladder by peristalsis.
[bio26-s1.8.7] Elastic bladder with sphincters: stores urine and allows controlled release. The urethra is the exit for urine from the body.
[bio26-s1.9.b] Because healthy urine contains no glucose or protein, finding either one in a urine sample tells a doctor that something is wrong.
[bio26-s1.9.0] Glucose in urine is a sign of diabetes, because blood glucose is so high that not all of it can be reabsorbed.
[bio26-s1.9.1] Protein in urine is a sign of damage to the kidney's filters, because healthy filters do not let protein through.
[bio26-s1.9.2] Urine tests are also used to detect drugs, for example in sport.
[bio26-s1.10.b] If the kidneys stop working, the body can no longer clear its wastes or balance its fluids. There are two treatments: a machine that does the kidney's job, or a replacement kidney.
[bio26-s1.10.0] If the kidneys stop working, urea, salts and water build up in the blood and the person will die without treatment.
[bio26-s1.10.1] Treatment is by dialysis or by a kidney transplant.
[bio26-s1.10.2] A transplant gives a working kidney, but the patient must take drugs to stop the immune system rejecting it, because the body treats the new kidney as foreign. There is more on this in Immunity.
[bio26-s1.11.b] A dialysis machine copies the kidney's filtering. It uses diffusion across a membrane to clean the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.0] The patient's blood is pumped through a machine where it flows past a selectively permeable membrane (one that lets only some substances through), with dialysis fluid on the other side.
[bio26-s1.11.1] Urea and excess salts diffuse out of the blood into the fluid, because their concentration is higher in the blood.
[bio26-s1.11.2] The fluid contains glucose and salts at normal blood concentrations, so these are not lost from the blood. Blood cells and proteins are too large to cross the membrane.
[bio26-s1.11.3] Dialysis must be repeated several times a week, because it does not work continuously the way healthy kidneys do.

CANDIDATES bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio27-s5: Diet
[bio27-s5.0.b] Food has to supply energy and raw materials for growth and repair. A balanced diet does this without leaving the body short of anything or overloaded with anything.
[bio27-s5.0.0] A balanced diet contains all the nutrients (carbohydrate, protein, lipid, vitamins and minerals), together with water and dietary fibre, in the right amounts and proportions to meet a person's needs, with enough energy but not too much.
[bio27-s5.0.1] Balanced means right proportions, not equal amounts from each food group. A person needs far more starchy food and vegetables than fats, for example.
[bio27-s5.0.2] Malnutrition is any diet that does not meet the body's needs. Undernutrition is too little energy or too little of a nutrient, e.g. iron-deficiency anaemia or rickets (a bone disease caused by too little vitamin D).
[bio27-s5.0.3] Overnutrition is too much energy or too much of a nutrient, e.g. obesity (a body mass well above the healthy range, from long-term excess energy) or too much salt.
[bio27-s5.0.4] Energy balance: if the energy taken in from food equals the energy used in metabolism (the body's chemical reactions) and activity, body mass stays the same.
[bio27-s5.0.5] Excess energy is stored as fat, causing weight gain. Too little energy means the body uses its glycogen (the short-term carbohydrate store), then fat, and eventually muscle protein, so weight is lost.
[bio27-s5.1.b] The Food Pyramid turns the idea of a balanced diet into a picture of how much of each food group to eat. The wider the shelf, the more servings you should have from it.
[bio27-s5.1.0] Ireland's Food Pyramid (Department of Health, 2016) is a guide to a balanced diet, in which most servings come from the lower shelves.
[bio27-s5.1.1] The lower shelves are vegetables, salad and fruit (5 to 7 servings a day), and wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[bio27-s5.1.2] Milk, yoghurt and cheese (3 servings a day, or 5 for ages 9 to 18, because teenagers are growing fast and need more calcium) and meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (2 servings) are in the middle.
[bio27-s5.1.3] Fats, spreads and oils are for very small amounts, and the top shelf, foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, is not for every day.
[bio27-s5.2.b] Body mass index (BMI) gives a quick number for whether a person's mass is suitable for their height. It is a screening tool and you may be asked to calculate and interpret it.
[bio27-s5.2.0] Body mass index (BMI) is mass in kilograms divided by the square of height in metres.
[bio27-s5.2.1] For adults, the World Health Organization classes 18.5 to 24.9 as a healthy weight, 25 to 29.9 as overweight and 30 or more as obese; below 18.5 is underweight.
[bio27-s5.2.2] BMI does not distinguish fat from muscle, so a muscular athlete can have a high BMI even with little body fat. It also does not show where fat is stored.
[bio27-s5.2.3] Children and teenagers are still growing, so they are assessed with growth charts for their age and sex instead of the adult categories.
[bio27-s5.2.4] Worked example: 70 kg ÷ (1.75 m × 1.75 m) = 70 ÷ 3.06 = 22.9, a healthy weight.
[bio27-s5.3.b] A balanced diet is not the same for everyone. What a person needs depends on their health, their age, their gender and how active they are, and each factor has a biological reason.
[bio27-s5.3.0] Health: illnesses change needs. People with coeliac disease (an immune reaction to gluten, a protein in wheat) must avoid gluten, and people with type 1 diabetes match carbohydrate intake to their insulin. There is more on coeliac disease in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio27-s5.3.1] People with high blood pressure are advised to eat less salt, and people with anaemia need more iron to make haemoglobin; people recovering from injury or surgery need more protein for repair, and people with lactose intolerance avoid or limit lactose (milk sugar) and need other calcium sources.
[bio27-s5.3.2] Age, children and teenagers: growing children need more protein, calcium and vitamin D for bones, and more energy per kilogram of body mass than adults. Babies' needs are met by breast milk or formula.
[bio27-s5.3.3] The teenage growth spurt raises energy, protein, calcium and iron needs, which is why the Food Pyramid gives 5 dairy servings for ages 9 to 18.
[bio27-s5.3.4] Age, older people: metabolic rate and activity usually fall, so less energy is needed. Protein (to limit muscle loss), calcium and vitamin D (to limit bone loss and osteoporosis, brittle bones), fibre (constipation is more common) and water (the sense of thirst weakens with age) remain important.
[bio27-s5.3.5] Gender: on average males have more muscle and a larger body, so they need more energy and protein. Females who menstruate lose iron in blood each month, so they need more iron. Pregnancy and breastfeeding raise the need for energy, protein, calcium and iron.
[bio27-s5.3.6] Folic acid (a B vitamin) is advised before and in early pregnancy, and pregnant women should avoid alcohol and liver, which is high in vitamin A.
[bio27-s5.3.7] Activity: active people use more energy, so they need more starchy carbohydrate (builds glycogen stores), protein for muscle repair, and water and salt to replace sweat; inactive (sedentary) people use less energy, so they need less, and eating as though active leads to weight gain.
[bio27-s5.4.b] Eating from every food group is not enough on its own. Eating a range of different foods is what makes sure nothing is missed.
[bio27-s5.4.0] No single food contains every nutrient in the right proportions, so a varied diet from all the food groups is the most reliable way to get all the vitamins, minerals, essential amino acids and essential fatty acids (the ones the body cannot make for itself).
[bio27-s5.4.1] Variety within groups also matters because different fruits and vegetables supply different vitamins, minerals and fibre types, e.g. citrus fruit for vitamin C, green leafy vegetables for iron, calcium and vitamin K, and carrots for beta-carotene.
[bio27-s5.4.2] Combining plant proteins, e.g. beans with cereal, supplies all the essential amino acids, because each food makes up for what the other lacks. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio27-s5.5.b] Water makes up most of the body and is constantly being lost, so a diet is only balanced if it also replaces water.
[bio27-s5.5.0] Water is lost every day in urine, sweat, breath and faeces, and must be replaced from drinks and food. Needs rise in hot weather, during exercise, with fever, vomiting or diarrhoea, and when breastfeeding. There is more on the roles of water in Water.
[bio27-s5.5.1] Signs of dehydration (too little water in the body) are thirst, dark-yellow urine in small volumes, headache, tiredness and poor concentration. The urine is dark and scant because the hormone ADH makes the kidneys hold on to water at Higher Level.
[bio27-s5.5.2] Severe dehydration is dangerous, especially in babies and older people.
[bio27-s5.5.3] Water is the best drink. Sugar-sweetened drinks supply water but also free sugars (sugars added to foods and drinks, explained under high-sugar diets below).
[bio27-s5.6.b] Fibre is the part of plant food that passes through you without being digested, yet it is essential for a healthy gut. It supplies almost no energy and is not absorbed.
[bio27-s5.6.0] Dietary fibre, or roughage, is plant material that human enzymes cannot digest, mainly cellulose from plant cell walls. There is more on cellulose in Structures of biomolecules.
[bio27-s5.6.1] Sources of fibre are wholegrain cereals and wholemeal bread, brown rice, vegetables, fruit (especially with skins), beans, peas and lentils, and nuts and seeds. There is no fibre in meat, fish, eggs or dairy produce.
[bio27-s5.6.2] Fibre adds bulk and absorbs water, so faeces are soft and large. This gives the gut muscles something to push against, keeps peristalsis (the waves of muscle contraction that move food along the gut) effective and prevents constipation.
[bio27-s5.6.3] Fibre makes food more filling and slows the absorption of sugar, which helps control body mass and blood glucose.
[bio27-s5.6.4] High-fibre diets are linked to a lower risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer.
[bio27-s5.6.5] Bacteria in the colon ferment some fibre, which supports a healthy gut microbiome (the community of microorganisms living in the gut).
[bio27-s5.6.6] Raising fibre intake suddenly can cause wind and bloating, and fibre needs extra water to work. Very large amounts of bran can reduce the absorption of some minerals such as iron and calcium.
[bio27-s5.7.b] Newspapers and adverts make many claims about food. Researching a claim in steps lets you judge whether the evidence is reliable before you accept it.
[bio27-s5.7.0] 1. Pose a specific, testable question, e.g. 'Does a vegan diet supply enough iron for teenage girls?'
[bio27-s5.7.1] 2. Find several reliable sources, such as public health bodies, peer-reviewed reviews (checked by other scientists before publication) and textbooks. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio27-s5.7.2] 3. Check who funded and wrote each source, and whether it sells a product, because money can bias what is reported.
[bio27-s5.7.3] 4. Judge the type of evidence. Most diet evidence comes from observational studies of large populations, which show links (correlations) but not cause, because people who choose a diet often differ in other ways; vegetarians, for example, on average smoke less and exercise more.
[bio27-s5.7.4] Controlled trials, where researchers assign the diets, give stronger evidence of cause but are usually short and small.
[bio27-s5.7.5] 5. Explain the biological mechanism behind each claim, naming the nutrient and the body process.
[bio27-s5.7.6] 6. Reach a balanced, justified conclusion for a particular group of people.
[bio27-s5.7.7] Example of funding bias: a 1967 review of diet and heart disease, by Harvard scientists in the New England Journal of Medicine, played down sugar and blamed dietary fat. In 2016 it emerged that the Sugar Research Foundation, the sugar industry's trade group, had paid for it without disclosure.
[bio27-s5.8.b] Some people leave animal foods out of their diet. The different types are defined by exactly which foods they exclude, and each has benefits and risks.
[bio27-s5.8.0] A vegetarian diet excludes meat and fish; most vegetarians eat eggs and dairy produce (lacto-ovo vegetarians). A vegan diet excludes all animal products, including eggs, dairy produce and honey. Pescatarians eat fish but not meat.
[bio27-s5.8.1] Vegetarian and vegan diets are usually higher in fibre, fruit and vegetables and lower in saturated fat, so on average blood cholesterol and BMI are lower. Observational studies link them to a lower risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
[bio27-s5.8.2] They avoid processed and red meat, which are linked to bowel cancer.
[bio27-s5.8.3] Producing plant food generally uses less land and water and releases less greenhouse gas than producing meat, which makes these diets more sustainable. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio27-s5.8.4] Ethical reasons include animal welfare.
[bio27-s5.9.b] Leaving out animal foods removes some useful nutrients. These risks call for planning, not avoidance, because each can be replaced by other foods or supplements.
[bio27-s5.9.0] Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods (with nutrients added) or supplements to prevent anaemia and nerve damage. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s5.9.1] Iron from plants is less easily absorbed than iron from meat, so vegans should eat iron-rich plants (beans, lentils, green leafy vegetables and fortified cereals) with vitamin C, which helps absorption.
[bio27-s5.9.2] Vegans must get calcium from fortified plant milks, tofu and green vegetables instead of dairy produce.
[bio27-s5.9.3] Iodine comes mainly from milk and seafood, so vegans may be short of it.
[bio27-s5.9.4] The long-chain omega-3 fatty acids are found mainly in oily fish, so vegans and vegetarians who avoid fish get little of them.
[bio27-s5.9.5] Plant proteins must be combined to supply all the essential amino acids, though this is easily done, e.g. beans with cereal.
[bio27-s5.9.6] Teenagers, pregnant women and young children have the highest needs, so a vegan diet for them needs careful planning.
[bio27-s5.10.b] Evidence about these diets mostly comes from large observational studies. Reading the results carefully means knowing what they can and cannot show.
[bio27-s5.10.0] A large UK study of people's diets and health over many years, EPIC-Oxford, found that vegetarians had a lower rate of heart disease but a slightly higher rate of stroke than meat eaters, and that vegans had more bone fractures.
[bio27-s5.10.1] Observational findings like these show links that may have other causes, because people who choose these diets also differ in other ways.
[bio27-s5.10.2] Judgement: a well-planned vegetarian or vegan diet can meet all needs, but it must replace the nutrients that meat, fish and dairy produce supply.
[bio27-s5.11.b] Not all sugar in food is the same. The recommendation applies only to free sugars, so you need to know exactly which sugars count.
[bio27-s5.11.0] Free sugars are sugars added to food and drink, plus the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juice. The sugar inside whole fruit, vegetables and milk is not counted as free sugar.
[bio27-s5.11.1] The World Health Organization (2015) recommends that free sugars make up less than 10% of daily energy intake, and ideally less than 5%.
[bio27-s5.12.b] Eating a lot of free sugar harms health in several ways, but a fair evaluation also gives the case for sugar. The amount and the frequency are what matter.
[bio27-s5.12.0] Tooth decay: plaque bacteria on teeth ferment sugar to acid, which damages tooth enamel. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s5.12.1] Excess energy from sugar is stored as fat, causing weight gain and obesity, which raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure and some cancers. Obesity is the main link between sugar and type 2 diabetes.
[bio27-s5.12.2] Sugary foods often supply energy with few other nutrients ('empty' energy) and replace more nutritious foods, and sugary drinks add energy without making a person feel full.
[bio27-s5.12.3] Sugar triggers dopamine release (a brain chemical linked to reward), which can drive overeating. There is more on this in Dopamine and endorphins.
[bio27-s5.12.4] Counter-arguments: glucose is the brain's main fuel, and sugar is a quick energy source, useful for example during long endurance exercise or for a person with diabetes whose blood glucose is too low.
[bio27-s5.12.5] Sugar in whole fruit comes with fibre and vitamins and is not the problem; the risk comes from the amount and how often it is eaten.
[bio27-s5.12.6] Policy link: Ireland introduced a tax on sugar-sweetened drinks in 2018.
[bio27-s5.12.7] Judgement: small amounts of free sugar are harmless; frequent, large amounts harm teeth and raise the risk of obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[bio27-s5.13.b] Fat is a necessary nutrient, but the type and the amount matter. Too much saturated fat is the main concern.
[bio27-s5.13.0] Saturated fats, found in butter, lard, fatty meat and cheese, raise the type of blood cholesterol (LDL) that is deposited in artery walls, narrowing them (atherosclerosis) and raising the risk of heart attack and stroke.
[bio27-s5.13.1] Artificial trans fats, once common in processed foods, are worse than saturated fats.
[bio27-s5.13.2] Replacing saturated fats with unsaturated oils (olive oil, nuts and oily fish) lowers this risk.
[bio27-s5.13.3] Counter-argument: fat is needed for energy, insulation, cell membranes, essential fatty acids and the absorption of vitamins A, D, E and K, so a very low-fat diet can cause deficiencies. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s5.13.4] Fat has about twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate, so fatty food makes weight gain easy.
[bio27-s5.14.b] People such as body-builders eat extra protein hoping to build muscle. The body does not simply store extra protein as muscle.
[bio27-s5.14.0] Extra protein beyond needs is not stored as muscle. Excess amino acids are deaminated (the nitrogen part is removed) and the urea is excreted, and the rest is used for energy or stored as fat. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio27-s5.14.1] Muscle growth depends on training, with enough protein and energy.
[bio27-s5.14.2] High-protein diets based on processed and red meat bring more saturated fat, and they are often low in fibre.
[bio27-s5.15.b] Processed foods have been changed by manufacture, such as being cooked, refined or packaged. Many fast and processed foods have a poor nutrient profile.
[bio27-s5.15.0] Many processed and fast foods are high in fat, sugar and salt and low in fibre, vitamins and minerals.
[bio27-s5.15.1] Implications include constipation, a higher risk of bowel cancer, heart disease, type 2 diabetes and obesity, and high salt intake linked to high blood pressure. There is more on salt in Minerals.
[bio27-s5.15.2] Counter-point: some processed foods are useful and nutritious, e.g. frozen vegetables, tinned beans and fortified cereals, so the label, not the word processed, is what matters.
[bio27-s5.16.b] Some people cut out dairy produce or gluten. For some this is medically necessary, but for others it is a choice that has costs.
[bio27-s5.16.0] Dairy-free and gluten-free diets are necessary for people with milk allergy, lactose intolerance (where lactose-free milk is another option) or coeliac disease.
[bio27-s5.16.1] For other people they bring no proven benefit and need care, since dairy produce is the main calcium and iodine source in many Irish diets, and gluten-free products are often lower in fibre and more expensive.
[bio27-s5.17.b] Some people try to lose weight fast by skipping meals or by eating very little (crash diets). This has several biological drawbacks.
[bio27-s5.17.0] Skipping meals and very low-energy (crash) diets run down glycogen stores, leading to tiredness and poor concentration.
[bio27-s5.17.1] The body breaks down muscle protein as well as fat, nutrients are likely to be missed, and weight lost quickly is often regained.

CANDIDATES bio29-s2: Circulation
[bio29-s2.0.b] Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to get rid of wastes. The circulatory system is the transport network that does this for all of them.
[bio29-s2.0.0] The human circulatory system has three parts: the heart, a pump; the blood vessels, a closed network of tubes made up of arteries, capillaries and veins; and the blood, the transport medium that carries the materials.
[bio29-s2.0.1] It is a closed system: blood stays inside the vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across the thin walls of the capillaries (the tiniest vessels, which pass right alongside the cells).
[bio29-s2.0.2] In a large, many-celled animal, diffusion (the slow, random spreading of molecules) alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut and kidneys) and cells deep in the body.
[bio29-s2.0.3] The circulatory system solves this by carrying materials quickly by mass flow (the whole liquid moving together), so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
[bio29-s2.0.4] Materials transported: oxygen (lungs to all cells); carbon dioxide (cells to lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine to liver to cells); urea (liver to kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands to target organs); water and salts.
[bio29-s2.0.5] Also transported: antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets (which seal wounds); and heat, carried from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin.
[bio29-s2.1.b] Humans have two circuits joined at the heart rather than one loop. Blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and is then sent out to the rest of the body.
[bio29-s2.1.0] Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. This does not mean two hearts; it is one heart with two sides.
[bio29-s2.1.1] The pulmonary circuit (pulmonary means 'of the lungs') runs heart to lungs to heart. The systemic circuit runs heart to body organs to heart.
[bio29-s2.1.2] The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit. There is more on the chambers in Investigation: the heart.
[bio29-s2.1.3] Full route of a red blood cell: vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, semilunar valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valve, aorta, artery, arteriole, capillaries of an organ, venule, vein, vena cava.
[bio29-s2.1.4] Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation. Heart as four boxes, lungs above, body organs below. Add arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae.
[bio29-s2.1.5] Diagram labels: right and left atrium and ventricle, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta, venae cavae, pulmonary circuit, systemic circuit. Shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue.
[bio29-s2.2.b] The pulmonary circuit has one job: to send blood that has given up its oxygen to the lungs to collect more. It starts at the right side of the heart.
[bio29-s2.2.0] 1. Deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) is pumped by the right ventricle (the lower right chamber, which pumps blood out of the heart) into the pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s2.2.1] 2. The pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung, so both lungs receive blood.
[bio29-s2.2.2] 3. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂, because oxygen diffuses in from the air in the alveoli (air sacs) and CO₂ diffuses out. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio29-s2.2.3] 4. Oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium (the upper left chamber, which receives blood coming into the heart).
[bio29-s2.3.b] The systemic circuit takes oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to every organ and brings it back. Its steps follow on from the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.3.0] 1. Oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve (the one-way valve between the left atrium and left ventricle) to the left ventricle.
[bio29-s2.3.1] 2. The left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, the body's largest artery.
[bio29-s2.3.2] 3. Arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ, where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes.
[bio29-s2.3.3] 4. Deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae (the large veins bringing blood from the upper and lower body) and into the right atrium.
[bio29-s2.3.4] 5. It passes through the tricuspid valve (the one-way valve between the right atrium and right ventricle) to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
[bio29-s2.4.b] A double circulation is better than a single loop for an active, warm-blooded animal. Passing the blood through the heart twice solves two problems: lost pressure and the risk of damage to the lungs.
[bio29-s2.4.0] (a) Blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all the body organs quickly.
[bio29-s2.4.1] (b) The two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit, to drive blood through the many organs, and lower in the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.4.2] The lower pulmonary pressure matters because the lung capillaries are thin-walled, so they are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs).
[bio29-s2.4.3] (c) The septum (the wall dividing the two sides of the heart) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated.
[bio29-s2.4.4] Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
[bio29-s2.5.b] Blood reaches each organ along its own named artery and leaves along its own named vein. Knowing these names lets you describe the route to any organ.
[bio29-s2.5.0] A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and is drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and each is usually named after the organ.
[bio29-s2.5.1] Lungs (pulmonary pathway): the pulmonary artery, from the right ventricle, carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins, to the left atrium, carry oxygenated blood. In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s2.5.2] Head and brain: the carotid arteries, in the neck, supply it, and the jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava. Arms: the subclavian arteries and veins. Legs: the iliac arteries and veins.
[bio29-s2.5.3] Kidneys: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered, and the renal veins return filtered blood, low in urea, to the inferior vena cava. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio29-s2.5.4] Heart muscle: the coronary arteries and cardiac veins, which supply the heart's own muscle with blood.
[bio29-s2.5.5] Liver and gut: the liver receives blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut). Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
[bio29-s2.5.6] Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels showing the heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
[bio29-s2.6.b] Most veins take blood straight back towards the heart. The vein from the gut is unusual because it detours through the liver first, so the liver gets the first chance to deal with what has just been absorbed.
[bio29-s2.6.0] A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. Blood therefore passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
[bio29-s2.6.1] The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the capillaries in the villi (the tiny finger-like folds of the small intestine wall), and from the walls of the stomach and large intestine.
[bio29-s2.6.2] It carries this blood to a second set of capillaries, the sinusoids, in the liver. After a meal this blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
[bio29-s2.6.3] Absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio29-s2.6.4] The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood, because it has already passed through the gut capillaries, so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery.
[bio29-s2.7.b] The circulatory system works as the delivery service for every other system. Each system either puts materials into the blood or takes materials out of it, and you can model these exchanges.
[bio29-s2.7.0] Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂. The circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and carries CO₂ back, so without circulation the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
[bio29-s2.7.1] Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals, small vessels in the villi) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells. The blood also supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
[bio29-s2.7.2] Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it. The kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume.
[bio29-s2.7.3] Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs, for example insulin from the pancreas to the liver and ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys. Adrenaline, carried in the blood, increases heart rate.
[bio29-s2.7.4] Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds.
[bio29-s2.7.5] Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells. Active muscles receive extra blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, and contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping to return blood to the heart.
[bio29-s2.7.6] Nervous system: the medulla oblongata (the part of the brain that controls automatic functions) adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles (small arteries), so blood is sent where it is needed. The brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
[bio29-s2.7.7] Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs, and widening or narrowing of the skin arterioles controls heat loss.
[bio29-s2.8.b] A model is a simplified picture that shows how the systems connect. A flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre makes it clear which materials travel where.
[bio29-s2.8.0] Diagram: a labelled flow diagram modelling these interactions, with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain. Label each arrow with the materials transferred: O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones and heat.
[bio29-s2.8.1] One limitation of the model is that it shows what moves but not the structure of the vessels or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
[bio29-s2.8.2] Worked example: during exercise, muscles respire faster, so breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s2.8.3] In the same example, heart rate rises and the arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them, while the arterioles to the gut narrow. Heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

CANDIDATES bio29-s3: Heartbeat
[bio29-s3.0.b] The heart does not wait for instructions to beat. A small patch of muscle in its own wall sets the pace and sends a wave of electrical signals that makes the chambers contract in the right order.
[bio29-s3.0.0] A heartbeat is one contraction and relaxation of the heart muscle, pumping blood out of the ventricles (the two lower chambers) into the arteries.
[bio29-s3.0.1] An adult heart beats about 70 times a minute at rest, a normal resting range being about 60 to 100, and it does so every minute of life.
[bio29-s3.0.2] Cardiac muscle (heart muscle) is myogenic: the beat starts within the heart muscle itself, without a nerve impulse. This is why a heart removed for transplant keeps beating for a time if it is supplied with oxygenated fluid.
[bio29-s3.0.3] The pacemaker is the sinoatrial node (SA node), a small patch of specialised cardiac muscle in the wall of the right atrium (an upper chamber), near where the superior vena cava (the large vein from the upper body) enters.
[bio29-s3.0.4] The SA node produces electrical impulses at regular intervals by itself. Each impulse starts one heartbeat, so the SA node sets the rate of the heartbeat.
[bio29-s3.1.b] The impulse from the SA node does not reach every part at once. It travels in a set route with a built-in pause, which makes the atria contract first and the ventricles second.
[bio29-s3.1.0] 1. The impulse from the SA node spreads across the walls of both atria, so the atria contract together, pushing blood into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.1] 2. A layer of non-conducting tissue between the atria and the ventricles stops the impulse passing straight to the ventricles, so the ventricles cannot contract too early.
[bio29-s3.1.2] 3. The impulse reaches the atrioventricular node (AV node), at the base of the right atrium near the septum (the wall between the two sides of the heart). The AV node delays it for a fraction of a second, giving the atria time to empty into the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.1.3] 4. The AV node passes the impulse down conducting fibres in the septum (the bundle of His) to the apex (the pointed lower tip of the heart), and then up through the ventricle walls (Purkinje fibres).
[bio29-s3.1.4] 5. The ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the aorta and pulmonary artery, the two arteries leaving the heart. The atria therefore contract before the ventricles, and the two sides beat together.
[bio29-s3.1.5] Diagram: a front-view outline of the heart showing the SA node in the wall of the right atrium, the AV node near the septum, the conducting fibres down the septum and up the ventricle walls, with arrows for the spread of the impulse.
[bio29-s3.2.b] The SA node sets a basic rhythm, but the body's needs change from moment to moment. Nerves and a hormone adjust the rate the SA node sets, so the heart can speed up or slow down.
[bio29-s3.2.0] The rate is changed to match the body's needs. It rises during exercise, because the muscles then need more oxygen and glucose and produce more CO₂, which the faster-flowing blood delivers and carries away.
[bio29-s3.2.1] Nervous control: the cardiac centre in the medulla oblongata (part of the brainstem) sends impulses along two sets of nerves to the SA node. One set, the sympathetic nerves, speeds up the heart rate; the other, the vagus nerve, a parasympathetic nerve, slows it down.
[bio29-s3.2.2] The medulla responds to information from receptors, which detect changes in the body. For example, a rise in CO₂ in the blood increases heart rate, and a rise in blood pressure slows it.
[bio29-s3.2.3] Hormonal control: adrenaline, released by the adrenal glands in stress, danger or excitement, acts on the SA node and increases the heart rate and the force of contraction.
[bio29-s3.2.4] Other factors that change heart rate include exercise and fitness, age (higher in babies and children), body temperature (a fever raises it), emotion, caffeine and nicotine, which raise it, and some medicines.
[bio29-s3.2.5] Artificial pacemaker: if the SA node fails, or the impulse does not reach the ventricles properly (heart block), the heart beats too slowly or irregularly. A small battery-powered device is implanted under the skin below the collarbone, with a wire, or lead, running through a vein into the heart.
[bio29-s3.2.6] The artificial pacemaker detects the heart's own rhythm and sends an electrical impulse to start a beat when one is missed or the rate is too slow. The batteries last several years and are replaced in a minor operation.
[bio29-s3.3.b] Each time the ventricles contract, they force a surge of blood into the arteries. You can feel this surge as the pulse, which gives an easy way to measure heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.0] The pulse is a wave of expansion of an artery wall, caused by the surge of blood forced into the arteries each time the ventricles contract. The stretched elastic wall then recoils. The pulse rate therefore equals the heart rate.
[bio29-s3.3.1] The pulse can be felt where an artery runs close to the skin over a bone or firm tissue. The usual sites are the radial artery at the wrist, on the thumb side, and the carotid artery in the neck, beside the windpipe.
[bio29-s3.3.2] Veins have no pulse, because the pressure wave has been lost in the capillaries (the tiny vessels between arteries and veins) before the blood reaches them.
[bio29-s3.3.3] Method for measuring pulse: 1. Sit quietly for a few minutes, so the reading is a resting one.
[bio29-s3.3.4] 2. Press lightly with the index and middle fingers, not the thumb, which has its own pulse, on the radial artery.
[bio29-s3.3.5] 3. Count the beats for 30 seconds and double the number to give beats per minute, or count for a full 60 seconds.
[bio29-s3.3.6] 4. Repeat three times and take the mean, which reduces the effect of any one miscount.
[bio29-s3.3.7] If asked what the pulse is, say it is a wave in the artery wall, not blood flowing past your finger.
[bio29-s3.4.b] Exercise makes the muscles need more oxygen, so the heart speeds up. Measuring the pulse before and after exercise shows this, and how quickly it recovers shows how fit someone is.
[bio29-s3.4.0] This is a standard class investigation with primary data (data you collect yourself). Measure the resting pulse; exercise, for example with step-ups for two minutes; then measure the pulse at once and every minute until it returns to the resting value.
[bio29-s3.4.1] The pulse rises during exercise and falls gradually afterwards. The time taken to return to the resting rate, the recovery time, is shorter in fitter people.
[bio29-s3.4.2] Fitter people also tend to have a lower resting pulse, because each beat pumps more blood.
[bio29-s3.4.3] Independent variable (the one you change): activity level (rest, or after exercise). Dependent variable (the one you measure): pulse rate in beats per minute.
[bio29-s3.4.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the type, duration and intensity of the exercise, the same person, the same counting method and the same posture.
[bio29-s3.5.b] Blood only flows round the body because the ventricles push it with force. That force, pressing on the walls of the vessels, is blood pressure, and when it stays too high it harms the body.
[bio29-s3.5.0] Blood pressure is the force that the blood exerts on the walls of the blood vessels, produced by the contraction of the ventricles.
[bio29-s3.5.1] Pressure is highest in the aorta and large arteries, falls as blood passes through the arterioles (small arteries) and capillaries, and is lowest in the veins and venae cavae.
[bio29-s3.5.2] Measurement: a sphygmomanometer, or blood pressure monitor, uses an inflatable cuff on the upper arm over the brachial artery. Pressure is given in millimetres of mercury (mmHg).
[bio29-s3.5.3] Pressure is given as two numbers: the higher pressure, when the ventricles contract, and the lower pressure, when the heart relaxes between beats. A healthy young adult at rest has a value of about 120/80 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.4] Blood pressure rises naturally during exercise or stress and falls during sleep. High blood pressure, or hypertension, means a resting value consistently at or above about 140/90 mmHg.
[bio29-s3.5.5] Hypertension usually has no symptoms, but it strains the heart, damages artery walls and raises the risk of heart attack, stroke and kidney damage.
[bio29-s3.5.6] Factors that raise blood pressure include too much salt in the diet, being overweight, lack of exercise, smoking, too much alcohol, stress, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.5.7] High blood pressure is controlled by lifestyle changes, such as less salt, exercise, weight loss and not smoking, and by medication.
[bio29-s3.6.b] The heart works non-stop, so its muscle needs a steady supply of oxygen and glucose. It cannot get these from the blood inside its chambers, so it has a separate circulation of its own.
[bio29-s3.6.0] The heart muscle cannot take oxygen and nutrients from the blood passing through its chambers: the wall is too thick for diffusion to reach most cells, and the blood on the left side passes too quickly. So the heart has its own circulation, the coronary circulation.
[bio29-s3.6.1] The two coronary arteries, left and right, are the first branches of the aorta, arising from its base just above the aortic semilunar valve (the valve at the exit of the left ventricle). They run over the surface of the heart, branching into smaller arteries that enter the muscle.
[bio29-s3.6.2] These small arteries supply a dense network of capillaries. This gives the cardiac muscle the oxygen and glucose for the aerobic respiration (energy release using oxygen) that keeps it working continuously.
[bio29-s3.6.3] Deoxygenated blood from the heart muscle is collected by the cardiac (coronary) veins, which run over the surface and join a large vein at the back of the heart, the coronary sinus, which empties into the right atrium.
[bio29-s3.6.4] Diagram: a front view of the heart showing the aorta, the left and right coronary arteries branching from its base and running over the surface, and the cardiac veins. Label each.
[bio29-s3.7.b] The coronary arteries are narrow, so damage to them has serious effects. When they narrow or block, the heart muscle is starved of oxygen.
[bio29-s3.7.0] Fatty deposits, called atheroma or plaque and containing cholesterol, can build up in the walls of the coronary arteries and narrow them (atherosclerosis), so less blood reaches the heart muscle. This can cause chest pain during exercise, called angina.
[bio29-s3.7.1] If a blood clot forms on a damaged plaque and blocks a coronary artery, the part of the heart muscle that it supplies is starved of oxygen and dies. This is a heart attack, or myocardial infarction.
[bio29-s3.7.2] Clot-dissolving enzymes given soon after a heart attack can break down the clot and restore blood flow to the starved muscle, so they limit the damage (see 'Industrial enzymes').
[bio29-s3.7.3] Risk factors: smoking, a diet high in saturated fat, which raises blood cholesterol, high blood pressure, obesity, lack of exercise, diabetes, older age and family history.
[bio29-s3.7.4] Prevention: not smoking, a balanced diet, regular exercise and a healthy weight, which each remove a risk factor.
[bio29-s3.7.5] Technology: a narrowed coronary artery can be widened by angioplasty, in which a balloon on a catheter (a thin tube) is inflated inside the artery and a small mesh tube, a stent, is left in place to hold it open.
[bio29-s3.7.6] Alternatively, the narrowed artery can be bypassed by a coronary artery bypass graft, using a piece of vein, often from the leg, or an artery to carry blood around the blockage.

CANDIDATES bio29-s5: Blood vessels
[bio29-s5.0.b] Human blood never leaves a set of tubes called blood vessels, which is why the system is described as closed. The vessels differ in structure, and the differences make sense once you know the circuit and the three-layered wall that most of them share.
[bio29-s5.0.0] Blood flows in a closed circuit: heart, arteries, arterioles (the smallest arteries), capillaries, venules (the smallest veins), veins, and back to the heart.
[bio29-s5.0.1] Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood towards the heart. Capillaries link them inside the organs, and it is across capillary walls that materials are exchanged with the cells.
[bio29-s5.0.2] The walls of arteries and veins have three layers. The inner layer is the endothelium, one cell thick and smooth, which reduces friction as blood slides past. The middle layer is smooth muscle and elastic fibres.
[bio29-s5.0.3] The outer layer is tough connective tissue containing collagen fibres, which protects the vessel and stops it over-stretching. The space in the centre, through which blood flows, is the lumen.
[bio29-s5.0.4] Capillaries have only the endothelium, with no muscle, elastic or connective layers. This is why they can act as exchange surfaces, as the capillary block below shows.
[bio29-s5.1.b] Blood leaves the heart in surges at high pressure, and arteries have to carry it to the organs without bursting. Each feature of an artery's structure helps it cope with that pressure and keep the blood moving.
[bio29-s5.1.0] Role: arteries carry blood away from the heart to the organs, at high pressure. All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery, which takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
[bio29-s5.1.1] Thick wall, with a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic fibres, to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the ventricles (the heart's lower pumping chambers) without bursting.
[bio29-s5.1.2] Elastic fibres let the wall stretch as each surge of blood enters, then recoil between beats. This pushes the blood on, keeps the pressure up between beats and smooths the flow. The stretching is felt as the pulse.
[bio29-s5.1.3] Narrow lumen relative to the thickness of the wall, which helps keep the pressure high. In section, an artery keeps a round shape, because its thick wall holds it open.
[bio29-s5.1.4] No valves, except the semilunar valves (pocket-shaped valves) where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart. The high pressure keeps blood moving forwards, so valves are not needed along the length.
[bio29-s5.1.5] Blood flows fast and in spurts, and a cut artery spurts bright red blood. Bright red is the colour of oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s5.1.6] Arterioles are the smallest arteries, leading into the capillary networks. Their walls have a high proportion of smooth muscle, which contracts to narrow them (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to widen them (vasodilation).
[bio29-s5.1.7] By narrowing or widening, arterioles control how much blood flows to each organ, for example more to the muscles in exercise and more to the skin when hot.
[bio29-s5.2.b] By the time blood has passed through the capillaries its pressure is low, so veins must return it to the heart without a strong push behind it. Their structure reduces resistance, and valves and the squeezing of nearby muscles keep blood moving one way.
[bio29-s5.2.0] Role: veins carry blood from the organs back towards the heart, at low pressure. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary veins, which bring oxygenated blood from the lungs.
[bio29-s5.2.1] Thin wall, with a thin middle layer and few elastic fibres, because the pressure is low and there is no surge to withstand. The wall is easily squashed, so in section a vein often looks flattened or irregular.
[bio29-s5.2.2] Wide lumen, which reduces resistance to the slow-moving blood, so it can flow with little pressure behind it.
[bio29-s5.2.3] Valves: pocket-shaped, or semilunar, valves are found especially in the veins of the limbs and let blood flow only towards the heart. When blood starts to flow backwards, it fills the pockets, which close the vein.
[bio29-s5.2.4] Skeletal muscle pump: contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins that run between them, pushing blood along, and the valves prevent backflow. This is why standing still for a long time makes blood pool in the legs.
[bio29-s5.2.5] Breathing movements lower the pressure in the thorax (the chest cavity) during inhalation, which draws blood towards the heart. Veins next to arteries are also squeezed by the arterial pulse.
[bio29-s5.2.6] Venules are small veins that collect blood from capillary networks and join to form veins.
[bio29-s5.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a vein showing an open valve, with blood flowing towards the heart, and a closed valve, with its pockets filled and blocking backflow. Add arrows, and label the wall, lumen and valve.
[bio29-s5.3.b] Capillaries are where the circulatory system does its real work, because this is where materials pass between the blood and the cells. Everything about their structure makes that exchange quick and gives it time to happen.
[bio29-s5.3.0] Role: the exchange of materials between the blood and the body cells. Capillaries form dense networks, called capillary beds, in every tissue, so that no cell is far from one.
[bio29-s5.3.1] The wall is only one cell thick, consisting of endothelium only, with no muscle or elastic layers, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio29-s5.3.2] The lumen is very narrow, only wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, which brings them close to the wall.
[bio29-s5.3.3] Capillaries are so numerous that their total cross-section is large, so blood flows slowly through them, giving time for exchange.
[bio29-s5.3.4] Leaky: there are small gaps between the endothelial cells, so plasma fluid and small dissolved molecules can pass out, and white blood cells can squeeze through. Red blood cells and large plasma proteins cannot.
[bio29-s5.3.5] Pressure falls as blood flows through the capillaries, so blood leaving them enters the venules at low pressure. That is why veins have no pulse.
[bio29-s5.4.b] Cells do not touch the blood directly. Instead, part of the plasma leaks out of the capillaries and bathes the cells, and exchange takes place through this fluid.
[bio29-s5.4.0] At the arterial end of a capillary bed (the end nearer the arteriole), the blood pressure is higher and forces plasma out through the capillary wall into the spaces between the cells. Plasma is water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts and oxygen.
[bio29-s5.4.1] This fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes every cell and forms the cells' internal environment. Red blood cells and plasma proteins stay in the capillary because they are too large to pass through the gaps.
[bio29-s5.4.2] Substances pass between tissue fluid and cells by diffusion and active transport. Oxygen and nutrients enter the cells; CO₂, urea and other wastes leave them.
[bio29-s5.4.3] At the venous end the pressure is lower, and the plasma proteins left in the blood draw water back in by osmosis. So most of the tissue fluid, now carrying CO₂ and wastes, returns to the capillary.
[bio29-s5.4.4] The small amount of excess fluid is drained away by lymph vessels and returned to the blood.
[bio29-s5.4.5] Diagram: a capillary bed between an arteriole and a venule, surrounded by body cells. Add arrows for fluid leaving at the arterial end and returning at the venous end, and for O₂ and nutrients entering the cells and CO₂ and wastes leaving them.
[bio29-s5.5.b] Each vessel's structure matches its job, so the clearest way to revise is to compare the three feature by feature. Learn the pairing of each feature with its reason, because exam questions ask you to relate structure to role.
[bio29-s5.5.0] Direction of flow: arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it towards the heart; capillaries link arterioles to venules within an organ.
[bio29-s5.5.1] Pressure: high and pulsing in arteries; falling through the capillaries; low and steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.2] Wall: thick, muscular and elastic in arteries to withstand pressure; thin, with little muscle and elastic tissue, in veins; one cell thick, endothelium only, in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.3] Lumen: narrow relative to the wall in arteries; wide in veins; just wide enough for one red blood cell in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.4] Valves: none in arteries, apart from at the heart; present in veins; none in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.5] Speed of flow: fast in arteries; slow in capillaries; slow but steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.6] Oxygen content: arteries usually carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood, except in the pulmonary circuit, and capillaries are where exchange happens. This is not how the vessels are defined; they are defined by direction of flow.
[bio29-s5.5.7] Exchange: no exchange with the tissues through the thick walls of arteries and veins; all exchange happens in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.8] Diagram: transverse sections of an artery (round, thick wall, narrow lumen), a vein (thin wall, same three layers, wide lumen, often flattened) and a capillary (ring of one or two endothelial cells round one red blood cell). Label each layer and the lumen.

CANDIDATES bio33-s3: Microorganisms in industry
[bio33-s3.0.b] Industry needs living things that work fast, cheaply and in a controlled space. Microorganisms (bacteria, yeasts and other fungi, and microscopic organisms such as protists) fit that need, for the reasons below.
[bio33-s3.0.0] Biotechnology is the use of living organisms or their parts, such as cells and enzymes, to make useful products or provide services.
[bio33-s3.0.1] Microorganisms are the most used organisms in biotechnology, for the six reasons that follow.
[bio33-s3.0.2] They reproduce very rapidly: some bacteria divide every 20 minutes in ideal conditions, so large amounts of product are made quickly.
[bio33-s3.0.3] They can be grown on cheap nutrients, including waste materials from other industries, such as whey from cheese-making and molasses (the dark syrup left over) from sugar refining.
[bio33-s3.0.4] They can be grown in any climate or season, in a controlled vessel that takes little space, so production does not depend on the weather or on farmland.
[bio33-s3.0.5] They carry out a huge range of chemical reactions at low temperatures and normal pressure, which saves energy compared with chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.0.6] They are easy to modify genetically, so they can be made to produce human proteins. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio33-s3.0.7] Many secrete their products into the medium (the liquid they grow in), which makes the products easier to collect.
[bio33-s3.1.b] What a company gets out of a microorganism falls into three groups. Being able to say which group an example belongs to makes it easy to give a named example in an answer.
[bio33-s3.1.0] The cells themselves are the product, for example yeast for baking and mycoprotein (protein from a fungus) for food.
[bio33-s3.1.1] Substances the cells make are the product, for example ethanol, antibiotics, enzymes and insulin.
[bio33-s3.1.2] Changes the cells make to a material are the product, for example milk turned to yogurt, slurry (animal waste) turned to biogas, and sewage treatment.
[bio33-s3.2.b] To get useful amounts of product, microorganisms are grown in a purpose-built vessel where every condition can be controlled. The first half of the design is the vessel itself and how food, oxygen and mixing reach the cells.
[bio33-s3.2.0] A bioreactor, or fermenter, is a vessel in which microorganisms, cells or enzymes are grown or used on a large scale under carefully controlled conditions to make a product. There is more on enzyme bioreactors in Industrial enzymes.
[bio33-s3.2.1] The vessel is made of stainless steel because it is strong, does not corrode, and is easy to sterilise with steam.
[bio33-s3.2.2] Inlets allow sterile nutrient medium and the inoculum, a pure starter culture of the microorganism, to enter the vessel.
[bio33-s3.2.3] A sterile air supply is bubbled in at the bottom through a sparger (a device that breaks the air into fine bubbles), giving oxygen to aerobic microorganisms. The air is filtered so that no contaminating microorganisms enter.
[bio33-s3.2.4] A motor-driven stirrer (impeller) keeps the cells, nutrients and oxygen evenly mixed, so no cell is starved, and it spreads heat evenly.
[bio33-s3.2.5] A water jacket, or cooling coils, with cold water flowing through it removes the heat released by the microorganisms' respiration, keeping the temperature at the optimum (the best temperature for growth).
[bio33-s3.3.b] The second half of the design keeps conditions steady while the culture grows and lets the product out. A computer watches the sensors, so the conditions stay at the optimum without constant manual checking.
[bio33-s3.3.0] Probes monitor temperature, pH, dissolved oxygen and foam level, and are linked to a computer.
[bio33-s3.3.1] Inlets for acid or alkali correct the pH when the probes show it drifting from the optimum.
[bio33-s3.3.2] A gas outlet releases CO₂ (made by the cells' respiration) and excess air.
[bio33-s3.3.3] An outlet at the base, the harvest tap, removes the culture and product.
[bio33-s3.3.4] Diagram: a labelled bioreactor showing the vessel, nutrient and inoculum inlets, sterile air inlet and sparger, stirrer and motor, water jacket with cold water in and warm water out, temperature, pH and oxygen probes, acid/alkali inlet, gas outlet and harvest outlet.
[bio33-s3.4.b] A bioreactor must contain only the chosen microorganism, and once the product is made it still has to be separated and purified before it can be used.
[bio33-s3.4.0] Before use, the vessel, pipes and medium are sterilised with steam, so that no contaminating microorganisms compete with the culture, use up nutrients or spoil the product.
[bio33-s3.4.1] This is aseptic technique (working so that unwanted microorganisms are kept out) on an industrial scale.
[bio33-s3.4.2] Downstream processing is everything done after the product is made: it is separated from the cells and medium by filtering or centrifuging (spinning at high speed), then purified, concentrated and packaged.
[bio33-s3.4.3] If the product is inside the cells, the cells are first broken open so that it can be released.
[bio33-s3.5.b] The pharmaceutical industry makes medicines, and microorganisms are the source of many of them. Antibiotics are the first example to learn.
[bio33-s3.5.0] Antibiotics are chemicals made by microorganisms that kill or inhibit bacteria. They do not work on viruses.
[bio33-s3.5.1] Penicillin is made by the fungus Penicillium, not a bacterium. Alexander Fleming discovered its effect in 1928, and Howard Florey and Ernst Chain purified it and showed it cured infections from 1940.
[bio33-s3.5.2] Penicillium is now grown in bioreactors, using strains selected and mutated to give far more penicillin than the original, which makes large-scale production possible.
[bio33-s3.5.3] Streptomycin, tetracycline and many other antibiotics are made by soil bacteria of the genus Streptomyces.
[bio33-s3.6.b] Genetic engineering lets bacteria and yeast make human medicines that once had to be taken from other sources. Vaccines also depend on microorganisms grown in culture.
[bio33-s3.6.0] The gene for human insulin is inserted into bacteria (E. coli) or yeast, which are grown in bioreactors. The insulin is then extracted and purified.
[bio33-s3.6.1] Human insulin was the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use, in 1982.
[bio33-s3.6.2] Before that, people with diabetes injected insulin extracted from the pancreases of pigs and cattle, which was in shorter supply and caused allergic reactions in some people.
[bio33-s3.6.3] Human growth hormone, for children who make too little, is made by genetically modified bacteria.
[bio33-s3.6.4] The hepatitis B vaccine is made by genetically modified yeast, which produces a hepatitis B virus surface protein (the antigen, the part the immune system learns to recognise) without the virus.
[bio33-s3.6.5] Other vaccines contain killed or weakened microorganisms grown in culture. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s3.7.b] In hospitals and clinics, microorganisms and the enzymes and toxins they make are used to diagnose disease and to treat it.
[bio33-s3.7.0] Diagnosis: the enzyme glucose oxidase, made by the fungus Aspergillus niger, is used in the test strips with which people with diabetes measure their blood glucose.
[bio33-s3.7.1] Diagnosis: Taq polymerase, a heat-stable enzyme from the bacterium Thermus aquaticus, which lives in hot springs, is used in PCR (a method that copies DNA) because it is not destroyed by the high temperatures PCR needs.
[bio33-s3.7.2] PCR detects pathogens, for example in COVID-19 tests, and is used in DNA profiling.
[bio33-s3.7.3] Treatment: faecal microbiota transplant, in which gut microorganisms from a healthy donor are placed in a patient's gut, is used to treat repeated infections with the bacterium Clostridioides difficile that antibiotics have failed to clear.
[bio33-s3.7.4] Treatment: probiotics, live microorganisms taken as food or supplements, are used to try to restore gut microorganisms after antibiotics; the evidence is mixed.
[bio33-s3.7.5] Treatment: the toxin of the bacterium Clostridium botulinum, in tiny purified doses (botulinum toxin, Botox), relaxes overactive muscles and is used to treat some muscle spasms and other conditions.
[bio33-s3.7.6] Medicines and vaccines are made in the pharmaceutical industry, so the medical uses here are those in diagnosis and treatment in hospitals and clinics. The two headings overlap.
[bio33-s3.8.b] Soil microorganisms can feed crops, protect them from insects and even carry new genes into them. Each use cuts the need for chemicals or improves the crop.
[bio33-s3.8.0] Nitrogen fixation (turning nitrogen gas from the air into a form plants can use): seeds of legumes such as clover, peas and beans can be coated with nitrogen-fixing bacteria (Rhizobium) before sowing.
[bio33-s3.8.1] The crop then forms root nodules and needs less nitrogen fertiliser. This is mutualism, a partnership in which both organisms benefit.
[bio33-s3.8.2] Biological pest control: the soil bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) makes a protein that is toxic to the larvae of certain insects, for example caterpillars, but harmless to humans and most other animals.
[bio33-s3.8.3] Bt is sprayed on crops as a biopesticide (a pesticide made from a living organism).
[bio33-s3.8.4] The Bt toxin gene has been put into some genetically modified crops, for example Bt maize, which then make their own insecticide.
[bio33-s3.8.5] Genetic modification of plants: the soil bacterium Agrobacterium tumefaciens naturally transfers part of a plasmid (a small ring of DNA) into plant cells.
[bio33-s3.8.6] Biotechnologists use Agrobacterium as a vector (a carrier) to insert useful genes into crop plants.
[bio33-s3.9.b] Farms produce large amounts of plant and animal waste. Microorganisms turn these into winter feed, energy and soil improver.
[bio33-s3.9.0] Silage: grass stored airtight, in a pit covered with plastic or in wrapped bales, is fermented by lactic acid bacteria, which convert its sugars to lactic acid.
[bio33-s3.9.1] The low pH stops other microorganisms rotting the silage, so it keeps as winter feed for cattle and sheep. Silage-making is widespread on Irish farms.
[bio33-s3.9.2] Anaerobic digestion: animal slurry, food waste and crop waste are broken down by microorganisms in sealed tanks without oxygen.
[bio33-s3.9.3] Methane-producing microorganisms (archaea, a group of single-celled organisms) produce biogas, which is mainly methane and is burned for heat or electricity. The remaining digestate is spread as fertiliser.
[bio33-s3.9.4] Composting: aerobic decomposition (breakdown using oxygen) of plant waste by bacteria and fungi produces compost, which returns humus and minerals to soil.
[bio33-s3.10.b] Yeast is a fungus that ferments sugar, which means it breaks it down without oxygen to make ethanol and CO₂. Bakers and brewers use the two products for different purposes.
[bio33-s3.10.0] Bread: baker's yeast (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) ferments sugars in dough, producing CO₂, which makes the dough rise.
[bio33-s3.10.1] The ethanol made in the dough evaporates during baking.
[bio33-s3.10.2] Beer and wine: yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (beer) or grape juice (wine) to ethanol and CO₂, under anaerobic conditions (without oxygen).
[bio33-s3.11.b] Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar in milk to lactic acid. The acid changes the milk's texture and taste, and it also helps to keep the food from spoiling.
[bio33-s3.11.0] Yogurt: milk is pasteurised (heated to kill unwanted microorganisms), cooled and inoculated with lactic acid bacteria, for example Lactobacillus and Streptococcus species.
[bio33-s3.11.1] The inoculated milk is incubated warm, at about 40–45 °C, for several hours, which is the temperature range that suits these bacteria.
[bio33-s3.11.2] The bacteria ferment lactose (the sugar in milk) to lactic acid, which lowers the pH, making the milk proteins thicken and giving the sour taste.
[bio33-s3.11.3] The low pH also preserves the yogurt, because few spoilage microorganisms grow in acid.
[bio33-s3.11.4] Cheese: lactic acid bacteria sour the milk, and rennet clots it into curds (the solid part of milk). Rennet, or chymosin, is now mostly made by genetically modified microorganisms.
[bio33-s3.11.5] Bacteria and moulds ripen the cheese and develop its flavour; the blue veins of blue cheeses are the mould Penicillium roqueforti.
[bio33-s3.12.b] Microorganisms are grown as food themselves, and they also make flavourings and preservatives.
[bio33-s3.12.0] Mycoprotein: the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown in large continuous fermenters (vessels fed continuously) on glucose and mineral salts.
[bio33-s3.12.1] Its hyphae (the thread-like cells of a fungus) are harvested, heated and pressed to make mycoprotein, sold as Quorn, a high-protein, high-fibre, low-fat meat substitute.
[bio33-s3.12.2] Vinegar is made when bacteria (Acetobacter) oxidise the ethanol in wine, cider or beer to acetic (ethanoic) acid.
[bio33-s3.12.3] Soy sauce is made by fermenting soya beans and wheat with the mould Aspergillus oryzae.
[bio33-s3.12.4] Citric acid, a flavouring and preservative in soft drinks, is made by the mould Aspergillus niger.
[bio33-s3.13.b] Microorganisms are also used outside medicine and food, wherever waste has to be broken down or a fuel made.
[bio33-s3.13.0] Sewage treatment: in the secondary stage, aerobic bacteria and protists in aerated tanks (activated sludge) feed on the organic matter in sewage, so the water can be returned to rivers without causing oxygen depletion. The sludge can be anaerobically digested to biogas.
[bio33-s3.13.1] Bioremediation (using living organisms to clean up pollution): microorganisms that break down pollutants, for example bacteria that digest oil, are used to clean up oil spills and contaminated land.
[bio33-s3.13.2] Biofuels: yeast ferments sugars from crops, for example sugar cane or maize, to ethanol, which is blended with petrol. There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio33-s3.14.b] When you are asked to discuss importance, give benefits and limits side by side. In health, microorganisms have transformed treatment, but they have also created a serious problem.
[bio33-s3.14.0] For: antibiotics have made most bacterial infections curable and have made surgery much safer.
[bio33-s3.14.1] For: insulin, vaccines and other medicines from microorganisms are cheap, pure and available in large amounts.
[bio33-s3.14.2] For: human insulin from microorganisms avoids reliance on animal pancreases and the reactions some people had to animal insulin.
[bio33-s3.14.3] Against: overuse of antibiotics in medicine and in farm animals has selected for resistant bacteria, so some antibiotics no longer work. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s3.14.4] Against: new antibiotics are expensive and slow to develop, so resistant bacteria cannot easily be replaced with new treatments.
[bio33-s3.15.b] Microorganisms help farmers and food producers, but the genetic technology involved raises concerns for some people.
[bio33-s3.15.0] For: fermentation preserves food and creates many foods.
[bio33-s3.15.1] For: mycoprotein and other microbial protein need far less land and water than livestock.
[bio33-s3.15.2] For: biopesticides and Rhizobium inoculants reduce the use of chemical pesticides and fertilisers, and silage gives winter feed.
[bio33-s3.15.3] Against: genetically modified microorganisms and crops raise concerns about safety, the environment and who controls the technology. There is more on this in Ethics of genetic technologies.
[bio33-s3.15.4] Against: insects can evolve resistance to the Bt toxin, and some people object to GM (genetically modified) ingredients.
[bio33-s3.16.b] Microorganisms can reduce pollution and energy use, but running them at industrial scale has costs of its own.
[bio33-s3.16.0] For: microorganisms turn wastes, such as whey, slurry, sewage and food waste, into useful products, reducing pollution.
[bio33-s3.16.1] For: biogas and bioethanol are renewable energy, and biological processes run at low temperatures, using less energy than chemical manufacture.
[bio33-s3.16.2] For: biotechnology and pharmaceutical manufacturing are major employers and exporters in Ireland.
[bio33-s3.16.3] Against: bioreactors need sterile conditions, energy for stirring and cooling, and careful disposal of waste culture.
[bio33-s3.16.4] Against: contamination of a batch can waste the whole product, and growing crops for biofuel uses land that could grow food.
[bio33-s3.17.b] A good discussion ends with a judgement. It weighs the benefits against the limits and gives a conclusion that depends on a stated condition.
[bio33-s3.17.0] On balance, microorganisms are essential to modern medicine, food production and waste treatment, and their importance is growing as biotechnology replaces fossil-fuel-based chemical processes. There is more on this in The cross-cutting themes, on health, sustainability and technology.
[bio33-s3.17.1] This judgement holds provided that antibiotic use and genetic modification are regulated, because the main limits are antibiotic resistance and the concerns about GM organisms.
[bio33-s3.18.b] When microorganisms are put into fresh medium, their numbers do not rise steadily. A growth curve records what actually happens over time, and the shape of the curve is the key to getting the best yield of a product.
[bio33-s3.18.0] A growth curve shows how the number of living cells in a population of microorganisms changes over time when they are grown in a closed system: a fixed volume of medium, with no nutrients added and no wastes removed (batch culture).
[bio33-s3.18.1] The number of cells is usually plotted as a logarithm, log₁₀ of the number of cells, because numbers rise from thousands to billions; on a log scale the exponential phase (the steep rise) is a straight line.
[bio33-s3.18.2] Diagram: a growth curve with log number of living cells on the y-axis and time on the x-axis, with the five phases labelled in order: lag, log (exponential), stationary, decline (death) and survival.
[bio33-s3.19.b] The curve begins with a pause and then a rapid rise. These two phases show what the cells need to get started and how fast they can grow when conditions are ideal.
[bio33-s3.19.0] Lag phase: little or no increase in cell number.
[bio33-s3.19.1] During the lag phase the cells adjust to the new medium: they take in water and nutrients, switch on genes, make the enzymes needed to use the nutrients present, and grow in size before dividing.
[bio33-s3.19.2] The lag phase is longer if the new conditions differ greatly from those the cells came from.
[bio33-s3.19.3] Log (exponential) phase: the cells divide at their maximum rate, as nutrients are plentiful and wastes are low.
[bio33-s3.19.4] The number doubles in each generation time (the time for a population to double), for example every 20–30 minutes, so the population grows exponentially.
[bio33-s3.19.5] This is when the cells grow fastest and make most of the products linked to growth, the primary metabolites. Primary metabolites include ethanol made by yeast and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.20.b] Growth cannot go on for ever in a closed system. As nutrients run out and waste builds up, the curve levels off, falls, and then flattens at a low level.
[bio33-s3.20.0] Stationary phase: the rate of cell division equals the rate of cell death, so the number of living cells stays constant. Cells are still dividing and dying; the population has not simply stopped.
[bio33-s3.20.1] The stationary phase is caused by nutrients running out, toxic waste products such as acids and ethanol building up, oxygen possibly running short, and changes in pH.
[bio33-s3.20.2] Many microorganisms make secondary metabolites, such as antibiotics, in this phase; some bacteria form endospores (tough resting cells that resist harsh conditions).
[bio33-s3.20.3] Decline (death) phase: the death rate is greater than the rate of division, so the number of living cells falls, because nutrients are exhausted and wastes are at toxic levels.
[bio33-s3.20.4] Dead cells may break open in the decline phase.
[bio33-s3.20.5] Survival phase: a small number of cells survive for a long time, with the number falling only slowly or levelling off.
[bio33-s3.20.6] Cells survive the survival phase by forming endospores (in some bacteria), by becoming inactive, or by using nutrients released from dead cells that have broken open.
[bio33-s3.21.b] Because the log phase is exponential, the number of cells can be worked out from the number of generations. The same idea works backwards to find the generation time.
[bio33-s3.21.0] Number of cells after n generations of the log phase = starting number × 2ⁿ.
[bio33-s3.21.1] Worked example: one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, for 2 hours: n = 120 ÷ 20 = 6 generations; number = 1 × 2⁶ = 64 cells.
[bio33-s3.21.2] Worked example: with 1000 starting cells and a generation time of 30 minutes, after 3 hours: n = 6, number = 1000 × 64 = 64 000.
[bio33-s3.21.3] Working out generation time from a graph or table: in the log phase the number rose from 1 × 10⁴ to 8 × 10⁴ cells in 90 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.4] 8 = 2³, so there were 3 generations in 90 minutes; generation time = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 minutes.
[bio33-s3.21.5] Reading a log-scale graph: each step of 1 on a log₁₀ axis is a tenfold increase, for example from log 4 = 10 000 cells to log 5 = 100 000 cells.
[bio33-s3.22.b] Industry can run a bioreactor in two ways. In batch culture the culture goes through every phase of the growth curve and is then harvested all at once.
[bio33-s3.22.0] Batch culture: the bioreactor is filled with a fixed amount of sterile medium, inoculated, and left to grow for a set time, with temperature, pH and oxygen controlled.
[bio33-s3.22.1] In batch culture nothing is added except air, acid or alkali, and nothing is removed until the end, when the whole contents are harvested.
[bio33-s3.22.2] The vessel is then emptied, cleaned and sterilised for the next batch, and the population passes through the lag, log and stationary phases.
[bio33-s3.22.3] Advantages: it is simpler to set up and control, and if a batch is contaminated only that batch is lost; it is best for products made in the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.22.4] Disadvantages: the vessel is idle while being emptied, cleaned and refilled, and for much of each run the cells are not in the log phase, so output per vessel is lower.
[bio33-s3.23.b] Metabolites are the substances cells make. Which phase of the growth curve gives the most product depends on the kind of metabolite wanted.
[bio33-s3.23.0] Primary metabolites are substances the cells make as part of normal growth, mostly in the log phase, for example ethanol by yeast, amino acids, enzymes, and the cells themselves as mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.23.1] Secondary metabolites are not needed for growth and are made mainly in the stationary phase, for example the antibiotic penicillin.
[bio33-s3.24.b] Continuous culture avoids the stop-and-start of batch culture by feeding the vessel and draining it at the same time. This holds the cells at their fastest-growing stage.
[bio33-s3.24.0] Continuous culture: fresh sterile nutrient medium is added continuously, and culture (cells, medium and product) is removed at the same rate, so the volume stays constant.
[bio33-s3.24.1] Nutrients never run out and wastes do not build up, so the population is held in the log phase for weeks. In continuous culture the population does not reach the stationary phase.
[bio33-s3.24.2] Advantages: high output from a smaller vessel, no idle time between batches, and a steady, uniform product.
[bio33-s3.24.3] Disadvantages: it is harder to control; if contamination enters, a long run is lost; mutations can arise over many generations; and it suits only products made during growth, for example mycoprotein.
[bio33-s3.25.b] Knowing the phases of the growth curve lets a producer take steps to get more product from each run. Each step matches one phase of the curve.
[bio33-s3.25.0] Shorten the lag phase by inoculating with a large volume of actively growing cells taken from the log phase of a culture grown in the same medium and at the same temperature, so the cells do not need to adjust.
[bio33-s3.25.1] Keep the log phase going as long as possible by holding temperature, pH and oxygen at the optimum with the probes, and by supplying fresh nutrients and removing wastes (continuous culture, for primary metabolites).
[bio33-s3.25.2] Harvest at the right phase: primary metabolites at the end of the log phase or continuously, and secondary metabolites, for example penicillin, in batch culture during the stationary phase, before the decline phase reduces yield.
[bio33-s3.25.3] Delay the stationary and decline phases by removing toxic products, for example ethanol, or by adding nutrients gradually (fed-batch culture, a batch to which nutrients are added during the run).

CANDIDATES bio6-s1: Viruses
[bio6-s1.0.b] A virus is far simpler than any cell. It is a tiny, non-cellular particle (not made of cells) built of nucleic acid inside a protein coat, and it cannot do anything much until it gets inside a living cell.
[bio6-s1.0.0] A virus can reproduce only inside a living host cell (the cell it infects), so it is an obligate intracellular parasite: it must live inside cells and cannot survive and multiply on its own.
[bio6-s1.0.1] Viruses have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), no cell membrane of their own and almost no enzymes.
[bio6-s1.0.2] Because of this, a virus must use the host cell's ribosomes, enzymes, ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and raw materials to make copies of itself. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio6-s1.1.b] Every virus has the same basic plan: a core of genetic information wrapped in protein, and in some viruses an extra outer layer. Each part has its own job.
[bio6-s1.1.0] Nucleic acid core: either DNA or RNA, never both. It carries the genes (instructions) for making new virus particles, and it may be single- or double-stranded.
[bio6-s1.1.1] Protein coat (capsid): made of many repeating protein subunits (capsomeres). It protects the nucleic acid inside it.
[bio6-s1.1.2] The proteins of the coat attach to specific receptor molecules on the surface of the host cell, a bit like a key fitting a lock. This is why each virus infects only particular species and particular cell types (host specificity).
[bio6-s1.1.3] Envelope (some viruses only, e.g. influenza virus and HIV): an outer lipid membrane (a layer of fats) taken from the host cell's membrane as the virus leaves the cell.
[bio6-s1.1.4] The envelope is studded with viral proteins called spikes, which help the virus attach to and enter new cells.
[bio6-s1.2.b] Viruses are extremely small and come in a few characteristic shapes, which is useful for recognising and naming them.
[bio6-s1.2.0] Size: most viruses are about 20–300 nm across, much smaller than a bacterium (about 1–10 µm). This is why they pass through filters that trap bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.1] Because they are so small, viruses can be seen only with an electron microscope, not with a light microscope.
[bio6-s1.2.2] Rod-shaped or helical viruses, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio6-s1.2.3] Many-sided or polyhedral viruses, e.g. adenovirus.
[bio6-s1.2.4] Spherical and enveloped viruses, e.g. influenza virus.
[bio6-s1.2.5] Complex viruses with a head and tail: bacteriophages, which are the viruses that infect bacteria.
[bio6-s1.2.6] Diagram: draw a bacteriophage and label head (protein coat), nucleic acid (DNA) inside the head, tail, tail fibres. Then draw an enveloped virus and label envelope, spike proteins, protein coat, RNA.
[bio6-s1.3.b] Viruses are hard to classify because they show some features of living things and lack others. First, the features that make them look alive.
[bio6-s1.3.0] They contain genetic material (DNA or RNA) that uses the same genetic code as cells, so their genes are read in the same way.
[bio6-s1.3.1] They reproduce, making many copies of themselves, but only inside a host cell.
[bio6-s1.3.2] They show heredity, passing genes on to new viruses.
[bio6-s1.3.3] They mutate and evolve by natural selection. For example, new strains of influenza appear every year, which is why the flu vaccine is updated.
[bio6-s1.3.4] Their structure is highly organised.
[bio6-s1.4.b] Against that evidence, viruses lack many of the features that all cells have. Weighing both sides gives the conclusion examiners expect.
[bio6-s1.4.0] They are not made of cells (they are acellular), so they do not fit the cell theory, which says living things are made of cells.
[bio6-s1.4.1] They have no metabolism (chemical reactions) of their own: no respiration, no nutrition and no excretion.
[bio6-s1.4.2] They do not grow and do not respond to stimuli.
[bio6-s1.4.3] They cannot reproduce outside a host cell, and outside a host they are inert particles, meaning they do nothing.
[bio6-s1.4.4] Some viruses can be crystallised like a chemical and remain infectious afterwards. Wendell Stanley crystallised tobacco mosaic virus in 1935.
[bio6-s1.4.5] Conclusion: viruses are usually described as on the border between living and non-living. Most biologists do not count them as living organisms, so they are not placed in any of the three domains or six kingdoms. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio6-s1.4.6] Others argue that a virus is alive only while it is inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery.
[bio6-s1.5.b] Viruses cause some of the most common and most serious human diseases. Their biology also explains why some usual treatments fail.
[bio6-s1.5.0] Viruses cause many human diseases: common cold (rhinoviruses), influenza (flu), measles, mumps, chickenpox, COVID-19 (caused by SARS-CoV-2), AIDS and hepatitis B.
[bio6-s1.5.1] AIDS is caused by HIV, which destroys helper T cells of the immune system (the white blood cells that coordinate the body's defence).
[bio6-s1.5.2] Some viruses cause cancer. Human papillomavirus (HPV) causes most cervical cancers, and a vaccine against HPV protects against them. There is more on this in Cell Division and Cancer.
[bio6-s1.5.3] Antibiotics do not work against viruses, because antibiotics target structures and processes that viruses do not have, such as bacterial cell walls and ribosomes.
[bio6-s1.5.4] Viral infections are treated with antiviral drugs, which are fewer and more specific than antibiotics, and are prevented by vaccination and hygiene.
[bio6-s1.5.5] Misusing antibiotics for viral infections contributes to antibiotic resistance in bacteria, since the bacteria are exposed to the drug for no benefit.
[bio6-s1.6.b] Although viruses cause disease, scientists have turned their features to medical use. This is one source of the beneficial uses that exam questions ask for.
[bio6-s1.6.0] Vaccines are made from weakened or inactivated viruses or from viral proteins, which train the immune system without causing the disease.
[bio6-s1.6.1] Harmless modified viruses are used as vectors (carriers) to deliver genes into human cells, in gene therapy and in some vaccines.
[bio6-s1.6.2] For example, some COVID-19 vaccines used a harmless adenovirus to carry the gene for a coronavirus spike protein.
[bio6-s1.6.3] Bacteriophages can be used to kill bacteria that are resistant to antibiotics. This is called phage therapy.
[bio6-s1.7.b] Viruses affect money as well as health. They damage farming and cost society a great deal, yet they are also used as tools and in industry.
[bio6-s1.7.0] Plant viruses reduce crop yields, e.g. tobacco mosaic virus (tobacco, tomatoes), potato viruses, and barley yellow dwarf virus in cereals, which is spread by aphids.
[bio6-s1.7.1] Animal viruses such as foot-and-mouth disease (cattle, sheep and pigs) and avian influenza (bird flu, poultry) lead to culling of infected herds and flocks, bans on exports and movement restrictions.
[bio6-s1.7.2] Costs to society: health-care costs, lost working days and, in a pandemic, the closure of businesses, schools and travel, e.g. COVID-19 from 2020.
[bio6-s1.7.3] Economic benefits: viruses are used as tools in genetic engineering, as vectors that carry genes into cells.
[bio6-s1.7.4] Viruses are used in biological control of pests (using a living organism to reduce a pest). Myxoma virus was released in Australia in 1950 to control rabbits.
[bio6-s1.7.5] Vaccine production is a major pharmaceutical industry.

CANDIDATES bio8-s5: Vitamins
[bio8-s5.0.b] Your body needs a handful of chemicals in tiny amounts that it cannot build for itself. These are the vitamins, and without them key processes stop working properly.
[bio8-s5.0.0] Vitamins are organic compounds (carbon-based molecules made by living things) needed in very small amounts for normal metabolism, growth and health. The body cannot make them, or cannot make enough, so they must come from the diet.
[bio8-s5.0.1] Vitamins supply no energy and are not used to build tissues in bulk. This is what separates them from carbohydrates, lipids and proteins, which are needed in large amounts.
[bio8-s5.0.2] Many vitamins act as coenzymes (helper molecules that enzymes need) or help enzymes work. For example, the B vitamins help release energy from food in respiration.
[bio8-s5.0.3] Some vitamins are needed to make particular substances, such as vitamin C for collagen (a structural protein). Others act as antioxidants that protect cells from damage (vitamins C and E).
[bio8-s5.0.4] Vitamin D controls the absorption of minerals: it controls how much calcium is taken in from food.
[bio8-s5.0.5] A lack of a vitamin causes a deficiency disease with characteristic symptoms. The disease is cured or prevented by supplying the vitamin.
[bio8-s5.0.6] Vitamins are grouped by what they dissolve in. Water-soluble: vitamin C and the B group. Fat-soluble: vitamins A, D, E and K.
[bio8-s5.1.b] Whether a vitamin dissolves in water or in fat decides how it is absorbed, carried, stored and lost, and so how often you must eat it and whether it can build up to harmful levels. Compare the two groups point by point.
[bio8-s5.1.0] Absorption: water-soluble vitamins dissolve in the watery contents of the gut and are absorbed directly into the blood. Fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in dietary fat and are absorbed along with it.
[bio8-s5.1.1] So a very low-fat diet, or a condition that prevents fat absorption, can cause a deficiency of vitamin A, D, E or K, because these vitamins cannot be absorbed without fat.
[bio8-s5.1.2] Transport: water-soluble vitamins travel dissolved in the blood plasma (the watery liquid part of blood). Fat-soluble vitamins are carried with lipids.
[bio8-s5.1.3] Storage of water-soluble vitamins: any excess is excreted by the kidneys in urine and most are not stored. They must therefore be eaten regularly, ideally daily, and a deficiency develops within weeks or months.
[bio8-s5.1.4] Storage of fat-soluble vitamins: they are stored in the liver and fatty tissue, so the body can draw on these reserves and they need not be eaten every day.
[bio8-s5.1.5] Toxicity (harmfulness): because excess water-soluble vitamins are excreted, overdose is rare. Fat-soluble vitamins can build up in the liver to harmful levels if large supplements are taken.
[bio8-s5.1.6] Too much vitamin A is toxic, which is why pregnant women are advised not to eat liver or take vitamin A supplements.
[bio8-s5.1.7] Cooking: water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) out of food into the cooking water, and vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and by long storage. Vegetables lose vitamin C when boiled for a long time; steaming, microwaving or eating them raw keeps more. Fat-soluble vitamins are not lost into cooking water.
[bio8-s5.2.b] Vitamin C is the standard example of a water-soluble vitamin. Its main job is to help build collagen, so a shortage weakens the body's connective tissue.
[bio8-s5.2.0] Vitamin C, also called ascorbic acid, is water-soluble.
[bio8-s5.2.1] Roles: it is needed to make collagen, the protein of connective tissue in skin, gums, blood vessel walls, bones and scar tissue. It also helps wounds heal, acts as an antioxidant, helps the gut absorb iron from plant foods, and supports the immune system.
[bio8-s5.2.2] Sources: citrus fruits (oranges, lemons, limes, grapefruit), kiwi fruit, strawberries, blackcurrants, peppers, broccoli, tomatoes and potatoes. It is best eaten fresh and raw, because it leaches into cooking water and is destroyed by heat and long storage.
[bio8-s5.2.3] Deficiency disease: scurvy. Without vitamin C, collagen is not made properly, so connective tissue weakens. This is why the symptoms involve gums, blood vessels and wounds.
[bio8-s5.2.4] Scurvy symptoms: swollen, bleeding gums and loose teeth; easy bruising and bleeding under the skin; wounds that heal slowly or reopen; tiredness and aching joints.
[bio8-s5.2.5] Untreated, scurvy can be fatal. It is cured quickly by vitamin C.
[bio8-s5.2.6] History (Health and Nature of Science link): scurvy killed many sailors on long voyages without fresh food. In 1747 James Lind, a Scottish naval surgeon, gave different treatments to groups of sailors with scurvy and found that those given oranges and lemons recovered. This was an early controlled trial.
[bio8-s5.3.b] Vitamin D is the standard example of a fat-soluble vitamin. Its main job is to help the gut take in calcium, which bones and teeth need to harden.
[bio8-s5.3.0] Vitamin D, also called calciferol, is fat-soluble.
[bio8-s5.3.1] Role: it increases the absorption of calcium, and phosphate, from food in the small intestine. It is therefore needed for the formation and hardening of bones and teeth. It also helps muscle function and the immune system.
[bio8-s5.3.2] Sources: the skin makes vitamin D when it is exposed to ultraviolet light in sunlight. Dietary sources are oily fish (e.g. salmon, mackerel and sardines), fish-liver oils such as cod-liver oil, egg yolk and liver, and fortified foods such as some milks, spreads and breakfast cereals.
[bio8-s5.3.3] Deficiency disease: rickets in children. Bones do not harden, so they stay soft and weak. The leg bones bend under the body's weight, giving bowed legs; growth is poor and the bones are painful.
[bio8-s5.3.4] In adults the equivalent condition is osteomalacia, which means softening of the bones. Low vitamin D also raises the risk of osteoporosis (thinning, brittle bones) in later life.
[bio8-s5.3.5] People at risk are those who get little sunlight: people living at northern latitudes like Ireland, where winter sunlight is too weak for the skin to make vitamin D, and people who stay indoors or cover their skin.
[bio8-s5.3.6] Other people at risk are people with darker skin living at high latitudes, older people, and babies. Irish health advice recommends a vitamin D supplement for babies in their first year.
[bio8-s5.4.b] You may choose other vitamins to answer questions on the role, source and deficiency of a vitamin. These three are fat-soluble, so they are stored in the body and absorbed with dietary fat.
[bio8-s5.4.0] Vitamin A (retinol) is fat-soluble and is needed for vision in dim light and for healthy skin and linings. Deficiency causes night blindness.
[bio8-s5.4.1] Vitamin A sources: liver, dairy produce, eggs and oily fish. Carrots and other orange and dark green vegetables contain beta-carotene, which the body converts to vitamin A.
[bio8-s5.4.2] Vitamin K is fat-soluble and is needed for blood clotting. Sources: green leafy vegetables. It is also made by bacteria in the large intestine.
[bio8-s5.4.3] Newborn babies have little vitamin K, so they are given it soon after birth to prevent bleeding.
[bio8-s5.4.4] Vitamin E is fat-soluble and is an antioxidant that protects cell membranes from damage. Sources: vegetable oils, nuts and seeds. No named deficiency disease is needed.
[bio8-s5.5.b] The B-group vitamins are water-soluble, so they are not stored well and must be eaten often. Several have a named deficiency disease and two are needed for healthy red blood cells.
[bio8-s5.5.0] The B-group vitamins are water-soluble and help enzymes release energy from food. Sources: wholegrain cereals, meat, eggs, dairy produce and beans.
[bio8-s5.5.1] Vitamin B1 (thiamine) deficiency causes beriberi; vitamin B3 (niacin) deficiency causes pellagra.
[bio8-s5.5.2] Folic acid (folate, a B vitamin) and vitamin B12 are needed to make red blood cells. A lack of either causes a form of anaemia (too few healthy red blood cells).
[bio8-s5.5.3] Vitamin B12 is found naturally only in animal foods, so vegans need fortified foods or supplements.
[bio8-s5.5.4] Women are advised to take folic acid before and in early pregnancy, as it reduces the risk of neural tube defects (faults in the development of the baby's spine and brain) such as spina bifida in the baby.
[bio8-s5.6.b] Some groups of people struggle to get enough of a vitamin from their normal diet. Two measures are used to protect them.
[bio8-s5.6.0] Food fortification, adding vitamins to foods such as breakfast cereals and milk, and taking supplements are used to prevent deficiency diseases in groups at risk. An example is the vitamin D supplement advised for babies in their first year.
