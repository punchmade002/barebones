CANDIDATES bio15-s2: Investigation: conditions for fermentation
[bio15-s2.0.b] Fermentation is a way of releasing energy from sugar without oxygen. This investigation asks what a fermenting microorganism needs before it will do it: sugar, living cells, no oxygen and a suitable temperature.
[bio15-s2.0.0] Fermentation is anaerobic respiration (respiration without oxygen) by microorganisms. Yeast, a single-celled fungus, ferments sugar to ethanol and carbon dioxide; lactic acid bacteria ferment sugar to lactic acid.
[bio15-s2.0.1] The two products of yeast fermentation are the evidence looked for in this investigation: CO₂, shown by bubbles and by limewater turning milky, and ethanol, shown by the iodoform test.
[bio15-s2.0.2] Condition 1, sugar: it is needed because it is the respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down), so it is the source of energy for fermentation.
[bio15-s2.0.3] Condition 2, living microorganisms: they are needed because their cells contain the enzymes (biological catalysts) that carry out fermentation. Dead cells have no working enzymes.
[bio15-s2.0.4] Condition 3, an anaerobic environment (no oxygen): yeast with oxygen respires mainly aerobically, producing CO₂ and water instead of ethanol, so oxygen must be kept out to make the cells ferment.
[bio15-s2.0.5] Condition 4, a suitable temperature: it lets the enzymes work at a good rate, because enzymes are slow when cold and denatured (permanently changed in shape, so no longer working) when too hot.
[bio15-s2.0.6] Yeast is a facultative anaerobe, meaning it can respire with or without oxygen, so the conditions decide which kind of respiration it carries out. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio15-s2.1.b] A fair test changes one thing at a time. Here you build one flask that has all four conditions and then a set of flasks that each differ from it in a single condition, so any difference in the result can be blamed on that condition.
[bio15-s2.1.0] Aim: to investigate the conditions necessary for fermentation by yeast: the presence of sugar, the presence of living yeast, the absence of oxygen and a suitable temperature.
[bio15-s2.1.1] Hypothesis (example): yeast will ferment glucose, producing carbon dioxide and ethanol, only when sugar and living yeast are present, oxygen is absent and the temperature is warm. Fermentation will be fastest at about 30–40 °C and will stop at high temperature, e.g. 60 °C.
[bio15-s2.1.2] Design: set up one full fermentation flask that meets all four conditions, then a series of flasks each identical except that one condition is missing or changed.
[bio15-s2.1.3] Independent variable (the one you change): the condition changed, which is sugar (present or absent), microorganism (living, dead or none), oxygen (present or absent) or temperature.
[bio15-s2.1.4] Dependent variable (the one you measure): the amount or rate of fermentation, measured as the rate of CO₂ production (bubbles per minute, or cm³ of gas in a set time), whether limewater turns milky, and whether ethanol is present at the end.
[bio15-s2.1.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the volume and concentration of glucose solution, the mass of yeast, the volume of the flask, the time allowed, and the temperature, except in the temperature series.
[bio15-s2.1.6] Control: a flask with glucose solution and yeast that has been boiled for a few minutes and cooled, set up identically. It shows that the CO₂ and ethanol come from living yeast, not from the glucose solution itself.
[bio15-s2.2.b] The apparatus has to do four jobs: hold the mixture, keep air out while letting gas escape, hold the temperature steady, and detect the two products.
[bio15-s2.2.0] Core equipment: conical flasks or boiling tubes; fermentation locks or rubber bungs fitted with delivery tubes; test tubes of limewater (calcium hydroxide solution); a balance and graduated cylinders; a hot plate or kettle to boil water; a water bath or incubator; a thermometer; a stopwatch; labels.
[bio15-s2.2.1] Materials: dried or fresh baker's yeast; glucose or sucrose; distilled water; liquid paraffin or vegetable oil, which will form a layer that keeps air out.
[bio15-s2.2.2] For the ethanol test: a filter funnel and filter paper, test tubes, potassium iodide solution, sodium hypochlorite solution (dilute bleach) and a water bath.
[bio15-s2.2.3] Optional: a gas syringe, or an inverted measuring cylinder in a trough of water, to collect the gas so its volume can be measured.
[bio15-s2.3.b] The full flask is the reference set-up that meets all four conditions. Every other flask is a copy of it with one condition removed or changed, so follow the steps in order.
[bio15-s2.3.0] 1. Boil distilled water for a few minutes and allow it to cool to about 30 °C. Boiling drives out dissolved oxygen and kills unwanted microorganisms; cooling stops the hot water killing the yeast.
[bio15-s2.3.1] 2. Dissolve glucose in the cooled, boiled water to make a glucose solution (e.g. 10 g in 100 cm³). Place it in a conical flask, add a set mass of yeast (e.g. 2 g) and swirl to mix.
[bio15-s2.3.2] 3. Gently pour a layer of liquid paraffin or vegetable oil on top of the solution, about 1 cm deep, to stop oxygen from the air dissolving in it. This makes the conditions anaerobic.
[bio15-s2.3.3] 4. Fit a fermentation lock containing water, or a bung with a delivery tube leading into a test tube of limewater, so that gas can escape but air cannot get in.
[bio15-s2.3.4] 5. Place the flask in a water bath or incubator at about 30 °C, and set up the control flask beside it.
[bio15-s2.3.5] 6. After the yeast has had time to start, about 10–15 minutes, count the bubbles passing through the fermentation lock or into the limewater in one minute, three times. Note when the limewater turns milky.
[bio15-s2.3.6] Alternatively, collect the gas over a set time in a gas syringe or inverted measuring cylinder.
[bio15-s2.3.7] 7. Leave the flasks until bubbling stops, a day or more, then test for ethanol.
[bio15-s2.4.b] To show that a condition is needed, remove it from the full flask and see what stops. Each flask below differs from the full flask in one condition only.
[bio15-s2.4.0] Sugar: a flask with yeast in boiled, cooled water only, with no glucose, under oil, at 30 °C. Expected result: very few or no bubbles, limewater stays clear and there is no ethanol. Conclusion: sugar is needed.
[bio15-s2.4.1] Microorganisms: a flask with glucose solution and boiled, dead yeast (the control), and, if wished, a flask with glucose solution only. Expected result: no bubbles, limewater stays clear and there is no ethanol. Conclusion: living microorganisms are needed.
[bio15-s2.4.2] Anaerobic environment: a flask with glucose solution and yeast, made with unboiled water, with no oil layer, open to the air or with air bubbled through it (using an aquarium pump), or shaken often.
[bio15-s2.4.3] Expected result: CO₂ is still produced, from aerobic respiration and often faster, but little or no ethanol is detected. Conclusion: ethanol is made when oxygen is absent.
[bio15-s2.4.4] Suitable temperature: identical full flasks placed at about 5 °C (fridge), 20 °C (room), 30 °C, 40 °C and 60 °C (water baths), each checked with a thermometer.
[bio15-s2.4.5] Expected result: very slow bubbling at 5 °C, where the enzymes are slowed but not denatured and speed up if warmed; faster at 20 °C; fastest at about 30–40 °C.
[bio15-s2.4.6] At 60 °C there is no fermentation, because the enzymes are denatured and the yeast killed, and fermentation does not restart on cooling. Conclusion: a suitable warm temperature is needed.
[bio15-s2.5.b] Bubbles prove only that CO₂ is made, which also happens in aerobic respiration. To show that fermentation really happened you must detect ethanol, and the iodoform test does this by making a visible solid.
[bio15-s2.5.0] 1. When bubbling has stopped, filter a sample of the liquid from each flask into a clean test tube, about 3 cm³. Filtering removes the yeast cells.
[bio15-s2.5.1] 2. Add about 3 cm³ of potassium iodide solution.
[bio15-s2.5.2] 3. Add about 5 cm³ of sodium hypochlorite solution and mix.
[bio15-s2.5.3] 4. Warm the tube gently in a water bath at about 60 °C for a few minutes.
[bio15-s2.5.4] 5. Allow the tube to cool and observe. Test water as a negative control and a little dilute ethanol as a positive control, so you know what no ethanol and definite ethanol look like.
[bio15-s2.5.5] Result: pale yellow crystals of iodoform (triiodomethane), with an antiseptic, hospital smell, form if ethanol is present; no crystals form without ethanol.
[bio15-s2.5.6] Alternative test: acidified potassium dichromate solution turns from orange to green when warmed with ethanol. Many schools avoid it because potassium dichromate is toxic.
[bio15-s2.6.b] The syllabus names bacteria as well as yeast, so the same four conditions can be tested with lactic acid bacteria. The product is different, so the way you detect fermentation changes too.
[bio15-s2.6.0] The specification allows bacteria. Lactic acid bacteria ferment the sugar lactose in milk to lactic acid, as in making yogurt.
[bio15-s2.6.1] Set up warm milk with a spoonful of live natural yogurt, the source of the bacteria, in clean, closed containers kept warm in an incubator or water bath. Compare with milk without yogurt, and with boiled yogurt.
[bio15-s2.6.2] Measure the fall in pH over several hours, with a pH meter, pH probe or universal indicator, because lactic acid makes the milk more acidic. The milk also thickens as the acid makes its protein clump.
[bio15-s2.6.3] No CO₂ is produced in lactic acid fermentation, so limewater is not used. The same four conditions can be tested.
[bio15-s2.7.b] This practical uses boiling water, corrosive chemicals, flammable ethanol and living cultures. Each hazard has a simple precaution.
[bio15-s2.7.0] Wear safety glasses. Sodium hypochlorite (bleach) is corrosive and irritant to skin and eyes; wash splashes off with plenty of water. Potassium dichromate, if used, is toxic and must be handled with gloves.
[bio15-s2.7.1] Take care with boiling water, the hot plate and water baths, and let the water cool before adding yeast.
[bio15-s2.7.2] Never seal a fermenting flask with a solid bung and no outlet: CO₂ builds up pressure and can force out the bung or crack the glass.
[bio15-s2.7.3] Ethanol is flammable, so keep it away from flames. Never taste the products; laboratory glassware and solutions are not food-safe.
[bio15-s2.7.4] Treat microorganisms with care: wash hands after the practical, and dispose of yeast and bacterial cultures as the teacher directs. Never eat the yogurt made in the laboratory.
[bio15-s2.8.b] Once the flasks have run, record the primary data (the results you collected yourself), compare each flask with the full flask, and decide whether the hypothesis is supported.
[bio15-s2.8.0] Record primary data in a table with one row per flask, giving the condition, the mean bubbles per minute (or volume of gas), whether limewater is milky or clear, and the ethanol test result (crystals or none).
[bio15-s2.8.1] For the temperature series, plot the mean rate on the y-axis against temperature on the x-axis.
[bio15-s2.8.2] Expected results: the full flask bubbles steadily, its limewater turns milky and its iodoform test is positive. The flasks without sugar or without living yeast show no CO₂ and no ethanol. The aerated flask gives CO₂ but little or no ethanol.
[bio15-s2.8.3] Also expected: the rate rises with temperature to a peak at about 30–40 °C and stops at 60 °C. The control shows no activity.
[bio15-s2.8.4] Conclusion: fermentation, the production of ethanol and CO₂, takes place only when sugar and living yeast are present, oxygen is absent and the temperature is suitable. State whether the results support the hypothesis.
[bio15-s2.8.5] Diagram: the fermentation set-up. A conical flask in a water bath, with a thermometer, containing glucose solution and yeast under a layer of oil, fitted with a fermentation lock or a delivery tube into a test tube of limewater.
[bio15-s2.8.6] Label each part. Optionally draw beside it the control flask with boiled yeast.
[bio15-s2.9.b] Your own results are primary data. Secondary data is information collected by others, and using it alongside yours shows whether your conclusion holds up. The conditions you tested are also the ones used in real fermentation.
[bio15-s2.9.0] Pool class results, or compare with published data, to check reproducibility (whether others get the same result). CO₂ production rates can be measured with a CO₂ sensor and data logger, or by the mass lost by a fermenting flask on a balance as CO₂ escapes.
[bio15-s2.9.1] Applications of the conditions: brewers and wine-makers exclude air so that yeast makes ethanol, and control the temperature; bakers keep dough warm so that yeast makes CO₂ quickly to raise it; yogurt makers incubate milk warm with bacteria. There is more on industrial uses in Microorganisms.
[bio15-s2.10.b] Each weakness below would make the results less reliable or the conclusion less certain. For each one, know what goes wrong and how to improve it.
[bio15-s2.10.0] Oxygen can enter if the water was not boiled, the oil layer is broken or the fermentation lock leaks. Some respiration is then aerobic and less ethanol is made.
[bio15-s2.10.1] Yeast can still ferment some sugar when oxygen is present if plenty of sugar is available, so the aerated flask may give a weak positive ethanol test. Compare the amount of crystals, not just their presence.
[bio15-s2.10.2] Bubbles vary in size and some CO₂ dissolves in the solution, so bubble counts are imprecise and underestimate the gas produced. Collecting the gas, or weighing the mass lost, is more accurate.
[bio15-s2.10.3] Limewater turns milky slowly in air, and very large amounts of CO₂ make the milkiness clear again. Set up a limewater tube open to the air for comparison, and observe at set times.
[bio15-s2.10.4] Temperature can drift, so check the water bath regularly. Yeast takes time to start, so begin timing after the same period in every flask.
[bio15-s2.10.5] Yeast samples differ. Use yeast from the same batch, mixed well, for every flask, and repeat each set-up at least three times so a mean can be taken.

CANDIDATES bio2-s1: Questioning and predicting
[bio2-s1.0.b] A scientific investigation starts with a question that evidence can answer. Questions of value, taste, morality or belief cannot be settled by investigation alone, so the first skill is telling the two kinds apart.
[bio2-s1.0.0] A question is appropriate for scientific investigation when it can be answered by collecting evidence through observation or measurement of the natural world.
[bio2-s1.0.1] It must refer to things that can be observed, measured or counted, and the answer must be capable of being tested against data. This is why a question about numbers, rates or changes in organisms qualifies.
[bio2-s1.0.2] Questions science cannot answer by investigation include questions of value, taste, morality or belief, e.g. “Is it wrong to keep animals in zoos?” or “Which flower is the most beautiful?”. No measurement can settle them, because people can weigh the same evidence differently.
[bio2-s1.0.3] Science can supply evidence that informs a question of value, e.g. how captivity affects an animal’s stress hormones (chemical signals released when an animal is under strain), but evidence alone cannot settle it. The ethical side of scientific work is dealt with in Designing and conducting investigations.
[bio2-s1.0.4] A broad question must be narrowed by naming the factor to be changed and the effect to be measured. “Does temperature affect enzymes?” becomes “How does temperature affect the rate at which catalase breaks down hydrogen peroxide?”
[bio2-s1.0.5] “Does salt affect plants?” becomes “How does the concentration of a salt solution affect the change in mass of potato cylinders?” Notice that both versions now name something you can change and something you can measure.
[bio2-s1.0.6] A good investigable question is specific, names one independent variable (the factor you change) and one dependent variable (the factor you measure), can be carried out with available equipment in the time available, and is safe and ethical to carry out.
[bio2-s1.1.b] Scientific investigation moves in a sequence from a question to a hypothesis, from the hypothesis to a prediction, and then to a test of the prediction. Hypothesis and prediction sound alike but are different things, and telling them apart is the key distinction.
[bio2-s1.1.0] Investigation usually begins with an observation or with existing knowledge that raises a question. The question leads to a hypothesis, the hypothesis leads to a prediction, and the prediction is tested by experiment or further observation.
[bio2-s1.1.1] A hypothesis is a proposed, testable explanation for an observation, or a proposed relationship between two variables (factors that can change), based on existing scientific knowledge. It explains why something might happen.
[bio2-s1.1.2] Example of a hypothesis: “Increasing the temperature increases the rate of catalase activity, because molecules move faster and collide more often with the active site, up to the temperature at which the enzyme denatures.” Catalase is an enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen.
[bio2-s1.1.3] A prediction is a specific statement of the result that will be observed if the hypothesis is correct, often written “If … then …”. It says what will happen in this particular experiment, not why.
[bio2-s1.1.4] Example of a prediction: “If potato cylinders are placed in sucrose solutions of increasing concentration, then their percentage change in mass will decrease, becoming negative in the more concentrated solutions.” It names a measurable result and gives no reason.
[bio2-s1.1.5] The hypothesis is the explanation: general, and it gives a reason. The prediction is the expected observation: specific, and a measurable outcome of the planned test. One hypothesis can generate several predictions, one for each test you could run.
[bio2-s1.1.6] A null hypothesis states that the independent variable has no effect on the dependent variable, e.g. “The concentration of sucrose has no effect on the change in mass of potato cylinders.” An experiment tests whether the data give reason to reject it.
[bio2-s1.1.7] Diagram: flow diagram of observation, question, hypothesis, prediction, test, data and conclusion, with an arrow from the conclusion back to a new question, showing that a hypothesis is supported or rejected and the cycle continues.
[bio2-s1.2.b] Not every explanation can be put to the test. A hypothesis is only useful to science if an investigation could show it to be wrong, and if it is built on real biology rather than a hunch.
[bio2-s1.2.0] A testable hypothesis is falsifiable: it is possible to imagine a result that would show it to be wrong. If no possible result could count against it, it cannot be tested.
[bio2-s1.2.1] “Plants grow better when people talk to them because they enjoy it” is not testable as stated, because enjoyment cannot be measured. “Plants exposed to sound grow taller than plants kept in silence” is testable.
[bio2-s1.2.2] A testable hypothesis names variables that can be measured or controlled (kept the same), and it states the direction of the expected effect: an increase or a decrease, not just “will have an effect”.
[bio2-s1.2.3] A hypothesis is developed from scientific theories and explanations, not from a guess, so the reasoning comes from known biology.
[bio2-s1.2.4] Collision theory (a reaction needs the reacting molecules to collide, so more collisions mean a faster rate) and denaturation (the enzyme’s active site losing its shape at high temperature) support an enzyme-rate hypothesis.
[bio2-s1.2.5] Osmosis supports a hypothesis about potato cylinders: water moves from a dilute solution (higher water concentration) to a more concentrated one across a partially permeable membrane. Cylinders therefore gain mass in distilled water and lose mass in concentrated sucrose solution.
[bio2-s1.2.6] Light as the energy source for photosynthesis supports a hypothesis about a light-intensity investigation, for example that more light gives a faster rate of photosynthesis.
[bio2-s1.2.7] A hypothesis is supported or not supported by data; a single investigation never proves it. This judgement is made at the conclusion stage. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio2-s1.3.b] Scientists can test the same hypothesis in several ways, and each gives different evidence. To evaluate and compare strategies, judge them against the same set of criteria, starting with the controlled laboratory experiment.
[bio2-s1.3.0] Criteria for comparing strategies: whether it can show cause and effect or only correlation (a link between two things); how many variables can be controlled; how realistic the conditions are; the sample size achievable; time, cost and equipment; safety; and ethics.
[bio2-s1.3.1] Controlled laboratory experiment: the investigator changes one variable and controls the others. Strength: the strongest evidence that a change in one variable causes a change in another, because nothing else is changing.
[bio2-s1.3.2] Laboratory limitation: artificial conditions may not match the real world, e.g. an enzyme in a test tube compared with an enzyme in a living cell.
[bio2-s1.3.3] Laboratory limitation: some variables cannot be changed in a lab for ethical or practical reasons.
[bio2-s1.4.b] Some questions cannot be answered by changing things in a lab. A field study works in the organisms’ own habitat, while secondary data lets you use evidence somebody else has already gathered.
[bio2-s1.4.0] Field study or survey (observational study): the investigator observes and measures organisms where they live without changing anything, e.g. comparing the number of a plant species in sun and shade.
[bio2-s1.4.1] Field strengths: the conditions are real, and large areas and many organisms can be studied.
[bio2-s1.4.2] Field limitations: many variables cannot be controlled, so they must be measured and recorded. A survey can show a correlation but not prove a cause, because other factors may be responsible.
[bio2-s1.4.3] Analysis of secondary data (data collected by somebody else), e.g. published datasets, databases of genetic information and government health statistics.
[bio2-s1.4.4] Secondary data strengths: large data sets, long time periods, or data the student could never collect, and the analysis is cheap and quick.
[bio2-s1.4.5] Secondary data limitations: the user did not control how the data were collected and may not know their quality, and the source may lack detail or show bias (a one-sided slant). There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio2-s1.5.b] Some questions are too slow, dangerous or unethical to test directly. Models and human trials handle these in different ways, and the best investigations often combine strategies.
[bio2-s1.5.0] Modelling and simulation use a physical model or computer simulation to test how a system behaves, e.g. a simulation of population growth. Strength: fast, safe, and able to test conditions impossible in reality.
[bio2-s1.5.1] A model is only as good as its assumptions (the simplifications built into it), so its predictions must be checked against real data. There is more on this in Models.
[bio2-s1.5.2] Trials with human participants, e.g. testing a treatment, are needed for questions about human health. Strength: direct evidence about people.
[bio2-s1.5.3] Human trials are limited by ethical restrictions, the need for informed consent (participants agree after being told the risks), high cost and large natural variation between people. This is why large samples and blinding (participants not knowing which treatment they get) are needed.
[bio2-s1.5.4] Often the best answer uses more than one strategy: a field survey suggests a hypothesis and a laboratory experiment tests it.

CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.

CANDIDATES bio24-s3: Seeds
[bio24-s3.0.b] After fertilisation, a flower's ovule (the small structure inside the ovary that holds the female egg cell) develops into a seed. The seed is the package that carries the next generation of a flowering plant.
[bio24-s3.0.0] A seed is a fertilised, mature ovule. It contains an embryo (a new plant in miniature), a food store and a protective coat, the testa.
[bio24-s3.0.1] It is the product of sexual reproduction in flowering plants, because it forms only after a male and a female sex cell have fused.
[bio24-s3.0.2] As the seed matures it loses most of its water and becomes dry and hard, and the activity of the embryo slows almost to a stop. This makes the seed compact and tough.
[bio24-s3.1.b] A seed is built from a few parts, each with one clear job: protect the embryo, be the embryo, or feed it. Learn each part by what it becomes and what it does.
[bio24-s3.1.0] Testa: the seed coat, formed from the outer layers (integuments) of the ovule. It protects the embryo from mechanical damage, from attack by insects, fungi and bacteria, and from drying out.
[bio24-s3.1.1] Embryo: the young plant, which developed from the zygote (the cell formed when the sex cells fuse) by mitosis (cell division that makes identical cells). It is made of the plumule, the radicle and one or two cotyledons.
[bio24-s3.1.2] Plumule: the embryonic shoot of the embryo. It will grow into the shoot (stem and leaves) of the new plant.
[bio24-s3.1.3] Radicle: the embryonic root of the embryo. It will grow into the first root of the new plant, and it points towards the micropyle.
[bio24-s3.1.4] Cotyledon: a seed leaf, part of the embryo. In some seeds the cotyledons absorb and store the food; in others they pass food from the endosperm to the growing embryo.
[bio24-s3.1.5] Endosperm: a food store outside the embryo, formed by double fertilisation. It supplies the embryo with food, mainly starch and also oils and proteins in some seeds, when it starts to grow into a seedling, before the seedling's leaves can photosynthesise.
[bio24-s3.1.6] Micropyle: a tiny pore in the testa, where the pollen tube entered the ovule. Hilum: the scar where the seed was attached to the inside of the fruit.
[bio24-s3.1.7] Food stored in seeds: starch (e.g. cereal grains and beans), oils (e.g. sunflower seeds and oilseed rape) and protein (e.g. peas and beans). These stores make seeds important human foods, such as cereals, pulses and vegetable oils. There is more on this in Metabolic roles and nutritional sources.
[bio24-s3.2.b] Flowering plants split into two groups by how many cotyledons (seed leaves) their embryo has, and seeds also differ in where the food is kept when mature. Maize and broad bean show the two patterns.
[bio24-s3.2.0] Monocotyledons have one cotyledon; dicotyledons have two. This difference is where the names come from.
[bio24-s3.2.1] Endospermic seeds keep the endosperm as the food store when mature. Non-endospermic seeds have absorbed the endosperm into the cotyledons as the seed developed, so the cotyledons hold the food instead.
[bio24-s3.2.2] Maize (a monocot): one cotyledon; a large endosperm is the food store; the cotyledon passes food from the endosperm to the embryo.
[bio24-s3.2.3] The maize grain is strictly a fruit, because its thin fruit wall is fused to the testa. This is why the fruit wall and testa are labelled together on a maize diagram.
[bio24-s3.2.4] Broad bean (a dicot): two large cotyledons store the food, and there is no endosperm in the mature seed.
[bio24-s3.2.5] Diagram: a broad bean seed opened, with one cotyledon removed, labelled testa, micropyle, hilum, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.2.6] Diagram: a vertical section of a maize grain, labelled fruit wall and testa (fused), endosperm, cotyledon, plumule and radicle.
[bio24-s3.3.b] A fruit does not grow on its own: it is the developing seeds inside that tell the flower to swell into a fruit. They do this with chemical messengers called growth regulators.
[bio24-s3.3.0] A growth regulator (plant hormone) is a chemical made in small amounts in one part of a plant that controls growth or development elsewhere. Developing seeds make growth regulators, mainly auxins (e.g. indoleacetic acid, IAA) and gibberellins.
[bio24-s3.3.1] After fertilisation the developing seeds release these growth regulators into the surrounding tissues. They stimulate the cells of the ovary wall (or, in some fruits, the receptacle, the swollen top of the flower stalk) to divide and enlarge, so the fruit grows.
[bio24-s3.3.2] Without fertilised seeds, most flowers simply wither and fall without forming a fruit, because no growth regulators are released to start fruit growth.
[bio24-s3.3.3] Evidence from strawberry: the fleshy part of a strawberry is the swollen receptacle, and the pips on its surface are tiny dry fruits (achenes), each with one seed.
[bio24-s3.3.4] In experiments by J. P. Nitsch (published 1950), removing the pips stopped the receptacle growing, and applying auxin to the de-seeded receptacle restored its growth. Removing pips from only one strip gave a strawberry that grew only where seeds remained.
[bio24-s3.3.5] The number and spread of seeds affect the size and shape of the fruit. Fruits with few or unevenly spread fertilised seeds are small or misshapen, e.g. poorly pollinated strawberries and apples, which is one reason good pollination matters to growers.
[bio24-s3.3.6] Technology link: seedless fruits (parthenocarpy, fruit formation without fertilisation). Growers spray unpollinated flowers with auxins (e.g. tomatoes) or gibberellins so that the fruit grows without seeds. Gibberellins are sprayed on seedless grape varieties to make the grapes larger.
[bio24-s3.3.7] Some plants form fruit naturally without seeds, e.g. cultivated bananas, which are sterile (triploid, having three sets of chromosomes) plants.
[bio24-s3.4.b] Pulling the block together: a seed is how a flowering plant makes new individuals, protects them, feeds them, spreads them and gets them through hard times.
[bio24-s3.4.0] A seed carries the next generation. The embryo is a new individual formed by sexual reproduction, with half its genes from each parent, so seeds give genetic variation within the species. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio24-s3.4.1] The testa protects the embryo, and the food store supplies it until the seedling can photosynthesise (make its own food using light).
[bio24-s3.4.2] Seeds, within fruits, allow the plant to spread to new places, away from the parent. This reduces competition between parent and offspring for light, water and nutrients.
[bio24-s3.4.3] Dry seeds with a tough testa can survive conditions that would kill the parent plant, such as winter or drought, so they carry the species through to a new growing season.
[bio24-s3.4.4] Developing seeds make the growth regulators that cause the fruit to form, and the fruit in turn protects and disperses the seeds.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio25-s3: Investigation: rate of osmosis
[bio25-s3.0.b] This investigation tests three factors that could change how fast osmosis happens, and the key problem is choosing something you can actually measure.
[bio25-s3.0.0] Aim: to investigate how temperature, the concentration gradient (the difference in concentration across the membrane) and surface area affect the rate of osmosis across a semi-permeable membrane.
[bio25-s3.0.1] Water entering a bag of sucrose solution increases the bag's mass, so the rate of osmosis is the change in mass per unit time (g per minute), or the percentage change in mass in a fixed time.
[bio25-s3.0.2] For potato tissue the percentage change in mass in a fixed time is used, because the pieces start at slightly different masses and a percentage makes them comparable.
[bio25-s3.0.3] An alternative for Visking bags (bags made from Visking tubing, a thin tubing with tiny pores) is the rise of liquid up a capillary tube attached to the bag, called an osmometer. The rate is then measured in mm per minute.
[bio25-s3.1.b] Every version of the investigation uses the same core kit and a range of sucrose solutions, and each value of the factor is repeated so that a stray result does not mislead you.
[bio25-s3.1.0] Equipment and materials: Visking tubing; thread or tubing clips; sucrose solutions and distilled water; syringes or measuring cylinders; beakers or boiling tubes; paper towels; stopwatch; scissors; labels; safety glasses.
[bio25-s3.1.1] For temperature and measuring: thermostatically controlled water baths (or beakers of water heated and topped up, with ice for low temperatures) and thermometers; a top-pan balance reading to 0.01 g.
[bio25-s3.1.2] For potato: a fresh potato, a white tile, a scalpel, a ruler and a cork borer (a metal tube that cuts cylinders of tissue).
[bio25-s3.1.3] Sucrose solutions: a 1.0 M (mol per litre) solution contains 342 g of sucrose made up to 1 litre with distilled water. This works because 342 is the mass of one mole of sucrose.
[bio25-s3.1.4] A range, such as 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 M, is made by diluting the 1.0 M solution with distilled water. For example, 2 cm³ of 1.0 M plus 8 cm³ of water gives 0.2 M. There is more on dilution in Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively.
[bio25-s3.1.5] Replicates (repeats): set up three bags, or three sets of potato pieces, for each value of the factor and calculate the mean, so that one odd result has less effect.
[bio25-s3.1.6] Repeat the whole investigation to check repeatability, which means getting similar results when the experiment is done again.
[bio25-s3.2.b] Two of the three investigations use a Visking tubing bag as a model cell. The bag has to be made the same way every time, or differences in the bags will hide the effect you are testing.
[bio25-s3.2.0] 1. Cut pieces of Visking tubing of the same length, e.g. 15 cm, and soak them in water for a few minutes until soft, so the tubing can be opened without tearing.
[bio25-s3.2.1] 2. Rub one end between finger and thumb to open the tube.
[bio25-s3.2.2] 3. Tie one end tightly with thread, or knot the tubing itself, so that nothing can leak out.
[bio25-s3.2.3] 4. Using a syringe, add the same volume, e.g. 10 cm³, of sucrose solution to each bag, so that the bags can be compared fairly.
[bio25-s3.2.4] 5. Squeeze out the air and tie the other end tightly, leaving the bag slightly slack so it can swell as water enters.
[bio25-s3.2.5] 6. Rinse the outside with distilled water to remove any sucrose, blot it dry with paper towel in the same way every time, and weigh it. Surface liquid would otherwise add to the mass.
[bio25-s3.2.6] 7. Record this starting mass, which you need in order to work out the change in mass.
[bio25-s3.3.b] Visking tubing is used because it behaves like a simple version of a cell membrane. Knowing what it lets through explains why the bag gains mass.
[bio25-s3.3.0] Visking tubing is semi-permeable: its tiny pores let water molecules through easily but hold back larger molecules.
[bio25-s3.3.1] Sucrose molecules pass only very slowly, so over a short experiment the bag behaves as a membrane permeable to water but not to sucrose, as cell membranes do. Starch is held back completely.
[bio25-s3.3.2] Visking tubing is semi-permeable (partially permeable), not selectively permeable in the way a living membrane is, because it only sorts molecules by size.
[bio25-s3.3.3] Diagram: a tied Visking bag of sucrose solution in a beaker of distilled water, arrow showing water entering; and an osmometer (bag tied to an upright, clamped capillary tube, ruler beside it). Label: Visking tubing, sucrose solution, distilled water, thread, capillary tube, scale.
[bio25-s3.4.b] The first factor is the concentration gradient. Here the bag holds sucrose solution and the beaker holds distilled water, so the gradient is set by how concentrated the solution inside the bag is.
[bio25-s3.4.0] Hypothesis: the greater the concentration gradient between the solution inside the bag and the water outside, the faster the rate of osmosis, because the difference in water concentration across the membrane is greater, so the net movement of water is faster.
[bio25-s3.4.1] Independent variable (the one you change): the concentration of sucrose inside the bag (0.2 to 1.0 M), with distilled water outside. The gradient therefore increases with concentration. Dependent variable (the one you measure): the rate of mass gain (g per minute).
[bio25-s3.4.2] Controlled variables (kept the same): temperature (all at room temperature, or in one water bath, e.g. 25 °C), length and width of tubing (surface area), volume of solution in the bag, volume of distilled water outside, time intervals and blotting method.
[bio25-s3.4.3] Control: a bag containing distilled water, placed in distilled water, so there is no concentration gradient. Its mass should not change, which shows that any change in the other bags is due to osmosis and not to handling, leaking or water left on the surface.
[bio25-s3.5.b] This is the main method. You weigh the bags at regular intervals to follow how fast each one gains mass.
[bio25-s3.5.0] 1. Make one bag for each concentration (three of each), plus the control, using the steps for making a Visking tubing bag.
[bio25-s3.5.1] 2. Weigh each bag.
[bio25-s3.5.2] 3. Place each bag in its own labelled beaker of distilled water, enough to cover it, and start the stopwatch.
[bio25-s3.5.3] 4. Every 10 minutes, for 40 to 60 minutes, remove each bag, blot it dry in the same way, weigh it, and return it to the water.
[bio25-s3.5.4] 5. Calculate the mean change in mass for each concentration and the rate in g per minute.
[bio25-s3.6.b] Living tissue can be used instead of a bag. A potato is made of cells whose membranes act as semi-permeable membranes, and the sap inside the cells is the solution. The egg is an optional demonstration.
[bio25-s3.6.0] Second method, potato tissue. 1. Use a cork borer to cut cylinders from one potato, remove the skin, and cut them all to the same length, e.g. 3 cm, on a tile.
[bio25-s3.6.1] 2. Blot and weigh each cylinder.
[bio25-s3.6.2] 3. Place three cylinders in each of a range of sucrose solutions (0.0 M, i.e. distilled water, and 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8 and 1.0 M) of the same volume, for the same time (e.g. at least 30 minutes; often left overnight) at the same temperature.
[bio25-s3.6.3] 4. Remove them, blot in the same way, reweigh, and calculate the percentage change in mass, which allows for the different starting masses.
[bio25-s3.6.4] 5. Plot percentage change in mass against sucrose concentration.
[bio25-s3.6.5] Third method (optional demonstration): an egg whose shell has been dissolved in vinegar over a day or two is held in by its membrane, which is semi-permeable. Placed in distilled water it gains mass, and in concentrated sugar syrup it loses mass.
[bio25-s3.6.6] The egg shows the direction of osmosis clearly, but it is slow and gives only one result per egg.
[bio25-s3.7.b] The second factor is temperature. Visking bags are used here, with the same sucrose concentration in every bag, so that temperature is the only thing that changes.
[bio25-s3.7.0] Hypothesis: the higher the temperature, the faster the rate of osmosis, because water molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy) and move faster, so more cross the membrane each second.
[bio25-s3.7.1] Independent variable: the temperature, e.g. 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 °C. Dependent variable: the rate of mass gain (g per minute) of the Visking bags.
[bio25-s3.7.2] Controlled variables: sucrose concentration (e.g. 0.5 M in every bag), volume of solution, length and width of tubing, volume of distilled water, time intervals and blotting method.
[bio25-s3.7.3] Control: at each temperature, a bag of distilled water in distilled water, which should show no mass change.
[bio25-s3.7.4] Visking tubing is used rather than potato for temperature, because high temperatures damage the cell membranes of living tissue, making them fully permeable, so the cells would no longer show osmosis.
[bio25-s3.8.b] The bags and the water must both reach the bath's temperature before the timing starts, or the bag will not be at the temperature you think it is.
[bio25-s3.8.0] 1. Set water baths to each temperature (use iced water for 10 °C) and check each with a thermometer.
[bio25-s3.8.1] 2. Place beakers of distilled water, and boiling tubes of the 0.5 M sucrose solution, in each bath until they reach its temperature.
[bio25-s3.8.2] 3. Make the bags with the warmed or cooled solution, using the steps for making a Visking tubing bag, and weigh them.
[bio25-s3.8.3] 4. Place three bags in the beaker of distilled water in each bath.
[bio25-s3.8.4] 5. Every 10 minutes for 40 minutes, remove, blot, weigh and return each bag, checking the bath temperature each time.
[bio25-s3.8.5] 6. Calculate the mean rate (g per minute) at each temperature.
[bio25-s3.9.b] The third factor is surface area. Cutting the same amount of potato into more pieces gives more membrane in contact with the solution while the volume stays the same, so only the surface area changes.
[bio25-s3.9.0] Hypothesis: the larger the surface area of membrane in contact with the solution, the faster the rate of osmosis, because more water molecules can cross the membranes at the same time.
[bio25-s3.9.1] Independent variable: the surface area of potato tissue, changed by cutting the same volume into more pieces. Dependent variable: the percentage change in total mass in a fixed time.
[bio25-s3.9.2] Controlled variables: the total volume (and starting mass) of potato, from the same potato with no skin; the concentration and volume of the solution (e.g. 0.8 M sucrose, or distilled water); temperature, time and blotting method.
[bio25-s3.9.3] Cut 2 cm × 2 cm × 2 cm cubes, each of volume 8 cm³. Leave one cube whole, with surface area 6 × 2 × 2 = 24 cm².
[bio25-s3.9.4] Cut one cube in half to give two 2 × 2 × 1 cm slabs, with surface area 2 × 16 = 32 cm².
[bio25-s3.9.5] Cut one cube into four 2 × 1 × 1 cm bars, with surface area 4 × 10 = 40 cm². Cut one cube into eight 1 cm cubes, with surface area 8 × 6 = 48 cm².
[bio25-s3.9.6] The total volume is 8 cm³ in every case, so only the surface area differs, which makes it a fair test.
[bio25-s3.9.7] No separate control is needed, because the treatments are compared with each other; the whole 2 cm cube is the reference. The surface area to volume idea links to Levels of organisation.
[bio25-s3.10.b] Each set of pieces is weighed as a group, so the result is the percentage change in the total mass of the set.
[bio25-s3.10.0] 1. Cut the pieces from one potato on a tile, as above, with three sets of each.
[bio25-s3.10.1] 2. Blot each set and weigh all its pieces together.
[bio25-s3.10.2] 3. Place each set in its own beaker containing the same volume of the same solution, at the same temperature.
[bio25-s3.10.3] 4. After a fixed time, e.g. 30 minutes, remove them, blot each piece in the same way, and reweigh the set.
[bio25-s3.10.4] 5. Calculate the percentage change in mass for each set, and the mean for each surface area.
[bio25-s3.11.b] Raw masses are only useful once they are turned into a rate or a percentage change. These are the formulas and two worked examples for you to copy the method from.
[bio25-s3.11.0] Change in mass = final mass minus initial mass. Rate = change in mass divided by time.
[bio25-s3.11.1] Percentage change in mass = (final mass minus initial mass) divided by initial mass, multiplied by 100. A gain is positive; a loss is negative.
[bio25-s3.11.2] Worked example, Visking bag: initial mass 12.40 g, mass after 30 minutes 13.30 g. Change = +0.90 g. Rate = 0.90 ÷ 30 = 0.03 g per minute. Percentage change = 0.90 ÷ 12.40 × 100 = 7.3%.
[bio25-s3.11.3] Worked example, potato: a cylinder of 2.50 g has a mass of 2.20 g after 30 minutes in 1.0 M sucrose. Change = −0.30 g, and percentage change = −0.30 ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −12%.
[bio25-s3.11.4] A cylinder of 2.00 g that becomes 2.30 g in distilled water has a change of +0.30 g, so percentage change = +0.30 ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +15%.
[bio25-s3.11.5] Graphs: plot the factor (independent variable) on the x-axis and the rate or percentage change in mass (dependent variable) on the y-axis, using the mean of the replicates.
[bio25-s3.11.6] Alternatively plot mass against time for each value, where a steeper line shows a faster rate, because mass is changing more quickly.
[bio25-s3.12.b] This investigation uses sharp tools, hot water and sugar solution, so each hazard needs a simple precaution.
[bio25-s3.12.0] Cut potato on a white tile, cutting away from the body and fingers; carry scalpels and cork borers safely.
[bio25-s3.12.1] Hot water baths: use a test-tube holder or tongs to handle beakers and bags in water above about 40 °C, and keep baths at or below 50 °C to avoid scalds.
[bio25-s3.12.2] Glass capillary tubes break easily and are sharp; handle and clamp them carefully, and wear safety glasses.
[bio25-s3.12.3] Mop up spilt sugar solution at once, as it makes floors slippery; keep water away from electrical sockets and water-bath controls.
[bio25-s3.12.4] Don't eat any food materials used. If using eggs, wash hands after handling raw egg.
[bio25-s3.13.b] Each factor gives a typical pattern. A good conclusion states the pattern, uses your own data and says whether it supports the hypothesis.
[bio25-s3.13.0] Concentration gradient (Visking): the bags gain mass, faster with higher sucrose concentration, and the control bag stays the same. Conclusion: the rate of osmosis increases as the concentration gradient increases.
[bio25-s3.13.1] In every bag the mass gain slows over time, because the entering water dilutes the solution inside, reducing the gradient, and the bag becomes tight.
[bio25-s3.13.2] Concentration (potato): cylinders gain mass and feel firm (turgid) in distilled water and dilute solutions, because water enters the cells. They lose mass and feel soft and bendy (flaccid) in concentrated solutions, because water leaves the cells.
[bio25-s3.13.3] The larger the difference between the solution and the cell sap, the larger the change. Where the graph line crosses zero change, the solution has the same water concentration as the cell sap (isotonic), so there is no net osmosis.
[bio25-s3.13.4] Temperature: the rate of mass gain increases steadily as temperature increases. Conclusion: the rate of osmosis increases with temperature.
[bio25-s3.13.5] There is no optimum and no sharp fall at high temperature, because no enzyme is involved and Visking tubing isn't denatured (it keeps its shape and does not stop working when hot).
[bio25-s3.13.6] Surface area: the percentage change in mass in the same time is greatest for the eight 1 cm cubes (48 cm²) and least for the whole cube (24 cm²). Conclusion: the rate of osmosis increases with surface area.
[bio25-s3.13.7] Each conclusion should quote the primary data (your own measured results), e.g. the mean rates, and say whether they support the hypothesis.
[bio25-s3.14.b] No school experiment is perfect. Knowing what can go wrong, and how to reduce it, lets you explain unexpected results and suggest improvements.
[bio25-s3.14.0] Blotting: leaving more or less surface water on bags or potato pieces changes the mass, so blot in exactly the same way each time. Potato cut into more pieces holds more surface liquid, which can exaggerate the surface-area effect.
[bio25-s3.14.1] Leaks: badly tied bags leak, so check the knots and discard any leaking bag. Some sucrose diffuses slowly out of Visking tubing, reducing the gradient over time.
[bio25-s3.14.2] Bags or pieces that differ in size, volume of solution or trapped air are not fair comparisons; measure volumes with a syringe and cut potato accurately.
[bio25-s3.14.3] Water-bath temperatures drift, and bags cool while being weighed; check the temperature at each reading and weigh quickly.
[bio25-s3.14.4] A balance reading only to 0.1 g or 1 g can't detect small changes; use one reading to 0.01 g.
[bio25-s3.14.5] Potato tissue varies, between different potatoes or between the centre and edge of one potato; cut all pieces from one potato and use replicates. Cut surfaces are damaged cells that don't behave normally.
[bio25-s3.14.6] Results from Visking tubing model a membrane, but they don't show what living, selectively permeable membranes do, such as moving substances actively.

CANDIDATES bio35-s3: Generating a DNA profile
[bio35-s3.0.b] A DNA profile is made by turning a tiny biological sample into a pattern of bands that can be compared between people. The process always follows the same five steps, and each step has its own reagents or equipment.
[bio35-s3.0.0] The steps are: 1. Collect a sample, because every cell with a nucleus carries the person's DNA.
[bio35-s3.0.1] 2. Extract the DNA, which means freeing it from the cells and separating it from everything else.
[bio35-s3.0.2] 3. Amplify (copy) the chosen repeat regions by PCR (the polymerase chain reaction), so there is enough DNA to detect.
[bio35-s3.0.3] 4. Separate the copies by size by gel electrophoresis.
[bio35-s3.0.4] 5. Make the bands visible and analyse (compare) the pattern.
[bio35-s3.0.5] In the earlier method, step 3 was replaced by cutting the DNA with restriction enzymes (enzymes that cut DNA at specific sequences). It is taught in the restriction enzymes block below.
[bio35-s3.0.6] Diagram: a flow diagram of the five steps, with the key reagent or equipment at each: sample (swab, blood) → extraction (detergent, salt, protease, cold ethanol) → PCR (primers, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, thermal cycler) → gel electrophoresis (agarose gel, wells, electric current) → stained bands compared.
[bio35-s3.1.b] DNA can be taken from any cell that has a nucleus, because the nucleus is where a human cell keeps its DNA. Extraction then frees the DNA from the cell in three steps, each aimed at removing one barrier.
[bio35-s3.1.0] Sources are any cells with a nucleus, e.g. white blood cells (mature red blood cells have no nucleus, so they contain no nuclear DNA), cheek cells from a mouth swab, skin cells, hair roots (not the cut hair shaft, which has no usable nucleus), saliva and semen.
[bio35-s3.1.1] Samples are collected with gloves and stored sealed and cold to avoid contamination (DNA from the collector or other sources getting in) and breakdown of the DNA.
[bio35-s3.1.2] Extraction, step 1 (lysis, meaning breaking open the cells): a detergent breaks down the phospholipid membranes (the cell membrane and the nuclear envelope), releasing the DNA.
[bio35-s3.1.3] Step 2: a protease enzyme digests proteins, including the histones (the proteins the DNA is wound around in a chromosome) bound to the DNA, leaving the DNA free.
[bio35-s3.1.4] Step 3: DNA is insoluble in cold ethanol, so it comes out of solution (precipitates) and can be collected. Salt helps the DNA strands clump together.
[bio35-s3.1.5] Forensic laboratories use the same principles (break cells, remove protein, purify DNA) with purified kits, because crime-scene samples are tiny and need careful handling.
[bio35-s3.2.b] The school version of extraction uses onion, a plant tissue, and everyday chemicals. The first five steps break the tissue down and protect the DNA from damage.
[bio35-s3.2.0] 1. Chop the onion finely, to break cell walls and give a large surface area.
[bio35-s3.2.1] 2. Add it to a beaker containing a solution of washing-up liquid (detergent) and table salt.
[bio35-s3.2.2] 3. Stand the beaker in a water bath at 60 °C for 15 minutes. The heat helps the detergent work and denatures enzymes that would break down the DNA; longer heating would start to break the DNA itself.
[bio35-s3.2.3] 4. Cool the beaker in an ice-water bath for 5 minutes, slowing any enzyme breakdown of the DNA.
[bio35-s3.2.4] 5. Blend in a food processor for no more than a few seconds, because longer blending would break the long DNA strands.
[bio35-s3.2.5] Kiwi or strawberry also work in place of onion.
[bio35-s3.3.b] The last four steps remove the debris, digest the proteins and then bring the DNA out of solution so it can be seen. Each reagent has one job.
[bio35-s3.3.0] 6. Filter through coffee filter paper (or several layers of muslin) to remove cell debris, collecting the liquid filtrate in a test tube.
[bio35-s3.3.1] 7. Add a few drops of protease to digest proteins.
[bio35-s3.3.2] 8. Pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side of the tilted test tube so it forms a layer on top of the filtrate.
[bio35-s3.3.3] 9. DNA appears at the boundary as white, stringy threads that can be wound onto a glass rod.
[bio35-s3.3.4] In short: detergent breaks the membranes, salt clumps the DNA, protease removes proteins and cold ethanol precipitates the DNA.
[bio35-s3.3.5] Safety for this practical: ethanol is highly flammable (no flames nearby); take care with blender blades and hot water; wear safety glasses.
[bio35-s3.4.b] A crime-scene sample may hold only a few cells, far too little DNA to see on a gel. PCR solves this by copying a chosen stretch of DNA many times over.
[bio35-s3.4.0] PCR makes millions to billions of copies of a chosen short region of DNA (here, the STR regions, short repeated sequences that differ between people) from a tiny sample, e.g. a single hair root or a spot of blood, so that there is enough DNA to see on a gel.
[bio35-s3.4.1] PCR is DNA replication carried out in a tube. It copies only the chosen region, not the whole genome.
[bio35-s3.4.2] The reaction mixture contains the extracted DNA (the template, the DNA to be copied).
[bio35-s3.4.3] It also contains primers, which are short single-stranded pieces of DNA made to be complementary to the sequences on either side of the region to be copied. This is why only that region is copied: the primers decide which region.
[bio35-s3.4.4] DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides onto an existing strand, so a primer is needed to start each new strand.
[bio35-s3.4.5] It also contains a supply of the four DNA nucleotides (A, T, C, G), the building blocks of the new strands, a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase), and a buffer solution.
[bio35-s3.4.6] The tubes are placed in a thermal cycler, a machine that changes the temperature automatically.
[bio35-s3.5.b] PCR involves repeated heating to a very high temperature, which would destroy most enzymes. The solution was an enzyme from a heat-loving organism.
[bio35-s3.5.0] Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium found in hot springs (first found in Yellowstone National Park, USA).
[bio35-s3.5.1] Its structure holds its shape at high temperatures, so it is not denatured (permanently unfolded) at 95 °C and works best at about 72 °C.
[bio35-s3.5.2] A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first heating step, and fresh enzyme would have to be added every cycle.
[bio35-s3.5.3] This is why PCR can be run automatically in a thermal cycler: the same enzyme survives every cycle.
[bio35-s3.6.b] Each PCR cycle has three temperature steps in a fixed order: hot, cool, then warm. Each step does one job in copying the DNA.
[bio35-s3.6.0] Step 1, denaturation: heat to about 95 °C. The hydrogen bonds between the complementary bases break and the two strands of each DNA molecule separate. Heat does here what helicase (the enzyme that unwinds DNA) does in cells.
[bio35-s3.6.1] Step 2, annealing: cool to about 55 °C (usually between 50 and 65 °C, depending on the primers). The primers bind (anneal) by complementary base pairing to their matching sequences on each single strand.
[bio35-s3.6.2] Step 3, extension: heat to about 72 °C, the optimum temperature of Taq polymerase. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to the end of each primer, complementary to the template strand, building a new strand.
[bio35-s3.6.3] Each double-stranded region becomes two.
[bio35-s3.6.4] Diagram: the PCR cycle as three stages in a circle: DNA at 95 °C → separated strands; primers binding at 55 °C; extension at 72 °C → two copies; “cycle repeats, doubling the DNA each time” (1 → 2 → 4 → 8). Labels: template strand, primer, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, new strand, three temperatures.
[bio35-s3.7.b] Because every cycle doubles the DNA, the number of copies grows very quickly. This is what makes a tiny sample usable.
[bio35-s3.7.0] Each cycle takes a few minutes and doubles the number of copies of the target region, so after n cycles one molecule gives 2ⁿ copies.
[bio35-s3.7.1] 10 cycles give 1,024 copies (2¹⁰); 30 cycles give 2³⁰, about 1 billion (1,073,741,824).
[bio35-s3.7.2] A run is usually 25–35 cycles and takes a few hours.
[bio35-s3.7.3] Kary Mullis invented PCR in 1983 and shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993.
[bio35-s3.7.4] Using Taq polymerase, an enzyme discovered in basic research on bacteria of hot springs, made PCR quick and automatic.
[bio35-s3.7.5] Other uses of PCR: diagnosing infections by detecting a pathogen’s DNA or RNA (e.g. PCR tests for COVID-19), detecting alleles that cause genetic diseases, and preparing DNA for sequencing (reading the order of bases).
[bio35-s3.8.b] Before PCR was used, DNA profiles were made by cutting the DNA rather than copying it. This is the method of Alec Jeffreys, who first developed DNA profiling.
[bio35-s3.8.0] In Alec Jeffreys’ original method, the extracted DNA was cut with restriction enzymes, which cut at specific recognition sequences on either side of the repeat regions.
[bio35-s3.8.1] Because people have different numbers of repeats between the cutting sites, the fragments differed in length between people, and these were separated by gel electrophoresis.
[bio35-s3.8.2] This needed much more DNA than PCR-based profiling, which has replaced it.
[bio35-s3.9.b] Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size using an electric current. Before the current is switched on, the gel, the buffer and the samples must be set up the right way round.
[bio35-s3.9.0] The gel is usually agarose, a jelly made from a polysaccharide extracted from seaweed; it is full of tiny pores that act like a sieve.
[bio35-s3.9.1] The gel is made with a row of small holes (wells) near one end and placed in a tank of buffer solution, which conducts electricity, with the wells at the negative electrode (cathode) end.
[bio35-s3.9.2] Each DNA sample is mixed with a loading dye, which is dense so the sample sinks into the well, and coloured so that the progress of the run can be seen.
[bio35-s3.9.3] Each sample is placed in its own well with a micropipette (a tool for measuring tiny volumes).
[bio35-s3.9.4] One well holds a DNA ladder (size marker): a mixture of fragments of known lengths, which lets you work out the size of the other fragments.
[bio35-s3.10.b] Once the current is on, the DNA moves and sorts itself by size. Because DNA is colourless, the bands then need to be made visible.
[bio35-s3.10.0] Running: when the current is switched on, the negatively charged DNA (its phosphate groups carry the charge) moves through the gel towards the positive electrode (anode).
[bio35-s3.10.1] Small fragments pass through the pores more easily and move faster, so after a set time fragments of different lengths are separated into bands, the smallest furthest from the wells.
[bio35-s3.10.2] The current is switched off before the loading dye reaches the end of the gel, so that no fragments run off the gel.
[bio35-s3.10.3] Making the bands visible: DNA is colourless, so the gel is stained. In schools a safe stain such as methylene blue is used, showing blue bands under white light.
[bio35-s3.10.4] Research laboratories use fluorescent stains that glow under ultraviolet light. The older stain ethidium bromide is a mutagen (it can cause mutations) and must be handled with gloves.
[bio35-s3.10.5] Modern forensic profiling uses primers labelled with fluorescent dyes and separates the fragments in a thin capillary tube in a machine, which reads the result as peaks.
[bio35-s3.10.6] Diagram: an electrophoresis tank, side and top views: gel in buffer, wells at the negative (black) end, positive (red) electrode at the other, power pack, ladder and sample lanes, arrow showing DNA direction. Labels: agarose gel, well, buffer solution, cathode (−), anode (+), DNA ladder, bands, “smaller fragments travel further”.
[bio35-s3.11.b] The final step compares the patterns of bands to decide whether DNA samples match.
[bio35-s3.11.0] The band positions (or peak sizes) of the samples are compared with each other and with the ladder, which gives the size of each fragment.
[bio35-s3.11.1] Matching at every locus (position on the DNA) indicates the same person.
[bio35-s3.11.2] Inheritance of one band per locus from each parent is used in paternity cases, because a child's DNA is a mix of the mother's and the father's.
[bio35-s3.11.3] There is more on this in Investigation: DNA-profile patterns.

CANDIDATES bio7-s2: Microscopy
[bio7-s2.0.b] Microscopes do two different jobs: they make things look bigger, and they let you see fine detail. Students often mix these up, but only the second one limits what a light microscope can show.
[bio7-s2.0.0] Magnification is how many times larger the image is than the real object, so a ×100 image is 100 times the real size.
[bio7-s2.0.1] Resolution (also called resolving power) is the ability to show two points that are close together as separate points. It decides how much detail can be seen.
[bio7-s2.0.2] Magnifying an image beyond the resolution limit only makes it bigger and blurrier, not more detailed, because the two close points have already merged into one blur.
[bio7-s2.0.3] Total magnification of a light microscope = eyepiece lens magnification × objective lens magnification. For example, a ×10 eyepiece with a ×40 objective gives ×400.
[bio7-s2.1.b] The light (optical) microscope passes light through a thin specimen and uses glass lenses to enlarge the image. These parts form the image and bring it into focus.
[bio7-s2.1.0] Eyepiece (ocular) lens: the lens you look through. It magnifies the image from the objective, usually ×10.
[bio7-s2.1.1] Objective lenses: mounted on a revolving nosepiece so they can be swapped. They are usually ×4 (scanning), ×10 (low power) and ×40 (high power), sometimes ×100 (oil immersion). They produce the first magnified image.
[bio7-s2.1.2] Stage with clips: the platform that holds the slide in place over the hole through which light passes.
[bio7-s2.1.3] Coarse focus knob: moves the stage (or tube) a long way quickly, so it is used for focusing at low power only.
[bio7-s2.1.4] Fine focus knob: moves the stage or tube a small distance to give a sharp image. It is the only focus used at high power.
[bio7-s2.2.b] Light has to reach the specimen in the right amount and direction, and the instrument needs a stable frame. These parts do that.
[bio7-s2.2.0] Light source: a built-in lamp, or a mirror that reflects light up through the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.1] Condenser: a lens under the stage that focuses light onto the specimen.
[bio7-s2.2.2] Diaphragm (iris): adjusts the amount of light reaching the specimen. Less light often improves contrast for pale, unstained cells, which are nearly transparent and look washed out in bright light.
[bio7-s2.2.3] Arm and base: support the microscope. The arm is used for carrying it.
[bio7-s2.2.4] Diagram: a light microscope labelled eyepiece, body tube, revolving nosepiece, objective lenses, stage, clips, condenser, diaphragm, light source (or mirror), coarse focus knob, fine focus knob, arm and base.
[bio7-s2.3.b] The method is built to protect the slide and the lenses. You always start on the lowest power, which has the widest view and the most room between lens and slide.
[bio7-s2.3.0] 1. Carry the microscope with one hand on the arm and the other under the base, so its weight is supported.
[bio7-s2.3.1] 2. Switch on the light and turn the lowest-power objective into place.
[bio7-s2.3.2] 3. Clip the slide on the stage with the specimen over the hole, so light can pass through it.
[bio7-s2.3.3] 4. Looking from the side, use the coarse focus to bring the objective close to the slide without touching it. Watching from the side stops you grinding the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.3.4] 5. Looking through the eyepiece, focus by moving the objective and slide apart until the image is clear.
[bio7-s2.3.5] 6. Use the fine focus to sharpen the image, and the diaphragm to adjust the light.
[bio7-s2.3.6] 7. Move the part to be studied to the centre of the field of view (the circle of specimen you can see), so it stays in view when you increase magnification.
[bio7-s2.3.7] 8. Turn the next objective into place and use only the fine focus.
[bio7-s2.3.8] 9. Record the total magnification with every drawing.
[bio7-s2.4.b] The image behaves in predictable ways as you change objective, and one safety rule matters for your eyes.
[bio7-s2.4.0] The image is inverted, meaning upside down and reversed left to right. This means moving the slide to the left moves the image to the right.
[bio7-s2.4.1] At higher magnification the field of view is smaller and the image is dimmer, so more light is needed.
[bio7-s2.4.2] At high power the objective is very close to the slide, which is why the coarse focus must not be used: it could crash the lens into the slide.
[bio7-s2.4.3] Never use a mirror to reflect direct sunlight into the microscope, as it can damage the eye.
[bio7-s2.5.b] To view a specimen with light passing through it, you mount it on a glass slide. The technique below is the general method that any slide uses.
[bio7-s2.5.0] A wet mount: the specimen is placed in a drop of water or stain on a clean glass slide and covered with a coverslip (a thin square of glass).
[bio7-s2.5.1] The specimen must be very thin, ideally one layer of cells thick, so that light can pass through it and the cells do not overlap.
[bio7-s2.5.2] Stains add contrast to transparent structures by colouring some parts more than others. Iodine solution is used for plant cells: it stains the nucleus yellow-brown and turns starch blue-black. Methylene blue is used for animal cells: it stains the nucleus dark blue.
[bio7-s2.5.3] Lowering the coverslip: touch one edge of the coverslip to the liquid at an angle of about 45° and lower it slowly with a mounted needle. This pushes air out ahead of it and avoids air bubbles, which appear as black-edged circles and can hide cells.
[bio7-s2.5.4] The coverslip keeps the specimen flat, stops it drying out, and protects the objective lens from the liquid. Excess liquid is removed by touching filter paper or a paper towel to the edge.
[bio7-s2.5.5] Stain can also be added after the coverslip is on. Put a drop at one edge and draw it under the coverslip with filter paper held at the opposite edge (irrigation). There is more on the full procedures in Investigation: plant and animal cells.
[bio7-s2.6.b] A light microscope cannot resolve anything much smaller than 0.2 µm. Electron microscopes get round this by using electrons instead of light, so they show far finer detail.
[bio7-s2.6.0] An electron microscope uses a beam of electrons instead of light, focused by electromagnets instead of glass lenses. Electrons have a much shorter wavelength than light, so resolution is far better.
[bio7-s2.6.1] The specimen is in a vacuum, because air would scatter the electrons, so it must be dead. The image is viewed on a screen and recorded digitally. It is black and white, and colour is added afterwards (false colour).
[bio7-s2.6.2] Transmission electron microscope (TEM): electrons pass through an extremely thin, stained section of a specimen. It gives a flat (2D) image of internal structure at the highest magnifications, e.g. mitochondria, ribosomes, nuclear pores and viruses.
[bio7-s2.6.3] Scanning electron microscope (SEM): a beam of electrons scans across the surface of a specimen coated with a very thin layer of metal, e.g. gold. Electrons bouncing off it form an image. It gives a 3D image of surfaces, e.g. the surface of cells, pollen grains and insects.
[bio7-s2.6.4] The SEM has lower resolution than the TEM and shows no internal structure unless the specimen is broken open.
[bio7-s2.6.5] The first electron microscope was built in Germany in 1931 by Max Knoll and Ernst Ruska.
[bio7-s2.7.b] The two types differ in how they work, what they can resolve and what they demand of the specimen. Compare them point by point.
[bio7-s2.7.0] Radiation: light for the light microscope, electrons for the electron microscope. Lenses: glass for the light microscope, electromagnets for the electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.1] Maximum useful magnification: about ×1000–1500 for the light microscope, compared with hundreds of thousands of times or more for electron microscopes.
[bio7-s2.7.2] Resolution: about 0.2 µm (200 nm) for the light microscope, compared with less than 1 nm for the TEM, roughly a thousand times better.
[bio7-s2.7.3] So the light microscope shows the nucleus, cell wall, vacuole and chloroplasts. Ribosomes, nuclear pores, the inner structure of mitochondria and viruses are too small and need an electron microscope.
[bio7-s2.7.4] Specimens: the light microscope can view living or dead specimens, with colour visible. The electron microscope needs dead specimens in a vacuum, and its images are black and white.
[bio7-s2.7.5] Preparation and cost: light microscopes are simple, quick, cheap and portable. Electron microscopes need complex preparation (thin sectioning, metal coating) that can create artefacts, structures that are not really in the living cell. They are very expensive, large, and need trained operators.
[bio7-s2.8.b] Microscopy has a long history and is still developing. Newer techniques tag, scan or freeze specimens to reveal what older methods could not.
[bio7-s2.8.0] Robert Hooke described and named “cells” from thin slices of cork in his book Micrographia (1665).
[bio7-s2.8.1] Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, using his own single-lens microscopes, was the first to see and describe bacteria and other single-celled organisms, in the 1670s.
[bio7-s2.8.2] Fluorescence microscopy: specific molecules are tagged with fluorescent dyes or fluorescent proteins that glow when lit with a particular colour of light. This shows exactly where a protein is in a living cell.
[bio7-s2.8.3] Green fluorescent protein (GFP), from a jellyfish, earned the 2008 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Osamu Shimomura, Martin Chalfie and Roger Tsien.
[bio7-s2.8.4] Confocal laser scanning microscopy: a laser scans one thin plane of the specimen at a time, giving sharp images that a computer combines into a 3D model.
[bio7-s2.8.5] Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy overcomes the light microscope’s normal resolution limit. It earned the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William Moerner.
[bio7-s2.8.6] Cryo-electron microscopy: samples are flash-frozen in a thin layer of ice instead of being dried and stained, so molecules keep their natural shape. It shows the structure of proteins and viruses in near-atomic detail.
[bio7-s2.8.7] Cryo-electron microscopy earned the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, awarded to Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank and Richard Henderson.
[bio7-s2.8.8] Cryo-electron microscopy was used to show the structure of the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein, which helped vaccine design.
[bio7-s2.9.b] Microscopes are everyday tools in medicine, research and forensics, and modern imaging adds automation.
[bio7-s2.9.0] Uses in health and technology: examining blood smears and biopsies to diagnose disease, including spotting cancer cells; identifying pathogens; research into how cells and viruses work; and forensic science, e.g. comparing hairs and fibres.
[bio7-s2.9.1] Digital cameras and computer image analysis now allow images to be measured, shared and analysed automatically.
