TARGET bio34-s1: The carbon cycle
[bio34-s1.0.b] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the elements living things are built from have to be reused. Carbon is the clearest example: it moves round and round between organisms and their surroundings.
[bio34-s1.0.0] A biogeochemical cycle, or nutrient cycle, is the continuous movement of a chemical element between living things (bio) and the non-living environment (geo): the air, water, rocks and soil.
[bio34-s1.0.1] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the same atoms are used again and again. Energy, by contrast, flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.0.2] Carbon is the element at the centre of every biomolecule (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids; see Biomolecules and metabolism), so every organism needs a constant supply. Producers (organisms that make their own food, such as plants) take it in as CO₂; consumers get it already built into food.
[bio34-s1.0.3] A store (reservoir) is a place where carbon is held: the atmosphere (as CO₂, and a little methane, CH₄); living organisms (biomass, the mass of living material); dead organic matter in soil; the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate); rocks such as limestone and chalk (calcium carbonate); and fossil fuels.
[bio34-s1.0.4] A process, or flux, is whatever moves carbon from one store to another. Stores are the boxes of the cycle and processes are the arrows between them.
[bio34-s1.0.5] CO₂ makes up only about 0.04% of the air (over 420 parts per million), yet it is the source of carbon for almost all life on Earth, because photosynthesis starts from it.
[bio34-s1.1.b] Carbon dioxide leaves the air in only a few ways. By far the most important is photosynthesis, but dissolving in water and shell building also lock carbon away.
[bio34-s1.1.0] Photosynthesis by plants, algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating algae) fixes CO₂ into glucose and then into the rest of their biomass, so they act as carbon sinks (stores that take in more carbon than they release).
[bio34-s1.1.1] Photosynthesis is the only major route by which carbon enters living things, so nearly all the carbon in a food chain started as CO₂ taken in by a producer.
[bio34-s1.1.2] Dissolving: CO₂ dissolves in the surface of the oceans, lakes and rivers, and aquatic producers take it up for photosynthesis.
[bio34-s1.1.3] Shell and skeleton building: many marine organisms (e.g. corals, molluscs and some phytoplankton) combine dissolved carbonate with calcium to make calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.
[bio34-s1.1.4] When these organisms die, their shells and skeletons settle on the sea floor and, over millions of years, form limestone and chalk. These rocks are very long-term stores because the carbon stays locked in them for so long.
[bio34-s1.2.b] Once carbon is in a producer it does not stay there. It passes along the food chain and into the dead matter that every organism leaves behind.
[bio34-s1.2.0] Feeding: carbon in the producer's biomass passes to herbivores and then carnivores when they eat and digest it. At each step most of it is respired away (broken down to release energy, giving off CO₂) rather than built into the consumer's body.
[bio34-s1.2.1] Each step in a food chain is a trophic level (a feeding level). There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.2.2] Egestion and excretion: carbon leaves animals in faeces and in wastes such as urea, and enters the dead organic matter store, as do shed leaves and dead organisms. This store is the food supply for decomposers.
[bio34-s1.3.b] Carbon dioxide is put back into the air by respiration, by decomposers, by burning and by geological release. If it were not returned, producers would run out of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.3.0] Respiration by plants, animals and microorganisms breaks down glucose and releases CO₂. Details are in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio34-s1.3.1] Plants respire all the time, day and night, so the respiration arrow must start from plants as well as animals. Over a growing season photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the plant as a whole gains carbon.
[bio34-s1.3.2] Combustion: burning biomass (wood, peat, forest fires) or fossil fuels combines the carbon with oxygen and releases it as CO₂ very quickly.
[bio34-s1.3.3] Natural geological release: volcanoes release CO₂ from rocks, and the weathering of limestone releases some. This is small compared with the biological fluxes and with fossil-fuel burning.
[bio34-s1.4.b] Dead organisms, faeces and fallen leaves contain a lot of carbon. Decomposers are the living things that break this matter down and return its carbon to the air, so the cycle can keep turning.
[bio34-s1.4.0] Decomposers are bacteria and fungi. They feed saprophytically (on dead organic matter): they secrete enzymes that digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products, and respire them, releasing CO₂ and minerals.
[bio34-s1.4.1] Say that decomposers digest the matter and then respire the products. The CO₂ comes from their respiration, not from the matter breaking down into CO₂ directly.
[bio34-s1.4.2] Without decomposers, carbon would stay locked in dead matter and the CO₂ supply for photosynthesis would run out. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi, and soil microbiomes are covered in The microbiome.
[bio34-s1.4.3] Decomposers work fastest when it is warm, moist and well aerated, because they respire aerobically (using oxygen).
[bio34-s1.4.4] In cold, waterlogged, acidic or oxygen-poor places decomposition is very slow, so dead matter builds up, e.g. as peat in bogs.
[bio34-s1.4.5] Anaerobic decomposition: where there is no oxygen (waterlogged soils, bogs, rice paddies, landfill, slurry tanks and the stomachs of cattle and sheep), some microorganisms break organic matter down to methane (CH₄) instead of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.4.6] The methane-producing microorganisms are archaea, a separate group of single-celled organisms from bacteria (see Domains of life). Methane in the air is slowly converted to CO₂.
[bio34-s1.5.b] Fossil fuels are a store of carbon that the natural cycle leaves almost untouched. People now burn them, which is why they matter so much to the carbon cycle.
[bio34-s1.5.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) are the remains of organisms that lived millions of years ago and were buried before they could decompose fully.
[bio34-s1.5.1] Coal formed mainly from land plants in ancient swamps. Oil and natural gas formed mainly from tiny marine organisms (plankton) buried in sea-floor mud. Heat and pressure over millions of years changed them into fuels.
[bio34-s1.5.2] The carbon in fossil fuels was fixed by photosynthesis long ago, so they store ancient solar energy and carbon. Peat is an early stage of the same process, which is why it is sometimes counted with them.
[bio34-s1.5.3] In the natural cycle almost no carbon leaves this store.
[bio34-s1.5.4] Burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution moves carbon locked away for millions of years into the atmosphere in a few centuries, far faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can take it back, so atmospheric CO₂ rises. Consequences are in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.b] Whether a store helps to lower or raise atmospheric CO₂ depends on whether carbon is flowing in or out. Photosynthesis shows how plants act as sinks; here the sink and source distinction is applied to every store and to the overall balance of the cycle.
[bio34-s1.6.0] A store acts as a sink when its inputs exceed its outputs, so it gains carbon, and as a source when its outputs exceed its inputs, so it releases carbon.
[bio34-s1.6.1] The same store can switch between the two: a growing forest is a sink; a mature forest roughly balances; a burning or felled forest is a source. A healthy wet bog is a sink; a drained or burning bog is a source.
[bio34-s1.6.2] The main natural sinks on a carbon-cycle diagram are land plants (forests and grassland, with the soil carbon under them); algae and phytoplankton in the oceans (with dissolving CO₂ and shells sinking to the sea floor); bogs and other wetlands; and limestone and fossil fuels as very long-term stores.
[bio34-s1.6.3] These sinks are compared, and the strategies that use them are described, in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.4] Before large-scale fossil-fuel burning, the CO₂ taken up by photosynthesis and the oceans each year was roughly balanced by the CO₂ released by respiration, decomposition and natural fires. Atmospheric CO₂ therefore stayed nearly constant, at about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution.
[bio34-s1.6.5] Deforestation and fossil-fuel burning have tipped this balance, so more CO₂ is now added than removed.
[bio34-s1.6.6] Seasonal pattern: atmospheric CO₂ falls each northern summer, when photosynthesis exceeds respiration over the large land area of the northern hemisphere, and rises each winter, when respiration and decomposition exceed photosynthesis. This happens on top of the long-term rise.
[bio34-s1.7.b] A model represents the cycle in a diagram and in words, and is used to explain and predict. It is only as good as its limits allow.
[bio34-s1.7.0] Diagram: draw boxes for the stores: carbon dioxide in the air; green plants and algae; animals; dead organisms and wastes; decomposers (bacteria and fungi); fossil fuels; oceans.
[bio34-s1.7.1] Diagram arrows, each labelled with its process: photosynthesis (air → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (plants and animals → dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → decomposers); respiration (plants, animals and decomposers → air).
[bio34-s1.7.2] Diagram arrows, continued: fossilisation (dead matter → fossil fuels, over millions of years); combustion (fossil fuels and wood → air); dissolving (air ↔ oceans).
[bio34-s1.7.3] Prediction (a): cutting down and burning a forest removes photosynthesis and adds combustion and the decomposition of the remains, so atmospheric CO₂ rises.
[bio34-s1.7.4] Prediction (b): draining a bog lets air in, so aerobic decomposers break down the peat and release CO₂.
[bio34-s1.7.5] Prediction (c): if decomposers were killed (e.g. by soil made very acidic or polluted), dead matter would accumulate, minerals would not be returned to the soil, and plant growth would eventually fall.
[bio34-s1.7.6] Limitation: a diagram shows the processes but not how much carbon moves or how fast. It also treats each store as one box, although the oceans, soils and forests each hold carbon for very different lengths of time.
[bio34-s1.7.7] Limitation: it simplifies feedbacks, e.g. warming speeds up decomposition, which releases more CO₂. It is also a snapshot that hides seasonal and long-term change. There is more on this in Models.

CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio15-s1: Respiration as a catabolic process
[bio15-s1.0.b] Every living cell needs a constant supply of usable energy. Respiration is the process that gets it, by breaking down food molecules and transferring the energy they hold to ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio15-s1.0.0] Respiration is the controlled, enzyme-catalysed breakdown of food, mainly glucose, in every living cell. The energy released is transferred to ATP, so respiration releases energy rather than making it.
[bio15-s1.0.1] Respiration is catabolic, meaning a large molecule such as glucose is broken down into smaller molecules and energy is released. The products are CO₂ and H₂O, or lactic acid, or ethanol and CO₂, depending on the conditions.
[bio15-s1.0.2] Photosynthesis is the matching anabolic process, because it builds small molecules up into large ones and takes in energy, whereas respiration breaks large ones down.
[bio15-s1.0.3] The energy is released in many small enzyme-controlled steps rather than all at once, as in burning. This is why much of it can be trapped as ATP instead of being lost as heat. Energy not trapped as ATP is released as heat, which keeps birds and mammals warm.
[bio15-s1.0.4] The ATP made is used for cell activities such as muscle contraction, active transport (moving substances across a membrane using energy) and building large molecules.
[bio15-s1.0.5] Carbon release: the carbon atoms of glucose leave the cell as CO₂, returning to the atmosphere (or water) the carbon that photosynthesis fixed (built into sugar).
[bio15-s1.0.6] Respiration by plants, animals and decomposers, together with burning, balances the uptake of carbon by photosynthesis in the carbon cycle. There is more on this in Nutrient Cycling.
[bio15-s1.0.7] Glucose is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule that is broken down). Glycogen (in animals) and starch (in plants) are first broken down to glucose, and fats and, when needed, proteins can also be respired.
[bio15-s1.1.b] Aerobic (with oxygen) respiration breaks glucose down completely, releasing energy. Knowing where the glucose and oxygen come from, and where the carbon dioxide and water go, shows how organisms and their surroundings are linked.
[bio15-s1.1.0] Aerobic respiration uses oxygen and breaks glucose down completely to carbon dioxide and water. Word equation: glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water + energy.
[bio15-s1.1.1] Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Its chemicals are those of the photosynthesis equation reversed, but it releases energy (as ATP and heat) instead of taking in light energy.
[bio15-s1.1.2] Source of glucose in plants: photosynthesis, or stored starch and sucrose transported in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant). In animals: digested food absorbed into the blood, or glycogen stored in the liver and muscles.
[bio15-s1.1.3] Source of oxygen in humans: the air, taken in through the lungs and carried to the cells by red blood cells, which is how it reaches every cell.
[bio15-s1.1.4] Source of oxygen in plants: the air, entering through stomata (leaf pores) and lenticels (pores in woody stems); soil air, taken in by the roots; and the plant's own photosynthesis. In aquatic organisms it is oxygen dissolved in water.
[bio15-s1.1.5] Fate of carbon dioxide: it diffuses out of cells. In humans it is carried in the blood to the lungs and breathed out; in plants it diffuses out through stomata or, in the light, is used in photosynthesis.
[bio15-s1.1.6] Fate of water: it joins the cell's water and is used in the cell or excreted. Some is breathed out as water vapour.
[bio15-s1.2.b] When oxygen is scarce or absent, cells can still release some energy from glucose, but not nearly as much. The products depend on the organism.
[bio15-s1.2.0] Anaerobic respiration releases energy from glucose without oxygen. Because the glucose is only partly broken down, much of the energy stays in the products, so much less energy is released per glucose and much less ATP is made than in aerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.2.1] In animals, such as human muscle during intense exercise, and in some bacteria, the product is lactic acid. Word equation: glucose → lactic acid + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₃H₆O₃ + energy. No CO₂ is produced.
[bio15-s1.2.2] In yeast, and in plant cells short of oxygen such as roots in waterlogged soil, the products are ethanol and carbon dioxide. Word equation: glucose → ethanol + carbon dioxide + energy. Balanced equation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + energy.
[bio15-s1.2.3] Anaerobic respiration in microorganisms is called fermentation. In baking, yeast's CO₂ makes dough rise and the ethanol evaporates in the oven. In brewing and wine-making, yeast's ethanol is the useful product. In making yogurt and cheese, bacteria's lactic acid is the useful product.
[bio15-s1.2.4] In human muscle during hard exercise, the lungs and blood cannot supply oxygen fast enough, so some respiration is anaerobic. Lactic acid builds up and the muscles tire.
[bio15-s1.2.5] Oxygen debt: after exercise, extra oxygen is needed to remove the lactic acid. It is carried in the blood to the liver, where much of it is converted back to glucose, and the rest is respired aerobically. This is why breathing and heart rate stay high after exercise.
[bio15-s1.2.6] Obligate anaerobes respire only anaerobically and are poisoned by oxygen, for example the bacteria that cause tetanus. Yeast can respire either way, so it is a facultative anaerobe.
[bio15-s1.3.b] Respiration happens in two stages in different parts of the cell. Stage 1 is the same whether or not oxygen is present, and what happens next depends on oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.0] Stage 1, glycolysis, takes place in the cytoplasm (cytosol, the fluid inside the cell) of every cell and does not need oxygen. 1. Glucose, with 6 carbon atoms, is split into two molecules of pyruvate, each with 3 carbon atoms. 2. A small amount of ATP is made.
[bio15-s1.3.1] With oxygen, stage 2 takes place in the mitochondria (the organelles where aerobic respiration is completed). 3. Pyruvate enters a mitochondrion and is broken down completely to CO₂ and water.
[bio15-s1.3.2] 4. Hydrogen removed from it is passed to oxygen, forming water, and a large amount of ATP is made. Stage 2 needs oxygen.
[bio15-s1.3.3] Without oxygen, pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm and is converted to lactic acid (in animals and some bacteria) or to ethanol and CO₂ (in yeast and plants). No further ATP is made, so anaerobic respiration is glycolysis followed by this conversion, all in the cytoplasm.
[bio15-s1.3.4] Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no mitochondria, so their aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and on the cell membrane.
[bio15-s1.3.5] Diagram: flow diagram. Glucose (cytoplasm), then glycolysis (small ATP), then 2 pyruvate. Oxygen present: mitochondrion, then CO₂, water, large ATP. No oxygen: cytoplasm, then lactic acid (animals) or ethanol and CO₂ (yeast, plants). Label cytoplasm, mitochondrion, stage 1, stage 2.
[bio15-s1.4.b] Aerobic and anaerobic respiration start the same way but differ in how far glucose is broken down and how much ATP results. Respiration also contrasts with photosynthesis: respiration is catabolic and happens in all living cells, while photosynthesis is anabolic and happens only in cells with chloroplasts.
[bio15-s1.4.0] Oxygen: needed in aerobic respiration; not needed in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.1] Location: aerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm and the mitochondria; anaerobic respiration takes place in the cytoplasm only.
[bio15-s1.4.2] Breakdown of glucose: complete, to CO₂ and H₂O, in aerobic respiration; incomplete, to lactic acid or to ethanol and CO₂, in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.3] Energy: a large amount of ATP in aerobic respiration; a small amount, only the ATP made in glycolysis, in anaerobic respiration, because the glucose is only partly broken down.
[bio15-s1.4.4] Stages: stage 1 followed by stage 2 in aerobic respiration; stage 1 followed by conversion of pyruvate in anaerobic respiration.
[bio15-s1.4.5] Respiration is catabolic, releases energy, uses O₂ and releases CO₂. It happens in all living cells all the time, in the cytoplasm and mitochondria.
[bio15-s1.4.6] Photosynthesis is anabolic, takes in light energy, uses CO₂ and releases O₂. It happens only in cells with chloroplasts, and only in the light.

CANDIDATES bio31-s7: Energy and nutrient transfer
[bio31-s7.0.b] Every ecosystem sorts its organisms by how they get energy and nutrients. Producers bring energy in, consumers pass it along, and decomposers return the nutrients to the soil or water.
[bio31-s7.0.0] Producers are autotrophs: organisms that make their own food from simple inorganic substances, mostly by photosynthesis, using light energy. They include green plants, algae, phytoplankton (microscopic floating algae) and photosynthetic bacteria.
[bio31-s7.0.1] Producers are the entry point of energy and matter into every food chain, because no other group can turn light energy and inorganic substances into organic food. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio31-s7.0.2] Consumers are heterotrophs, meaning they cannot make their own food and obtain energy and nutrients by eating other organisms. Primary consumers (herbivores) eat producers, e.g. rabbits, slugs, caterpillars and limpets.
[bio31-s7.0.3] Secondary consumers eat primary consumers, e.g. thrushes, frogs and crabs. Tertiary consumers eat secondary consumers, e.g. sparrowhawks, foxes and herons.
[bio31-s7.0.4] A top (apex) consumer is at the end of a chain and is not normally eaten. Carnivores eat animals; omnivores eat plants and animals, e.g. badgers, foxes and humans.
[bio31-s7.0.5] Decomposers, mainly bacteria and fungi, feed on dead organisms and waste. They secrete enzymes onto the dead matter and absorb the products (saprophytic nutrition), and so release minerals and CO₂ for producers to reuse.
[bio31-s7.0.6] Detritivores are animals that eat dead organic matter (detritus), e.g. earthworms and woodlice. They break it into smaller pieces, which gives decomposers a larger surface to act on, so decomposition is faster. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio31-s7.1.b] A food chain shows who eats whom, and each step is a trophic (feeding) level. Following the chain shows how energy and nutrients are passed on from one organism to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.0] A trophic (feeding) level is the position an organism occupies in a food chain, according to how it obtains its energy. Producers are trophic level 1, primary consumers level 2, secondary consumers level 3 and tertiary consumers level 4.
[bio31-s7.1.1] A food chain is a sequence of organisms in which each is eaten by the next, showing the transfer of energy and nutrients from one trophic level to the next.
[bio31-s7.1.2] Arrows point from the organism that is eaten towards the organism that eats it, which is the direction of energy flow ("is eaten by").
[bio31-s7.1.3] Irish examples, one per ecosystem. Grassland: grass → rabbit → fox. Hedgerow or woodland: oak leaves → caterpillar → blue tit → sparrowhawk. Rocky shore: seaweed → periwinkle → crab → herring gull.
[bio31-s7.1.4] Two more Irish examples. Freshwater: phytoplankton → water flea → minnow → heron (or pike). Sea: phytoplankton → zooplankton → herring → seal.
[bio31-s7.1.5] Grazing food chains start with living producers. Detritus (decomposer) food chains start with dead organic matter, e.g. dead leaves → earthworm → blackbird. Much of the energy in a woodland passes through detritus chains.
[bio31-s7.1.6] Food chains rarely have more than four or five trophic levels, because so much energy is lost at each level that too little is left to support a further level.
[bio31-s7.2.b] Real organisms rarely eat just one thing or get eaten by just one thing. A food web joins the separate food chains of an ecosystem into one network, which is why it is a more realistic picture.
[bio31-s7.2.0] A food web is a set of interlinked food chains in an ecosystem. It is more realistic than a single chain because most organisms eat more than one type of food and are eaten by more than one predator.
[bio31-s7.2.1] An organism may also feed at more than one trophic level, as an omnivore does, or as a fox does when it eats rabbits and also beetles.
[bio31-s7.2.2] Example grassland and hedgerow web to draw: grass → rabbit, grass → slug and grass → caterpillar; grass seeds → wood mouse; slug → thrush and caterpillar → thrush; rabbit → fox; wood mouse → fox and wood mouse → barn owl; thrush → sparrowhawk.
[bio31-s7.2.3] Decomposers (bacteria, fungi and earthworms) feed on dead material from every level of this web, because every organism eventually dies or produces waste.
[bio31-s7.2.4] Food webs show interdependence: a change in one population affects many others, directly through feeding and indirectly through competition for the same food. For example, the fox and the barn owl both eat wood mice, so they compete.
[bio31-s7.2.5] Diagram: the food web drawn with arrows pointing towards the consumer, organisms placed in rows by trophic level, and decomposers shown at the side receiving arrows from all levels.
[bio31-s7.3.b] Energy enters an ecosystem once, as sunlight, and then dwindles as it is passed along a food chain. Most of it is lost at every step, and this explains both the shape of food chains and how we should feed people.
[bio31-s7.3.0] Energy enters ecosystems as light. Producers capture only a small fraction of the light that reaches them, around 1–2%, and store it as chemical energy in organic molecules. The rest is reflected, passes through leaves, or is lost as heat.
[bio31-s7.3.1] On average only about 10% of the energy in one trophic level is passed on to, and stored in, the next. The figure varies, roughly from 5% to 20%, so about 90% is lost at each transfer.
[bio31-s7.3.2] Respiration releases energy for movement, growth, active transport and keeping warm, and this ends up as heat lost to the surroundings. Birds and mammals lose the most, because they keep a constant body temperature.
[bio31-s7.3.3] Roots, bark, wood, bones and fur are left uneaten. Undigested food is egested as faeces, e.g. cellulose eaten by herbivores, and some energy is lost in excretory products such as urea. The energy in uneaten parts, faeces and dead bodies passes to decomposers, not to the next level.
[bio31-s7.3.4] Energy flows in one direction through an ecosystem and is not recycled: it enters as light and leaves as heat, so an ecosystem needs a continuous input of energy from the Sun.
[bio31-s7.3.5] Worked example (illustrative, using the 10% figure, not measured data): grass in a field stores 10 000 kJ, the rabbits that eat it store about 1 000 kJ, and the foxes about 100 kJ. A fourth level would store about 10 kJ, too little to support a population.
[bio31-s7.3.6] So there are fewer top predators, they often need large territories, and they are especially vulnerable to habitat loss.
[bio31-s7.3.7] Consequence for food production: eating plants directly, a shorter chain, uses the energy captured by crops more efficiently than feeding crops to animals and then eating the animals. A given area of land can therefore feed more people on plant-based food.
[bio31-s7.4.b] Matter, unlike energy, passes along a food chain in the form of the food organisms eat. Some is lost at each level, but the ecosystem as a whole does not lose it, because decomposers send it back to producers.
[bio31-s7.4.0] Nutrients (matter) pass between trophic levels when one organism eats another. The carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids of the food are digested, absorbed and built into the consumer's own body (assimilated).
[bio31-s7.4.1] The elements involved are carbon, hydrogen and oxygen (in all biomolecules), nitrogen (in proteins and nucleic acids), phosphorus (in DNA, ATP and phospholipids), and minerals such as calcium and iron.
[bio31-s7.4.2] The amount of matter passed on also decreases at each level. Much of the food eaten is respired (carbon leaves as CO₂, hydrogen as water), egested in faeces, or excreted (nitrogen as urea or ammonia). So biomass (the mass of living material) also decreases up a food chain.
[bio31-s7.4.3] Unlike energy, matter is not lost from the ecosystem. Decomposers break down dead bodies, faeces and urine, releasing CO₂ and minerals such as nitrate, phosphate and ammonium that producers absorb and reuse, so nutrients cycle.
[bio31-s7.4.4] Contrast: energy flows one way through the ecosystem and is lost as heat, whereas nutrients cycle and are reused again and again.
[bio31-s7.4.5] Persistent substances that are not broken down or excreted, such as the insecticide DDT and mercury, pass up the food chain with the food and build up in the bodies of organisms at each level. They reach the highest concentrations in top consumers, which is called biomagnification.
[bio31-s7.4.6] Biomagnification is how DDT thinned the eggshells of birds of prey, which sit at the top of food chains and so carry the highest doses.
[bio31-s7.4.7] Diagram: a flow diagram of one food chain, with energy arrows getting narrower at each level, heat-loss arrows from every level, and a separate loop of nutrient arrows from all levels to decomposers and back to producers.
[bio31-s7.5.b] A food chain or web is a model: a simplified picture that lets you work out what happens when one population goes up or down. Following a set method keeps the prediction logical.
[bio31-s7.5.0] The method has five steps. 1. Find the organisms that eat the changed species (its predators).
[bio31-s7.5.1] 2. Find what the changed species eats (its food).
[bio31-s7.5.2] 3. Find its competitors, the species that share its food.
[bio31-s7.5.3] 4. Predict the direction of each change, giving reasons.
[bio31-s7.5.4] 5. Note other effects, e.g. on plants and habitat.
[bio31-s7.5.5] Worked prediction using the food web above, if a disease such as myxomatosis (a viral disease of rabbits) kills most of the rabbits: (a) grass is grazed less and grows taller, providing more food and cover for slugs, caterpillars and wood mice.
[bio31-s7.5.6] Continued: (b) foxes lose their main prey, so they eat more wood mice and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds, and fox numbers may fall. (c) Wood mice decline from the extra fox predation, so barn owls, which compete with foxes for wood mice, have less food.
[bio31-s7.5.7] Real outcomes depend on how flexible each predator's diet is, because a predator that can switch to other food will be affected less.
[bio31-s7.6.b] Changes spread through a web both when a species is removed and when one is added. Because the model is simplified, its predictions have limits.
[bio31-s7.6.0] Removing a top predator can cause a trophic cascade, a chain of effects passing down the food web. In Ireland, with no wolves, deer numbers increase, they browse young trees, and the woodland cannot regenerate, which affects every species that depends on the woodland.
[bio31-s7.6.1] Adding a species also spreads through a web. An invasive filter-feeder such as the zebra mussel removes phytoplankton at the base of a lake food web, reducing food for zooplankton and for the fish that eat them. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio31-s7.6.2] Food chains and webs simplify real feeding, because many links are left out and diets change with the season and with age.
[bio31-s7.6.3] They usually show no numbers or amounts of energy, which pyramids do, and decomposers are often left out. There is more on models in general in Models.
