TARGET bio16-s3: DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle
[bio16-s3.0.b] Before a cell divides, it has to copy its DNA, the molecule that carries its genetic instructions. This copying is called DNA replication, and it is what lets one cell become two with the same instructions.
[bio16-s3.0.0] DNA replication is the process by which a DNA molecule makes an exact copy of itself, giving two identical DNA molecules.
[bio16-s3.0.1] In eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus) it happens in the nucleus during interphase, the growing phase before the cell divides. It is the DNA-synthesis part of the cell cycle, the repeating sequence of events in a dividing cell.
[bio16-s3.0.2] Every chromosome in the nucleus is copied once, and only once, per cell cycle, so the DNA is doubled exactly one time before each division.
[bio16-s3.0.3] Replication is needed because a cell that divides must give each daughter cell a complete and identical set of genetic instructions. Without replication each daughter cell would receive only half the DNA.
[bio16-s3.1.b] A DNA molecule is a double helix (a twisted ladder) made of two strands held together by pairs of bases. Replication works by pulling the two strands apart and using each one as a pattern for a new partner strand. Follow the steps in order.
[bio16-s3.1.0] 1. Enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions) unwind the double helix and separate, or unzip, the two strands by breaking the weak hydrogen bonds between the base pairs. The strong bonds of each sugar–phosphate backbone are not broken, so each strand stays intact.
[bio16-s3.1.1] 2. Each separated single strand acts as a template (pattern) for building a new strand, because its exposed bases decide which nucleotides can attach.
[bio16-s3.1.2] 3. Free DNA nucleotides in the nucleus line up against the exposed bases of each template strand by complementary base pairing: A pairs with T, and C pairs with G.
[bio16-s3.1.3] 4. Enzymes join the new nucleotides to each other to form a new sugar–phosphate backbone, making a complementary strand; hydrogen bonds form between the new and old bases.
[bio16-s3.1.4] 5. Each new DNA molecule winds back into a double helix. The result is two DNA molecules, each made of one original (parent) strand and one new strand.
[bio16-s3.1.5] This is why replication is described as semi-conservative: half of each new molecule is conserved from the parent. There is more on the evidence for this in Modelling DNA replication and information flow.
[bio16-s3.2.b] Replication has to be accurate, because any error is passed on to every daughter cell. Fixed base pairing and checking enzymes are what keep the copies exact.
[bio16-s3.2.0] Because base pairing is fixed, each new strand is an exact complement of its template, so both new molecules have the same base sequence as the original.
[bio16-s3.2.1] For example, a section of DNA with one strand ATTGCA and the other strand TAACGT separates: ATTGCA is a template for a new TAACGT, and TAACGT is a template for a new ATTGCA, giving two molecules identical to the original.
[bio16-s3.2.2] Enzymes make replication fast and accurate; they also check the new strands and correct most mistakes. A rare uncorrected mistake changes the base sequence, which is a mutation (a change in the DNA).
[bio16-s3.2.3] Diagram: a short section of DNA (about four base pairs) as a ladder: parent molecule, strands separating with free nucleotides pairing, two daughter molecules each with one old strand (dark) and one new strand (light). Label parent strand, new strand, hydrogen bonds broken, free nucleotide, complementary base pair, enzyme.
[bio16-s3.3.b] Replication copies the DNA, but copying alone does not make two cells. Mitosis (the division of the nucleus) and cytokinesis (the division of the rest of the cell) then share the copies out, so each new cell ends up with a full set.
[bio16-s3.3.0] Replication in interphase doubles the DNA. Afterwards each chromosome consists of two identical copies joined together, so the cell temporarily has twice the normal amount of DNA. A human cell still has 46 chromosomes, but each one is double.
[bio16-s3.3.1] Mitosis then separates the two copies of every chromosome, sending one copy to each end of the cell, so each daughter nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome: 46 chromosomes in each human daughter cell, with the same number and the same genes as the parent.
[bio16-s3.3.2] Cytokinesis then divides the cell, so the amount of DNA in each daughter cell returns to the amount in the parent cell before replication. Each daughter cell can then grow and repeat the cycle.
[bio16-s3.3.3] Replication and mitosis together keep the genetic information the same in every cell of a multicellular organism, and pass identical copies to offspring in asexual reproduction. There is more on these roles in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s3.3.4] Order matters: replication must be complete before mitosis begins, which the cell checks, and it happens only once per cycle. Otherwise daughter cells would get missing or extra DNA. When this control fails, cells can be damaged or become cancerous.
[bio16-s3.3.5] Diagram: a graph of DNA per cell (y-axis, arbitrary units) against time (x-axis) over two cell cycles. DNA is steady at one level in early interphase, rises to double during replication, stays double until the end of mitosis, then drops back at cytokinesis. Label interphase, DNA replication, mitosis, cytokinesis.

CANDIDATES bio16-s1: The cell cycle
[bio16-s1.0.b] Before you can follow the cell cycle you need to know what is being copied and shared out: the chromosomes. How many a cell has, and how they are arranged, decides whether it is diploid or haploid.
[bio16-s1.0.0] A chromosome is one long DNA molecule wound around proteins, carrying many genes (the units of inheritance). There is more on this in Chromosome structure and genes.
[bio16-s1.0.1] Between divisions the chromosomes are long, thin and spread out as chromatin (DNA with its proteins). Before division they coil up (condense) and become short and thick, so they can be seen with the light microscope when stained.
[bio16-s1.0.2] Homologous chromosomes are a pair of chromosomes of the same size and shape, with the same genes at the same positions, one inherited from each parent; the alleles (versions of a gene) on them may differ. Human body cells contain 23 such pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.3] Diploid (2n): a cell or organism with two sets of chromosomes, so that its chromosomes lie in homologous pairs. Human body (somatic) cells are diploid, 2n = 46. The 46 counts chromosomes, not pairs.
[bio16-s1.0.4] Haploid (n): a cell with one set of chromosomes, one chromosome from each homologous pair. Human gametes (sperm and egg cells) are haploid, n = 23. Haploid means one complete set, not half a chromosome.
[bio16-s1.0.5] When two haploid gametes fuse at fertilisation, the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) is diploid again (23 + 23 = 46), so the chromosome number stays the same from generation to generation.
[bio16-s1.0.6] Haploid cells are made by meiosis, a division that halves the chromosome number; mitosis keeps the chromosome number the same. There is more on this in Mitosis and meiosis.
[bio16-s1.0.7] Each species has its own chromosome number: human 2n = 46, the fruit fly Drosophila 2n = 8, the garden pea 2n = 14. The number does not reflect how complex an organism is.
[bio16-s1.1.b] Cells don't just divide and stop: each one lives through a repeating sequence of events called the cell cycle. It shows how a cell prepares for division and then carries it out.
[bio16-s1.1.0] The cell cycle is the sequence of events in the life of a cell, from the time it is formed by division until it divides itself into two daughter cells.
[bio16-s1.1.1] It has three main activities, in this order. First, growth: the cell makes new proteins and organelles (the working parts of a cell) and gets bigger.
[bio16-s1.1.2] Second, DNA synthesis: every chromosome is copied, so the cell has two copies of its genetic information to hand out.
[bio16-s1.1.3] Third, division: the nucleus divides so that each new nucleus gets one copy of every chromosome, then the cytoplasm divides to give two cells.
[bio16-s1.1.4] The phase between divisions, when growth and DNA synthesis happen, is interphase. A cell spends most of its life in interphase, about 90% of the cycle, so division is short.
[bio16-s1.1.5] In many human cells grown in the laboratory, one cycle takes about 24 hours.
[bio16-s1.1.6] Interphase is not a resting stage. The cell is very active, carrying out its normal functions as well as preparing to divide.
[bio16-s1.1.7] One cycle, including division, produces two daughter cells, each with the same number and type of chromosomes as the parent cell and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s1.2.b] Not every cell divides at the same pace, and no cell divides without being told it is safe to. Whether a cell cycles depends on its job and on control checks built into the cycle.
[bio16-s1.2.0] Some cells leave the cycle once they are specialised and stop dividing, for example most mature nerve cells. Mature red blood cells have no nucleus and can't divide.
[bio16-s1.2.1] Other cells divide often throughout life, for example cells of the skin, the lining of the gut and the bone marrow, and in plants the cells at root and shoot tips (meristems, regions where growth happens).
[bio16-s1.2.2] The cycle is controlled. Proteins inside the cell check at several points (checkpoints) that the cell is large enough, that the DNA has been copied correctly and is undamaged, and that the chromosomes are attached correctly before the cell moves on.
[bio16-s1.2.3] Signals from other cells (growth factors, chemical messages) tell a cell whether to divide. There is more on how loss of this control leads to cancer in Cancer.
[bio16-s1.2.4] Diagram: the cell cycle as a circle. Large sector: interphase (cell growth and DNA replication). Small sector: division (mitosis, then cytokinesis). Arrow for direction. Higher Level: also label G1, S, G2 and the four stages of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.3.b] Interphase is long and busy, and it has three parts: G1, S and G2. Together they prepare the cell so that division can be quick and accurate.
[bio16-s1.3.0] G1 (first growth phase): the cell grows, makes proteins (including the enzymes needed to copy DNA) and organelles, and carries out its normal job.
[bio16-s1.3.1] S (synthesis) phase: DNA replication, so each chromosome now consists of two identical copies called sister chromatids, joined at the centromere (the narrow point where they are held together).
[bio16-s1.3.2] G2 (second growth phase): further growth and more organelles, for example mitochondria. The proteins of the spindle (the fibres that later pull chromosomes apart) are made, and ATP is stored for division.
[bio16-s1.3.3] A cell that stops dividing leaves the cycle from G1 into a non-dividing state (sometimes called G0), for example mature nerve cells.
[bio16-s1.3.4] Throughout interphase the nuclear membrane (envelope) is intact, the nucleolus is visible and the chromosomes are uncoiled as chromatin, so individual chromosomes can't be seen.
[bio16-s1.4.b] Mitosis is the division of the nucleus. It runs as one smooth process, but it is split into four stages to make it easier to describe, and each stage has a characteristic look and job.
[bio16-s1.4.0] Nuclear division (mitosis) is a continuous process, divided for convenience into four stages in this order: prophase, metaphase, anaphase and telophase. It is followed by cytokinesis, which is division of the cytoplasm and not a stage of mitosis.
[bio16-s1.4.1] Prophase: the chromosomes condense (shorten and thicken) and become visible, each as two sister chromatids joined at a centromere. The nucleolus disappears, the nuclear membrane breaks down, and spindle fibres (protein tubes called microtubules) begin to form.
[bio16-s1.4.2] In animal cells, two centrioles move to opposite ends (poles) of the cell and the spindle forms between them. Plant cells have no centrioles but still form a spindle.
[bio16-s1.4.3] Metaphase: the chromosomes line up singly on the equator (middle) of the spindle; each is attached by its centromere to spindle fibres from both poles.
[bio16-s1.4.4] Anaphase: the centromeres split and the spindle fibres shorten, pulling the sister chromatids apart to opposite poles, centromere first, which gives a V shape.
[bio16-s1.4.5] Once separated, each chromatid is called a chromosome, so each pole now has a full set of identical chromosomes. Energy for the movement comes from ATP, the cell's energy carrier.
[bio16-s1.4.6] Telophase: the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil back into chromatin; a nuclear membrane forms around each set; the nucleoli reappear; the spindle breaks down. There are now two nuclei, each genetically identical to the original.
[bio16-s1.4.7] Diagram: the four stages of mitosis in an animal cell, 2n = 4 (one long and one short homologous pair, shaded to show the two parents). Labels: chromosome, sister chromatids, centromere, spindle fibres, centriole, pole, equator, nuclear membrane (breaking down, re-forming), cell membrane, cleavage furrow (end of telophase).
[bio16-s1.5.b] You can tell which stage a cell is in just by looking at how its chromosomes are arranged under the microscope. Counting dividing cells then tells you how fast a tissue is dividing.
[bio16-s1.5.0] In stained cells from a growing root tip (for example onion or garlic) viewed at ×400, most cells are in interphase, seen as a nucleus with no visible chromosomes, because interphase is the longest part of the cycle.
[bio16-s1.5.1] Dividing cells are identified by the arrangement of their chromosomes: condensed and scattered for prophase; in a line across the middle for metaphase.
[bio16-s1.5.2] Two groups moving apart is anaphase; two groups at opposite ends, with new nuclei forming, is telophase.
[bio16-s1.5.3] The mitotic index is the percentage of cells in a sample that are dividing. For example, 24 dividing cells out of 200 gives (24 ÷ 200) × 100 = 12%.
[bio16-s1.5.4] A high mitotic index shows a tissue that is dividing rapidly, such as a root tip or a tumour.
[bio16-s1.6.b] After the nucleus has divided, the cell still has to split in two. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm, and plant and animal cells do it differently because plant cells have a rigid wall.
[bio16-s1.6.0] Cytokinesis is the division of the cytoplasm to form two separate daughter cells. It usually starts during late telophase. Organelles such as mitochondria are shared between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.1] In animal cells the cell membrane is pulled inwards around the middle of the cell, forming a groove (cleavage furrow) that deepens until the cell pinches into two.
[bio16-s1.6.2] In plant cells the rigid cell wall can't pinch in. Instead, vesicles (small membrane sacs) from the Golgi apparatus collect across the middle of the cell and fuse to form a cell plate.
[bio16-s1.6.3] The cell plate grows outwards to the sides and becomes the new cell membranes and cell wall between the two cells.
[bio16-s1.6.4] Diagram: an animal cell with a cleavage furrow beside a plant cell with a cell plate forming, each labelled.

CANDIDATES bio16-s2: Mitosis and meiosis
[bio16-s2.0.b] When a cell divides, its genetic information has to go somewhere. In mitosis the information is passed on unchanged, so the new cells are exact genetic copies of the cell they came from.
[bio16-s2.0.0] Mitosis is nuclear division (division of the nucleus, which holds the chromosomes) that produces two nuclei, each with the same number of chromosomes as the parent nucleus and genetically identical to it.
[bio16-s2.0.1] A diploid cell (one with two sets of chromosomes) gives two diploid cells (2n to 2n). A haploid cell (one with a single set), in organisms that have haploid stages, gives haploid cells (n to n).
[bio16-s2.0.2] Every chromosome is copied exactly before mitosis. During mitosis the two identical copies of each chromosome are separated, so each new nucleus receives one copy of every chromosome. This is one division giving two daughter cells.
[bio16-s2.0.3] Homologous chromosomes (the matching pair, one from each parent) do not pair up in mitosis. There is more on this in 'DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle'.
[bio16-s2.0.4] Cells produced by mitosis from one parent cell form a clone: a group of genetically identical cells or organisms.
[bio16-s2.0.5] Mitosis on its own produces no genetic variation, because the information is copied exactly. The only source of difference is a rare mistake in copying, called a mutation.
[bio16-s2.1.b] Sexual reproduction needs cells with half the usual number of chromosomes, and it also produces variety. Meiosis does both: it halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genetic information, so no two cells are alike.
[bio16-s2.1.0] Meiosis is nuclear division that halves the chromosome number, producing four haploid nuclei from one diploid nucleus (2n to n). It involves two divisions, one after the other, after the DNA has been copied once.
[bio16-s2.1.1] In the first division, homologous chromosomes pair up and then separate, one of each pair going to each new cell, so each cell receives one complete set. In the second division the two copies of each chromosome separate.
[bio16-s2.1.2] The four cells differ from each other and from the parent because the homologous pairs line up and separate independently of one another (random, or independent, assortment). Each gamete (sex cell) therefore receives a random mixture of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
[bio16-s2.1.3] With 23 pairs, one person can make 2²³ (more than 8 million) different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes. This is the basis of Mendel's Law of Independent Assortment. There is more on this in 'Mendel's Laws'.
[bio16-s2.1.4] Homologous chromosomes can also exchange matching segments during the first division (crossing over), mixing their alleles (versions of a gene) further.
[bio16-s2.1.5] Random fertilisation adds further variation, since any one of a huge number of different sperm may fuse with any one of a huge number of different eggs. So offspring of sexual reproduction differ from their parents and from each other, except identical twins. Variation from sexual reproduction and its role in evolution are in 'Variation'.
[bio16-s2.1.6] Meiosis keeps the chromosome number constant across generations: gametes are haploid, so the zygote (the cell formed at fertilisation) is diploid (n + n = 2n). Without meiosis the number would double every generation.
[bio16-s2.2.b] A unicellular organism is a single cell, so when it divides it is reproducing. Mitosis gives it a fast way to reproduce, and meiosis gives some of them a way to add variation.
[bio16-s2.2.0] In unicellular eukaryotes (organisms made of one cell with a nucleus) such as Amoeba and yeast, mitosis followed by division of the cell is asexual reproduction: one organism becomes two genetically identical organisms. In yeast this happens by budding.
[bio16-s2.2.1] Asexual reproduction is fast, needs only one parent and passes on a successful set of genes unchanged, so a population can grow quickly when conditions are good.
[bio16-s2.2.2] The drawback is that all the offspring are alike, so one change in conditions, such as a new disease, can affect them all.
[bio16-s2.2.3] Some unicellular eukaryotes also reproduce sexually, using meiosis to produce varied haploid cells, often when conditions become poor. For example, starved diploid yeast cells undergo meiosis to form four haploid spores, which can survive harsh conditions.
[bio16-s2.2.4] The variation from meiosis makes it more likely that some offspring survive a changed environment.
[bio16-s2.2.5] Prokaryotes (bacteria, which have no nucleus) have neither mitosis nor meiosis. They copy their single circular chromosome and divide by binary fission. There is more on this in 'Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells'.
[bio16-s2.3.b] A multicellular organism uses mitosis to build and maintain its body, and meiosis only to make cells for sexual reproduction. Keeping these jobs separate matters for what offspring inherit.
[bio16-s2.3.0] Mitosis for growth: a multicellular organism develops from a single cell, the zygote, by repeated mitosis, so every body cell has the same genetic information as the zygote.
[bio16-s2.3.1] In plants, growth by mitosis is concentrated at the tips of roots and shoots (regions called meristems).
[bio16-s2.3.2] Mitosis for repair and replacement: cells that wear out or are damaged are replaced, for example when a cut heals, when skin cells and the cells lining the gut are replaced, and when new blood cells are made in the bone marrow (stem cells there divide by mitosis).
[bio16-s2.3.3] Mitosis for asexual reproduction: some multicellular organisms, including many plants, also reproduce asexually by mitosis, producing clones of the parent. Methods of asexual reproduction in plants are not on the specification.
[bio16-s2.3.4] Meiosis for sexual reproduction takes place only in the reproductive organs. In humans it happens in the testes (forming sperm) and the ovaries (forming eggs). There is more on how human gametes form in 'Gametes and fertilisation'. In flowering plants it happens in the anthers and the ovules, leading to the pollen grains and the egg cell.
[bio16-s2.3.5] Because body cells are made by mitosis and gametes by meiosis, a change to the DNA of a body cell, such as a skin cell, is not passed to offspring. A change in a cell that gives rise to gametes can be.
[bio16-s2.3.6] There is more on this in 'Mutations', which is Higher Level only.
[bio16-s2.4.b] Both divisions share machinery but do different jobs. Comparing them point by point shows how mitosis keeps information the same and meiosis varies it.
[bio16-s2.4.0] Similarities: both are nuclear divisions of eukaryotic cells; both follow DNA replication (copying of the DNA) in interphase (the period between divisions); both use a spindle (a structure of fibres) to separate chromosomes; both are followed by cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm).
[bio16-s2.4.1] Number of divisions and cells: mitosis has one division and gives two cells; meiosis has two divisions and gives four cells.
[bio16-s2.4.2] Chromosome number: mitosis keeps it the same (2n to 2n); meiosis halves it (2n to n), so its cells are haploid.
[bio16-s2.4.3] Genetic make-up of the cells produced: mitosis gives cells identical to each other and to the parent; meiosis gives cells that differ from each other and from the parent. Homologous chromosomes do not pair in mitosis; in meiosis they pair and then separate.
[bio16-s2.4.4] Where it happens: mitosis in body cells throughout the organism; meiosis only in the reproductive organs (testes, ovaries, anthers and ovules).
[bio16-s2.4.5] Role of meiosis: it produces gametes or spores for sexual reproduction, giving variation and keeping the chromosome number constant across generations.
[bio16-s2.4.6] Role in unicellular and multicellular organisms: mitosis serves growth, repair, replacement and asexual reproduction in both.
[bio16-s2.4.7] Diagram: two flow diagrams for a cell with 2n = 4 (one long, one short homologous pair, parents' chromosomes shaded differently). Mitosis: two cells, 2n = 4, same shading as the parent. Meiosis: four cells, n = 2, different shadings. Label diploid, haploid, homologous pair.

CANDIDATES bio10-s1: Nucleotides, DNA and RNA
[bio10-s1.0.b] Cells need a way to store instructions for building and running themselves. They use nucleic acids, long molecules made by joining many small units, and the unit that is repeated is the nucleotide. There are four kinds of nucleotide, which differ in their base.
[bio10-s1.0.0] Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are large molecules (polymers) built from many small units called nucleotides joined in a chain, much as beads are threaded on a string.
[bio10-s1.0.1] DNA is deoxyribonucleic acid; RNA is ribonucleic acid. Both contain the elements carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen and phosphorus.
[bio10-s1.0.2] Each nucleotide has three parts: a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, a phosphate group, and a nitrogenous base (a nitrogen-containing molecule) attached to the sugar.
[bio10-s1.0.3] The sugar is deoxyribose in DNA and ribose in RNA. Deoxyribose has one fewer oxygen atom than ribose, which is where its name comes from: deoxy means an oxygen has been removed.
[bio10-s1.0.4] Each nucleic acid has four bases. DNA: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). RNA: adenine (A), uracil (U), cytosine (C) and guanine (G); uracil takes the place of thymine.
[bio10-s1.0.5] Adenine and guanine are larger, double-ringed bases, called purines; cytosine, thymine and uracil are smaller, single-ringed bases, called pyrimidines.
[bio10-s1.0.6] A nucleotide is the building block of DNA and RNA, and its base is one letter of the genetic information. The order, or sequence, of bases along a strand carries the information, just as the order of letters makes a word.
[bio10-s1.0.7] Modified nucleotides also carry energy. There is more on this in ATP.
[bio10-s1.0.8] Diagram: one nucleotide drawn as a circle (phosphate) joined to a pentagon (sugar) joined to a rectangle (base), labelled phosphate, deoxyribose or ribose, and nitrogenous base.
[bio10-s1.1.b] One nucleotide holds one letter of information, so to store a whole message the cell must link many of them together. The way they link leaves a strong chain with the bases free to carry the code.
[bio10-s1.1.0] Nucleotides join by bonds between the sugar of one nucleotide and the phosphate of the next. These bonds are made in condensation reactions, in which a molecule of water is removed for each bond.
[bio10-s1.1.1] The repeating sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate chain is the sugar–phosphate backbone. The bonds within it are strong covalent bonds, not hydrogen bonds.
[bio10-s1.1.2] The bases stick out from the backbone, one per sugar. The backbone is the same in every DNA molecule; only the sequence of bases differs, so the bases carry the information and the backbone gives strength and support.
[bio10-s1.2.b] DNA is not one chain but two, held together in a regular pattern. This shape explains how DNA can store information and be copied accurately.
[bio10-s1.2.0] DNA is double-stranded: two strands of nucleotides lie side by side, with the sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside and the bases pointing inwards, like the rails and rungs of a ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.1] The bases pair up: adenine always pairs with thymine (A–T) and cytosine always pairs with guanine (C–G). These are complementary base pairs.
[bio10-s1.2.2] Each pair is one purine with one pyrimidine, so every rung is the same width and the ladder stays an even shape.
[bio10-s1.2.3] The bases in a pair are held together by hydrogen bonds (weak attractions between molecules): two between A and T, and three between C and G.
[bio10-s1.2.4] Each hydrogen bond is weak, so the strands can be separated, or unzipped, when DNA is copied or read. There are millions of hydrogen bonds, so the molecule as a whole is stable.
[bio10-s1.2.5] The two linked strands are twisted around each other into a spiral called a double helix, like a twisted ladder.
[bio10-s1.2.6] Because pairing is fixed, the two strands are complementary: the sequence of one strand decides the sequence of the other. For example, the strand ATGCCTAG pairs with TACGGATC.
[bio10-s1.2.7] Diagram: a short section of DNA drawn as a straight ladder of about four base pairs, labelled sugar (deoxyribose), phosphate, sugar–phosphate backbone, base pairs A–T and C–G, and hydrogen bonds; with a sketch of the twisted double helix.
[bio10-s1.3.b] Because A always pairs with T and C always pairs with G, the amounts of the bases in double-stranded DNA are linked. This lets you work out the percentage of every base from just one.
[bio10-s1.3.0] In double-stranded DNA the amount of A equals the amount of T, and the amount of C equals the amount of G, because every A is paired with a T and every C with a G.
[bio10-s1.3.1] For example, if 20% of the bases in a DNA sample are adenine, then 20% are thymine. That leaves 60% shared equally between cytosine and guanine, so each is 30%.
[bio10-s1.4.b] The order of bases in DNA is the cell's set of instructions. DNA is kept in different places in different kinds of cell.
[bio10-s1.4.0] DNA stores the genetic information (genes) that controls the cell's activities by coding for proteins. There is more on genes and proteins in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio10-s1.4.1] DNA is copied before a cell divides, so each new cell receives the full set of information, which means that it is passed from parents to offspring.
[bio10-s1.4.2] In eukaryotic cells (cells with a nucleus), DNA is found mostly in the nucleus, in the chromosomes, with small amounts in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
[bio10-s1.4.3] In bacteria, DNA forms a loop in the cytoplasm and is also found in small rings called plasmids.
[bio10-s1.5.b] The structure of DNA was worked out by combining physical evidence with model building. It is a good example of how scientists build on each other's data.
[bio10-s1.5.0] The double-helix model was published by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953. It was based on X-ray diffraction images of DNA (patterns made when X-rays pass through DNA, which reveal its shape) made by Rosalind Franklin, working with Maurice Wilkins.
[bio10-s1.6.b] DNA stays safe in the nucleus, so the cell needs a working copy of its instructions to take out and use. That working copy is RNA, a similar molecule with a few key differences.
[bio10-s1.6.0] RNA is usually single-stranded: one chain of nucleotides with its own sugar–phosphate backbone. RNA molecules are much shorter than DNA molecules.
[bio10-s1.6.1] RNA nucleotides contain ribose instead of deoxyribose, and uracil instead of thymine. Uracil pairs with adenine (A–U) whenever RNA pairs with DNA or with another RNA.
[bio10-s1.6.2] A single strand can fold back on itself where its bases pair up, giving some RNA molecules a fixed shape.
[bio10-s1.6.3] RNA uses the information in DNA to make proteins, so it acts as the go-between for DNA and the protein-making machinery.
[bio10-s1.6.4] Three main types of RNA are named here, with no more detail. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries a copy of a gene from the nucleus to a ribosome (the structure that makes proteins).
[bio10-s1.6.5] Transfer RNA (tRNA) brings amino acids to the ribosome; ribosomal RNA (rRNA) forms part of the ribosome. There is more on this in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s1.6.6] RNA is made in the nucleus and works in the cytoplasm, so it carries information out to where proteins are made. It is found in the nucleus (including the nucleolus), the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.6.7] Diagram: a short single RNA strand of about four nucleotides, labelled ribose, phosphate, backbone and bases, including uracil.
[bio10-s1.7.b] Put side by side, DNA and RNA differ in structure, stability and job. The differences fit their roles: DNA is a long-term store and RNA is a short-lived working copy.
[bio10-s1.7.0] Strands: DNA is a double-stranded helix, which suits long-term storage; RNA is usually single-stranded.
[bio10-s1.7.1] Sugar: DNA contains deoxyribose; RNA contains ribose.
[bio10-s1.7.2] Bases: DNA has A, T, C and G; RNA has A, U, C and G, with uracil instead of thymine.
[bio10-s1.7.3] Pairing: A–T in DNA; A–U in RNA.
[bio10-s1.7.4] Size and life span: DNA is very long and stable and is kept for the life of the cell; RNA molecules are shorter and are broken down after use, so each copy is only a temporary message.
[bio10-s1.7.5] Location: DNA is mainly in the nucleus, with some in mitochondria and chloroplasts; RNA is in the nucleus, the cytoplasm and the ribosomes.
[bio10-s1.7.6] Function: DNA stores the genetic information; RNA carries and uses that information to make proteins.

CANDIDATES bio17-s1: Modelling DNA replication and information flow
[bio17-s1.0.b] Before a cell divides it must copy all of its DNA, a process called replication, so that each new cell gets a complete set of genetic instructions. Knowing where, when and how fast this happens is the starting point for the model.
[bio17-s1.0.0] Replication takes place in the nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) during the S phase of interphase, the stage of the cell cycle between divisions when the cell copies its DNA. There is more on this in The cell cycle.
[bio17-s1.0.1] In eukaryotes (cells with a nucleus) replication begins at many points along each chromosome at once, so the very long DNA molecules are copied quickly.
[bio17-s1.0.2] Bacteria start replication at one point on their circular chromosome, because their DNA is a single, much smaller loop.
[bio17-s1.0.3] Replication needs energy, which is supplied by ATP (the cell's energy-carrying molecule) and by the nucleotides themselves as they are added.
[bio17-s1.1.b] Replication uses each old strand of DNA as a pattern for a new one, and two key enzymes (proteins that speed up a chemical reaction) do the main jobs. Learn the stages in order and name the enzyme that does each job.
[bio17-s1.1.0] 1. The enzyme DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and separates the two strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds (the weak links) between complementary bases. This opens a Y-shaped replication fork where copying happens.
[bio17-s1.1.1] 2. Each exposed strand acts as a template, a pattern to copy. Free DNA nucleotides, which are made in the cell and are present in the nucleus, pair with the exposed bases by complementary base pairing (A–T, C–G).
[bio17-s1.1.2] 3. The enzyme DNA polymerase joins each new nucleotide to the growing strand, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone of the new strand. It adds nucleotides only where they pair correctly with the template, so the copy matches the original.
[bio17-s1.1.3] 4. DNA polymerase also proofreads: it checks each new base pair and removes and replaces most wrongly paired nucleotides. Other repair enzymes correct many of the rest.
[bio17-s1.1.4] A mistake that is not corrected becomes a permanent change in the base sequence, a point mutation (a change to one base in the DNA). There is more on this in Mutations.
[bio17-s1.1.5] 5. Each new DNA molecule, made of one template strand plus its new complementary strand, twists into a double helix. The two new molecules are identical to each other and to the original.
[bio17-s1.1.6] The two new molecules remain joined as two sister chromatids (identical copies of a chromosome) at a centromere, the narrow point where they are held together, until they separate at anaphase of mitosis.
[bio17-s1.1.7] Diagram: a replication fork, labelled: parent (original) DNA double helix, DNA helicase at the fork, hydrogen bonds being broken, the two template strands, free nucleotides, DNA polymerase adding nucleotides, the new complementary strands, and the two daughter molecules, each with one old strand (dark) and one new strand (light).
[bio17-s1.2.b] Once the base-pairing structure of DNA was known, there was more than one way the copying could work. Scientists had to decide between three models, and the one that proved correct is called semi-conservative.
[bio17-s1.2.0] Semi-conservative replication: each new DNA molecule contains one strand from the original molecule (conserved, meaning kept) and one newly made strand. This is why it is called half-conserving.
[bio17-s1.2.1] Watson and Crick predicted this in 1953 from the base-pairing model, because each strand can act as a template for its partner. There is more on this in Models.
[bio17-s1.2.2] Conservative replication: the original double helix stays intact, and a completely new molecule is made.
[bio17-s1.2.3] Dispersive replication: each new molecule is a patchwork of old and new pieces in both strands, so old and new DNA are mixed along each strand.
[bio17-s1.3.b] In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl tested the three models by labelling DNA so that old and new strands could be told apart by their weight. Learn the method and the results, because you may be asked to interpret them.
[bio17-s1.3.0] 1. They grew the bacterium E. coli for many generations in a medium whose only nitrogen was the heavy isotope ¹⁵N (a heavier form of nitrogen), so all its DNA was “heavy”.
[bio17-s1.3.1] 2. They moved the bacteria to a medium containing only normal, light ¹⁴N, so any new DNA strand would be built from light nitrogen.
[bio17-s1.3.2] 3. After each generation they extracted the DNA and spun it at high speed in a caesium chloride solution, which forms a density gradient, so DNA settles as a band at a level that matches its density.
[bio17-s1.3.3] Before the transfer there is one heavy band, because all the DNA is made with ¹⁵N.
[bio17-s1.3.4] After one generation there is one band of intermediate density, so conservative replication, which would give one heavy and one light band, is ruled out.
[bio17-s1.3.5] After two generations there are one intermediate band and one light band, of equal size. This rules out dispersive replication, which would give a single band at every generation, becoming lighter each time.
[bio17-s1.3.6] Only the semi-conservative model fits both results.
[bio17-s1.3.7] Diagram: three centrifuge tubes side by side (generation 0, 1, 2) showing band positions heavy; intermediate; intermediate and light; with a strand diagram under each showing heavy strands dark and light strands pale.
[bio17-s1.4.b] The semi-conservative model is precise enough to make exact predictions about base sequences and numbers of nucleotides. Because A always pairs with T and C with G, you can work these out step by step.
[bio17-s1.4.0] Predicting daughter molecules: given a parent molecule with strands ATGCCA and TACGGT, the strands separate.
[bio17-s1.4.1] ATGCCA is the template for a new TACGGT, and TACGGT is the template for a new ATGCCA, because each base can only pair with its partner.
[bio17-s1.4.2] The two daughter molecules are identical to the parent, each with one old strand and one new strand.
[bio17-s1.4.3] Counting nucleotides: copying a DNA molecule of 1,000 base pairs makes two new strands of 1,000 nucleotides each, so 2,000 free nucleotides are used.
[bio17-s1.4.4] If 30% of the parent's bases are adenine, then 30% of the free nucleotides used are A and 30% are T.
[bio17-s1.4.5] The remaining free nucleotides are 20% C and 20% G, because A = T and C = G in double-stranded DNA. There is more on this in Nucleotides, DNA and RNA.
[bio17-s1.5.b] Two more skills use the model: working out how many molecules exist after several rounds of replication, and building a hands-on model to show how copying works. A good model also has limits that you should be able to name.
[bio17-s1.5.0] Generations: one DNA molecule replicated three times gives 2³ = 8 molecules, because the number of molecules doubles each time.
[bio17-s1.5.1] In a Meselson–Stahl-type experiment only 2 of these 8 still contain an original heavy strand, because each original strand is kept whole and never broken up.
[bio17-s1.5.2] Physical model: build a short double strand from paper or plastic nucleotide pieces in four colours, one per base, that fit only their correct partners.
[bio17-s1.5.3] Separate the strands, add free pieces to each, and compare the two products with the original. Use two shades for old and new pieces to show semi-conservation.
[bio17-s1.5.4] Strength of the physical model: it shows templates, base pairing and identical products.
[bio17-s1.5.5] Limitations of the physical model: it is flat and short, and does not show the helix, the enzymes, the energy needed, or the speed and accuracy of copying in cells. There is more on this in Models.
[bio17-s1.6.b] A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for one protein. The instructions are not used directly: they are first copied into a molecule of RNA, and that copy is then used to build the protein.
[bio17-s1.6.0] Genetic information flows from DNA to mRNA to protein: DNA → transcription → mRNA → translation → protein. Replication, by contrast, copies DNA to DNA.
[bio17-s1.6.1] Francis Crick called this one-way flow from nucleic acid to protein the “central dogma” of molecular biology.
[bio17-s1.6.2] Transcription: in the nucleus, the base sequence of one gene is copied into a molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA), using one DNA strand as the template.
[bio17-s1.6.3] The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore (a small channel in the nuclear envelope) and travels to a ribosome in the cytoplasm, either free or attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum. The DNA stays in the nucleus.
[bio17-s1.6.4] Translation: at the ribosome (the structure that builds proteins), the mRNA's codons (groups of three bases) are read, and transfer RNA molecules bring the matching amino acids.
[bio17-s1.6.5] The amino acids are joined in the order set by the codons to make a specific polypeptide (a chain of amino acids), which folds into a functional protein. There is more on this in Transcription and translation.
[bio17-s1.6.6] Diagram: a eukaryotic cell showing a gene on DNA in the nucleus → transcription → mRNA passing through a nuclear pore → ribosome in the cytoplasm → translation → polypeptide folding into a protein. Label: nucleus, DNA, gene, mRNA, nuclear pore, ribosome, tRNA bringing amino acid, polypeptide.
[bio17-s1.7.b] The ribosome works from an mRNA copy of a gene rather than from the DNA itself, for three reasons. There is also one well-known exception to the usual direction of flow.
[bio17-s1.7.0] The DNA is kept safe in the nucleus and is not damaged by being used directly.
[bio17-s1.7.1] Many mRNA copies can be made from one gene, and each is read by many ribosomes, so a lot of protein can be made quickly.
[bio17-s1.7.2] mRNA is broken down after use, so the cell can switch protein production on and off.
[bio17-s1.7.3] Exception: retroviruses such as HIV carry RNA and use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to make DNA from their RNA, so information flows from RNA to DNA. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio17-s1.7.4] Information never flows from protein back to nucleic acid.
