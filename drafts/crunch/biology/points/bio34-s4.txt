TARGET bio34-s4: Climate change and biological strategies
[bio34-s4.0.b] The Earth is kept warm by gases in the atmosphere that act a little like the glass of a greenhouse. The effect works in four steps, and it is the basis for everything else about climate change.
[bio34-s4.0.0] 1. Sunlight, which is mostly visible and short-wave radiation, passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth's surface.
[bio34-s4.0.1] 2. The warm surface gives off long-wave infrared radiation, which is heat, back towards space.
[bio34-s4.0.2] 3. Greenhouse gases in the atmosphere (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and nitrous oxide) absorb some of this infrared and re-emit it in all directions, including back down to the surface.
[bio34-s4.0.3] 4. As a result, the lower atmosphere and the surface are warmer than they would otherwise be, because some of the heat that would have escaped is returned to the surface.
[bio34-s4.0.4] The natural greenhouse effect is essential for life: without it the Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of about 15 °C, which is too cold for most life.
[bio34-s4.0.5] The enhanced greenhouse effect is the extra warming caused by human increases in greenhouse gases, on top of the natural effect.
[bio34-s4.0.6] Climate change is the long-term change in the Earth's climate, including temperature, rainfall and extreme weather, and it is now driven mainly by the enhanced greenhouse effect. Global warming is the rise in average surface temperature, which is only one part of climate change.
[bio34-s4.0.7] Diagram: the greenhouse effect. Draw the Sun, the Earth's surface and a layer of greenhouse gases. Show incoming sunlight passing through, outgoing infrared, some infrared escaping to space and some re-emitted back to the surface. Label sunlight, infrared (heat), greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄).
[bio34-s4.1.b] Three gases, released by burning fossil fuels, by deforestation, by living things and by human farming, add most to the enhanced greenhouse effect. They differ in how much is released, how strongly each warms the air and how long it lasts.
[bio34-s4.1.0] Carbon dioxide: atmospheric CO₂ has risen from about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution to over 420 parts per million today. This comes mainly from burning fossil fuels and from deforestation. There is more on this in The carbon cycle.
[bio34-s4.1.1] CO₂ is the largest single cause of human-caused warming because so much of it is released and it stays in the atmosphere for centuries.
[bio34-s4.1.2] Methane (CH₄): its concentration has more than doubled since before the Industrial Revolution.
[bio34-s4.1.3] Methane sources include anaerobic microorganisms (archaea, which live without oxygen) in the stomachs (rumens) of cattle and sheep, released mainly by belching; manure and slurry stores; rice paddies; landfill sites; natural wetlands and bogs; and leaks from natural-gas wells and pipelines.
[bio34-s4.1.4] Kilogram for kilogram, methane traps far more heat than CO₂: over 100 years about 30 times as much, and over 20 years about 80 times as much. Note that this is per kilogram, not per molecule.
[bio34-s4.1.5] Methane lasts only about 12 years in the air before it is broken down to CO₂, whereas CO₂ persists for centuries. So cutting methane slows warming quickly.
[bio34-s4.1.6] Nitrous oxide (N₂O) comes from fertilised and waterlogged soils and from manure, where it is formed by denitrification (bacteria turning nitrate into gases). It is a third biological greenhouse gas, and kilogram for kilogram it is about 270 times as powerful as CO₂ over 100 years.
[bio34-s4.2.b] Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions have an unusual pattern, because farming rather than transport or industry is the biggest source. The law sets targets that farming must also meet.
[bio34-s4.2.0] Agriculture is the largest single source of Ireland's greenhouse gas emissions, mainly methane from cattle and nitrous oxide from fertiliser and manure, because Ireland has a large, grass-fed cattle herd.
[bio34-s4.2.1] Ireland's law, the Climate Action and Low Carbon Development (Amendment) Act 2021, sets a 51% cut in greenhouse gas emissions by 2030 compared with 2018, and climate neutrality by 2050. Agriculture's own target is a 25% cut by 2030.
[bio34-s4.3.b] Scientists do not rely on one observation. Several independent lines of evidence show that rising greenhouse gases are the cause of recent warming, and governments have responded to them.
[bio34-s4.3.0] Evidence: greenhouse gases absorb infrared, a property measured in the laboratory since the nineteenth century, so the mechanism is known.
[bio34-s4.3.1] Evidence: direct measurements show CO₂ and methane rising together with global temperature.
[bio34-s4.3.2] Evidence: air bubbles trapped in ancient ice cores show that CO₂ and temperature rose and fell together over hundreds of thousands of years.
[bio34-s4.3.3] Evidence: the observed warming pattern, in which the lower atmosphere warms while the upper atmosphere cools, fits greenhouse gases and not a brighter Sun, because a brighter Sun would warm every layer.
[bio34-s4.3.4] The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concluded in 2021 that it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the climate, by about 1.1 °C for 2011–2020 compared with 1850–1900.
[bio34-s4.3.5] The Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2 °C and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 °C. There is more on scientific consensus in Evaluating evidence.
[bio34-s4.4.b] Good scientific discussion also says what is less certain. Warming can also set off feedbacks, where the effect of warming makes more warming more likely.
[bio34-s4.4.0] Uncertainty: correlation alone does not prove cause. Here the physics of infrared absorption supplies the mechanism.
[bio34-s4.4.1] Uncertainty: exactly how much warming a given rise in CO₂ causes, and how regional rainfall will change, are less certain than the direction of change, because feedbacks are hard to model.
[bio34-s4.4.2] Feedback: warming speeds up decomposition by soil microorganisms, releasing more CO₂, which causes further warming. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio34-s4.4.3] Feedback: thawing permafrost, which is frozen soil in the Arctic, lets stored organic matter decompose, releasing CO₂ and methane.
[bio34-s4.4.4] Feedback: warmer oceans dissolve less CO₂, so more stays in the air.
[bio34-s4.4.5] Feedback: drought and fires turn forests from sinks into sources.
[bio34-s4.5.b] Climate change matters biologically because living things are adapted to particular temperatures, seasons and chemistry. Each of these changes has its own effect.
[bio34-s4.5.0] Species' ranges move towards the poles or uphill to stay cool, and species that cannot move or adapt face extinction. There is more on this in Biodiversity loss and conservation.
[bio34-s4.5.1] The timing of events such as flowering, breeding and migration changes, so species may get out of step with their food.
[bio34-s4.5.2] More CO₂ dissolving in the oceans makes them more acidic (ocean acidification), which makes it harder for corals, shellfish and some phytoplankton to build calcium carbonate shells.
[bio34-s4.5.3] Warmer seas cause coral bleaching.
[bio34-s4.5.4] Insects that spread disease can spread to new areas as temperatures rise. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio34-s4.6.b] Living photosynthesisers on land and in the sea are biological carbon sinks, and they differ in how long they hold the carbon they fix.
[bio34-s4.6.0] Forests and other land plants: trees store carbon in wood for decades or centuries, and in roots and soil. The sink is strong while trees grow. A mature forest takes in roughly what it releases.
[bio34-s4.6.1] The store in a forest is lost quickly if the forest burns, dies or is felled, because the carbon returns to the air as CO₂.
[bio34-s4.6.2] Phytoplankton and algae: microscopic algae and cyanobacteria in the oceans carry out about half of all photosynthesis on Earth.
[bio34-s4.6.3] When phytoplankton die or are eaten, some of their carbon sinks to the deep ocean and sea floor, which is called the biological pump. There it can stay for centuries or longer.
[bio34-s4.6.4] Seaweeds such as kelp, and seagrass meadows and saltmarshes, also store carbon, which is called blue carbon.
[bio34-s4.7.b] Some of the biggest stores of carbon are not living plants at all but waterlogged peat and soil, where dead plant matter has built up.
[bio34-s4.7.0] Bogs and other peatlands: waterlogged, acidic, low-oxygen conditions stop decomposition, so a healthy bog accumulates peat (partly rotted plant matter) very slowly over thousands of years.
[bio34-s4.7.1] Peatlands cover only about 3% of the world's land but hold more carbon than all the world's forests. A bog is a sink only while it stays wet.
[bio34-s4.7.2] Soils: soil organic matter (humus) stores more carbon than all plants and the atmosphere together. Grassland and undisturbed soils keep it, while ploughing and draining release it because air reaches the organic matter and it decomposes.
[bio34-s4.8.b] The sinks differ in how fast they take up carbon, how much they hold and how securely. Comparing them shows why none of them is enough on its own.
[bio34-s4.8.0] Bogs store the most carbon per hectare and for the longest time, but gain it very slowly. Forests take up carbon fastest but store it less securely. Oceans are the largest active sink, but they are becoming more acidic and absorb less as they warm.
[bio34-s4.8.1] All natural sinks together absorb only about half of the CO₂ humans release each year, so sinks can slow the rise but cannot replace cutting emissions.
[bio34-s4.9.b] Biological strategies for cutting atmospheric CO₂ are judged by how they work, the case for, the case against and an overall judgement. The simplest strategy is to keep the carbon already stored where it is.
[bio34-s4.9.0] How it works: protecting existing forests, bogs and wetlands keeps carbon in place, so it is never released.
[bio34-s4.9.1] For: it is the quickest and cheapest way to avoid emissions, with large benefits for biodiversity and water.
[bio34-s4.9.2] Against: land is valuable for farming or development, and protection needs enforcement and payment to landowners. There is more on the ethics in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s4.9.3] Judgement: it is the most effective first step, because it avoids emissions instead of waiting for new growth to recapture them.
[bio34-s4.10.b] Planting trees uses photosynthesis to pull CO₂ out of the air. The benefits are real, but the details of what is planted and where matter a great deal.
[bio34-s4.10.0] How it works: afforestation (planting new forests) and reforestation (replanting cleared land) use photosynthesis to fix CO₂ into wood.
[bio34-s4.10.1] For: they take up carbon for decades. Native broadleaved woodland also supports biodiversity, reduces flooding, and gives timber that can replace concrete and steel.
[bio34-s4.10.2] Against: they are slow, taking decades to reach full uptake. Fire, storms and disease can release the carbon, and they need land that could grow food.
[bio34-s4.10.3] Against: conifers planted on deep peat can release more carbon from the drained peat than the trees take up, because draining lets the peat decompose.
[bio34-s4.10.4] Against: dense plantations of one non-native species, in Ireland mostly Sitka spruce, support less wildlife.
[bio34-s4.10.5] Ireland's forest cover is about 11%, among the lowest in the EU.
[bio34-s4.10.6] Judgement: valuable if the right trees are planted in the right places, but slow.
[bio34-s4.11.b] Ireland has drained a great deal of peat, which turns a carbon store into a CO₂ source. Putting the water back reverses this.
[bio34-s4.11.0] How it works: blocking drains so the water table rises. Conditions become anaerobic again, decomposition stops, and sphagnum moss starts forming peat.
[bio34-s4.11.1] For: it quickly stops the large CO₂ release from drained peat, restores rare habitat, and holds water to reduce flooding. Bord na Móna's cutaway bogs are being rewetted on a large scale.
[bio34-s4.11.2] Against: rewetted bogs give off some methane from anaerobic decomposition. New peat forms very slowly, so the gain is mainly avoided CO₂ loss rather than fast uptake.
[bio34-s4.11.3] Against: the land can no longer be used for turf or farming.
[bio34-s4.11.4] Judgement: strongly beneficial for Ireland because so much peat is drained.
[bio34-s4.12.b] Three further strategies use algae, soil management or plants as fuel. Each looks attractive, but each has a limit that you should be able to name.
[bio34-s4.12.0] Algae and phytoplankton: seaweed farming and algae grown in tanks fix CO₂ fast, and the algae can be made into food, feed or biofuel. For: algae grow much faster than land plants and need no farmland or fresh water.
[bio34-s4.12.1] Against algae: if the algae are eaten or burned, the carbon returns to the air, so the sink lasts only if the carbon is stored.
[bio34-s4.12.2] Against algae: adding iron to the ocean to cause phytoplankton blooms has been tested, but little of the carbon was shown to reach the deep ocean, and it risks harming marine ecosystems, for example by creating low-oxygen zones. It is therefore not an accepted strategy.
[bio34-s4.12.3] Judgement on algae: promising for fuels and food, but unproven as large-scale storage.
[bio34-s4.12.4] Increasing soil carbon: keeping grassland unploughed, adding manure and compost, growing cover crops over winter, reduced tillage and planting hedgerows add organic matter to soil. For: this also improves soil fertility and water-holding.
[bio34-s4.12.5] Against increasing soil carbon: soils fill up (saturate) after some decades, the gain is hard to measure, and it is lost again if the land is ploughed.
[bio34-s4.12.6] Bioenergy in place of fossil fuels: biofuels and wood or other biomass burned in power stations release CO₂ that the plants recently took in, so in principle they add little CO₂. Capturing and storing the CO₂ from burning biomass could even remove carbon.
[bio34-s4.12.7] Against bioenergy: fuel crops compete with food for land, clearing land for them releases carbon, and growing, harvesting and transporting them uses fossil fuel. So biofuels are not carbon-neutral without this qualification.
[bio34-s4.13.b] Because most Irish methane comes from the rumens of cattle, the most direct strategies change what cattle eat, how they are bred, or how many there are.
[bio34-s4.13.0] Changes to cattle diet and breeding: feed additives (for example the chemical 3-NOP, and some seaweeds) inhibit the methane-producing microorganisms in the rumen, cutting methane in trials.
[bio34-s4.13.1] Better-quality feed and earlier finishing of cattle mean less methane per kilogram of meat or milk. Breeding cattle that naturally produce less methane is being researched.
[bio34-s4.13.2] For diet and breeding: they tackle Ireland's largest methane source directly without cutting production.
[bio34-s4.13.3] Against diet and breeding: additives are hard to give to cattle that graze outdoors for most of the year. Long-term effects and costs are still being studied. Some seaweeds contain a compound (bromoform) with health concerns, and breeding is slow.
[bio34-s4.13.4] For fewer livestock and dietary change: fewer cattle produce less methane and need less land, which could be used for forests or bogs. Eating less red meat and dairy reduces demand.
[bio34-s4.13.5] Against fewer livestock and dietary change: farming is central to Ireland's rural economy and exports. Cutting the Irish herd may only move production to countries with higher emissions per animal, which is called carbon leakage.
[bio34-s4.13.6] Against fewer livestock: meat and milk are good sources of protein, iron and calcium.
[bio34-s4.13.7] Judgement on fewer livestock and dietary change: a fair transition needs support for farmers.
[bio34-s4.14.b] Methane is also made in waste, and nitrous oxide comes from fertilised soil. Capturing waste methane for fuel, and cutting the gases at source, are the remaining strategies.
[bio34-s4.14.0] Anaerobic digestion of slurry, food waste and grass: microorganisms break down the waste without oxygen in a sealed tank, producing biogas, which is mainly methane. The biogas is burned for heat or electricity, or upgraded to replace natural gas, and the digestate (the solid left over) is used as fertiliser.
[bio34-s4.14.1] For anaerobic digestion: it captures methane that would otherwise escape from open slurry stores and landfill, replaces a fossil fuel, and recycles nutrients.
[bio34-s4.14.2] Against anaerobic digestion: the plants are expensive and leaks release methane. Burning the biogas still releases CO₂, although it is recent carbon rather than fossil carbon.
[bio34-s4.14.3] Other methods: capping landfill sites and collecting their gas; composting food waste aerobically (with oxygen) instead of sending it to landfill; reducing food waste; and covering slurry stores.
[bio34-s4.14.4] Rewetted bogs and rice paddies show that wet, anaerobic soil makes methane, which is a trade-off with storing carbon.
[bio34-s4.14.5] Nitrous oxide: using clover in grassland, applying fertiliser precisely, and using protected urea fertiliser, which releases nitrogen more slowly, all reduce nitrous oxide. They also reduce nitrate leaching. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s4.15.b] A post-fossil-fuel world is one in which energy, fuels, materials and chemicals no longer come from coal, oil and gas. Biology can help by providing renewable replacements made from recently fixed carbon, and by protecting the sinks that absorb CO₂.
[bio34-s4.15.0] Biofuels and biogas: bioethanol is made by fermentation of sugars by yeast, and enzymes that break down cellulose for fuel help make it. There is more on this in Industrial enzymes.
[bio34-s4.15.1] Biofuels and biogas: biodiesel is made from plant oils and waste cooking oil. Biogas comes from anaerobic digestion, and fuels are also made from algae.
[bio34-s4.15.2] Bio-based materials: bioplastics are made from plant starch or sugars, such as polylactic acid (PLA), and some are biodegradable. Wood is used in construction, and plant fibres replace oil-based fibres.
[bio34-s4.15.3] Microorganisms as factories, or biomanufacturing: bacteria, yeasts and algae grown in fermenters (bioreactors) can make medicines, enzymes, food proteins and chemicals that are now made from oil. They use sugars or waste as raw materials.
[bio34-s4.15.4] Food and farming: breeding crops, including by genetic technologies, that tolerate drought, heat and new pests and need less fertiliser.
[bio34-s4.15.5] Food and farming: using legumes and soil microbiomes instead of manufactured fertiliser, which is made with fossil energy, and reducing food waste.
[bio34-s4.15.6] Biology also monitors and understands change, by measuring sinks, tracking species' ranges and predicting the spread of disease.
[bio34-s4.15.7] Limits: biomass cannot replace all fossil fuels, because land is needed for food and nature. Bio-based products are not automatically sustainable, since they depend on how the raw material is grown. Biology therefore works alongside other renewables, such as wind and solar, and alongside using less energy.

CANDIDATES bio34-s1: The carbon cycle
[bio34-s1.0.b] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the elements living things are built from have to be reused. Carbon is the clearest example: it moves round and round between organisms and their surroundings.
[bio34-s1.0.0] A biogeochemical cycle, or nutrient cycle, is the continuous movement of a chemical element between living things (bio) and the non-living environment (geo): the air, water, rocks and soil.
[bio34-s1.0.1] Matter is not created or destroyed, so the same atoms are used again and again. Energy, by contrast, flows through an ecosystem once and is lost as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.0.2] Carbon is the element at the centre of every biomolecule (carbohydrates, lipids, proteins and nucleic acids; see Biomolecules and metabolism), so every organism needs a constant supply. Producers (organisms that make their own food, such as plants) take it in as CO₂; consumers get it already built into food.
[bio34-s1.0.3] A store (reservoir) is a place where carbon is held: the atmosphere (as CO₂, and a little methane, CH₄); living organisms (biomass, the mass of living material); dead organic matter in soil; the oceans (dissolved CO₂, hydrogencarbonate and carbonate); rocks such as limestone and chalk (calcium carbonate); and fossil fuels.
[bio34-s1.0.4] A process, or flux, is whatever moves carbon from one store to another. Stores are the boxes of the cycle and processes are the arrows between them.
[bio34-s1.0.5] CO₂ makes up only about 0.04% of the air (over 420 parts per million), yet it is the source of carbon for almost all life on Earth, because photosynthesis starts from it.
[bio34-s1.1.b] Carbon dioxide leaves the air in only a few ways. By far the most important is photosynthesis, but dissolving in water and shell building also lock carbon away.
[bio34-s1.1.0] Photosynthesis by plants, algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating algae) fixes CO₂ into glucose and then into the rest of their biomass, so they act as carbon sinks (stores that take in more carbon than they release).
[bio34-s1.1.1] Photosynthesis is the only major route by which carbon enters living things, so nearly all the carbon in a food chain started as CO₂ taken in by a producer.
[bio34-s1.1.2] Dissolving: CO₂ dissolves in the surface of the oceans, lakes and rivers, and aquatic producers take it up for photosynthesis.
[bio34-s1.1.3] Shell and skeleton building: many marine organisms (e.g. corals, molluscs and some phytoplankton) combine dissolved carbonate with calcium to make calcium carbonate shells and skeletons.
[bio34-s1.1.4] When these organisms die, their shells and skeletons settle on the sea floor and, over millions of years, form limestone and chalk. These rocks are very long-term stores because the carbon stays locked in them for so long.
[bio34-s1.2.b] Once carbon is in a producer it does not stay there. It passes along the food chain and into the dead matter that every organism leaves behind.
[bio34-s1.2.0] Feeding: carbon in the producer's biomass passes to herbivores and then carnivores when they eat and digest it. At each step most of it is respired away (broken down to release energy, giving off CO₂) rather than built into the consumer's body.
[bio34-s1.2.1] Each step in a food chain is a trophic level (a feeding level). There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio34-s1.2.2] Egestion and excretion: carbon leaves animals in faeces and in wastes such as urea, and enters the dead organic matter store, as do shed leaves and dead organisms. This store is the food supply for decomposers.
[bio34-s1.3.b] Carbon dioxide is put back into the air by respiration, by decomposers, by burning and by geological release. If it were not returned, producers would run out of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.3.0] Respiration by plants, animals and microorganisms breaks down glucose and releases CO₂. Details are in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio34-s1.3.1] Plants respire all the time, day and night, so the respiration arrow must start from plants as well as animals. Over a growing season photosynthesis exceeds respiration, so the plant as a whole gains carbon.
[bio34-s1.3.2] Combustion: burning biomass (wood, peat, forest fires) or fossil fuels combines the carbon with oxygen and releases it as CO₂ very quickly.
[bio34-s1.3.3] Natural geological release: volcanoes release CO₂ from rocks, and the weathering of limestone releases some. This is small compared with the biological fluxes and with fossil-fuel burning.
[bio34-s1.4.b] Dead organisms, faeces and fallen leaves contain a lot of carbon. Decomposers are the living things that break this matter down and return its carbon to the air, so the cycle can keep turning.
[bio34-s1.4.0] Decomposers are bacteria and fungi. They feed saprophytically (on dead organic matter): they secrete enzymes that digest the matter outside their cells, absorb the products, and respire them, releasing CO₂ and minerals.
[bio34-s1.4.1] Say that decomposers digest the matter and then respire the products. The CO₂ comes from their respiration, not from the matter breaking down into CO₂ directly.
[bio34-s1.4.2] Without decomposers, carbon would stay locked in dead matter and the CO₂ supply for photosynthesis would run out. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi, and soil microbiomes are covered in The microbiome.
[bio34-s1.4.3] Decomposers work fastest when it is warm, moist and well aerated, because they respire aerobically (using oxygen).
[bio34-s1.4.4] In cold, waterlogged, acidic or oxygen-poor places decomposition is very slow, so dead matter builds up, e.g. as peat in bogs.
[bio34-s1.4.5] Anaerobic decomposition: where there is no oxygen (waterlogged soils, bogs, rice paddies, landfill, slurry tanks and the stomachs of cattle and sheep), some microorganisms break organic matter down to methane (CH₄) instead of CO₂.
[bio34-s1.4.6] The methane-producing microorganisms are archaea, a separate group of single-celled organisms from bacteria (see Domains of life). Methane in the air is slowly converted to CO₂.
[bio34-s1.5.b] Fossil fuels are a store of carbon that the natural cycle leaves almost untouched. People now burn them, which is why they matter so much to the carbon cycle.
[bio34-s1.5.0] Fossil fuels (coal, oil and natural gas) are the remains of organisms that lived millions of years ago and were buried before they could decompose fully.
[bio34-s1.5.1] Coal formed mainly from land plants in ancient swamps. Oil and natural gas formed mainly from tiny marine organisms (plankton) buried in sea-floor mud. Heat and pressure over millions of years changed them into fuels.
[bio34-s1.5.2] The carbon in fossil fuels was fixed by photosynthesis long ago, so they store ancient solar energy and carbon. Peat is an early stage of the same process, which is why it is sometimes counted with them.
[bio34-s1.5.3] In the natural cycle almost no carbon leaves this store.
[bio34-s1.5.4] Burning fossil fuels since the Industrial Revolution moves carbon locked away for millions of years into the atmosphere in a few centuries, far faster than photosynthesis and the oceans can take it back, so atmospheric CO₂ rises. Consequences are in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.b] Whether a store helps to lower or raise atmospheric CO₂ depends on whether carbon is flowing in or out. Photosynthesis shows how plants act as sinks; here the sink and source distinction is applied to every store and to the overall balance of the cycle.
[bio34-s1.6.0] A store acts as a sink when its inputs exceed its outputs, so it gains carbon, and as a source when its outputs exceed its inputs, so it releases carbon.
[bio34-s1.6.1] The same store can switch between the two: a growing forest is a sink; a mature forest roughly balances; a burning or felled forest is a source. A healthy wet bog is a sink; a drained or burning bog is a source.
[bio34-s1.6.2] The main natural sinks on a carbon-cycle diagram are land plants (forests and grassland, with the soil carbon under them); algae and phytoplankton in the oceans (with dissolving CO₂ and shells sinking to the sea floor); bogs and other wetlands; and limestone and fossil fuels as very long-term stores.
[bio34-s1.6.3] These sinks are compared, and the strategies that use them are described, in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s1.6.4] Before large-scale fossil-fuel burning, the CO₂ taken up by photosynthesis and the oceans each year was roughly balanced by the CO₂ released by respiration, decomposition and natural fires. Atmospheric CO₂ therefore stayed nearly constant, at about 280 parts per million before the Industrial Revolution.
[bio34-s1.6.5] Deforestation and fossil-fuel burning have tipped this balance, so more CO₂ is now added than removed.
[bio34-s1.6.6] Seasonal pattern: atmospheric CO₂ falls each northern summer, when photosynthesis exceeds respiration over the large land area of the northern hemisphere, and rises each winter, when respiration and decomposition exceed photosynthesis. This happens on top of the long-term rise.
[bio34-s1.7.b] A model represents the cycle in a diagram and in words, and is used to explain and predict. It is only as good as its limits allow.
[bio34-s1.7.0] Diagram: draw boxes for the stores: carbon dioxide in the air; green plants and algae; animals; dead organisms and wastes; decomposers (bacteria and fungi); fossil fuels; oceans.
[bio34-s1.7.1] Diagram arrows, each labelled with its process: photosynthesis (air → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (plants and animals → dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → decomposers); respiration (plants, animals and decomposers → air).
[bio34-s1.7.2] Diagram arrows, continued: fossilisation (dead matter → fossil fuels, over millions of years); combustion (fossil fuels and wood → air); dissolving (air ↔ oceans).
[bio34-s1.7.3] Prediction (a): cutting down and burning a forest removes photosynthesis and adds combustion and the decomposition of the remains, so atmospheric CO₂ rises.
[bio34-s1.7.4] Prediction (b): draining a bog lets air in, so aerobic decomposers break down the peat and release CO₂.
[bio34-s1.7.5] Prediction (c): if decomposers were killed (e.g. by soil made very acidic or polluted), dead matter would accumulate, minerals would not be returned to the soil, and plant growth would eventually fall.
[bio34-s1.7.6] Limitation: a diagram shows the processes but not how much carbon moves or how fast. It also treats each store as one box, although the oceans, soils and forests each hold carbon for very different lengths of time.
[bio34-s1.7.7] Limitation: it simplifies feedbacks, e.g. warming speeds up decomposition, which releases more CO₂. It is also a snapshot that hides seasonal and long-term change. There is more on this in Models.

CANDIDATES bio14-s1: Photosynthesis as an anabolic process
[bio14-s1.0.b] Photosynthesis is how plants make their own food. They capture energy from light and use it to build glucose, a sugar, out of two simple substances they take from their surroundings: carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.0.0] Green plants, algae and some bacteria, such as cyanobacteria, photosynthesise. They use light energy, absorbed by the green pigment chlorophyll, to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water, and they release oxygen as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.0.1] Because they make their own food from simple inorganic substances, these organisms are called autotrophs (self-feeders). Animals and fungi can't do this, so they must eat food that was made by an autotroph.
[bio14-s1.0.2] The key change is an energy conversion: light energy becomes chemical energy, stored in the bonds of the glucose molecule. Almost every food chain starts with this conversion, so nearly all the energy living things use was first captured by photosynthesis.
[bio14-s1.0.3] Photosynthesis is also where the oxygen in the air comes from. Without it, there would be no oxygen for aerobic respiration.
[bio14-s1.0.4] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow, because both are needed for the reaction but neither is a reacting substance.
[bio14-s1.0.5] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll above the arrow. Each side has 6 carbon, 12 hydrogen and 18 oxygen atoms. Six CO₂ molecules are needed because each one supplies one of glucose's six carbon atoms.
[bio14-s1.1.b] Every reaction in a cell either builds molecules up or breaks them down. Photosynthesis is a building-up reaction, and the exam expects you to say why in two parts.
[bio14-s1.1.0] An anabolic reaction builds a large molecule from smaller ones and takes in energy to do it. Photosynthesis fits both parts of that definition.
[bio14-s1.1.1] First, it builds a large organic molecule, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), from small inorganic molecules, carbon dioxide and water.
[bio14-s1.1.2] Second, it takes in energy rather than releasing it. The energy comes from light, and it ends up stored in the glucose.
[bio14-s1.1.3] Respiration is the opposite, catabolic process: it breaks glucose back down into carbon dioxide and water and releases the stored energy. There is more on this in Respiration as a catabolic process.
[bio14-s1.2.b] A plant has to gather carbon dioxide, water and light, and then deal with the oxygen and glucose it makes. Each one has a definite route into or out of the leaf.
[bio14-s1.2.0] Carbon dioxide diffuses from the air into the leaf through the stomata, small pores found mainly on the lower surface. It moves through the air spaces inside the leaf to the mesophyll cells, where photosynthesis happens. Some also comes from the plant's own respiring cells.
[bio14-s1.2.1] Plants that live under water can't take gas from the air, so they absorb carbon dioxide dissolved in the water, partly as hydrogencarbonate ions.
[bio14-s1.2.2] Water is absorbed from the soil by root hair cells, by osmosis, and carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem (the plant's water-carrying tubes). Only a tiny fraction of this water is used in photosynthesis; most evaporates from the leaves in transpiration.
[bio14-s1.2.3] Light comes from the sun, or from artificial lights in greenhouses and laboratories.
[bio14-s1.2.4] Chlorophyll absorbs mainly red and blue light and reflects green light. This is why leaves look green: green is the colour the plant isn't using.
[bio14-s1.2.5] Oxygen is partly used by the plant's own cells for respiration. The rest diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata into the air, or into the water for aquatic plants.
[bio14-s1.2.6] The oxygen released comes from splitting water molecules, not from carbon dioxide. This was shown in the 1940s: when plants were given water containing a heavy form (isotope) of oxygen, ¹⁸O, the oxygen gas they released contained ¹⁸O.
[bio14-s1.2.7] Glucose is partly used straight away in the plant's own respiration, which releases its energy as ATP for the cell's work. The rest is turned into sucrose, starch and cellulose.
[bio14-s1.3.b] Glucose is a good fuel, but it isn't ideal for moving around the plant, storing or building with. So plants convert it into three other carbohydrates, each suited to one job.
[bio14-s1.3.0] Sucrose (for transport): glucose is joined to fructose to form the disaccharide sucrose. Sucrose is carried from the leaves in the phloem (the plant's food-carrying tubes) to growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[bio14-s1.3.1] Sucrose suits transport because it is soluble, so it travels dissolved in the phloem, and less reactive than glucose. It is not a reducing sugar, so it isn't used up by the cells it passes on the way.
[bio14-s1.3.2] Starch (for storage): glucose is joined into long chains to form the polysaccharide starch. Starch grains build up in the chloroplasts during the day, and longer-term stores are kept in storage organs and seeds, such as potato tubers and cereal grains.
[bio14-s1.3.3] Starch suits storage because it is insoluble, so large amounts can be stored without drawing water into the cell by osmosis, and it is compact.
[bio14-s1.3.4] Cellulose (for building): glucose is joined into the polysaccharide cellulose, which forms the strong walls around plant cells. Because every plant cell has a wall, cellulose is the most abundant organic compound on Earth.
[bio14-s1.3.5] Plants also use glucose to make lipids, such as the oils in seeds, and, combined with nitrates taken from the soil, amino acids for proteins.
[bio14-s1.4.b] When a plant photosynthesises, it pulls carbon out of the air and locks it inside its own body. This is why forests, bogs and the oceans matter in the fight against climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.0] A carbon sink is anything that takes in more carbon from the atmosphere than it releases, and stores it. Photosynthesis is the only major biological process that removes carbon dioxide from the air.
[bio14-s1.4.1] The carbon from CO₂ is fixed (built into organic molecules) as glucose, then as sucrose, starch, cellulose and the rest of the plant's body, its biomass.
[bio14-s1.4.2] The carbon stays locked away for as long as the biomass lasts: for decades or centuries in the wood of trees, and for thousands of years in peat bogs, because waterlogged, acidic conditions slow down decomposition.
[bio14-s1.4.3] Algae and phytoplankton (tiny floating photosynthesisers) in the oceans also fix carbon, and some of it is locked up in ocean sediments when they die.
[bio14-s1.4.4] Respiration, decomposition and burning return the stored carbon to the air as CO₂.
[bio14-s1.4.5] Forests, peatlands and the oceans' algae and phytoplankton are the main natural carbon sinks. Cutting down forests, draining or burning peat and burning fossil fuels all release stored carbon and add to the CO₂ that drives climate change.
[bio14-s1.4.6] Fossil fuels are themselves stored photosynthesis: coal, oil and gas hold carbon that plants and algae fixed millions of years ago, which is why burning them adds extra CO₂ to the air.
[bio14-s1.5.b] Photosynthesis happens inside chloroplasts, and it doesn't happen in one go. It runs as two linked stages in two different parts of the chloroplast: the first captures energy from light, and the second uses that energy to build glucose.
[bio14-s1.5.0] In a leaf, most chloroplasts are in the palisade mesophyll cells, just below the upper surface, because that is where light is strongest. Spongy mesophyll cells and guard cells also have some.
[bio14-s1.5.1] Stage 1, the light-dependent stage, takes place on the thylakoid membranes, the flattened sacs inside the chloroplast that are stacked into piles called grana.
[bio14-s1.5.2] 1. Chlorophyll absorbs light energy.
[bio14-s1.5.3] 2. The energy is used to split water molecules into oxygen, protons (H⁺) and electrons. This splitting is called photolysis, meaning splitting by light.
[bio14-s1.5.4] 3. The oxygen is released as a by-product.
[bio14-s1.5.5] 4. The energy is trapped in ATP, and the protons and high-energy electrons are picked up by a hydrogen carrier molecule, ready for stage 2.
[bio14-s1.5.6] Stage 2, the light-independent stage, takes place in the stroma, the fluid that fills the chloroplast around the thylakoids.
[bio14-s1.5.7] 1. Carbon dioxide enters the stroma. 2. It is combined with the hydrogen (protons and electrons) brought from stage 1. 3. Energy from the ATP made in stage 1 drives these reactions. 4. Glucose is formed.
[bio14-s1.6.b] The two stages are like a factory with a delivery service between them. Stage 1 loads up carriers with energy and hydrogen, stage 2 unloads them to build glucose, and the empty carriers go back to be reloaded.
[bio14-s1.6.0] Stage 1 supplies the two things stage 2 needs: energy, as ATP, and hydrogen, on the hydrogen carrier.
[bio14-s1.6.1] Stage 2 returns the spent carriers to stage 1: ATP comes back as ADP, and the carrier comes back empty, so both can be reused.
[bio14-s1.6.2] The reactions of stage 2 are controlled by enzymes, which is why this stage, and so the whole process, is affected by temperature.
[bio14-s1.6.3] The light-independent stage doesn't use light directly, but it stops soon after dark, because stage 1 stops and the supply of ATP and hydrogen runs out.
[bio14-s1.6.4] Older books call the light-independent stage the dark stage. That name is misleading: the stage happens in the light, at the same time as stage 1.
[bio14-s1.6.5] Diagram: a chloroplast outline with stage 1 on a thylakoid stack (inputs light and H₂O; output O₂) and stage 2 in the stroma (input CO₂; output glucose → sucrose, starch, cellulose). Arrows: ATP and hydrogen to the stroma; ADP and the empty carrier back.

CANDIDATES bio34-s2: The nitrogen cycle
[bio34-s2.0.b] Every living thing needs nitrogen, yet the huge store in the air is out of reach for almost all of them. Nitrogen therefore has to be converted into usable forms and then recycled, or life would run out of it.
[bio34-s2.0.0] Living things need nitrogen to make amino acids and proteins, nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and chlorophyll (the green pigment in plants). Minerals goes into why plants need it and the signs of deficiency.
[bio34-s2.0.1] About 78% of the air is nitrogen gas (N₂), but plants and animals cannot use it. The two atoms are held together by a very strong triple bond that few organisms can break.
[bio34-s2.0.2] Plants can absorb nitrogen only as ions dissolved in soil water, mainly nitrate (NO₃⁻) and also ammonium (NH₄⁺). So nitrogen must be converted, or fixed, before it enters food chains, and recycled from dead matter.
[bio34-s2.0.3] The forms to recognise are nitrogen gas (N₂); ammonia (NH₃) and ammonium (NH₄⁺); nitrite (NO₂⁻); nitrate (NO₃⁻); and organic nitrogen, which is nitrogen built into proteins, nucleic acids and urea (the waste molecule in mammals' urine).
[bio34-s2.1.b] Nitrogen fixation converts nitrogen gas from the air into nitrogen compounds that living things can use, such as ammonium, ammonia or nitrate. The biological route is carried out by bacteria, either living freely or in partnership with plants.
[bio34-s2.1.0] Free-living nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the soil and in water convert N₂ to ammonium, which they use to make their own proteins. When they die, decomposition releases the nitrogen to the soil.
[bio34-s2.1.1] Other nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in root nodules (small swellings on the roots) of legumes, the pea family, for example clover, peas and beans. They fix nitrogen for the plant.
[bio34-s2.1.2] The bacteria give the plant ammonium, which it uses to make amino acids. The plant gives the bacteria carbohydrates from photosynthesis and a protected, low-oxygen home. Both benefit, so this is mutualism.
[bio34-s2.1.3] Fixation takes a lot of energy, supplied as ATP from the bacteria's respiration, and uses an enzyme that oxygen damages. This is why nodules keep oxygen levels low: they contain a red pigment that binds oxygen, so active nodules are pink inside.
[bio34-s2.1.4] Legumes grow well in nitrogen-poor soil because their bacteria supply the nitrogen. When they die or are ploughed in, the nitrogen in their tissues enriches the soil.
[bio34-s2.1.5] Clover grown with grass, and legumes in crop rotation (growing different crops in turn on the same field), reduce the need for nitrogen fertiliser. This is why farmers grow clover.
[bio34-s2.2.b] Bacteria are not the only route from nitrogen gas to usable nitrogen. Lightning does a little of it naturally, and humans do a great deal of it in factories.
[bio34-s2.2.0] Atmospheric fixation by lightning: the energy of lightning makes nitrogen react with oxygen to form nitrogen oxides, which dissolve in rain and reach the soil as nitrate. This is a small contribution compared with biological fixation.
[bio34-s2.2.1] Industrial fixation by the Haber process: nitrogen from the air combines with hydrogen (made mainly from natural gas) at high temperature and pressure to make ammonia.
[bio34-s2.2.2] The ammonia is turned into nitrogen fertilisers such as ammonium nitrate and urea, which farmers spread to give crops usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.2.3] Human activity, mainly the Haber process, now fixes at least as much nitrogen each year as all natural processes on land. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s2.3.b] Once nitrogen is in the soil as nitrate or ammonium, it moves into living things and along food chains. Assimilation means the plant builds absorbed nitrogen into its own molecules.
[bio34-s2.3.0] Uptake: plant roots absorb nitrate and ammonium from soil water. Nitrate is taken in by active transport, against its concentration gradient, using ATP, and travels to the leaves in the xylem (the tubes that carry water up the plant).
[bio34-s2.3.1] Assimilation: the plant uses the nitrogen, together with sugars from photosynthesis, to make amino acids, then proteins, and also nucleic acids and chlorophyll.
[bio34-s2.3.2] Feeding: animals get nitrogen only by eating plants or other animals. They digest the proteins into amino acids and build their own proteins from them.
[bio34-s2.3.3] Excretion and death: animals excrete excess nitrogen (in mammals, as urea in urine). Faeces, urine, dead plants and dead animals return organic nitrogen to the soil.
[bio34-s2.4.b] Dead matter and wastes are full of organic nitrogen that plants cannot use directly. Decomposition is the step that turns it back into a usable form.
[bio34-s2.4.0] Decomposers (bacteria and fungi) feed on dead organisms, faeces and urine. They digest the proteins and other nitrogen compounds and release the nitrogen as ammonia, which dissolves in soil water as ammonium (NH₄⁺). This step is often called ammonification (putrefaction).
[bio34-s2.4.1] Without decomposition, the nitrogen in dead matter would be locked up and the soil would run out of usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.4.2] Decomposition is fastest in warm, moist, aerated soil (soil with air spaces), because these conditions suit the decomposers. There is more on this in The carbon cycle.
[bio34-s2.4.3] Ammonium is the starting point for nitrification. Some ammonium is also taken up directly by plants.
[bio34-s2.5.b] Ammonium from decomposition is not the form plants take up most. Nitrifying bacteria in the soil convert it to nitrate in two steps.
[bio34-s2.5.0] Nitrification is the conversion of ammonium into nitrate by nitrifying bacteria in the soil, in two steps. First, one group of nitrifying bacteria oxidises ammonium (NH₄⁺) to nitrite (NO₂⁻). Second, a second group oxidises nitrite to nitrate (NO₃⁻).
[bio34-s2.5.1] Nitrifying bacteria are aerobic: they need oxygen. Nitrification is therefore fastest in well-drained, well-aerated soil. Ploughing and draining help it, while waterlogging (soil with all its air spaces filled with water) stops it.
[bio34-s2.5.2] Nitrifying bacteria are autotrophic (they make their own food): they get energy from these chemical reactions (chemosynthesis) rather than from light or from eating other organisms.
[bio34-s2.5.3] Nitrate is the form plants absorb most. It is also very soluble and is not held by soil particles, so heavy rain easily washes it out of the soil (leaching) into ground water, rivers and lakes. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s2.6.b] Nitrification builds up nitrate in the soil. Denitrification is the opposite journey, returning nitrogen to the air and completing the cycle.
[bio34-s2.6.0] Denitrification is the conversion of nitrate in the soil back into nitrogen gas (N₂) by denitrifying bacteria. This returns nitrogen to the atmosphere and completes the cycle.
[bio34-s2.6.1] Denitrifying bacteria work in anaerobic conditions (no oxygen), such as waterlogged, compacted or poorly drained soils and the mud of lakes and marshes. They use nitrate in place of oxygen in respiration.
[bio34-s2.6.2] Denitrification removes usable nitrogen from the soil, lowering fertility, so farmers drain wet land to stop it.
[bio34-s2.6.3] It also gives off some nitrous oxide (N₂O), a greenhouse gas (one that traps heat in the atmosphere). There is more on this in Climate change and biological strategies.
[bio34-s2.6.4] In wetlands and water treatment, denitrification is useful because it removes excess nitrate before it reaches rivers.
[bio34-s2.7.b] Fixation, decomposition, nitrification and denitrification are the four named processes of the cycle. Comparing them side by side shows what each converts, who does it, and what it needs.
[bio34-s2.7.0] Fixation: N₂ becomes ammonium. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living or in nodules) do it. It is anaerobic inside a nodule, and it adds usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.7.1] Decomposition: organic nitrogen becomes ammonium. Bacteria and fungi do it. It is aerobic or anaerobic, and it recycles nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.7.2] Nitrification: ammonium becomes nitrite and then nitrate. Nitrifying bacteria do it. It needs oxygen, and it makes the form plants use most.
[bio34-s2.7.3] Denitrification: nitrate becomes N₂. Denitrifying bacteria do it. It needs no oxygen, and it removes usable nitrogen.
[bio34-s2.8.b] A model is useful because it lets you work out what happens when conditions change. It also has limits, and exams may ask you to name them.
[bio34-s2.8.0] Prediction (a): if a field becomes waterlogged, nitrification stops and denitrification increases, so soil nitrate falls and plants become nitrogen-deficient.
[bio34-s2.8.1] Prediction (b): if a farmer grows clover, fixation increases, so there is more soil nitrogen the next season.
[bio34-s2.8.2] Prediction (c): heavy rain after fertiliser is spread leaches nitrate into a river. There is more on this in Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s2.8.3] Limitation: the model shows the processes but not their rates or amounts.
[bio34-s2.8.4] Limitation: it does not show where nitrogen is lost, such as by leaching or by ammonia escaping from slurry (liquid animal waste), unless these are added.
[bio34-s2.8.5] Limitation: real soils contain many more microorganisms and steps than the four named groups. There is more on general limitations of models in Models.
[bio34-s2.9.b] The nitrogen cycle is modelled as a labelled diagram: boxes for the stores of nitrogen and arrows for the processes that move nitrogen between them.
[bio34-s2.9.0] Arrows for the gains of usable nitrogen: fixation (air → nodules → plant; air → free-living bacteria → soil ammonium); lightning (air → nitrate); Haber process and fertiliser (air → soil nitrate).
[bio34-s2.9.1] Arrows for the movement and recycling of nitrogen: absorption (soil nitrate → plants); feeding (plants → animals); death and excretion (→ dead matter); decomposition (dead matter → ammonium); nitrification (ammonium → nitrite → nitrate); denitrification (nitrate → air).
[bio34-s2.9.2] Diagram: boxes for nitrogen gas in air; plants (proteins); animals (proteins); dead organisms and wastes (faeces, urine); soil ammonium; nitrite; soil nitrate; legume root nodules. Join them with the labelled arrows.

CANDIDATES bio34-s3: Ethics and sustainability of nutrient cycling
[bio34-s3.0.b] Nutrient cycles run naturally, but people now speed them up and shift material between stores. That raises two kinds of question: will the environment cope in future, and is it fair who gains and who pays?
[bio34-s3.0.0] Humans now move huge amounts of carbon and nitrogen between stores. Burning fossil fuels and clearing forests and bogs moves carbon into the air, and making and spreading fertiliser, and keeping large numbers of livestock, adds fixed nitrogen (nitrogen in usable compounds, not nitrogen gas) to soils and water.
[bio34-s3.0.1] A sustainability issue arises when a practice damages the environment's ability to support future generations. An ethical issue arises when people disagree about what is right, especially who gets the benefits and who bears the costs.
[bio34-s3.0.2] Ethical questions to ask of every issue: who benefits and who is harmed, now, elsewhere and in the future; who should pay to prevent or repair the harm, under the polluter pays principle (the polluter meets the cost), which is part of EU environmental law.
[bio34-s3.0.3] Also ask: whether the harm is fair (justice); whether we should act before harm is proven (the precautionary principle); and whether the people affected have a say.
[bio34-s3.1.b] Eutrophication is the enrichment of rivers, lakes or coastal water with nutrients, mainly nitrate and phosphate, leading to excessive growth of algae and plants and a loss of oxygen. It is the main way nutrient pollution harms water, and the later issues keep returning to it.
[bio34-s3.1.0] Nitrate is leached from fertilised fields after rain. Leached means washed down through the soil into streams and groundwater, and nitrate is very soluble, so it is lost easily.
[bio34-s3.1.1] Slurry (a liquid mix of animal dung and urine) and farmyard manure are washed off the land, especially if spread before heavy rain or on wet or frozen ground, because the soil cannot absorb them.
[bio34-s3.1.2] Untreated or poorly treated sewage and septic tanks release nutrients, as do phosphate-containing detergents and runoff from forestry and peat extraction.
[bio34-s3.1.3] Phosphate is as important as nitrate: it is a major cause of eutrophication and is often the main one in lakes, so nitrogen is not the only culprit.
[bio34-s3.2.b] The harm to fish is not caused by the nutrients directly. It follows a chain, and the key link is that decomposers (organisms that break down dead matter) use up the oxygen.
[bio34-s3.2.0] 1. Extra nitrate and phosphate enter the water.
[bio34-s3.2.1] 2. Algae grow very rapidly, forming an algal bloom that turns the water green and covers the surface.
[bio34-s3.2.2] 3. The bloom blocks light, so rooted plants and the algae below it die.
[bio34-s3.2.3] 4. Algae have short lives, so huge numbers die.
[bio34-s3.2.4] 5. Aerobic decomposers (bacteria) feed on the dead algae and plants, multiply and respire, using up dissolved oxygen, so the water's biochemical oxygen demand (the oxygen it needs for decomposition) rises.
[bio34-s3.2.5] 6. Dissolved oxygen falls, so fish, especially salmon and trout, which need high oxygen, and invertebrates die.
[bio34-s3.2.6] 7. The decomposition of the dead fish and plants uses still more oxygen, and anaerobic decomposition (without oxygen) may release foul-smelling gases.
[bio34-s3.2.7] Diagram: flow chart of the sequence. Labels: nutrients in, algal bloom, plants below die, algae die, decomposers increase, oxygen falls, fish die.
[bio34-s3.3.b] Some blooms do more than starve the water of oxygen. They produce poisons, which turns a wildlife problem into a human one.
[bio34-s3.3.0] Some blooms are of blue-green algae, which are bacteria, not true algae. They make toxins harmful to animals, pets and people, so lakes may be closed for swimming, and the cost of treating drinking water rises.
[bio34-s3.3.1] Example: in summer 2023, Lough Neagh in Northern Ireland, the largest freshwater lake in Ireland and Britain, had widespread blue-green algal blooms. They were linked to excess nutrients from farming and wastewater, and to an unusually warm summer.
[bio34-s3.4.b] To evaluate nitrogen fertiliser you weigh its benefits against its costs to water, air and climate, and against fairness between those who gain and those who pay. First, the case for it.
[bio34-s3.4.0] Nitrogen is usually the nutrient that limits crop and grass growth (the one in shortest supply, so plants grow only as fast as it allows), so fertiliser greatly raises yields.
[bio34-s3.4.1] Food grown with fertiliser made by the Haber process, the industrial process that makes ammonia from nitrogen in the air, feeds about half of the world's people.
[bio34-s3.4.2] Higher yields mean less land must be cleared for farming, which spares forests and wild habitats.
[bio34-s3.4.3] In Ireland, grass-based livestock farming depends on nitrogen for grass growth, and farming supports rural incomes and exports.
[bio34-s3.5.b] Nitrogen that crops do not take up does not disappear. It escapes into water, where it causes the problems of eutrophication and a health risk in drinking water.
[bio34-s3.5.0] Leached nitrate and washed-off slurry cause eutrophication, harming fisheries, angling, tourism and wildlife.
[bio34-s3.5.1] Nitrate in drinking water above the EU limit of 50 mg per litre is a health risk, especially to bottle-fed babies, because it reduces the blood's ability to carry oxygen. This is called blue baby syndrome.
[bio34-s3.5.2] Water above the limit must then be treated or the source abandoned, which is a cost to everyone who relies on it.
[bio34-s3.6.b] Fertiliser also causes harm away from water. Making it, using it and keeping livestock all add gases to the air, and some of the nitrogen falls back onto wild land.
[bio34-s3.6.0] Making fertiliser by the Haber process uses a lot of energy, mainly from natural gas, and releases CO₂.
[bio34-s3.6.1] Extra soil nitrate increases denitrification (bacteria converting nitrate to nitrogen gases), which releases nitrous oxide (N₂O), a powerful greenhouse gas.
[bio34-s3.6.2] Ammonia escapes from slurry and urine. In Ireland agriculture produces about 99% of ammonia emissions.
[bio34-s3.6.3] Ammonia falling on bogs, heaths and woods acts as a fertiliser, letting a few fast-growing plants crowd out species adapted to low nutrients, which reduces biodiversity.
[bio34-s3.6.4] Ammonia also forms fine particles in the air that harm human health.
[bio34-s3.7.b] Fertiliser pollution is an ethical issue because the people who gain are not always the people who pay. Both sides have a fair point.
[bio34-s3.7.0] The farmer gains the yield, but others pay the cost: downstream communities, anglers, people drinking the water and future generations.
[bio34-s3.7.1] The polluter pays principle therefore argues that farmers should bear the cost of prevention.
[bio34-s3.7.2] Against this, farm incomes are often low and food must stay affordable, and consumers who want cheap food share responsibility.
[bio34-s3.7.3] Rules must be fair to small farmers as well as large ones, who would find the same cost harder to meet.
[bio34-s3.7.4] Justice: globally, many poor farmers use too little fertiliser and suffer low yields and hunger, while richer regions use too much. The same chemical is a benefit in one place and a pollutant in another.
[bio34-s3.8.b] Because nitrate pollution crosses farm boundaries, the EU sets limits that Ireland must apply. These are the rules to know.
[bio34-s3.8.0] The EU Nitrates Directive (1991) requires member states to protect water from farm nitrate.
[bio34-s3.8.1] Under it, livestock manure may not add more than 170 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year. Ireland has had a derogation (special permission to go above this) allowing some intensive farms more, under stricter conditions.
[bio34-s3.8.2] Slurry and fertiliser may not be spread in a winter closed period or near watercourses, and farms must have enough slurry storage so that slurry is not spread at the wrong time.
[bio34-s3.8.3] Ireland applies these rules through its Nitrates Action Programme.
[bio34-s3.9.b] Farmers can cut nitrogen losses without giving up fertiliser. Each response works for a biological reason, and together they lead to a judgement.
[bio34-s3.9.0] Test soil and apply only the nitrogen the crop needs, when it is growing, and not before heavy rain, because excess nitrogen is lost to water.
[bio34-s3.9.1] Grow clover or other legumes with grass, and legumes in crop rotation, so that nitrogen fixation (bacteria turning nitrogen gas into usable nitrogen) replaces some fertiliser.
[bio34-s3.9.2] Leave uncultivated buffer strips of vegetation beside rivers, which absorb nutrients before they reach the water.
[bio34-s3.9.3] Spread slurry close to the ground using low-emission methods, such as a trailing shoe, to cut ammonia loss.
[bio34-s3.9.4] Protect wetlands, where denitrification removes nitrate, and treat sewage fully.
[bio34-s3.9.5] Judgement: nitrogen fertiliser is needed to feed the world, so banning it would be unethical. But applying more than crops can use wastes money and transfers the costs to others and to the future.
[bio34-s3.9.6] The sustainable and fair position is to use less fertiliser, use it more precisely, and recycle nitrogen in manure and legumes.
[bio34-s3.10.b] Instead of losing nutrients in waste, we can return them to the soil. This is the core idea of sustainable nutrient cycling.
[bio34-s3.10.0] Sustainable nutrient cycling means returning nutrients from wastes to the soil instead of losing them. Methods include slurry and manure used as fertiliser at the right time and rate, and composting of garden and food waste.
[bio34-s3.10.1] In composting, decomposers (bacteria and fungi) turn organic matter into a nutrient-rich soil improver.
[bio34-s3.10.2] Other methods include treated sewage sludge spread on land, and anaerobic digestion (breakdown without oxygen) of slurry and food waste.
[bio34-s3.10.3] Anaerobic digestion produces biogas (methane) for fuel and a digestate (the solid left over) that is used as fertiliser.
[bio34-s3.11.b] Recycling looks like an easy win, but it has limits. A good evaluation gives both sides and then a judgement.
[bio34-s3.11.0] For: it cuts the need for manufactured fertiliser and the fossil energy used to make it.
[bio34-s3.11.1] For: it keeps wastes out of landfill, where they would decompose anaerobically and release methane.
[bio34-s3.11.2] For: it improves soil structure and soil organic matter, which is a carbon store.
[bio34-s3.11.3] Against: manure and sludge can carry pathogens, and sludge may contain heavy metals and other pollutants, so it must be treated and tested.
[bio34-s3.11.4] Against: spreading at the wrong time causes the same pollution as fertiliser does.
[bio34-s3.11.5] Against: anaerobic digesters are costly to build, and leaks of biogas release methane.
[bio34-s3.11.6] Against: many people are uneasy about food grown on land treated with sewage sludge.
[bio34-s3.11.7] Judgement: recycling is more sustainable than manufactured fertiliser alone when it is treated, tested and spread at the right time and rate.
[bio34-s3.12.b] Forests hold carbon in wood and soil and take it in from the air. Clearing them reverses this and raises ethical questions about who has the right to use the land.
[bio34-s3.12.0] Tropical forests are cleared, often by burning, mainly for cattle ranching (for example in the Amazon in Brazil), for soya grown largely as animal feed, and for oil-palm plantations (for example in Indonesia and Malaysia).
[bio34-s3.12.1] Deforestation turns a carbon sink (a store that takes in more carbon than it gives out) into a source, removes nutrients with the crop, exposes soil to erosion, and destroys habitats of very high biodiversity.
[bio34-s3.12.2] For clearing: poorer countries argue that they have the same right to develop their land as richer countries had when they cleared their own forests, and that farming gives jobs and food.
[bio34-s3.12.3] Against clearing: the losses (carbon released, species extinct, rainfall changed) affect the whole world and future generations, and indigenous people who live in the forests lose their homes and way of life.
[bio34-s3.12.4] Consumers in richer countries share responsibility, because they buy the beef, soya-fed meat and palm oil.
[bio34-s3.12.5] Responses: protected areas; payments to countries for keeping forests; and EU rules that ban imports of products linked to recent deforestation.
[bio34-s3.13.b] Bogs are a store of carbon, and their habitats cannot be replaced on any human timescale. In Ireland, cutting and draining them is a debate about tradition, jobs and climate.
[bio34-s3.13.0] Peatlands cover about a fifth of Ireland's land area. For centuries turf was cut by hand for household fuel. In the twentieth century bogs were drained and milled by Bord na Móna for electricity and horticultural compost.
[bio34-s3.13.1] Drained peat decomposes aerobically (with oxygen) and releases CO₂, so it is lost as a carbon store. Raised bogs are also rare habitats for specialised species such as sphagnum moss.
[bio34-s3.13.2] For continuing to cut peat: families have traditional rights to cut turf on their bog (turbary rights). Turf is a cheap local fuel and part of rural culture, and peat extraction gave jobs to Midlands communities.
[bio34-s3.13.3] Against cutting peat: burning peat releases more CO₂ per unit of energy than coal. Bog habitats cannot be replaced on any human timescale, and the climate cost falls on everyone and on the future.
[bio34-s3.14.b] Ireland has begun to end peat extraction, but doing so is not free of conflict. The same fairness questions apply on a global scale to fossil fuels.
[bio34-s3.14.0] Bord na Móna ended peat harvesting in January 2021.
[bio34-s3.14.1] Cutting on protected raised bogs (Special Areas of Conservation) is restricted, which has caused conflict with turf-cutting families.
[bio34-s3.14.2] Bogs are being rewetted, and a Just Transition fund supports workers and communities that depended on peat.
[bio34-s3.14.3] Judgement on peat: fairness to the people who lose income is part of the judgement, and it must be weighed against the carbon and habitat losses.
[bio34-s3.14.4] Fossil fuels as an ethical issue: the countries that industrialised first have released most of the CO₂ in the atmosphere, while many of the people most harmed by the resulting climate change, in low-lying and poor countries, have contributed least.
[bio34-s3.14.5] Future generations will bear costs they did not choose, which is intergenerational justice (fairness between generations). There is more on the biological strategies to respond in Climate change and biological strategies.
