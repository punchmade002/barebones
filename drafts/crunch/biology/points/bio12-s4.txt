TARGET bio12-s4: The importance of the theory
[bio12-s4.0.b] In science, a theory is a well-tested explanation, not a guess. Evolution by natural selection (the process by which better-suited individuals survive and reproduce more) meets every part of that definition.
[bio12-s4.0.0] Evolution by natural selection is a broad explanation of a huge range of observations, including the diversity of life, adaptation, shared features, the fossil record and the distribution of species, so one idea accounts for many different kinds of evidence.
[bio12-s4.0.1] It is supported by evidence from many independent sources and it makes testable predictions. It could be shown false by evidence, but no such evidence has been found.
[bio12-s4.0.2] It is falsifiable (open to being proved wrong). Finding fossils of mammals in rocks older than the first fish, or finding that the DNA of closely related species was no more similar than that of distant species, would contradict it.
[bio12-s4.0.3] The everyday meaning of theory (a guess or hunch) is not the scientific meaning. Saying evolution is only a theory misunderstands the word: in science, a theory is the highest status an explanation reaches.
[bio12-s4.0.4] The cell theory (all living things are made of cells) and the germ theory of disease (microorganisms cause many diseases) are theories in the same sense, and nobody calls them guesses.
[bio12-s4.0.5] Two parts should be distinguished. The fact of evolution is that living things have changed over time and share common ancestors, which the evidence shows directly. The theory of natural selection is the explanation of how that change happens.
[bio12-s4.1.b] Acceptance of evolution rests on how often it has been tested and how well it has survived. Each new kind of evidence was a chance for it to fail, and it did not.
[bio12-s4.1.0] It has been tested for over 160 years, since 1859 (the year Darwin published On the Origin of Species), by scientists in every field of biology, and also in geology, chemistry and physics, for example radiometric dating of the Earth.
[bio12-s4.1.1] Evidence that was not known to Darwin has confirmed it: genetics (Mendel's work, recognised from 1900), the structure of DNA (1953), molecular comparisons of DNA and proteins, and many transitional fossils (fossils showing features intermediate between two groups).
[bio12-s4.1.2] Each new kind of evidence could have contradicted the theory, and did not. This is why independent confirmation counts for so much.
[bio12-s4.1.3] Its predictions have been confirmed. Tiktaalik, a transitional fossil, was found in rocks of the age the theory predicted, and trees built from DNA match those built from anatomy.
[bio12-s4.1.4] Evolution can also be seen happening: bacteria evolve resistance to new antibiotics and insects evolve resistance to pesticides, as the theory predicts.
[bio12-s4.1.5] It is accepted by almost all biologists and by scientific academies around the world, and is the framework used in all biological research. Acceptance is based on evidence, not on authority or majority opinion.
[bio12-s4.1.6] Like all theories, it has been refined: it was combined with genetics into the modern synthesis. Scientists still debate details such as how fast evolution happens and the roles of chance and of epigenetics (chemical changes that switch genes on or off without changing the DNA sequence).
[bio12-s4.1.7] Debate about details within the theory is normal science, not doubt about evolution itself.
[bio12-s4.2.b] Darwin was not the first to propose that species change. Comparing his explanation with an earlier rival, Lamarck's, shows why natural selection is the better one.
[bio12-s4.2.0] Lamarck's theory (Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, 1809) said that organisms change during their lives through the use or disuse of body parts, and pass these acquired characteristics to their offspring. For example, giraffes stretching to reach leaves would have offspring with longer necks.
[bio12-s4.2.1] Acquired characteristics are not passed on through DNA, the molecule that carries inherited information, so they do not reach the next generation. A weightlifter's children are not born with large muscles.
[bio12-s4.2.2] In the 1880s August Weismann cut off the tails of mice over several generations, yet their offspring still had normal tails. This was direct evidence against inheritance of acquired characteristics.
[bio12-s4.2.3] Natural selection works with variation that already exists in a population, so it explains adaptation without any need for the organism to try. It is also supported by genetics.
[bio12-s4.2.4] Darwin's explanation of the giraffe: giraffes with slightly longer necks, through inherited variation, reached more food in times of shortage, so they survived and reproduced more and passed on the alleles (versions of a gene) for longer necks.
[bio12-s4.2.5] Epigenetic inheritance is a small, limited exception, in which some chemical changes to genes can be passed on. It does not restore Lamarck's theory.
[bio12-s4.3.b] A scientific explanation has to be testable. Comparing natural selection with special creation shows why evolution counts as the best available explanation.
[bio12-s4.3.0] Special creation, the belief that species were created separately in their present forms, is a matter of religious faith rather than a scientific theory, because it makes no testable predictions.
[bio12-s4.3.1] Special creation does not explain the evidence, such as homologous structures (similar bone layouts in different species), vestigial structures (reduced structures with little or no use), the order of fossils, or the pattern of DNA similarity.
[bio12-s4.3.2] Many religious people and organisations accept evolution. Science and faith answer different kinds of questions, so accepting one does not require rejecting the other.
[bio12-s4.3.3] Evolution by natural selection explains all the evidence together, with one mechanism, and makes predictions. No other explanation does this, so it is the best available explanation.
[bio12-s4.4.b] Evolution is not just something that happened in the distant past. It can be observed today, especially in organisms with short generations (so changes build up quickly) and large populations (so there is plenty of variation).
[bio12-s4.4.0] Examples of evolution observed now include antibiotic-resistant bacteria such as MRSA, the Galápagos finches' beaks after the 1977 drought, the peppered moth, and the new cordgrass species Spartina anglica.
[bio12-s4.4.1] Viruses evolve fast. New variants of the virus that causes COVID-19 (SARS-CoV-2), such as Delta and Omicron, spread because their mutations helped them spread or escape immunity, so vaccines were updated.
[bio12-s4.4.2] Flu vaccines are updated every year for the same reason: the flu viruses change, so last year's vaccine may no longer match.
[bio12-s4.4.3] Pests and weeds evolve resistance to the pesticides and herbicides used against them, because the few resistant individuals survive and breed.
[bio12-s4.4.4] Cancer cells in a tumour can evolve resistance to the drugs used to treat them, by the same process of selection.
[bio12-s4.4.5] Human activity is now a strong selection pressure (a factor that decides which individuals survive): antibiotic and pesticide use, climate change and habitat loss. Highly specialised species with slow reproduction are least able to evolve fast enough.
[bio12-s4.5.b] Evolution is the idea that ties the rest of biology together. The geneticist Theodosius Dobzhansky wrote in 1973 that “nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution”.
[bio12-s4.5.0] It explains the unity of life (shared cells, DNA, genetic code and ATP, from common ancestry) and its diversity (adaptation and speciation, the formation of new species).
[bio12-s4.5.1] Classification: modern classification is based on evolutionary relationships (phylogeny), so species are grouped by how closely they are related, and new DNA evidence keeps refining it.
[bio12-s4.5.2] Form and function: it explains why structures suit their functions, since natural selection favoured individuals whose structures did the job better.
[bio12-s4.5.3] Genetics and technology: comparing the genomes (complete sets of DNA) of different species to find which genes are shared and what they do depends on evolutionary relationships. There is more on this in Bioinformatics: genome databases.
[bio12-s4.5.4] Limits of the theory: it explains how life changed and diversified once it existed. How the first living cells arose from non-living chemicals is a separate question, still being researched, and is not part of the theory of natural selection.
[bio12-s4.6.b] Evolutionary thinking has practical uses. It helps people in medicine, agriculture and conservation to predict and manage change in living things.
[bio12-s4.6.0] Medicine and health: it guides the responsible use of antibiotics to slow resistance (antibiotic stewardship), the yearly updating of flu vaccines, the tracking of new virus variants, and the treatment of cancers that evolve drug resistance.
[bio12-s4.6.1] Because of shared ancestry, studies of other organisms can tell us about human biology and disease. For example, mice share many genes with humans.
[bio12-s4.6.2] Agriculture and food: selective breeding of crops and animals (artificial selection, where people choose which individuals breed), and managing pesticide use to slow resistance.
[bio12-s4.6.3] Keeping varied crop genes protects against disease, because a variable crop is unlikely to be wiped out by one pathogen. This is the lesson of the Irish potato.
[bio12-s4.6.4] Conservation and sustainability: it shows why genetic variation in a population matters for its survival when the environment changes, since some individuals may carry alleles that suit the new conditions.
[bio12-s4.6.5] Small, inbred populations of endangered species are at risk because they have little variation. Specialised species are vulnerable to climate change and habitat loss because they cannot adapt quickly.

CANDIDATES bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio1-s1: The nature of scientific knowledge
[bio1-s1.0.b] Science is both a body of knowledge and a process for building it. It explains the natural world using evidence gathered by observation and experiment, and that evidence requirement is what separates scientific claims from opinions.
[bio1-s1.0.0] A scientific claim must be testable against evidence from the natural world. Questions of value or morals, such as “should we clone animals?”, can be informed by science but cannot be answered by it, because no experiment can measure what is right or wrong.
[bio1-s1.0.1] Scientific knowledge is evidence-based (empirical): ideas are accepted because they agree with observations and experimental results, not because of who proposed them. A famous name does not make an idea correct.
[bio1-s1.0.2] An observation is what is directly detected with the senses or instruments, such as “the broth went cloudy”. An inference is an explanation drawn from an observation, such as “microorganisms grew in the broth”. Scientists keep them separate, as an inference can be wrong when the observation is right.
[bio1-s1.1.b] Scientific ideas are never declared final, yet the best of them last for generations. Two ideas explain why: ideas are open to revision, and they must be capable of being shown wrong.
[bio1-s1.1.0] Scientific knowledge is tentative but durable: any idea can be revised if new evidence contradicts it, yet well-tested ideas survive for a long time. The cell theory (the idea that all living things are made of cells) has survived more than 150 years of testing.
[bio1-s1.1.1] Tentative does not mean weak or a guess. It means the idea is held open to revision, while still being backed by a large amount of evidence.
[bio1-s1.1.2] An idea must be falsifiable: it must be possible, in principle, to imagine an observation that would show the idea is wrong. The philosopher Karl Popper made falsifiability his test of whether an idea is scientific.
[bio1-s1.1.3] Evidence can support an idea or refute it, but it can never prove an idea true for all time, because a later observation might contradict it. One reliable contrary result can disprove an idea.
[bio1-s1.2.b] Textbooks sometimes present “the scientific method” as a fixed recipe, but real science is messier. The first half of the usual cycle takes a scientist from noticing something to having data.
[bio1-s1.2.0] There is no single fixed scientific method. The process is often complex and iterative (it loops back on itself), following many different paths, but it usually involves the stages below.
[bio1-s1.2.1] 1. Observation: the scientist notices something in the natural world.
[bio1-s1.2.2] 2. Question: the scientist asks why or how it happens.
[bio1-s1.2.3] 3. Hypothesis: a proposed, testable explanation is put forward.
[bio1-s1.2.4] 4. Prediction: the scientist works out what should be found if the hypothesis is correct.
[bio1-s1.2.5] 5. Experiment or further observation: the prediction is tested.
[bio1-s1.2.6] 6. Collection and analysis of data: results are gathered and worked on to see what they show.
[bio1-s1.3.b] Once the data is in, the work is not finished. The second half of the cycle checks the result and shares it, so that other scientists can judge it.
[bio1-s1.3.0] 7. Conclusion: the hypothesis is judged to be supported or rejected by the data.
[bio1-s1.3.1] 8. Repetition and checking: the work is repeated, so that one odd result is not relied on.
[bio1-s1.3.2] 9. Comparison with existing knowledge: the result is set against what is already known.
[bio1-s1.3.3] 10. Publication in a scientific journal after peer review, which means other experts check the work before it is published.
[bio1-s1.3.4] 11. Testing of the findings by other scientists, who try to confirm or challenge them.
[bio1-s1.3.5] 12. Over time, well-supported findings are developed into a theory or law.
[bio1-s1.3.6] A rejected hypothesis leads to a new or modified hypothesis, so the cycle starts again. This is why the process is a loop and not a straight line.
[bio1-s1.3.7] Diagram: the cycle drawn as a loop with arrows. Labels: observation, question, hypothesis, prediction, experiment, data analysis, conclusion, repeat and check. Arrow from “hypothesis rejected” back to “new hypothesis”; “publish and peer review” leading out to “theory or law”.
[bio1-s1.4.b] The cycle suggests that every investigation is a laboratory experiment, but that is only one way of testing ideas. Science also needs imagination.
[bio1-s1.4.0] Not all science is experimental. Some fields test ideas by observation and comparison, for example fossil records and comparative anatomy as evidence for evolution, or field surveys of ecosystems.
[bio1-s1.4.1] Science is creative: imagining hypotheses and designing ways to test them takes imagination, and chance observations can start new lines of work, such as the discovery of penicillin, the standard example. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio1-s1.5.b] No scientist works alone for long. Science is a shared activity in which claims are expected to face challenge.
[bio1-s1.5.0] Scientists work in teams, build on others’ published work and expect their claims to be checked and challenged, because a claim that survives checking is more reliable.
[bio1-s1.5.1] Scepticism means doubting a claim until the evidence for it is good. It is a norm of science, not a personal attack on the scientist. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio1-s1.6.b] Scientists are human, so the way science is done reflects the world they live in.
[bio1-s1.6.0] Science is influenced by the society, culture, funding and technology of its time, which affect what questions get investigated and what can be measured.
[bio1-s1.6.1] Individual scientists can be biased. There is more on the types of bias in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio1-s1.7.b] The scientific method is powerful, but it has limits. Knowing them helps you judge how far a result can be trusted.
[bio1-s1.7.0] Limited by current knowledge and technology: some questions cannot yet be investigated. Cells could not be observed until microscopes were invented, and the internal structure of organelles could not be observed until electron microscopes were available.
[bio1-s1.7.1] The basis of the investigation may be wrong: if the theory or assumption a hypothesis rests on is false, the conclusions may be false too.
[bio1-s1.7.2] Human error and interpretation: data can be misread, and scientists can interpret the same results in different ways.
[bio1-s1.7.3] Experimental design: a poorly designed experiment can give results that seem to support a hypothesis when they do not. There is more on this in Designing and conducting investigations.
[bio1-s1.7.4] Applying results to nature: conditions in a laboratory are simpler and more controlled than in a living organism or an ecosystem, so results may not apply outside the laboratory. A drug that works on cells in a dish may not work in a person.
[bio1-s1.7.5] Science cannot answer questions of value, ethics or the supernatural, and it cannot prove a hypothesis absolutely, because evidence can only support or refute an idea.
[bio1-s1.8.b] A hypothesis is the first step from an observation towards an explanation. It is the lowest-status kind of scientific idea, since it has yet to be tested.
[bio1-s1.8.0] A hypothesis is a proposed, testable explanation for an observation. It makes predictions that can be checked by experiment or observation.
[bio1-s1.8.1] A hypothesis may be supported or rejected; it is never proved.
[bio1-s1.8.2] Example: in the early 1980s Robin Warren and Barry Marshall proposed that stomach ulcers are caused by infection with the bacterium Helicobacter pylori, not by stress and excess acid as was then believed. There is more on writing a testable hypothesis in Questioning and predicting.
[bio1-s1.9.b] In everyday speech “theory” means a hunch. In science it means the opposite: the most thoroughly tested kind of explanation.
[bio1-s1.9.0] A theory is a broad, well-tested explanation of a wide range of observations, supported by many confirmed hypotheses and by evidence from many independent sources.
[bio1-s1.9.1] A theory explains why something happens and makes predictions. It is the highest status an explanation reaches in science.
[bio1-s1.9.2] A theory is not a guess, so the everyday phrase “just a theory” misuses the word.
[bio1-s1.9.3] The cell theory states that all living things are made of one or more cells, that the cell is the basic unit of structure and function of life, and that all cells come from pre-existing cells.
[bio1-s1.9.4] Matthias Schleiden proposed in 1838 that plants are made of cells. Theodor Schwann extended this to animals in 1839. Rudolf Virchow added in 1855 that all cells arise from existing cells (“omnis cellula e cellula”).
[bio1-s1.9.5] Other biological theories include the theory of evolution by natural selection, proposed by Darwin and Wallace, and the germ theory of disease, which holds that many diseases are caused by microorganisms. There is more on the first in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
[bio1-s1.10.b] A law is a different kind of knowledge from a theory. Where a theory explains, a law describes a pattern.
[bio1-s1.10.0] A law, or principle, is a statement, often mathematical, that describes a pattern that always occurs under stated conditions. It describes what happens but does not explain why.
[bio1-s1.10.1] Biology has few laws because living systems are variable, so patterns rarely hold without exception.
[bio1-s1.10.2] Mendel’s laws of segregation and independent assortment describe how alleles (versions of a gene) pass to offspring. They have known exceptions, for example linked genes. There is more on this in Mendel's Laws.
[bio1-s1.10.3] The laws of thermodynamics apply to living things. Energy is not created or destroyed, only converted, as when light energy is converted to chemical energy in photosynthesis.
[bio1-s1.11.b] Examiners now ask you to tell these three apart. They differ in scope, purpose and the evidence behind them, and none is a stage on the way to another.
[bio1-s1.11.0] Scope: a hypothesis concerns one observation, a theory covers a wide range of observations, and a law covers one pattern.
[bio1-s1.11.1] Purpose: a hypothesis proposes an explanation, a theory gives a tested explanation, and a law describes a pattern.
[bio1-s1.11.2] Evidence: a hypothesis has little or none yet, a theory rests on a large body of evidence from many independent sources, and a law is repeated observation without exception under stated conditions.
[bio1-s1.11.3] All three can be revised or rejected if the evidence demands it.
[bio1-s1.11.4] A theory does not grow up into a law: they are different kinds of knowledge. A law describes a pattern and a theory explains it. A theory can explain a law; for example, Mendel’s laws are explained by the behaviour of chromosomes in meiosis.
[bio1-s1.11.5] A scientific fact is a repeatable observation, for example “DNA is found in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells”. A model is a simplified representation used to explain or predict. There is more on this in Models.
[bio1-s1.12.b] Because scientific knowledge is tentative, it changes over time. The cases that follow show this in action.
[bio1-s1.12.0] Ideas change because of new evidence that the old idea cannot explain, new technology that allows new observations (for example microscopes and DNA sequencing), reinterpretation of existing evidence, and replication (repeating of the work) by other scientists.
[bio1-s1.12.1] Change is usually gradual refinement. Occasionally a whole framework is replaced; the historian Thomas Kuhn called this a paradigm shift, in his book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (1962).
[bio1-s1.13.b] For a long time people believed that small creatures could appear from non-living matter. Two careful experiments, a couple of centuries apart, showed otherwise.
[bio1-s1.13.0] Old idea (spontaneous generation): living things such as maggots and microorganisms arise from non-living matter.
[bio1-s1.13.1] In 1668 Francesco Redi put meat in jars that were open, sealed or covered with gauze. Maggots appeared only on the meat in the open jars, which flies could reach.
[bio1-s1.13.2] In the gauze-covered jars, flies laid their eggs on the gauze, not on the meat, so no maggots appeared.
[bio1-s1.13.3] In the early 1860s Louis Pasteur boiled broth in swan-neck flasks, which have an S-shaped neck. Air could enter, but dust and microorganisms were trapped in the bend, and the broth stayed clear.
[bio1-s1.13.4] When the neck was broken off, or the broth was tipped into the bend, microorganisms grew in it. This showed the microorganisms came from the dust and not from the broth itself.
[bio1-s1.13.5] New idea: living things come only from other living things (biogenesis), which supported the germ theory.
[bio1-s1.14.b] The cell theory was not announced in one go. It grew as better microscopes let scientists observe more.
[bio1-s1.14.0] The cell theory (all living things are made of cells, the cell is the basic unit of life, and all cells come from pre-existing cells) was built up by several scientists over decades as microscopes improved.
[bio1-s1.14.1] It is still being extended. Mitochondria and chloroplasts have been found to contain their own DNA.
[bio1-s1.15.b] This case shows how a well-established belief was overturned by a bacterium that doctors thought could not exist in the stomach.
[bio1-s1.15.0] Old idea: ulcers are caused by stress, spicy food and excess acid, and no bacterium could survive stomach acid.
[bio1-s1.15.1] Warren observed curved bacteria in biopsies (small samples of tissue) of inflamed stomach linings. Marshall and Warren cultured (grew in the laboratory) Helicobacter pylori in 1982.
[bio1-s1.15.2] In 1984 Marshall drank a culture of the bacteria, developed gastritis (an inflamed stomach lining) and treated it with antibiotics.
[bio1-s1.15.3] Other scientists repeated the findings, and trials showed that antibiotics cured most ulcers.
[bio1-s1.15.4] New idea: most stomach ulcers are caused by H. pylori infection and are treated with antibiotics.
[bio1-s1.15.5] Marshall and Warren received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2005.
[bio1-s1.15.6] The case shows that a new idea is often resisted at first and is accepted when the evidence is reproduced.
[bio1-s1.16.b] In this case the evidence was clear, yet the idea was ignored for years because nobody could say why it worked.
[bio1-s1.16.0] In 1847 Ignaz Semmelweis, at Vienna General Hospital, noticed that far more mothers died of childbed (puerperal) fever on the ward run by doctors than on the ward run by midwives.
[bio1-s1.16.1] The doctors who ran that ward came from dissecting corpses, while the midwives did not.
[bio1-s1.16.2] He made staff wash their hands in chlorinated lime solution, and deaths fell sharply.
[bio1-s1.16.3] Many doctors rejected his idea because it lacked an explanation. It was accepted only after the germ theory of disease explained it.
[bio1-s1.16.4] The case shows that evidence without an explanatory theory can be ignored.
[bio1-s1.17.b] The same pattern of old idea, new evidence and refined idea appears again in other topics in this course.
[bio1-s1.17.0] The five-kingdom system of classifying living things was replaced by three domains and six kingdoms.
[bio1-s1.17.1] The lock-and-key model of enzyme action was refined to the induced-fit model, and the membrane “sandwich” model was replaced by the fluid mosaic model. There is more on these in Domains of life, Classification and Models.

CANDIDATES bio12-s3: Evidence for evolution
[bio12-s3.0.b] Fossils are the actual remains or traces of organisms that lived long ago. To use them as evidence you need to know how they form and how old they are.
[bio12-s3.0.0] A fossil is the preserved remains or traces of an organism that lived long ago, for example bones, shells, teeth, footprints or impressions of leaves.
[bio12-s3.0.1] Most fossils form when an organism is quickly buried in sediment (mud or sand) in water. Its soft parts decay, and minerals gradually replace the hard parts as the sediment becomes sedimentary rock (rock formed from compacted sediment).
[bio12-s3.0.2] Whole organisms are occasionally preserved in amber (hardened tree resin), ice or peat, which is why these finds are rare and valuable.
[bio12-s3.0.3] In undisturbed layers of sedimentary rock, deeper layers are older, because new sediment settles on top of old. So fossils in deeper layers are older than those above them.
[bio12-s3.0.4] Radiometric dating measures the decay of radioactive elements in rocks, and gives ages in years rather than just older or younger.
[bio12-s3.0.5] The Earth is about 4.6 billion years old. The oldest widely accepted fossils, of simple bacteria-like cells, are about 3.5 billion years old.
[bio12-s3.0.6] Diagram: a cross-section of rock layers with fossils, the deepest layer labelled oldest and the top labelled youngest, showing simpler forms in the lower layers; optionally, three horse feet from Hyracotherium to the modern horse showing toe reduction.
[bio12-s3.1.b] Laid out in order of age, the fossils show a pattern: life changes over time in a consistent direction. This is what evolution predicts and is why the record counts as evidence.
[bio12-s3.1.0] The oldest rocks contain only simple, single-celled life, so complex organisms had not yet appeared.
[bio12-s3.1.1] More complex organisms appear in younger rocks in a consistent order, for example fish before amphibians, and reptiles before birds and mammals.
[bio12-s3.1.2] Most species that ever lived are extinct, so the living world is only a small part of the history of life.
[bio12-s3.1.3] In many groups a series of fossils shows gradual change over time, which is what you would expect if one form slowly gave rise to another.
[bio12-s3.2.b] Some fossils show features of two different groups. These are called transitional fossils, and Archaeopteryx and Tiktaalik are two examples.
[bio12-s3.2.0] Archaeopteryx was found in Germany in 1861, two years after On the Origin of Species, and is about 150 million years old.
[bio12-s3.2.1] It had feathers and wings like a bird, but also teeth, a long bony tail and clawed fingers like a reptile (a dinosaur). So it shows features of both groups.
[bio12-s3.2.2] Tiktaalik was found in 2004 on Ellesmere Island in the Canadian Arctic and is about 375 million years old. It was a fish with scales, fins and gills.
[bio12-s3.2.3] It also had a flat head, a neck, and fin bones like the bones of a limb, so it was intermediate between fish and the first four-limbed land animals.
[bio12-s3.2.4] The team searched rocks of that age because the theory predicted such an animal should have lived then, and the prediction was confirmed. There is more on this in The importance of the theory.
[bio12-s3.3.b] A long series of horse fossils shows gradual change in one group. The record is also incomplete, so you need to know why gaps exist and why they do not weaken the case.
[bio12-s3.3.0] The horse series runs over more than 50 million years, from Hyracotherium, a small animal about the size of a dog with four toes on its front feet, to the modern horse.
[bio12-s3.3.1] The modern horse is much larger, stands on a single toe (the hoof), and has teeth suited to grazing. This matches a change from forest to open grassland.
[bio12-s3.3.2] The fossil record is incomplete, because fossils form only in rare conditions and soft-bodied organisms seldom fossilise.
[bio12-s3.3.3] Many fossils have also been destroyed by erosion or movements of the Earth, and many have not yet been found.
[bio12-s3.3.4] The gaps do not contradict evolution, and every new fossil found has fitted the expected order.
[bio12-s3.4.b] Comparing the bodies of different species shows similarities that are hard to explain unless the species share ancestors. Structures with the same basic plan but different jobs are called homologous.
[bio12-s3.4.0] Homologous structures have the same basic structure because they were inherited from a common ancestor, but are adapted for different functions.
[bio12-s3.4.1] The pentadactyl (five-digit) limb is the classic example: the human arm (grasping), bat wing (flying), whale flipper (swimming) and horse leg (running).
[bio12-s3.4.2] All of them have one upper bone (humerus), two lower bones (radius and ulna), wrist bones (carpals), palm bones (metacarpals) and finger bones (phalanges), differently shaped and sized.
[bio12-s3.4.3] This is divergent evolution: one ancestral form adapted in different directions, so the same bones were modified for different uses.
[bio12-s3.4.4] Diagram: the forelimbs of a human, a bat and a whale side by side, each bone group shaded the same way in all three; label humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals and phalanges.
[bio12-s3.5.b] Not every similarity points to a shared ancestor, and some structures have lost their original job. Analogous and vestigial structures show two different things.
[bio12-s3.5.0] Analogous structures have the same function but different structures and origins, for example the wing of a bird (bones and feathers) and the wing of an insect (a thin membrane of chitin with no bones).
[bio12-s3.5.1] Analogous structures are not evidence of common ancestry. They show that similar environments select similar solutions, which is called convergent evolution.
[bio12-s3.5.2] Vestigial structures are reduced, with little or no function, but were functional in the organism's ancestors.
[bio12-s3.5.3] Examples are the human coccyx (the remains of a tail), the human appendix (a much-reduced part of the gut, larger in plant-eating mammals), the small pelvic and leg bones inside some whales, and the tiny wings of flightless birds such as the kiwi.
[bio12-s3.5.4] Vestigial structures make sense only if the organism descended from ancestors that used them.
[bio12-s3.6.b] Embryos (animals in the earliest stages of development) can reveal ancestry that adult bodies hide. Vertebrates, the animals with backbones, are a good example.
[bio12-s3.6.0] Comparative embryology compares the development of embryos. The early embryos of all vertebrates (fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals) look very similar: all have a tail and a series of pouches in the throat region (pharyngeal pouches).
[bio12-s3.6.1] In fish these pouches develop into gills; in humans they develop into parts of the ear, jaw and throat, and the tail is reduced to the coccyx.
[bio12-s3.6.2] The shared early stages suggest that all vertebrates inherited the same basic plan of development from a common ancestor, which was then modified in each group.
[bio12-s3.6.3] Limitations: the similarity is in early development, not in adult structures, and embryos do not pass through the adult forms of their ancestors.
[bio12-s3.6.4] In the 19th century Ernst Haeckel published drawings that exaggerated the similarity of embryos; modern photographs confirm the shared features but not the exaggeration.
[bio12-s3.6.5] Diagram: early embryos of a fish, a chicken and a human side by side, labelled tail and pharyngeal pouches.
[bio12-s3.7.b] Evolution predicts that the more closely two species are related, the more similar their DNA and proteins should be. Comparing these molecules tests that prediction independently of anatomy.
[bio12-s3.7.0] Phylogeny is the evolutionary history of a group; phylogenetic trees show which species share the most recent common ancestors. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s3.7.1] Species closely related by anatomy and fossils should also have the most similar DNA and proteins, because they have had less time to accumulate differences since their common ancestor.
[bio12-s3.7.2] All living things use DNA, the same four bases, almost the same genetic code and the same ATP (the energy-carrying molecule of cells). This is evidence that all life shares one origin.
[bio12-s3.7.3] Human and chimpanzee DNA is about 98–99% identical in comparable sequences; human DNA is less similar to that of a mouse, and less again to that of a fish.
[bio12-s3.7.4] Cytochrome c is a protein used in aerobic respiration in nearly all eukaryotes (organisms whose cells have a nucleus). Its amino-acid sequence is identical in humans and chimpanzees.
[bio12-s3.7.5] It differs by one amino acid between humans and rhesus monkeys, and differs more in more distantly related organisms such as yeast.
[bio12-s3.7.6] The trees built from DNA and proteins largely agree with those built from anatomy and fossils. Independent lines of evidence giving the same pattern are strong support for evolution.
[bio12-s3.7.7] Molecular evidence has also corrected some classifications based on appearance, for example the domains of life (the three largest groupings of living things).
[bio12-s3.8.b] Bacteria can become resistant to antibiotics, and the way this happens is natural selection taking place in front of us. Once you can describe the steps, you can explain it in an exam.
[bio12-s3.8.0] An antibiotic is a drug that kills or stops the growth of bacteria; antibiotics have no effect on viruses. Antibiotic resistance is the ability of bacteria to survive an antibiotic that would normally kill them.
[bio12-s3.8.1] 1. A bacterial population varies: a few bacteria carry a mutation (or a resistance gene on a plasmid, a small ring of DNA) that makes them resistant, for example by producing an enzyme that breaks down the antibiotic.
[bio12-s3.8.2] 2. The antibiotic kills the non-resistant bacteria.
[bio12-s3.8.3] 3. The resistant bacteria survive and, with less competition, reproduce rapidly by binary fission (splitting in two), as often as every 20 minutes in ideal conditions.
[bio12-s3.8.4] 4. They pass the resistance gene to their offspring, and can pass plasmids carrying it to other bacteria, even of other species.
[bio12-s3.8.5] 5. The population soon becomes mostly resistant, and the antibiotic no longer works.
[bio12-s3.8.6] Diagram: four panels of a bacterial population: mostly non-resistant cells with a few resistant ones (shaded), then antibiotic added and non-resistant cells die, then resistant cells multiply, then the population is mostly resistant.
[bio12-s3.9.b] A common mistake is to think antibiotics create resistance. The evidence shows that resistance is already there, and that selection simply favours it.
[bio12-s3.9.0] The resistance mutations are present before the antibiotic is used: the antibiotic selects them but does not cause them.
[bio12-s3.9.1] In 1952 Joshua and Esther Lederberg showed this by growing bacteria that had never met the antibiotic and finding resistant colonies already among them.
[bio12-s3.9.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many common antibiotics and causes infections that are hard to treat, especially in hospitals.
[bio12-s3.9.3] Natural selection is observed directly, within years, because bacteria have huge populations and very short generation times.
[bio12-s3.9.4] Overuse of antibiotics, for example prescribing them for viral infections or in farm animals, speeds up resistance by applying selection more often. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio12-s3.10.b] Natural selection can be seen in wild populations too, and the way species are spread across the world supports common descent.
[bio12-s3.10.0] Peter and Rosemary Grant studied the medium ground finch on the Galápagos island of Daphne Major. In a drought in 1977, small soft seeds became scarce and mainly large, hard seeds were left.
[bio12-s3.10.1] Most finches died, but birds with deeper (larger) beaks, which could crack the hard seeds, survived more, and their offspring had, on average, deeper beaks than the generation before.
[bio12-s3.10.2] Natural selection was measured happening in one generation.
[bio12-s3.10.3] Biogeography (the distribution of species): species on oceanic islands resemble those on the nearest mainland rather than those on islands with similar conditions elsewhere. The Galápagos species resemble South American species.
[bio12-s3.10.4] This is explained by colonisation from the mainland followed by evolution, not by separate creation for each environment.
[bio12-s3.11.b] Speciation is the formation of new species, and it is evidence that evolution can produce the diversity of life. One route to speciation is when a population is split by a physical barrier.
[bio12-s3.11.0] A species is a group that can interbreed to produce fertile offspring, so a new species has formed when two populations can no longer interbreed: they are reproductively isolated. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio12-s3.11.1] 1. A population is split by a barrier, such as a sea, a mountain range or a river, so the two groups no longer interbreed.
[bio12-s3.11.2] 2. Different mutations arise in each group.
[bio12-s3.11.3] 3. The environments differ, so natural selection favours different characteristics in each.
[bio12-s3.11.4] 4. Over many generations the two populations become so different that they could not interbreed even if they met; they are now separate species.
[bio12-s3.11.5] Diagram: a flow diagram of geographical speciation: one population, then a barrier divides it, then different mutations and selection on each side, then two species that can't interbreed.
[bio12-s3.12.b] The finches of the Galápagos show geographical speciation in a real place. Different islands acted as separate environments.
[bio12-s3.12.0] More than a dozen species of finch on the Galápagos are descended from a common ancestor that arrived from the South American mainland.
[bio12-s3.12.1] Populations on different islands, eating different foods, evolved beaks of different shapes, for example large, deep beaks for cracking seeds and thin, pointed beaks for catching insects.
[bio12-s3.12.2] Why it is evidence: new species have been seen to form, and the pattern of species (groups of similar species on neighbouring islands) is what the theory predicts.
[bio12-s3.13.b] A barrier is not always needed. In plants, a change in the number of chromosomes can make a new species almost at once.
[bio12-s3.13.0] In plants, a new species can form in one or a few generations when a change in chromosome number (polyploidy, having extra sets of chromosomes) makes offspring unable to breed with the parent species. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s3.13.1] The cordgrass Spartina anglica arose in southern England in the late 1800s from a cross between a native European cordgrass and an introduced American one.
[bio12-s3.13.2] The chromosomes then doubled, which made the hybrid fertile. It is now a separate, widespread species, including on Irish coasts.
[bio12-s3.14.b] Each source of evidence has weak points, but together they make a strong case because they are independent of each other.
[bio12-s3.14.0] Each source has limitations: an incomplete fossil record, embryos similar only early on, and analogous structures that can mislead.
[bio12-s3.14.1] The sources are independent of each other and all point to the same conclusion: common descent with change over time, driven largely by natural selection.
[bio12-s3.14.2] Antibiotic resistance, the finch beaks, the peppered moth and new species such as Spartina anglica show evolution happening now. There is more on the peppered moth in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
