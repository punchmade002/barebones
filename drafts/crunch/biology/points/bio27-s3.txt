TARGET bio27-s3: Chemical breakdown
[bio27-s3.0.b] Food is made of large molecules that are too big to pass into the blood. Chemical digestion cuts them into small units using enzymes (biological catalysts made by the body).
[bio27-s3.0.0] Chemical digestion is the hydrolysis of large food molecules into their small, soluble units by digestive enzymes: each bond is broken by adding a molecule of water, which is what hydrolysis means.
[bio27-s3.0.1] Carbohydrates become simple sugars, proteins become amino acids, and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. These small units are soluble and small enough to be absorbed.
[bio27-s3.0.2] Each digestive enzyme is specific to one type of substrate (the molecule it acts on) and works best at its optimum pH, the pH of the part of the gut where it acts.
[bio27-s3.0.3] The gut changes pH along its length: neutral in the mouth, acid in the stomach and alkaline in the small intestine. This suits the different enzymes working in each place.
[bio27-s3.0.4] Most digestive enzymes are released from glands into the gut cavity, so digestion there is extracellular, meaning it happens outside cells.
[bio27-s3.1.b] Digestion begins in the mouth, where food is mixed with saliva. Saliva moistens the food and also starts the digestion of starch.
[bio27-s3.1.0] Saliva is produced by the three pairs of salivary glands and released into the mouth.
[bio27-s3.1.1] The pH in the mouth is about 7 (neutral to slightly alkaline), which is the optimum for salivary amylase, the enzyme in saliva.
[bio27-s3.1.2] Salivary amylase digests starch to maltose (a sugar made of two glucose units). The product of an amylase is maltose.
[bio27-s3.1.3] Food is in the mouth only briefly, so only a little starch is digested there. Stomach acid denatures (destroys the shape of) the amylase soon after it reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s3.1.4] Saliva also contains water, which dissolves food so that it can be tasted and moistens it, and mucus, which lubricates swallowing.
[bio27-s3.1.5] Saliva also contains lysozyme, an enzyme that kills some bacteria.
[bio27-s3.2.b] In the stomach, food meets a strongly acidic fluid called gastric juice. It begins protein digestion and also protects the body from bacteria that arrive in food.
[bio27-s3.2.0] Gastric juice is produced by gastric glands in the stomach lining and released into the stomach. It contains hydrochloric acid, pepsin (released as inactive pepsinogen) and mucus.
[bio27-s3.2.1] The pH in the stomach is about 2 (strongly acidic), which is the optimum for pepsin.
[bio27-s3.2.2] Hydrochloric acid kills most bacteria in food, which is a defence against infection. It also provides the acid pH that pepsin needs.
[bio27-s3.2.3] Hydrochloric acid converts inactive pepsinogen to active pepsin, and it stops salivary amylase, which cannot work at this pH.
[bio27-s3.2.4] Pepsin is a protease (a protein-digesting enzyme): it hydrolyses peptide bonds inside protein chains, breaking proteins into shorter chains called polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.2.5] The product of a protease is polypeptides. Complete protein digestion needs further enzymes in the small intestine to give amino acids.
[bio27-s3.2.6] Pepsin is made in the inactive form pepsinogen so that it does not digest the gastric gland cells that make it. It becomes active only in the stomach cavity.
[bio27-s3.2.7] A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus barrier fails, the lining is damaged, causing a stomach ulcer.
[bio27-s3.3.b] Fat digestion needs help before enzymes can act, and the acid from the stomach has to be dealt with. Bile does both jobs when food enters the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s3.3.0] Bile is produced by the liver, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, and released through the bile duct into the duodenum when chyme (the partly digested, acidic food mixture leaving the stomach), especially fatty chyme, arrives.
[bio27-s3.3.1] Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments and sodium hydrogencarbonate. It contains no enzymes.
[bio27-s3.3.2] Bile salts emulsify fats, which means they break large fat droplets into tiny droplets. This is a physical change, not a chemical one. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s3.3.3] Sodium hydrogencarbonate is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach. This raises the pH to suit the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes and stops the action of pepsin.
[bio27-s3.3.4] Bile pigments are excretory wastes from the breakdown of haemoglobin from old red blood cells in the liver. They give faeces their brown colour.
[bio27-s3.4.b] The pancreas adds a second fluid to the duodenum, one that carries most of the enzymes needed to digest starch, protein and fat. Because the duodenum is alkaline, these enzymes work best there.
[bio27-s3.4.0] Pancreatic juice is produced by the pancreas and released through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. It contains sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralises acid, and three enzymes: pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.4.1] Pancreatic amylase digests the remaining starch (the starch not digested in the mouth) to maltose.
[bio27-s3.4.2] Trypsin is a protease, released in an inactive form and activated in the duodenum. It digests proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptides.
[bio27-s3.4.3] Lipase digests fats and oils (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. The product of a lipase is fatty acids and glycerol, not fatty acids alone.
[bio27-s3.4.4] Lipase acts on the tiny droplets made by emulsification, because they give it a much larger surface to work on.
[bio27-s3.4.5] The pH in the duodenum and small intestine is about 8 (slightly alkaline), because of the sodium hydrogencarbonate in bile and pancreatic juice. This suits pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.5.b] The last stage of digestion is done by enzymes made by the cells lining the small intestine. They finish breaking down the sugars and peptides that earlier enzymes produced.
[bio27-s3.5.0] The cells lining the villi (the tiny finger-like projections of the small intestine) make enzymes that complete digestion.
[bio27-s3.5.1] Many of these enzymes are held in the membranes of the lining cells, so the last step of digestion happens right at the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s3.5.2] Maltase digests maltose to two glucose molecules. Sucrase digests sucrose to glucose and fructose. Lactase digests lactose to glucose and galactose.
[bio27-s3.5.3] Peptidases digest peptides to amino acids.
[bio27-s3.5.4] These enzymes work at a pH of about 8, the same alkaline conditions as the duodenum.
[bio27-s3.5.5] Lactose intolerance: people who stop making lactase after childhood cannot digest lactose, so bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, causing bloating, wind and diarrhoea. There is more on this in Variation and Diet.
[bio27-s3.6.b] This table pulls together where each enzyme is made, where it acts, its pH and its products. Each line has the same pattern so you can compare them quickly.
[bio27-s3.6.0] Salivary amylase – made in salivary glands – acts in mouth – pH about 7 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.1] Pepsin (protease) – made in gastric glands of stomach wall – acts in stomach – pH about 2 – protein → polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.6.2] Pancreatic amylase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.3] Trypsin (protease) – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – proteins and polypeptides → peptides.
[bio27-s3.6.4] Lipase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.6.5] Maltase – made in the wall of the small intestine – acts there – pH about 8 – maltose → glucose.
[bio27-s3.6.6] Peptidases – made in the wall of the small intestine – act there – pH about 8 – peptides → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.6.7] Bile salts (not an enzyme) – made in liver, stored in gallbladder – act in duodenum – emulsify fats.

[bio27-s3.7.0] Carbohydrate model: starch is digested by salivary amylase in the mouth (pH 7) to maltose. The remaining starch is digested by pancreatic amylase in the duodenum (pH 8) to maltose. Maltose is digested by maltase in the small intestine wall to glucose.
[bio27-s3.7.1] Sucrose and lactose are digested by sucrase and lactase in the small intestine wall. Cellulose is not digested, because humans make no enzyme for it, so it passes on as fibre.
[bio27-s3.7.2] Protein model: protein → (pepsin, stomach, pH 2) → polypeptides → (trypsin, duodenum, pH 8) → shorter peptides → (peptidases, small intestine wall) → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.7.3] Fat model: large fat droplets → (bile salts, duodenum: emulsification, a physical change) → tiny droplets → (lipase from the pancreas, duodenum, pH 8) → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.7.4] Fat is digested only in the small intestine, after emulsification.
[bio27-s3.7.5] Using the models to predict: if the bile duct is blocked, for example by gallstones, fats are poorly emulsified and digested, and undigested fat appears in the faeces.
[bio27-s3.7.6] If the pancreas releases too little juice, as in cystic fibrosis, digestion of all three nutrients is reduced, which is why patients take enzyme capsules. There is more on this in Industrial enzymes.

[bio27-s3.8.b] Visking tubing is a simple physical model of the small intestine. It shows why food must be digested into small molecules before it can pass into the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.0] Visking (dialysis) tubing is a selectively permeable membrane: it lets small molecules (water, glucose and maltose) through but not large ones (starch). It models the wall of the small intestine, and the water around it models the blood.

CANDIDATES bio27-s1: The digestive system
[bio27-s1.0.b] Humans can't make their own food, so every meal has to be taken in, broken down and moved into the body before cells can use it. This happens in four stages: ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.
[bio27-s1.0.0] Humans are heterotrophs, meaning they obtain food from other organisms rather than making it. They are also omnivores: they eat both plant and animal material.
[bio27-s1.0.1] Ingestion is taking food into the body through the mouth, which is where every meal enters the gut.
[bio27-s1.0.2] Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. It is both mechanical (physical breakdown into smaller pieces) and chemical (hydrolysis, the splitting of large molecules by enzymes).
[bio27-s1.0.3] Digestion is necessary because large molecules such as starch, proteins and fats cannot pass through the wall of the gut or through cell membranes. It converts them to small soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.
[bio27-s1.0.4] Cells then use the absorbed molecules to build the body's own molecules or for respiration. This use of absorbed molecules is called assimilation: for example, amino acids are built into proteins and glucose is respired to release energy.
[bio27-s1.0.5] Absorption is the passage of the small soluble products of digestion, together with water, vitamins and minerals, through the wall of the gut into the blood or lymph. It happens mainly in the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.0.6] Egestion is the removal of undigested and unabsorbed material, mainly dietary fibre, as faeces through the anus. This material is left over because the body cannot digest or absorb it.
[bio27-s1.0.7] Egestion is not excretion. Faeces were never part of the body's metabolism (the chemical reactions inside cells), except for bile pigments, whereas excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells.
[bio27-s1.1.b] The alimentary canal, or gut, is the pathway food follows through the body. Its muscular wall and lining are built to move food along and to protect the gut itself.
[bio27-s1.1.0] The alimentary canal, or gut, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. Food passes in order through the mouth, pharynx (throat), oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus.
[bio27-s1.1.1] The gut wall contains two layers of smooth muscle, circular and longitudinal, which move food along by peristalsis (waves of muscle contraction that push food onwards).
[bio27-s1.1.2] The inner lining of the gut makes mucus. Mucus lubricates the food so it slides along, and protects the lining from digestive enzymes and acid, which would otherwise damage the gut's own cells.
[bio27-s1.1.3] Sphincters are rings of muscle that control movement between parts of the gut, for example at the entrance and exit of the stomach (the exit one is the pyloric sphincter) and at the anus.
[bio27-s1.2.b] The first three organs prepare food and carry it onwards. The mouth breaks it up and starts starch digestion, the oesophagus transports it, and the stomach stores and churns it while starting protein digestion.
[bio27-s1.2.0] The mouth is where food is ingested and chewed by the teeth, then mixed with saliva from three pairs of salivary glands. The tongue shapes it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. Starch digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.1] The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries food by peristalsis from the pharynx, down through the chest behind the trachea (windpipe) and through the diaphragm (the sheet of muscle below the lungs), to the stomach. No digestion or absorption happens in it.
[bio27-s1.2.2] During swallowing the epiglottis (a flap) covers the entrance to the trachea, so food goes down the oesophagus and not into the lungs.
[bio27-s1.2.3] The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the upper left of the abdomen, just below the diaphragm. It stores food for a few hours, which means a meal can be eaten quickly and released to the intestine gradually.
[bio27-s1.2.4] The stomach churns food with gastric juice (acid and a protease, an enzyme that digests protein) into a thick liquid called chyme. The acid kills most bacteria in food, and protein digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.5] The pyloric sphincter at the stomach's exit releases chyme a little at a time into the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.3.b] After the stomach, chyme enters the small intestine, where digestion is finished and most absorption happens. What is left passes to the large intestine, which recovers water and stores the waste until it leaves the body.
[bio27-s1.3.0] The small intestine is the longest part of the gut, coiled in the abdomen. Its first part, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas, and most chemical digestion happens there.
[bio27-s1.3.1] The later parts, the jejunum and the ileum, complete digestion and absorb most digested food and water through millions of villi (tiny finger-like projections of the lining that give a large surface for absorption).
[bio27-s1.3.2] The large intestine is wider but shorter than the small intestine; "large" refers to its width, not its length. Its parts are the caecum, with the appendix attached, the colon and the rectum.
[bio27-s1.3.3] The appendix is a small dead-end tube with no digestive role in humans, so food is not digested in it.
[bio27-s1.3.4] The colon absorbs water and some minerals from the remaining material, and is home to many bacteria. It is not an excretory organ, because what leaves is egested, not excreted.
[bio27-s1.3.5] The rectum is the last part of the large intestine, which stores faeces until egestion, so waste does not have to leave the body continuously.
[bio27-s1.3.6] The anus is the opening at the end of the gut, closed by sphincter muscles. The outer sphincter is under voluntary control, so egestion can be delayed until it is convenient.
[bio27-s1.4.b] Some organs help digestion without food ever entering them. They make secretions that reach the gut through ducts (narrow tubes), so they are called associated organs.
[bio27-s1.4.0] The salivary glands are three pairs, around the mouth and below the jaw, releasing saliva into the mouth through ducts. Saliva contains water, mucus and the enzyme salivary amylase.
[bio27-s1.4.1] The liver is the largest internal organ, in the upper right of the abdomen just below the diaphragm. Its digestive role is to make bile.
[bio27-s1.4.2] The liver also receives the absorbed products of digestion through the hepatic portal vein (the vein carrying blood from the gut to the liver), stores glycogen and deaminates amino acids (removes their nitrogen-containing part).
[bio27-s1.4.3] The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile. It squeezes bile through the bile duct into the duodenum when fatty food arrives there. It makes no bile itself.
[bio27-s1.4.4] The pancreas is a soft, pinkish gland behind the stomach, lying in the curve of the duodenum. It makes pancreatic juice, which contains enzymes for starch, protein and fat and sodium hydrogencarbonate, and this flows through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum.
[bio27-s1.4.5] The pancreas is also an endocrine gland (a ductless gland that releases hormones into the blood) making insulin, so it has both ducted (exocrine) and ductless (endocrine) roles.
[bio27-s1.4.6] The liver, gallbladder and pancreas are associated organs: food never passes through them, and their secretions reach the gut through ducts. Food does pass through the mouth, stomach and intestines.
[bio27-s1.5.b] A labelled diagram shows how the gut and its associated organs sit in relation to each other. Getting the positions right matters as much as the labels.

[bio27-s1.6.b] Following one mouthful from start to finish pulls the stages of nutrition together. Each part of the gut has its own main job.
[bio27-s1.6.0] Mouth: ingestion; mechanical and chemical digestion begin.
[bio27-s1.6.1] Oesophagus: transport of food to the stomach, because the mouth and stomach are too far apart for food to move unaided.
[bio27-s1.6.2] Stomach: storage and churning; protein digestion begins.
[bio27-s1.6.3] Duodenum: most chemical digestion, with bile and pancreatic juice.
[bio27-s1.6.4] Ileum: digestion is completed and absorption takes place.
[bio27-s1.6.5] Colon: absorption of water, so the remaining waste becomes more solid.
[bio27-s1.6.6] Rectum: storage of faeces.
[bio27-s1.6.7] Anus: egestion, the removal of faeces from the body.

CANDIDATES bio13-s2: Enzyme structure and specificity
[bio13-s2.0.b] An enzyme is a protein, and the way its chain is folded decides what it can do. Folding builds a small pocket called the active site (the region where the substrate, the molecule the enzyme acts on, binds and is converted).
[bio13-s2.0.0] An enzyme's three-dimensional shape is set by the sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain (its primary structure), and that sequence is set by its gene, so the gene ultimately decides the shape.
[bio13-s2.0.1] The chain coils and folds (secondary structure), then folds further into a precise globular (rounded) shape (tertiary structure); some enzymes are made of more than one polypeptide (quaternary structure).
[bio13-s2.0.2] The active site is formed by folding: it is made of a small number of amino acids, often far apart in the chain, that folding brings together into a pocket or cleft.
[bio13-s2.0.3] Most of the rest of the enzyme holds these few active-site amino acids in exactly the right positions, so the whole molecule matters even though only a small part touches the substrate.
[bio13-s2.0.4] The R groups (side groups, the part of each amino acid that differs from the others) of the active-site amino acids give the site its shape and its pattern of charges and of water-attracting and water-repelling regions.
[bio13-s2.0.5] The R groups bind the substrate by weak attractions (hydrogen bonds and ionic attractions between opposite charges), and some take part directly in the reaction.
[bio13-s2.1.b] An enzyme acts only on particular substrates, a property called specificity. It comes from the active site's shape and chemistry, and that shape is only as stable as the bonds that hold the protein folded.
[bio13-s2.1.0] Specificity depends on two things: the shape of the active site (only a substrate of complementary shape fits) and its chemistry (only a substrate with matching charges and groups is held in the right position).
[bio13-s2.1.1] A molecule of similar shape but with the wrong charges is not catalysed, because its groups are not held in the correct position.
[bio13-s2.1.2] The shape is held by hydrogen bonds, by ionic bonds between charged R groups, and by attractions between water-repelling R groups; these are all weak bonds.
[bio13-s2.1.3] Disulfide (sulfur) bridges between cysteine units (cysteine is an amino acid that contains sulfur) are strong covalent bonds that also hold the shape.
[bio13-s2.1.4] Because most of these bonds are weak, the shape is easily changed, which is why enzymes are sensitive to conditions.
[bio13-s2.1.5] Diagram: a folded polypeptide chain drawn as a looping ribbon, with three or four amino acids (circles) from distant parts of the chain meeting to form a labelled active site; label polypeptide chain, R groups of active-site amino acids, active site, a hydrogen bond and a disulfide bridge.
[bio13-s2.2.b] An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its exact shape. Anything that alters the shape of the protein, or the amino acids that make it, changes how well it works.
[bio13-s2.2.0] Activity depends on the active site keeping its shape.
[bio13-s2.2.1] Heat makes the enzyme molecule vibrate until weak bonds break; a change in pH changes the charges on R groups (by adding or removing hydrogen ions), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds.
[bio13-s2.2.2] Either way the active site loses its shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured (permanently changed in shape and unable to work). The effects on rate, and the investigations, are in “Investigation: rate of enzyme activity”.
[bio13-s2.2.3] A change in a single amino acid can change the shape or charge of the active site, so a mutation in the gene can make an enzyme work less well or not at all.
[bio13-s2.2.4] Example: in phenylketonuria the enzyme that converts phenylalanine is faulty, so the conversion does not happen properly.
[bio13-s2.2.5] Some enzymes need a cofactor, such as a metal ion, held in or near the active site to complete its shape or chemistry; without it the enzyme is inactive.
[bio13-s2.3.b] Enzymes differ in how narrowly they choose a substrate. Some enzymes also have structures that keep their shape in conditions that would ruin most others.
[bio13-s2.3.0] Some enzymes act on only one substrate (absolute specificity): for example, urease acts only on urea, breaking it into ammonia and carbon dioxide.
[bio13-s2.3.1] Others act on a group of substrates that share the same type of bond (group specificity): for example, lipase acts on many different triglycerides, and proteases such as pepsin break peptide bonds in many different proteins.
[bio13-s2.3.2] A rigid lock-and-key active site does not explain group specificity well, because one fixed shape cannot fit many different substrates; the induced-fit model does.
[bio13-s2.3.3] Enzymes that work in extreme conditions have structures that hold their shape there: for example, Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, stays active at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s2.3.4] Pepsin keeps its shape in the strong acid of the stomach, where most enzymes are denatured.
[bio13-s2.4.b] The induced-fit model describes an active site that is flexible, not a fixed mould. It explains how an enzyme grips its substrate, strains it and releases the products, then is ready to go again.
[bio13-s2.4.0] The induced-fit model says the active site is flexible, not rigid. Before binding, its shape is close to, but not exactly, complementary to the substrate; binding of the substrate induces (causes) a change in the enzyme's shape that produces a close fit.
[bio13-s2.4.1] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and starts to bind to the active site through weak attractions to its R groups.
[bio13-s2.4.2] 2. Binding causes the enzyme to change shape: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove taking the shape of a hand, forming a tightly fitting enzyme–substrate complex.
[bio13-s2.4.3] 3. The change in shape puts strain on particular bonds in the substrate, or brings reacting groups of two substrates close together in the correct orientation.
[bio13-s2.4.4] 3 (continued). The R groups of the active site may lend or take hydrogen ions or electrons, which lowers the activation energy (the energy needed to start the reaction), and the substrate is converted to products.
[bio13-s2.4.5] 4. The products no longer fit the active site closely, so they are released.
[bio13-s2.4.6] 5. The enzyme returns to its original shape, unchanged and ready to bind another substrate molecule.
[bio13-s2.5.b] The earlier lock-and-key model treated the active site as rigid. Induced fit replaced it because it explains observations that a rigid site cannot.
[bio13-s2.5.0] Example: the enzyme hexokinase (used in the first stage of respiration) closes around its substrate glucose like a clamp when glucose binds; structural studies of enzymes showed this change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.1] Induced fit explains why some enzymes act on several similar substrates: a flexible site can mould around each of them.
[bio13-s2.5.2] Induced fit explains how binding itself helps catalysis: the shape change strains the substrate's bonds, lowering the activation energy.
[bio13-s2.5.3] Induced fit explains why molecules of similar shape may bind but not react: they do not induce the correct change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.4] Lock and key remains a useful simple model of specificity.
[bio13-s2.5.5] Diagram: induced fit in four labelled stages: (1) an enzyme whose active site does not quite match the approaching substrate; (2) substrate binding and the active site changing shape around it, forming the enzyme–substrate complex, with arrows showing the change.
[bio13-s2.5.6] Diagram (continued): (3) products formed in the active site; (4) products released and the enzyme back in its original shape. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, change in shape, enzyme–substrate complex and products.
[bio13-s2.5.7] Diagram (contrast): include a one-line sketch of lock and key, with a rigid active site that already fits the substrate exactly.
[bio13-s2.6.b] The two models differ in whether the active site can move. Setting them side by side shows why induced fit explains more.
[bio13-s2.6.0] Active site: rigid, with a fixed shape that exactly matches the substrate (lock and key); flexible, changing shape when the substrate binds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.1] Fit: an exact fit before binding (lock and key); a close fit only after binding (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.2] Role of binding: it holds the substrate only (lock and key); the change of shape itself helps the reaction by straining bonds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.3] Specificity explained: absolute specificity only (lock and key); both absolute and group specificity (induced fit), because a flexible site can mould around several similar substrates.

CANDIDATES bio8-s2: Structures of biomolecules
[bio8-s2.0.b] Carbohydrates are the first of the four main biomolecules, the large carbon-based molecules of living things. They share one simple pattern in their formula and fall into three groups by size.
[bio8-s2.0.0] Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. Their general formula is Cx(H₂O)y, so hydrogen and oxygen are in the ratio 2 : 1, as in water. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is an example.
[bio8-s2.0.1] The name means “hydrated carbon” because of that ratio, but the molecule does not contain water molecules. The H and O atoms are simply bonded into the sugar in the same 2 : 1 proportion.
[bio8-s2.0.2] There are three groups by size: monosaccharides (one sugar unit), disaccharides (two units) and polysaccharides (many units).
[bio8-s2.0.3] Mono- and disaccharides are sugars: sweet, soluble in water and crystalline.
[bio8-s2.0.4] Polysaccharides are not sweet and are insoluble or only slightly soluble, which suits them to storage and structure, because a molecule that does not dissolve stays put inside a cell or in a wall.
[bio8-s2.1.b] Monosaccharides are the simplest carbohydrates: a single sugar unit. They are the monomers (single building-block units) from which every other carbohydrate is made.
[bio8-s2.1.0] Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is the main respiratory substrate (the molecule broken down in respiration to release energy) and the product of photosynthesis.
[bio8-s2.1.1] Fructose, the fruit sugar found in fruit and honey, has the same formula, C₆H₁₂O₆, but a different arrangement of atoms. Two molecules can share a formula and still be different sugars.
[bio8-s2.1.2] Galactose also has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆ and is a unit of lactose, the sugar in milk.
[bio8-s2.1.3] Glucose, fructose and galactose are all six-carbon sugars (hexoses).
[bio8-s2.1.4] Ribose and deoxyribose are five-carbon monosaccharides (pentoses). Ribose is found in RNA nucleotides and deoxyribose in DNA nucleotides. There is more on this in DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code.
[bio8-s2.1.5] Diagram: glucose drawn as a simple six-sided ring (hexagon) labelled glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆. Atom-by-atom structural formulas are not needed.
[bio8-s2.2.b] Two monosaccharides can be joined to make a disaccharide. The way they join is the same reaction that later builds starch and other large molecules.
[bio8-s2.2.0] A disaccharide forms when two monosaccharides join by condensation, a reaction that releases one molecule of water. The link between the sugar units is a glycosidic bond.
[bio8-s2.2.1] Disaccharides have the formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, which is 2 × C₆H₁₂O₆ minus H₂O, because one water molecule is lost when the two units join.
[bio8-s2.2.2] Hydrolysis (splitting a bond by adding water) splits a disaccharide back into monosaccharides. It is the reverse of condensation.
[bio8-s2.2.3] Maltose (malt sugar) is glucose + glucose. It forms when starch is digested by amylase (an enzyme that breaks down starch) and is found in germinating grain.
[bio8-s2.2.4] Sucrose (table sugar, cane or beet sugar) is glucose + fructose. It is the form in which plants transport sugar in the phloem (the tissue that carries sugars around the plant).
[bio8-s2.2.5] Lactose (milk sugar) is glucose + galactose and is found in milk.
[bio8-s2.2.6] Diagram: two hexagons joined, with H₂O removed by condensation and returned by hydrolysis; label maltose = glucose + glucose.
[bio8-s2.3.b] A polysaccharide is a very long chain of sugar units. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all made of glucose, yet they do very different jobs because of how the units are linked and whether the chain branches.
[bio8-s2.3.0] A polysaccharide is a polymer (a long molecule made of many repeating units) of many monosaccharide units, often thousands, joined by condensation. Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all polymers of glucose; they differ in how the glucose units are linked and whether the chains branch.
[bio8-s2.3.1] Starch is the storage carbohydrate of plants, held as starch grains in cells of potato tubers, seeds and cereal grains. It is a mixture of amylose, an unbranched chain that coils into a helix, and amylopectin, a branched chain.
[bio8-s2.3.2] Being insoluble, starch does not draw water into cells by osmosis (water moving in towards a more concentrated solution), and its compact shape stores much glucose in a small space.
[bio8-s2.3.3] Glycogen is the storage carbohydrate of animals and fungi, stored mainly in the liver and muscles. It is more highly branched than amylopectin, so it has many chain ends from which glucose can be released quickly when energy is needed.
[bio8-s2.3.4] Cellulose is the structural carbohydrate of plants, forming the cell wall. Its glucose units are linked in a different orientation from starch, giving long, straight, unbranched chains that lie side by side. Many hydrogen bonds (weak attractions between neighbouring chains) hold them together, forming strong fibres.
[bio8-s2.3.5] Humans have no enzyme to digest cellulose, so it passes through the gut as dietary fibre (roughage). There is more on cellulose in Biomolecules and metabolism.
[bio8-s2.3.6] Chitin is a polysaccharide that contains nitrogen. It forms the cell walls of fungi and the exoskeletons (hard outer coverings) of insects and other arthropods.
[bio8-s2.3.7] Diagram: a chain of hexagons for starch (unbranched amylose and branched amylopectin), a more branched chain for glycogen, and parallel straight chains linked by cross-links for cellulose fibres.
[bio8-s2.4.b] Lipids are the second biomolecule group. The triglyceride, one of the main kinds, is built from just two kinds of smaller molecule joined by condensation.
[bio8-s2.4.0] Lipids are fats, oils and related compounds such as phospholipids. They contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, with much less oxygen in proportion to carbon and hydrogen than carbohydrates, so they are not of the form Cx(H₂O)y. Phospholipids also contain phosphorus.
[bio8-s2.4.1] Lipids are insoluble in water but dissolve in organic solvents such as ethanol.
[bio8-s2.4.2] A triglyceride is one glycerol molecule joined to three fatty acids. Glycerol is a small molecule with three carbons, each carrying an –OH group, so it can bond to three fatty acids.
[bio8-s2.4.3] A fatty acid is a long hydrocarbon chain of carbon and hydrogen atoms with an acid (carboxyl, –COOH) group at one end.
[bio8-s2.4.4] Each fatty acid joins to glycerol by condensation, so forming one triglyceride releases three water molecules. The bond formed is an ester bond.
[bio8-s2.4.5] Hydrolysis by lipase (an enzyme that digests fats) reverses this, giving glycerol and fatty acids. There is more on this in The Digestive System.
[bio8-s2.4.6] Diagram: a triglyceride drawn as an E shape, with a vertical glycerol bar and three horizontal fatty acid tails, labelled glycerol, fatty acid and 3 H₂O removed.
[bio8-s2.5.b] Fats and oils have the same basic unit, the triglyceride. They differ in the fatty acids attached, and that decides whether they are solid or liquid at room temperature.
[bio8-s2.5.0] There are many different fatty acids, varying in chain length and in whether they contain carbon–carbon double bonds. Glycerol combines with different fatty acids to give many different fats and oils.
[bio8-s2.5.1] Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds, so the chain holds the maximum hydrogen. Their straight chains pack closely, so fats rich in them are solid at room temperature.
[bio8-s2.5.2] Saturated examples are butter, lard and the fat on meat. These are mostly animal fats.
[bio8-s2.5.3] Unsaturated fatty acids have one or more C=C double bonds, which put kinks (bends) in the chain. Kinked chains pack loosely, so oils are liquid at room temperature.
[bio8-s2.5.4] Unsaturated examples are olive oil, sunflower oil and fish oils. These are mostly from plants and fish.
[bio8-s2.6.b] A phospholipid is a triglyceride with one change: a phosphate group takes the place of a fatty acid. That small change gives the molecule one end that likes water and one that avoids it.
[bio8-s2.6.0] A phospholipid is glycerol joined to two fatty acids and a phosphate-containing group in place of the third fatty acid.
[bio8-s2.6.1] The phosphate head is hydrophilic (attracted to water). The two fatty acid tails are hydrophobic (repelled by water).
[bio8-s2.6.2] In water, phospholipids arrange themselves in a double layer (bilayer), with the heads facing the watery fluid on each side and the tails pointing inwards, away from the water.
[bio8-s2.6.3] This bilayer is the basis of every cell membrane. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes and The Unit of Life — The Cell.
[bio8-s2.6.4] Diagram: a phospholipid drawn as a circle (head) with two wavy tails, labelled hydrophilic phosphate head and hydrophobic fatty acid tails; and a section of a bilayer.
[bio8-s2.7.b] Proteins are the third biomolecule group. Every protein is a chain built from the same 20 kinds of amino acid, joined by peptide bonds.
[bio8-s2.7.0] Proteins contain carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen, and sometimes sulfur, phosphorus or other elements. Sulfur is in some amino acids such as cysteine and methionine, phosphorus is in casein (the main protein of milk), and iron is in haemoglobin. There is more on this in Minerals.
[bio8-s2.7.1] Proteins are made of amino acids. There are 20 different amino acids in the proteins of living things.
[bio8-s2.7.2] Each amino acid has a central carbon atom bonded to an amino group (–NH₂), an acid (carboxyl, –COOH) group, a hydrogen atom and a variable side group (R group). Only the R group differs between amino acids.
[bio8-s2.7.3] Humans cannot make some amino acids (essential amino acids), so these must be taken in from food.
[bio8-s2.7.4] Amino acids join by condensation between the –COOH group of one and the –NH₂ group of the next, releasing water. The bond formed is a peptide bond.
[bio8-s2.7.5] Two amino acids form a dipeptide; a short chain is a peptide; a long chain is a polypeptide. A protein is one or more polypeptides folded into a particular shape.
[bio8-s2.7.6] The sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide (its primary structure) is set by a gene. Because 20 amino acids can be arranged in any order and length, a vast number of different proteins is possible.
[bio8-s2.7.7] Diagram: the general structure of an amino acid, a central carbon with –NH₂, –COOH, –H and –R attached; and two amino acids joined by a peptide bond with H₂O released.
[bio8-s2.8.b] A polypeptide chain does not stay straight. It folds into a precise shape, and that shape is what lets a protein do its job. Damage the shape and the job is lost.
[bio8-s2.8.0] The chain coils into a helix or folds into a pleated sheet, held by hydrogen bonds (secondary structure). This folds further into a precise three-dimensional shape (tertiary structure), held by bonds between R groups, including sulfur bridges between cysteine units.
[bio8-s2.8.1] Some proteins consist of several polypeptides fitted together (quaternary structure). Haemoglobin has four polypeptide chains.
[bio8-s2.8.2] The shape determines the function, because other molecules must fit it. An enzyme’s active site or an antibody’s binding site fits only particular molecules.
[bio8-s2.8.3] Heat, extremes of pH and some chemicals break the bonds holding the shape, so the protein loses its shape and function. This is denaturation, usually permanent, e.g. egg white turns solid and white when cooked. There is more on this in Enzymes.
[bio8-s2.8.4] Fibrous proteins are long strands, insoluble and structural, such as keratin (hair, nails) and collagen (skin, tendons).
[bio8-s2.8.5] Globular proteins are folded into compact, roughly spherical shapes and are usually soluble, e.g. enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin and insulin.
[bio8-s2.9.b] Nucleic acids are the fourth biomolecule group. Like the other groups, they are built from repeating units, which are called nucleotides.
[bio8-s2.9.0] Nucleic acids (DNA and RNA) are polymers of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group and a nitrogenous base, so nucleic acids contain C, H, O, N and P. There is more on this in DNA, RNA and the Genetic Code.
[bio8-s2.10.b] Putting the four groups side by side makes the differences easy to compare: which elements each contains, what its units are, which bond joins them and an example.
[bio8-s2.10.0] Carbohydrate: C, H, O; monosaccharides; glycosidic bond; glucose, sucrose, starch.
[bio8-s2.10.1] Lipid: C, H, O (plus P in phospholipids); glycerol and fatty acids; ester bond; triglycerides, phospholipids.
[bio8-s2.10.2] Protein: C, H, O, N (sometimes S, P or other elements); amino acids; peptide bond; enzymes, keratin.
[bio8-s2.10.3] Nucleic acid: C, H, O, N, P; nucleotides; DNA, RNA.

CANDIDATES bio27-s2: Mechanical breakdown
[bio27-s2.0.b] Before food can be absorbed it has to be broken down in two ways. Mechanical breakdown makes the pieces smaller; chemical breakdown then splits the molecules themselves. This subsection is about the first kind.
[bio27-s2.0.0] Mechanical (physical) digestion breaks food into smaller pieces, or fat into smaller droplets, without changing the molecules: no chemical bonds are broken and no new substances are formed. Chemical digestion, done by enzymes (biological catalysts that speed up reactions), does break bonds. There is more on this in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.0.1] Smaller pieces have a much larger total surface area, so enzymes can act on more of the food at once and digestion is faster. Think of how sugar dissolves faster as powder than as a lump.
[bio27-s2.0.2] Mechanical breakdown also makes food soft and moist enough to swallow, and mixes it with digestive juices (the watery secretions that contain enzymes), so the enzymes reach all of the food.
[bio27-s2.0.3] The four mechanisms are chewing by the teeth (mouth), peristalsis (the whole gut), churning (stomach), and emulsification of fats by bile salts (duodenum, the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s2.1.b] Digestion starts in the mouth, where the teeth cut and crush food into small pieces. Each type of tooth has a shape that suits its job.
[bio27-s2.1.0] Chewing (mastication) breaks food into small pieces and mixes it with saliva. The tongue rolls the moistened food into a bolus (a soft ball of food) for swallowing, and mucus in saliva lubricates the bolus so it slides down easily.
[bio27-s2.1.1] Incisors are at the front of the mouth. They are chisel-shaped with sharp, flat edges, which suits them for biting and cutting food.
[bio27-s2.1.2] Canines, one on each side of the incisors in each jaw, are pointed, so they grip and tear food. Human canines are small compared with those of carnivores (meat-eating animals).
[bio27-s2.1.3] Premolars and molars at the back are broad with ridged surfaces (cusps), for crushing and grinding food. Premolars have two cusps; molars are larger with more cusps.
[bio27-s2.1.4] The jaw moves sideways as well as up and down, so the broad surfaces of the molars can grind food rather than only crush it.
[bio27-s2.1.5] Diagram: (a) the four types of teeth side by side, labelled incisor, canine, premolar and molar, with their roles; (b) a vertical section of a molar labelled crown, root, enamel, dentine, pulp cavity, nerves and blood vessels, gum, cement and jawbone.
[bio27-s2.2.b] The teeth in your mouth can be summarised in a short code called the dental formula. Knowing how a tooth is built also shows why it is hard-wearing yet sensitive.
[bio27-s2.2.0] The dental formula gives the number of each type of tooth in one half of the upper jaw over one half of the lower jaw, in the order incisors, canines, premolars, molars.
[bio27-s2.2.1] The adult human dental formula is 2.1.2.3 over 2.1.2.3, so there are 16 teeth on each side of the mouth (8 in each quarter) and 32 teeth in total.
[bio27-s2.2.2] Child's milk (deciduous) teeth have the formula 2.1.0.2 over 2.1.0.2, giving 20 teeth in total. They have no premolars and only two molars in each quarter, so the adult set has 12 more teeth.
[bio27-s2.2.3] The third molars are the wisdom teeth. They appear last, often in the late teens or later, or not at all.
[bio27-s2.2.4] A tooth has a crown above the gum and a root fixed in the jawbone. Enamel, the hardest substance in the body, covers the crown, so it can withstand biting and chewing.
[bio27-s2.2.5] Dentine, a bone-like layer, lies beneath the enamel. The pulp cavity in the centre contains nerves and blood vessels, which is why a damaged tooth hurts. Cement holds the root in its socket.
[bio27-s2.3.b] Enamel is very hard, but acid can still dissolve it. Tooth decay is the result of bacteria turning the sugar in the mouth into acid.
[bio27-s2.3.0] Bacteria in plaque (a sticky film on the teeth) feed on sugars and release acid. The acid dissolves enamel and then dentine, forming a cavity.
[bio27-s2.3.1] Eating and drinking sugary food less often gives the bacteria less to feed on, so less acid is made.
[bio27-s2.3.2] Brushing with fluoride toothpaste removes plaque and hardens enamel. Drinking fluoridated water (water with fluoride added) also hardens enamel, so it resists the acid. There is more on fluoride in Minerals.
[bio27-s2.3.3] Regular dental check-ups let decay be found and treated early, before the cavity reaches the pulp cavity.
[bio27-s2.4.b] Food has to travel the whole length of the gut, even though the gut has no hands or pump. Peristalsis does this with waves of muscle, and it also helps break the food up.
[bio27-s2.4.0] Peristalsis is a wave of muscular contraction that passes along the wall of the gut, pushing food along. It happens from the oesophagus (the food pipe) to the rectum, and also in the ureters (the tubes from the kidneys to the bladder).
[bio27-s2.4.1] The gut wall has an inner layer of circular muscle and an outer layer of longitudinal muscle. Both are smooth, involuntary muscle, which means they work without conscious control.
[bio27-s2.4.2] 1. The circular muscle just behind the bolus contracts, narrowing the gut and squeezing the food forwards.
[bio27-s2.4.3] 2. The circular muscle just in front of the bolus relaxes, widening the gut to receive it. The wave then moves along, carrying the food with it.
[bio27-s2.4.4] Peristalsis is automatic (involuntary) once food is swallowed, and it works against gravity: a person can swallow while lying down or upside down, and the food still reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s2.4.5] Peristalsis also mixes food with digestive juices and breaks it up, so it is part of mechanical breakdown. In the small intestine it keeps moving the contents past the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s2.4.6] Dietary fibre adds bulk that the muscles can grip and push against, so a high-fibre diet keeps peristalsis effective and prevents constipation. There is more on this in Diet.
[bio27-s2.4.7] Diagram: a section of the gut showing a bolus with contracted circular muscle (narrow) behind it and relaxed muscle (wide) in front, with an arrow for the direction of movement; label circular muscle, longitudinal muscle, bolus, contraction and relaxation.
[bio27-s2.5.b] The stomach is a muscular bag that holds food for a few hours. Its walls churn the food, which is the third mechanical process after chewing and peristalsis.
[bio27-s2.5.0] The stomach wall has three layers of smooth muscle: longitudinal, circular and an oblique (diagonal) layer. Having muscle running in three directions means their contractions churn the food, breaking it up and mixing it thoroughly with gastric juice (the stomach's digestive secretion).
[bio27-s2.5.1] After a few hours of churning, food becomes chyme, a thick, creamy liquid. The sphincters (rings of muscle that close an opening) at the entrance and exit stay closed during churning, so food is held in the stomach.
[bio27-s2.5.2] The pyloric sphincter, at the exit of the stomach, relaxes now and then to let small amounts of chyme into the duodenum. This means the small intestine is never overloaded and has time to digest and neutralise each portion.
[bio27-s2.5.3] The stomach also has a chemical role, using acid and pepsin. There is more on this in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.6.b] Fat does not mix with water, so it forms large droplets in the watery gut. Bile salts solve this by breaking fat into tiny droplets, a mechanical step that gives the fat-digesting enzyme far more surface to work on.
[bio27-s2.6.0] Fats and oils do not mix with the watery contents of the gut, so they form large droplets. Lipase (the fat-digesting enzyme, which works in water) can therefore reach only the droplet surfaces.
[bio27-s2.6.1] Bile salts are made by the liver and enter the duodenum in bile. Each bile-salt molecule has one end that attracts water (hydrophilic) and one end that attracts fat (hydrophobic).
[bio27-s2.6.2] The bile salts gather on the surface of fat droplets with the fat-loving end inwards. With the churning of the gut they break the large droplets into many tiny droplets and stop them joining together again.
[bio27-s2.6.3] This is emulsification, and the mixture of tiny droplets in water is an emulsion.
[bio27-s2.6.4] Emulsification is mechanical: the fat molecules are unchanged and no bonds are broken. It greatly increases the surface area of fat exposed to lipase, so fat is digested much faster.
[bio27-s2.6.5] Bile contains no enzymes. Its other roles, neutralising stomach acid and excreting bile pigments (waste products), are in Chemical breakdown.
[bio27-s2.6.6] Washing-up liquid emulsifies grease in the same way, which is why bile salts are often compared to a detergent.
[bio27-s2.6.7] Diagram: one large fat droplet, then, after bile salts, many small droplets each coated with bile-salt molecules, labelled large surface area for lipase.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.

Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.
