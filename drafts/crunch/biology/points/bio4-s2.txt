TARGET bio4-s2: Explaining biological phenomena
[bio4-s2.0.b] Biology covers an enormous range of material, but a few big ideas keep turning up. Learning to use them as tools lets you explain almost any living thing or event, whichever chapter it comes from.
[bio4-s2.0.0] A biological phenomenon is any observable event or feature of living things, e.g. a person sweating on a hot day, bread going mouldy, or a population of rabbits crashing.
[bio4-s2.0.1] Explaining a phenomenon means giving its causes and mechanism (how it happens, step by step), not just describing what you can see. A description says what happened; an explanation says why and how.
[bio4-s2.0.2] Biologists explain phenomena using a small number of big ideas that apply across all of biology: systems; interdependence; the unity and diversity of life; form fits function; and the transfer of information, matter and energy.
[bio4-s2.0.3] The most important further idea is evolution by natural selection, which explains both unity and diversity. The list of big ideas is open-ended, and evolution is the one that ties the others together.
[bio4-s2.0.4] Evolution is the unifying explanation. All living things share common ancestors, so they share features (unity). Natural selection acting on variation in different environments has produced adaptations and many species (diversity).
[bio4-s2.1.b] A living thing is not a pile of separate parts. It is a set of parts that work together, and thinking of it as a system explains why a problem in one part can show up somewhere else.
[bio4-s2.1.0] A system is a set of interacting parts that work together as a whole. A system has inputs and outputs, and its behaviour depends on how the parts interact, not just on the parts themselves.
[bio4-s2.1.1] Properties that appear only from the interactions of parts are called emergent properties. For example, consciousness arises from interacting neurons (nerve cells), not from any single neuron.
[bio4-s2.1.2] Biological systems exist at every level of organisation: molecules, organelles (the working parts inside a cell), cells, tissues, organs, organ systems, organisms, populations, communities, ecosystems and the biosphere (all life on Earth together).
[bio4-s2.1.3] Examples: the circulatory system, in which the heart, vessels and blood together transport materials; the digestive system; the nervous system; an ecosystem, in which living and non-living parts interact; and a cell, whose organelles work together.
[bio4-s2.1.4] Systems are regulated by feedback. Negative feedback reverses a change and keeps the system stable, e.g. the control of blood glucose and of thyroxine (a hormone). There is more on this in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio4-s2.1.5] Systems thinking means looking at how a change in one part affects others. For example, a fall in red blood cell count (anaemia) reduces oxygen delivery, so cells respire less aerobically (using oxygen), causing tiredness.
[bio4-s2.1.6] Organ systems also depend on each other. Breathing supplies oxygen, which the circulatory system transports to respiring cells, so a fault in either system affects the same cells.
[bio4-s2.2.b] No organism lives alone. Each depends on other organisms and on its non-living surroundings, so a change to one reaches others, sometimes in surprising ways.
[bio4-s2.2.0] Interdependence means that organisms depend on each other and on their non-living environment, so a change to one affects others.
[bio4-s2.2.1] Feeding relationships in food webs are an example: removing a predator can let its prey increase and overgraze (eat too much of what they feed on).
[bio4-s2.2.2] Pollination of flowers by insects is an example: the insects get nectar, and the flowers get their pollen carried to other flowers. Seed dispersal by animals is another.
[bio4-s2.2.3] Decomposers recycle nutrients that plants need, so plants depend on them for the minerals they take up. This is part of the carbon and nitrogen cycles.
[bio4-s2.2.4] Nitrogen-fixing bacteria live in the root nodules (small swellings on the roots) of legumes such as clover. The gut microbiome (the community of microbes in the gut) and its human host also depend on each other.
[bio4-s2.2.5] Between organisms and the environment, plants depend on light, water, CO₂ and minerals.
[bio4-s2.2.6] Photosynthesis and respiration link organisms through the carbon cycle: producers release the oxygen that consumers use, and consumers release the CO₂ that producers use.
[bio4-s2.2.7] Within an organism, organ systems depend on each other. Muscles need the nervous system to stimulate them, and the circulatory system to supply glucose and oxygen.
[bio4-s2.3.b] Living things look wildly different, yet at the cellular and molecular level they share the same basic features. That sameness is evidence that they all descend from a common ancestor.
[bio4-s2.3.0] Unity means that all living things share basic features, which is evidence that they share a common ancestor.
[bio4-s2.3.1] All living things are made of cells, with a phospholipid membrane (the thin surface layer around the cell), cytoplasm (the jelly-like contents) and ribosomes (the structures that build proteins).
[bio4-s2.3.2] All use DNA as their genetic material. The genetic code is almost universal: the same codons (three-base units of the code) code for the same amino acids in nearly all organisms.
[bio4-s2.3.3] Because the code is shared, a human gene can be expressed (used to make its protein) in bacteria in genetic engineering. This is why the same code is read correctly in both.
[bio4-s2.3.4] All use ATP to transfer energy, all build proteins from the same set of 20 amino acids, and all use enzymes (biological catalysts).
[bio4-s2.3.5] All show the characteristics of life, the set of features that define a living thing.
[bio4-s2.4.b] If life shares so much, why are there so many different kinds? Diversity is the other half of the picture, and it has identifiable causes.
[bio4-s2.4.0] Diversity means the great variety of living things, at the levels of genes (variation within a species), species, and ecosystems (biodiversity).
[bio4-s2.4.1] Mutation (a change in DNA) creates new alleles (versions of a gene), which is where new variation first comes from.
[bio4-s2.4.2] Sexual reproduction, through meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes) and random fertilisation, shuffles alleles into new combinations. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio4-s2.4.3] Natural selection in different environments favours different adaptations, so populations in different places change in different directions. Populations that are separated can become new species.
[bio4-s2.4.4] Biologists organise diversity by classification into three domains (Bacteria, Archaea and Eukaryota) and six kingdoms, based increasingly on genetic evidence.
[bio4-s2.4.5] Unity and diversity are explained together by evolution: shared ancestry gives unity, and adaptation and speciation (the formation of new species) give diversity.
[bio4-s2.4.6] For example, the forelimbs of humans, bats and whales have the same set of bones, which shows unity from a common ancestor. They have different shapes for grasping, flying and swimming, which shows diversity from adaptation.
[bio4-s2.5.b] In living things, the shape or structure of a part (its form) matches the job it does (its function). Spotting that match, and saying how it works, is one of the most reliable ways to explain a feature.
[bio4-s2.5.0] Form fits function means that the structure of a molecule, cell, organ or organism suits the job it does. Natural selection explains why: individuals whose structures did the job better survived and reproduced more.
[bio4-s2.5.1] Adaptations do not arise because organisms need them. Variation already exists, and natural selection favours the better-adapted individuals.
[bio4-s2.5.2] Molecules: an enzyme’s active site (the part where the reaction happens) has a shape complementary to its substrate, so it catalyses one reaction. The DNA double helix, with complementary base pairs, allows accurate copying.
[bio4-s2.5.3] Red blood cells are biconcave discs with no nucleus, giving a large surface area and more room for haemoglobin to carry oxygen. Neurons have long axons and a myelin sheath that carry impulses quickly over long distances.
[bio4-s2.5.4] Root hair cells have long extensions that increase the surface area for absorbing water and minerals. Sperm cells have a tail for swimming and many mitochondria to supply energy. Guard cells change shape to open and close stomata (the pores in leaves).
[bio4-s2.5.5] Alveoli (the air sacs in the lungs) have walls one cell thick, a moist lining, a large total surface area and a rich blood supply, for fast gas exchange. Villi in the small intestine increase the surface area for absorption.
[bio4-s2.5.6] Arteries have thick, muscular, elastic walls to withstand high pressure, while veins have thinner walls and valves to stop backflow. Xylem vessels are hollow, dead tubes strengthened with lignin to carry water.
[bio4-s2.5.7] The left ventricle has a thicker muscular wall than the right because it pumps blood all round the body.
[bio4-s2.6.b] Living things run on instructions and signals. Information is passed between generations, between cells and around the body.
[bio4-s2.6.0] Genetic information passes from DNA to RNA to protein, so genes control traits. There is more on this in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio4-s2.6.1] Information passes from parent to offspring through gametes (sex cells), and from cell to daughter cells by DNA replication (copying the DNA) and mitosis (the division that makes identical cells).
[bio4-s2.6.2] Information is also transferred within the body by nerve impulses, which are fast, and by hormones, which are slower.
[bio4-s2.6.3] Between cells, information is transferred by chemical signals, e.g. neurotransmitters (chemicals that cross the gap between neurons).
[bio4-s2.7.b] Living things are built from atoms that are not made new. The same atoms are passed around again and again.
[bio4-s2.7.0] Matter is not created or destroyed but is recycled. Atoms of carbon, nitrogen and other elements cycle between organisms and the environment through feeding, excretion, death and decomposition.
[bio4-s2.7.1] Within organisms, matter is taken in by digestion and absorption, gas exchange and root uptake.
[bio4-s2.7.2] Matter is then transported in blood, xylem and phloem, and built into new molecules (anabolism) or broken down (catabolism).
[bio4-s2.8.b] Energy behaves differently from matter. It enters ecosystems from the Sun, moves through living things and leaves as heat, so it must be constantly resupplied.
[bio4-s2.8.0] Energy flows through living systems in one direction and is not recycled. Light energy from the Sun is converted to chemical energy in glucose by photosynthesis.
[bio4-s2.8.1] Respiration releases that energy and stores it in ATP, which transfers it to cell activities.
[bio4-s2.8.2] At each step some energy is lost as heat, so energy decreases at each feeding level of a food chain. This limits food chains to a few links.
[bio4-s2.8.3] Contrast: matter cycles, whereas energy flows through the system and is lost as heat.
[bio4-s2.8.4] Diagram: a simple ecosystem. Energy as a one-way arrow from the Sun through producers, consumers and decomposers, with heat-loss arrows at each level. Matter (carbon) as a loop from decomposers to air and soil and back to producers.
[bio4-s2.9.b] Most real questions need more than one idea. This five-step method gives a reliable way to build a full explanation.
[bio4-s2.9.0] 1. Describe the phenomenon precisely, so you know exactly what you are explaining.
[bio4-s2.9.1] 2. Choose the ideas that apply. More than one usually does.
[bio4-s2.9.2] 3. Explain the mechanism step by step, naming the structures and processes involved.
[bio4-s2.9.3] 4. Link structure to function, and cause to effect, using words such as “so” and “which allows”.
[bio4-s2.9.4] 5. If asked, use evolution to explain why the feature exists.
[bio4-s2.10.b] Why do breathing and heart rate rise when you exercise? Four of the big ideas combine to explain it.
[bio4-s2.10.0] Transfer of energy: muscles need more ATP, so they respire faster.
[bio4-s2.10.1] Transfer of matter: more oxygen must be supplied and more CO₂ removed.
[bio4-s2.10.2] Systems and interdependence: the breathing, circulatory and muscular systems work together. The brain detects the rise in CO₂ in the blood and increases the breathing rate, a feedback response.
[bio4-s2.10.3] Form fits function: alveoli and capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) suit fast exchange. There is more on CO₂ control in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio4-s2.11.b] Why can removing a top predator (an animal at the top of a food chain) reduce plant diversity? Follow the change through the food web.
[bio4-s2.11.0] Interdependence: the predator’s prey, e.g. a herbivore, increases because fewer are eaten.
[bio4-s2.11.1] Transfer of matter and energy: more energy flows to the herbivores, which eat more of the producers (the plants).
[bio4-s2.11.2] Systems: one change spreads through the food web.
[bio4-s2.11.3] Diversity: the plants that the herbivores prefer decline, so species diversity can fall.

CANDIDATES bio5-s2: Levels of organisation
[bio5-s2.0.b] Living things are organised in levels of increasing complexity. Each level is made of units of the level below working together, so a whole organism can be understood by building up from its cells.
[bio5-s2.0.0] The order is cell → tissue → organ → organ system → organism. Learn it as a ladder: each step up combines the units of the step below.
[bio5-s2.0.1] Below the cell, cells are built of organelles (small structures inside the cell, each with a job), which are built of biomolecules (e.g. proteins, lipids). There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
[bio5-s2.0.2] Cell: the basic unit of life. In a multicellular organism (one made of many cells) cells are specialised (differentiated), so that the structure of each cell suits one job.
[bio5-s2.0.3] Tissue: a group of similar cells with the same structure, working together to carry out a particular function. Because the cells are alike, the tissue does one job well.
[bio5-s2.0.4] Organ: a structure made of several different tissues working together to carry out one or more functions. Having different tissues is what separates an organ from a tissue.
[bio5-s2.0.5] Organ system: a group of organs working together to carry out a major function, such as digesting food.
[bio5-s2.0.6] Organism: an individual living thing, whose organ systems work together to keep it alive.
[bio5-s2.0.7] Diagram: a five-step flow chart from cell to organism, with a human example at each level (muscle cell → muscle tissue → stomach → digestive system → human) and a plant example (xylem vessel → vascular tissue → leaf → shoot system → plant).
[bio5-s2.1.b] A specialised cell has a structure that suits its particular job. Cells in a multicellular organism are specialised, and each example below links a feature of the cell to its function.
[bio5-s2.1.0] Unicellular organisms (those made of a single cell), e.g. Amoeba, yeast and bacteria, carry out every life process in one cell, so that cell is not specialised for a single job.
[bio5-s2.1.1] Differentiation: in a multicellular organism, all body cells contain the same DNA, but each cell type uses (expresses) only some of its genes, so cells become specialised.
[bio5-s2.1.2] Unspecialised cells that can still become several cell types are called stem cells. There is more on their uses in Genetic Engineering.
[bio5-s2.1.3] Red blood cell: no nucleus and a biconcave disc shape (a disc dipped in on both faces), giving more room for haemoglobin (the pigment that carries oxygen) and a large surface area for carrying oxygen.
[bio5-s2.1.4] Nerve cell (neuron): long thin extensions carry impulses (electrical signals) over long distances quickly, which suits a cell that links distant parts of the body.
[bio5-s2.1.5] Sperm cell: a tail for swimming, and many mitochondria in the middle section to release the energy that powers the tail.
[bio5-s2.1.6] Root hair cell: a long extension gives a large surface area for absorbing water and minerals from the soil.
[bio5-s2.1.7] Palisade cell of a leaf: packed with chloroplasts (the green structures where photosynthesis happens) for photosynthesis. Guard cells change shape to open and close the stomata (the pores in the leaf surface).
[bio5-s2.2.b] Animal bodies are built from four main tissue types. Each is a group of similar cells doing one kind of job.
[bio5-s2.2.0] Epithelial tissue lines and covers surfaces, e.g. the lining of the gut and the skin surface.
[bio5-s2.2.1] Connective tissue supports and connects other tissues, e.g. bone, cartilage and blood. Blood counts as a connective tissue.
[bio5-s2.2.2] Muscle tissue contracts (shortens) to cause movement. There are three types: skeletal, smooth and cardiac (heart muscle).
[bio5-s2.2.3] Nervous tissue carries impulses, e.g. in the brain, spinal cord and nerves.
[bio5-s2.3.b] Plants also have tissue types, each a group of similar cells with one job. Together they build plant organs such as the leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.0] Dermal tissue is the epidermis, a protective outer layer covering the plant.
[bio5-s2.3.1] Vascular tissue carries materials around the plant: xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries food.
[bio5-s2.3.2] Ground tissue makes up the bulk of the plant, carrying out photosynthesis and storage, e.g. the mesophyll (the inner tissue) of a leaf.
[bio5-s2.3.3] Meristematic tissue consists of dividing cells at root and shoot tips, where growth happens, so new cells for growth are made there.
[bio5-s2.3.4] A leaf contains dermal, ground and vascular tissue, which shows that an organ is made of several tissue types. Plant organs are root, stem, leaf and flower.
[bio5-s2.4.b] Organs are made of several tissue types, and organs that cooperate form organ systems. Each system has one major function.
[bio5-s2.4.0] The stomach is an organ containing epithelial tissue lining it, smooth muscle that churns food, connective tissue and nervous tissue. Four different tissues working together make it an organ.
[bio5-s2.4.1] Other human organs include the heart, lungs, kidneys and brain.
[bio5-s2.4.2] Digestive system: breaks down and absorbs food. Circulatory system: transports materials around the body.
[bio5-s2.4.3] Breathing system: gas exchange (taking in oxygen and releasing carbon dioxide). Nervous system: fast coordination.
[bio5-s2.4.4] Endocrine system: hormonal coordination. Musculoskeletal system: support and movement.
[bio5-s2.4.5] Urinary system: excretion (removing wastes) and water balance. Reproductive system: reproduction. Immune system: defence.
[bio5-s2.4.6] Plants have two systems. The root system anchors the plant and absorbs water and minerals.
[bio5-s2.4.7] The shoot system (stem, leaves and flowers) carries out photosynthesis, transport and reproduction.
[bio5-s2.5.b] Size changes how a living thing gets what it needs. The bigger an organism, the harder it is to supply every cell, which is why large organisms need specialised systems.
[bio5-s2.5.0] As an organism gets bigger, its volume increases faster than its surface area, so its surface area to volume ratio falls.
[bio5-s2.5.1] Worked example: a 1 cm cube has a surface area of 6 cm² and a volume of 1 cm³, a ratio of 6:1. A 2 cm cube has a surface area of 24 cm² and a volume of 8 cm³, a ratio of 3:1.
[bio5-s2.5.2] A unicellular organism has a large surface area to volume ratio, so diffusion across its membrane is enough to supply oxygen and food and to remove wastes.
[bio5-s2.5.3] In a large multicellular organism most cells are far from the surface, so diffusion alone is too slow to reach them.
[bio5-s2.5.4] Multicellular organisms therefore need specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gut lining, root hairs, leaves) to take in materials, and transport systems (blood and heart; xylem and phloem) to carry them to every cell.
[bio5-s2.5.5] They also need coordination systems (nervous and hormonal) that link all the parts and keep conditions constant (homeostasis).
[bio5-s2.5.6] Division of labour: each cell type does one job very efficiently, but no cell can survive alone, so the cells depend on each other.
[bio5-s2.6.b] Being made of many specialised cells brings benefits, but it also has costs. Comparing the two shows why both unicellular and multicellular life exist.
[bio5-s2.6.0] Advantage: a larger size, which means fewer predators and access to light or food.
[bio5-s2.6.1] Advantage: cells inside the body are protected from the environment, because they are surrounded by other cells.
[bio5-s2.6.2] Advantage: efficiency from specialisation, since each cell type does one job well.
[bio5-s2.6.3] Advantage: the organism can survive the death of individual cells, which are replaced.
[bio5-s2.6.4] Disadvantage: more energy and resources are needed to build and maintain the body.
[bio5-s2.6.5] Disadvantage: damage to one vital organ or system can kill the whole organism, because the other cells depend on it.
[bio5-s2.6.6] Disadvantage: growth to reproductive age takes longer than for unicellular organisms, which can divide within hours.
