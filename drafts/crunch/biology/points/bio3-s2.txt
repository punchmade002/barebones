TARGET bio3-s2: Science, scientists and society
[bio3-s2.0.b] Scientists add to society in several ways, and the words 'discovery' and 'invention' mean different things. Knowing the difference, and how research is organised and paid for, lets you describe any scientist's contribution accurately.
[bio3-s2.0.0] A discovery is finding something that already exists in nature but was not known, for example the structure of DNA, or that a mould kills bacteria. The thing was always there; the scientist is the first to find and describe it.
[bio3-s2.0.1] An invention is creating a new device, process or product that did not exist before, for example the electron microscope, PCR (a method of copying small samples of DNA) or a vaccine.
[bio3-s2.0.2] Discoveries often lead to inventions. The discovery of penicillin's effect on bacteria led to the invention of methods to mass-produce it, so that it could be given to many patients.
[bio3-s2.0.3] Basic (pure) research aims to understand nature with no particular use in mind. Applied research aims to solve a practical problem, such as finding a cure for a disease.
[bio3-s2.0.4] Many applications come from basic research done decades earlier. For example, the enzymes used in PCR and in DNA profiling (identifying people from their DNA) came from the study of bacteria, long before anyone planned such uses.
[bio3-s2.0.5] Most discoveries are now made by teams, often international, working in universities, hospitals, public research institutes and companies. They are funded by governments, the EU, charities and industry, and who funds research influences which questions are studied. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio3-s2.0.6] Scientists also contribute by advising governments, for example with public health advice in a pandemic (a disease outbreak spreading across many countries). They assess the risks of new technologies, communicate with the public, and warn about problems, as Rachel Carson did in the DDT case study.
[bio3-s2.1.b] To evaluate the impact of a scientific contribution is to judge it using evidence. Four factors give you a checklist: economic, social, sustainability and ethical. Each comes with questions you can ask about any discovery or technology.
[bio3-s2.1.0] Economic means the costs and benefits in money and jobs. Ask who pays for the research and the product, and who profits (companies, through patents). Ask whether it creates jobs or industries.
[bio3-s2.1.1] Economic questions also cover savings and affordability. Ask whether it saves money, for example fewer days lost to illness or higher crop yields, and whether poorer people and countries can afford it.
[bio3-s2.1.2] Social means the effects on people's health, lives and communities, and on how people live. Ask who benefits and who is harmed, and whether it changes life expectancy or quality of life. Ask whether it increases or reduces inequality, and how the public accepts it.
[bio3-s2.1.3] Sustainability means meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Ask what the effects are on the environment, biodiversity, resources and climate, and whether the benefits are long-lasting.
[bio3-s2.1.4] Sustainability questions also cover side effects. Ask whether it causes problems such as resistance (pests or bacteria no longer killed by a chemical or drug) or pollution.
[bio3-s2.1.5] Ethical means whether something is right or wrong. Ask whether it is fair (justice) and whether it does good and avoids harm. Ask whether people were informed and gave consent, whether animals were used and, if so, whether their use was justified, and who decides.
[bio3-s2.1.6] Ethical questions are answered by reasoning about values, informed by evidence. This means science alone cannot settle them: the facts can show what happens, but people must decide what is acceptable.
[bio3-s2.2.b] Ethical questions in biology are not left to opinion alone. Researchers and doctors use a small set of agreed principles to decide what is acceptable, and these give you the vocabulary for the ethical factor.
[bio3-s2.2.0] The four principles of medical ethics are autonomy (respecting people's right to decide for themselves, through informed consent, which means agreeing after being told the risks and benefits), beneficence (doing good), non-maleficence (doing no harm) and justice (treating people fairly and sharing benefits and burdens fairly).
[bio3-s2.2.1] For animal research, the 3Rs were proposed by Russell and Burch in 1959. Replacement means using non-animal methods where possible, Reduction means using as few animals as possible, and Refinement means minimising pain and distress.
[bio3-s2.2.2] The precautionary principle says that where an action might cause serious harm and the science is uncertain, you should take care or not proceed until there is more evidence. In other words, uncertainty is a reason for caution, not for going ahead.
[bio3-s2.2.3] International research conventions, for example the Declaration of Helsinki, set ethical rules for research across countries. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio3-s2.3.b] Penicillin is an antibiotic (a medicine that kills bacteria), and it began the era of antibiotics. Its story shows a discovery turning into an invention through the work of different scientists, and how its benefits are now at risk.
[bio3-s2.3.0] What happened: in 1928 Alexander Fleming, at St Mary's Hospital in London, noticed that a mould (Penicillium) contaminating a culture plate (a dish in which bacteria are grown) of Staphylococcus bacteria had killed the bacteria around it.
[bio3-s2.3.1] Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, at the University of Oxford, purified penicillin and showed that it cured infections, from 1940. It was mass-produced during the Second World War.
[bio3-s2.3.2] Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945. Fleming did not purify or mass-produce penicillin; Florey and Chain did, so Fleming made the discovery and the Oxford team turned it into a usable drug.
[bio3-s2.3.3] Social impact (positive): bacterial infections that were often fatal, such as infected wounds, pneumonia and sepsis (blood poisoning), became curable. Surgery and childbirth became much safer, and the era of antibiotics began.
[bio3-s2.3.4] Economic impact (positive): penicillin created a large pharmaceutical industry, healthier workers and fewer deaths, and antibiotics are cheap to make.
[bio3-s2.3.5] Sustainability impact (negative): overuse and misuse of antibiotics in medicine and farming have selected for resistant bacteria, such as MRSA, by natural selection. This threatens to make infections untreatable again, and Fleming warned of this in his Nobel lecture in 1945. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio3-s2.3.6] Ethical impact: access is unequal in poorer countries. Using antibiotics to make farm animals grow faster, which has been banned in the EU since 2006, raises resistance for everyone. Prescribing responsibly, known as antibiotic stewardship, is now an ethical duty.
[bio3-s2.3.7] Judgement: one of the most beneficial discoveries in medicine, whose benefits are now threatened by the way it has been used.
[bio3-s2.4.b] Insulin is the hormone that lowers blood glucose, and people with type 1 diabetes cannot make enough of it. This case study shows a discovery that saved lives, a decision to give up profit, and a later invention that changed how the medicine is made.
[bio3-s2.4.0] What happened: in 1921 at the University of Toronto, Frederick Banting and Charles Best extracted insulin from the pancreases of dogs and showed that it lowered blood glucose in diabetic dogs.
[bio3-s2.4.1] In 1922 insulin was first used to treat a person with type 1 diabetes, a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson. Social impact (positive): type 1 diabetes changed from a fatal disease to a manageable condition.
[bio3-s2.4.2] Banting and John Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923. The discoverers sold the patent (the legal right to profit from an invention) to the University of Toronto for a token sum so that insulin could be made widely available.
[bio3-s2.4.3] Insulin was extracted from cattle and pig pancreases until 1982, when human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria became the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio3-s2.4.4] Economic impact: a large global insulin industry has grown. In some countries, notably the USA, high insulin prices mean that some patients cannot afford enough.
[bio3-s2.4.5] Sustainability impact (positive): making insulin in bacteria in fermenters (large tanks where microbes are grown) gives an unlimited, consistent supply without relying on slaughterhouse animals.
[bio3-s2.4.6] Ethical impact: experiments on dogs should be assessed using the 3Rs. Animal-derived insulin raised religious and vegetarian concerns that human insulin from bacteria removes. Fair access is a justice issue.
[bio3-s2.4.7] Judgement: overwhelmingly positive, but access is unequal.
[bio3-s2.5.b] DDT is a chemical that kills insects. It saved lives, but it also did lasting damage to wildlife. This case study shows how a scientist can contribute by warning society about a problem, and how one technology can have major benefits and major harms.
[bio3-s2.5.0] What happened: in 1939 Paul Müller discovered that the chemical DDT is a powerful insecticide. He received the Nobel Prize in 1948. DDT was used to control insects that spread malaria and typhus, and to protect crops.
[bio3-s2.5.1] In 1962 the American biologist Rachel Carson published Silent Spring, presenting evidence that DDT and other pesticides were harming wildlife, especially birds.
[bio3-s2.5.2] DDT is not broken down quickly, so it builds up in food chains and reaches high concentrations in top predators such as birds of prey. There it causes thin eggshells that break. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio3-s2.5.3] The USA banned most uses of DDT in 1972. The Stockholm Convention (2001) restricts it worldwide but allows its use to control malaria-carrying mosquitoes.
[bio3-s2.5.4] Social impact (positive): millions of people were protected from malaria and typhus, and food production increased. Economic impact: DDT was cheap and effective, so it raised crop yields, but chemical companies attacked Carson's work.
[bio3-s2.5.5] Sustainability impact (negative): persistence and build-up in food chains harmed birds of prey and other wildlife, and insects evolved resistance to DDT. Silent Spring helped to start the modern environmental movement.
[bio3-s2.5.6] Ethical impact: weighing human lives saved from malaria against harm to wildlife and long-term ecological damage, applying the precautionary principle, and respecting the public's right to be told the risks.
[bio3-s2.5.7] Judgement: the same scientific contribution can have major benefits and major harms, and scientists also contribute by evaluating technologies.
[bio3-s2.6.b] Ivermectin is a drug against parasitic worms, developed with the help of an Irish scientist. It shows how basic research on soil microorganisms can lead to a medicine, and how a company can share a discovery with people who cannot pay for it.
[bio3-s2.6.0] What happened: William Campbell, born in 1930 in Ramelton, Co. Donegal, and a graduate of Trinity College Dublin, worked at the drug company Merck in the USA.
[bio3-s2.6.1] Satoshi Ōmura in Japan isolated a soil bacterium, a Streptomyces species, that produced useful compounds. Campbell's team found that one of them, avermectin, killed parasitic worms, and developed the drug ivermectin from it. Ōmura isolated the bacterium; Campbell did not.
[bio3-s2.6.2] Ivermectin is used against river blindness (onchocerciasis), caused by a parasitic worm spread by the bites of blackflies, and against lymphatic filariasis. Social impact (positive): it has protected many millions of people in Africa and Latin America from river blindness and other parasitic diseases.
[bio3-s2.6.3] Campbell and Ōmura shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Tu Youyou, who discovered the antimalarial drug artemisinin.
[bio3-s2.6.4] Economic impact: the drug was first sold as a veterinary drug for livestock and pets, which made it highly profitable. Healthier people can work and attend school.
[bio3-s2.6.5] Ethical impact (positive): since 1987 Merck has donated the human form, Mectizan, free for as long as it is needed, through the Mectizan Donation Program. This is a model of a company sharing a discovery with people who cannot pay.
[bio3-s2.6.6] Sustainability impact (negative): heavy use in livestock has led to resistance in some livestock parasites, and residues in animal dung can harm dung-feeding insects.
[bio3-s2.6.7] Judgement: a discovery from basic research on soil microorganisms, with an enormous health benefit, shared ethically.
[bio3-s2.7.b] Thalidomide shows what can go wrong when a drug is released without enough testing. Unlike the other case studies, its main lesson is a warning, and its legacy is the strict system of drug testing we have today.
[bio3-s2.7.0] What happened: thalidomide was sold from 1957, first in West Germany and then in many countries including Ireland and the UK, as a sedative. It was given to pregnant women for morning sickness.
[bio3-s2.7.1] It had not been tested for effects on the developing foetus. It caused severe birth defects, especially shortened or missing limbs, in more than 10,000 babies before it was withdrawn in 1961.
[bio3-s2.7.2] In the USA, Frances Kelsey, a reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), refused to approve it because the safety evidence was inadequate. Her refusal shows why independent review matters.
[bio3-s2.7.3] Social impact (negative): lifelong disability for survivors and their families, and loss of public trust in medicines. Economic impact: compensation costs, and new regulation made developing drugs slower and more expensive.
[bio3-s2.7.4] Ethical impact (negative): patients were harmed by a drug released without adequate testing. Survivors campaigned for decades for compensation and an apology.
[bio3-s2.7.5] Lasting effect: much stricter laws on testing drugs before sale, including testing for effects in pregnancy, and systems to report side effects.
[bio3-s2.7.6] Thalidomide is now used, under strict controls, to treat leprosy complications and a blood cancer, multiple myeloma.
[bio3-s2.7.7] Judgement: shows why scientists and regulators must test new technologies carefully, and why independent review matters.
[bio3-s2.8.b] The syllabus asks you to research a scientist's contribution and present it, not just recall case studies. This is the method to follow, whichever scientist or team you choose.
[bio3-s2.8.0] 1. Choose a scientist or team and their discovery or invention.
[bio3-s2.8.1] 2. Research using reliable sources (sources you can trust, such as scientific journals and established institutions): who the scientists were, the problem, what they did and how, the evidence, and how the work was received. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio3-s2.8.2] 3. Describe the impact under the four factors (economic, social, sustainability and ethical), positive and negative, using specific evidence.
[bio3-s2.8.3] 4. Make and justify a judgement: say whether the overall impact was positive or negative, and give your reasons.
[bio3-s2.8.4] 5. Present the work for the audience, for example as a poster, a slide presentation or a short report, with references so that others can check your sources.

CANDIDATES bio1-s2: Science as a global enterprise
[bio1-s2.0.b] Science is not done by one person or one country working alone. Scientists everywhere work on shared problems and build on each other’s published results, and that is what makes it a global enterprise.
[bio1-s2.0.0] Scientists in many countries work on the same problems, share data and build on each other’s published results. Knowledge is not owned by one country or person.
[bio1-s2.0.1] Because claims are tested by people with different backgrounds and assumptions, mistakes and hidden bias are more likely to be spotted than if one group checked its own work.
[bio1-s2.0.2] Large problems need international teams, shared funding and shared equipment. The Human Genome Project (1990–2003) sequenced the human genome (read out all of a person’s DNA) through an international consortium of 20 research groups in six countries: the USA, the UK, Japan, France, Germany and China.
[bio1-s2.0.3] Under the Bermuda Principles (1996), the Human Genome Project released its sequence data publicly within 24 hours, free for anyone to use, so other researchers could start work straight away.
[bio1-s2.0.4] Speed through sharing: in January 2020 Chinese scientists published the genome sequence of the new coronavirus (SARS-CoV-2) online. Research groups around the world used it within days to design tests and begin developing vaccines.
[bio1-s2.0.5] Funding is international too. Irish researchers are funded by Research Ireland, the state agency formed in 2024 from Science Foundation Ireland and the Irish Research Council, and by the EU research programme Horizon Europe (2021–2027), which funds teams that include several countries.
[bio1-s2.0.6] Science needs four things to work across borders: clear communication, international conventions, peer review and reproducibility. Each gets its own block below. There is more on funding as a source of bias in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio1-s2.1.b] A discovery only helps science once other scientists can read it, understand it and use it. So scientists publish in a standard form and use precise language that anyone in the field can follow.
[bio1-s2.1.0] Scientists communicate mainly through papers in scientific journals. They also use conferences, review articles (summaries of many studies on one topic) and shared online databases such as genome databases (stores of DNA sequences). There is more on genome databases in Bioinformatics: genome databases.
[bio1-s2.1.1] A research paper has a standard structure: title and authors; abstract (a short summary); introduction (background and the aim or hypothesis); methods (exactly what was done, with materials and conditions).
[bio1-s2.1.2] The paper then gives results (data in tables and graphs); discussion (what the results mean, the limitations, and comparison with other work); and references to other work.
[bio1-s2.1.3] The methods section must be detailed enough for another scientist to repeat the work, which makes reproducibility (others getting the same result) possible.
[bio1-s2.1.4] Scientific language must be precise and unambiguous. Terms have one agreed meaning (e.g. “theory” in science is not “a guess”), quantities are given with units, and uncertainty is stated rather than hidden. English is the main shared language of international science.
[bio1-s2.1.5] Open access journals make papers free to read for everyone, not only subscribers, so more people can use the findings.
[bio1-s2.1.6] Preprints are papers posted online before peer review (checking by independent experts), e.g. on bioRxiv, launched 2013, and medRxiv, launched 2019, for medical research. They speed up sharing, as in the COVID-19 pandemic, but readers must remember that they have not yet been checked.
[bio1-s2.1.7] Poor communication slows science. Gregor Mendel published his results on inheritance in pea plants in 1866, in the journal of the Natural History Society of Brünn (now Brno, Czech Republic). They were little read, and their importance was recognised only when other scientists reached the same conclusions independently in 1900.
[bio1-s2.1.8] Communicating with the public (news, policy and outreach) is also part of a scientist’s work. There is more on evaluating media reports in Evaluating evidence, and on presenting your own findings in Communicating results.
[bio1-s2.2.b] For scientists in different countries to compare and repeat each other’s work, they must use the same units, names and rules. These shared agreements are called international conventions.
[bio1-s2.2.0] An international convention is a set of rules or standards agreed by scientists or governments across countries, so that results mean the same everywhere and can be compared and repeated. Standards also cover laboratory safety signs (hazard symbols) and depositing data in public databases.
[bio1-s2.2.1] SI units (Système International) are the agreed units of measurement: metre (length), kilogram (mass), second (time), mole (amount of substance) and kelvin (temperature), with degrees Celsius also widely used.
[bio1-s2.2.2] Prefixes such as milli- and micro- are used with SI units, e.g. mm, and µm, the unit for cell sizes. The unit nm is used for organelles and molecules, which are smaller still.
[bio1-s2.2.3] Binomial nomenclature gives each species a two-part Latin name, genus then species, e.g. Homo sapiens. The genus starts with a capital letter, the species name is lower case, and the name is printed in italics or underlined when handwritten.
[bio1-s2.2.4] Carl Linnaeus established the system; the starting point for plant names is his Species Plantarum (1753). One scientific name avoids confusion between common names, which differ between languages and regions. There is more on this in Classification.
[bio1-s2.2.5] Other naming conventions: human genes have agreed symbols, written in italics, e.g. the CFTR gene, whose faulty alleles (versions of a gene) cause cystic fibrosis. The protein is written without italics. Chemical names and formulas follow agreed rules, e.g. CO₂ and C₆H₁₂O₆.
[bio1-s2.2.6] Ethical conventions set rules for research. The Declaration of Helsinki (World Medical Association, 1964) sets out ethical principles for research on people, including informed consent and independent ethical review. It followed the Nuremberg Code (1947), which was written after the trials of Nazi doctors.
[bio1-s2.2.7] The Convention on Biological Diversity (1992, agreed at the Rio Earth Summit) commits countries to conserving biodiversity, using it sustainably and sharing fairly the benefits of genetic resources. CITES (1973) controls international trade in endangered species.
[bio1-s2.3.b] Before a paper is published, other experts check it. This is peer review, a quality check that follows a set sequence of steps.
[bio1-s2.3.0] Peer review is the evaluation of a scientific paper by independent experts in the same field, the author’s “peers”, before it is published.
[bio1-s2.3.1] Steps 1 to 3: the scientists submit the paper to a journal; the editor checks that it suits the journal and is of reasonable quality, and may reject it straight away; if it passes, the editor sends it to usually two or three independent experts, called reviewers or referees.
[bio1-s2.3.2] Step 4: the reviewers check whether the methods are suitable, the data support the conclusions, the analysis is sound, the work is original and important, ethical rules were followed and other work is properly referenced.
[bio1-s2.3.3] Step 5: they recommend accept, accept with minor revisions, revise and resubmit (major revisions), or reject.
[bio1-s2.3.4] Steps 6 and 7: the authors revise the paper and reply to each comment, and the editor makes the final decision before the paper is published.
[bio1-s2.3.5] Single-blind review: the reviewers know who the authors are, but the authors do not know the reviewers. This lets reviewers judge freely, but they may be swayed by the authors’ reputation.
[bio1-s2.3.6] Double-blind review: neither side knows the other, which reduces bias from the authors’ reputation, gender or country. Open review: names, and sometimes reports, are published.
[bio1-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flowchart of the seven steps, with a loop from “revise” back to “reviewers”.
[bio1-s2.4.b] Peer review is valuable, but it is not a guarantee of truth. Knowing what it can and cannot do explains why scientists keep checking work after it is published.
[bio1-s2.4.0] Peer review filters out poor-quality or unsupported work, improves papers before publication, and gives readers some confidence in published work. A peer-reviewed paper is a more reliable source than one that is not. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio1-s2.4.1] Reviewers do not normally repeat the experiments, so they cannot detect well-hidden fraud or errors in raw data. Peer review does not guarantee that a paper is correct.
[bio1-s2.4.2] Reviewers can be biased, e.g. against ideas that challenge their own, or towards famous laboratories. The process is slow, and reviewers are usually unpaid volunteers.
[bio1-s2.4.3] Some low-quality “predatory” journals charge authors to publish while doing little or no real review, so a paper in such a journal has not really been checked.
[bio1-s2.4.4] Checking continues after publication. Other scientists read, cite and try to repeat the work; errors lead to published corrections, and serious errors or misconduct lead to retraction, when the journal withdraws the paper.
[bio1-s2.4.5] Hwang Woo-suk’s team published papers in the journal Science in 2004 and 2005 claiming to have made stem cells from cloned human embryos. The data were found to be fabricated, and the papers were retracted in 2006. Peer review had not caught the fraud; scrutiny after publication did.
[bio1-s2.5.b] One result on its own is not enough to count as knowledge. Science accepts a finding only when others can get the same result, which is why reproducibility matters.
[bio1-s2.5.0] A result is reproducible if other scientists, working independently in other laboratories, get the same result when they repeat the work, using the same or different methods. There is more on repeatability and reproducibility in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio1-s2.5.1] Science relies on reproducibility because a single result may be due to chance, error, an unusual sample, bias or fraud. Agreement between independent groups shows that the result is a real feature of nature.
[bio1-s2.5.2] Only reproduced findings become accepted knowledge and can support theories. For example, the H. pylori explanation of stomach ulcers (that a bacterium causes them) was accepted once others reproduced the findings. There is more on this in The nature of scientific knowledge.
[bio1-s2.5.3] Reproducibility depends on clear communication: full methods, raw data and materials must be shared so that others can repeat the work.
[bio1-s2.5.4] Not all published results reproduce. Scientists at the company Amgen reported in 2012 that they could confirm the findings of only 6 of 53 “landmark” preclinical cancer studies (early studies done before testing in patients).
[bio1-s2.5.5] Causes of failure include small samples, selective reporting of positive results, pressure to publish, poorly described methods and, rarely, fraud.
[bio1-s2.5.6] Responses include sharing data openly, registering study plans in advance and publishing replication studies (repeats of earlier work), so that results are checked and not hidden if they disagree.
