TARGET bio22-s7: Emerging diseases and society
[bio22-s7.0.b] A virus can cause a new disease without being new to the world: it only has to be new to the species it infects. Most of the new viruses that infect people come from other animals.
[bio22-s7.0.0] A novel virus is one not previously known to infect a species, usually because it has jumped from another host (a jump called spillover) or has changed by mutation or by mixing of genes.
[bio22-s7.0.1] Most new human viruses come from animals. A disease passed from animals to people is called a zoonosis, and the animals involved are especially bats, birds and other mammals.
[bio22-s7.0.2] Contact between wildlife and people increases when people clear forests, hunt or trade wild animals, or farm animals intensively, because each of these creates new opportunities for a virus to cross from one species to another.
[bio22-s7.1.b] Two viruses that reached people from animals changed the world in very different ways: one spread silently over decades, the other around the globe within months.
[bio22-s7.1.0] HIV crossed into humans from a related virus, SIV (simian immunodeficiency virus), in chimpanzees in central Africa in the early twentieth century, probably through hunting and butchering them for meat.
[bio22-s7.1.1] HIV was identified in 1983 by Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Luc Montagnier at the Institut Pasteur, Paris (Nobel Prize 2008).
[bio22-s7.1.2] HIV's long symptom-free period let it spread worldwide unnoticed for decades, because infected people had no reason to suspect they carried it.
[bio22-s7.1.3] There is still no HIV vaccine, but antiretroviral therapy (drugs that stop the virus replicating) has turned it from a fatal disease into a long-term condition. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s7.1.4] SARS-CoV-2, the coronavirus that causes COVID-19, was first identified in Wuhan, China, in December 2019. It spreads by droplets and aerosols (tiny airborne particles), including from people without symptoms, and air travel carried it worldwide.
[bio22-s7.1.5] Its genome sequence was shared online in January 2020, and vaccines were developed and approved within a year.
[bio22-s7.1.6] Mutations produced more transmissible variants (forms that spread more easily), so vaccines were updated to match them.
[bio22-s7.2.b] Animal viruses show how a virus moves between wildlife, farm animals and people, and what controlled each outbreak. For each one, know what it is, where it came from, how it spread and how it was stopped.
[bio22-s7.2.0] Avian influenza H5N1 is a highly pathogenic (very disease-causing) bird flu virus that kills poultry and wild birds. A strain that spread worldwide from about 2020, carried by migrating wild birds, has infected many mammals, and in 2024 it was found in dairy cattle in the USA.
[bio22-s7.2.1] Human infections with H5N1 are rare and mostly occur in people in close contact with infected birds or animals. The concern is that mutation, or mixing with a human flu virus, could produce a strain that spreads between people.
[bio22-s7.2.2] Avian influenza is controlled by culling infected flocks, keeping poultry housed when the risk is high (which keeps them away from wild birds), and surveillance.
[bio22-s7.2.3] Ebola virus causes severe haemorrhagic fever (fever with bleeding). Fruit bats are thought to be its natural reservoir, the animal host in which the virus normally lives. The 2014–2016 epidemic in West Africa killed more than 11,000 people.
[bio22-s7.2.4] Ebola spreads by contact with the body fluids of sick or dead people, so caring for the sick and traditional burial practices spread it. It was controlled by isolation, contact tracing, safe burials and, later, a vaccine.
[bio22-s7.2.5] Nipah virus emerged in Malaysia in 1998–1999, passing from fruit bats to pigs on farms near fruit trees, and from pigs to pig farmers, causing brain inflammation (encephalitis).
[bio22-s7.2.6] Nipah was controlled by culling over a million pigs. It shows how farming changes create new routes from wildlife to people.
[bio22-s7.2.7] Schmallenberg virus, an animal virus, was identified in 2011 in Germany and is named after the town where it was found. Spread by biting midges, it causes birth defects in lambs and calves when the mother is infected in early pregnancy.
[bio22-s7.2.8] It spread across northern Europe, including Ireland, within about a year.
[bio22-s7.3.b] Plants get emerging virus diseases too. Tomato brown rugose fruit virus (ToBRFV) is a recent example that threatens a major glasshouse crop.
[bio22-s7.3.0] ToBRFV is a tobamovirus, related to tobacco mosaic virus, first identified in the Middle East in the mid-2010s, and it then spread to many countries.
[bio22-s7.3.1] It infects tomatoes and peppers, causing wrinkled, discoloured fruit that can't be sold, so growers lose income.
[bio22-s7.3.2] It spreads by contact (hands, tools and clothing), in infected seed and in the trade in plants and fruit, and it stays infectious on surfaces for long periods.
[bio22-s7.3.3] It is serious because it overcomes the resistance genes bred into tomato varieties to protect them against related viruses, so the older protection no longer works.
[bio22-s7.3.4] Control: EU plant-health rules on imported seed and plants, testing of seed, hygiene and disinfection in glasshouses, destroying infected crops, and breeding new resistant varieties.
[bio22-s7.4.b] Several human activities and natural changes help plant viruses reach new places and crops. Because plants cannot be treated once infected, prevention is the only real defence.
[bio22-s7.4.0] Why plant viruses emerge: global trade in seeds and plants; monocultures of genetically uniform crops (one variety grown over huge areas, so one virus can overcome them all); insect vectors (carriers) spreading into new areas as the climate warms; and mutation overcoming plants' resistance genes.
[bio22-s7.4.1] Plants have no adaptive immune system (the antibody- and T-cell-based defence animals use) and can't be vaccinated or cured of viral infection, so control depends on prevention: clean seed and stock, controlling vectors, removing infected plants, quarantine and resistant varieties.
[bio22-s7.4.2] Economic and food-security importance: crop losses raise food prices and threaten farmers' incomes.
[bio22-s7.4.3] Diseases of staple crops (the main food crops) are most serious for poor countries that depend on one crop, because there is no alternative food if it fails.
[bio22-s7.5.b] The immune system is meant to attack invaders and leave the body alone. In an autoimmune disease it gets this wrong and attacks the body's own tissues.
[bio22-s7.5.0] An autoimmune disease is one in which the immune system attacks the body's own cells or tissues as if they were foreign, because it fails to tell self from non-self.
[bio22-s7.5.1] Normally, lymphocytes (white blood cells that recognise antigens) that react to self are removed during maturation, and the rest are held in check by suppressor (regulatory) T cells.
[bio22-s7.5.2] In autoimmune disease this tolerance (not attacking self) breaks down, and antibodies or killer T cells attack a self antigen, an antigen that is part of the body's own cells.
[bio22-s7.6.b] Each autoimmune disease is defined by which tissue the immune system attacks, and the symptoms follow from the job that tissue does.
[bio22-s7.6.0] Type 1 diabetes: T cells destroy the insulin-producing cells of the pancreas, causing high blood glucose. It is treated with insulin injections for life. There is more on this in Glands, locations and hormones.
[bio22-s7.6.1] Multiple sclerosis: the myelin sheath (the insulating layer) around nerve fibres in the brain and spinal cord is damaged, slowing or blocking impulses.
[bio22-s7.6.2] Rheumatoid arthritis: the lining of the joints becomes inflamed, causing painful, swollen and damaged joints.
[bio22-s7.6.3] Coeliac disease: eating gluten, a protein in wheat, barley and rye, triggers an immune attack that damages the villi of the small intestine (the finger-like folds that absorb food), causing poor absorption. Treatment is a lifelong gluten-free diet.
[bio22-s7.6.4] Graves' disease: antibodies stimulate the thyroid gland, producing too much thyroxine.
[bio22-s7.7.b] Autoimmune diseases have no single cause. Inherited genes make some people more susceptible, and something in the environment then sets the disease off.
[bio22-s7.7.0] Causes: a combination of inherited susceptibility, since some alleles make these diseases more likely and they run in families, and environmental triggers.
[bio22-s7.7.1] Infections can trigger autoimmunity when a pathogen's antigen resembles a self antigen, so antibodies made against the pathogen also attack the body.
[bio22-s7.7.2] Rheumatic fever: antibodies against Streptococcus bacteria, from a throat infection, attack the heart valves.
[bio22-s7.7.3] Guillain–Barré syndrome is a paralysis that can follow some infections, including Campylobacter food poisoning and Zika virus.
[bio22-s7.8.b] Autoimmune diseases are not infectious, yet they are grouped with emerging diseases because more people have several of them than before. Treatment can manage them but rarely cures them.
[bio22-s7.8.0] Autoimmune diseases are grouped with emerging diseases because the number of people with several autoimmune diseases has been rising in many countries.
[bio22-s7.8.1] The hygiene hypothesis proposes that less early-life exposure to microorganisms leaves the immune system poorly regulated. It is supported by some evidence but not proven, so it remains a hypothesis. There is more on this in The microbiome.
[bio22-s7.8.2] An autoimmune disease is not caught from another person. AIDS is an immune deficiency caused by a virus, not an autoimmune disease.
[bio22-s7.8.3] There is usually no cure. Treatments replace what is lost (insulin, thyroid hormone), avoid the trigger (a gluten-free diet), or suppress the immune system with drugs such as corticosteroids, which relieves symptoms but makes infections more likely.
[bio22-s7.9.b] Knowing which factors make a disease spread lets us target each one. Each strategy below is linked to the factor it acts on, which is how exams expect you to present it. There is more on the factors in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s7.9.0] Surveillance and early detection: doctors and vets must report notifiable diseases (ones the law requires them to report), and laboratories sequence pathogens' genomes to detect new variants and resistance.
[bio22-s7.9.1] Agencies such as the World Health Organization and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control share data. Early detection lets control start while case numbers are low.
[bio22-s7.9.2] Reducing R (targets R₀, contact and transmission): isolating cases, contact tracing and quarantine reduce the infectious period in the community, because infected people have fewer days in which to pass the disease on.
[bio22-s7.9.3] Masks, ventilation, hand hygiene and physical distancing reduce the chance of transmission per contact, and limiting mass gatherings reduces the number of contacts. The aim is to bring R (the average number of people each case infects) below 1, so the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s7.9.4] Raising immunity (targets the immunity level): vaccination campaigns aimed at the herd-immunity threshold (the proportion immune that stops an outbreak spreading), with priority for the most vulnerable.
[bio22-s7.9.5] Updating vaccines to match new variants targets mutation.
[bio22-s7.10.b] Other strategies target how pathogens travel, how crops are protected, how resistance develops and how animal viruses reach people in the first place.
[bio22-s7.10.0] Limiting mobility (targets movement): travel restrictions and screening; animal movement bans and culling during outbreaks of foot-and-mouth disease or avian influenza; and disinfecting vehicles and footwear at farms.
[bio22-s7.10.1] Plant biosecurity: plant passports and import inspections, quarantine of imported plants, and bans on importing high-risk plants (e.g. ash from infected areas).
[bio22-s7.10.2] Plant biosecurity also uses certified disease-free seed and planting material, removing and destroying infected plants, and breeding resistant varieties, which counters genetic uniformity.
[bio22-s7.10.3] Fighting resistance (targets resistance): antimicrobial stewardship, meaning prescribing antibiotics only when needed; infection control in hospitals; restricting antibiotic use in farm animals; rotating pesticides and fungicides; and developing new drugs.
[bio22-s7.10.4] Reducing spillover: controlling vectors, regulating wildlife trade and markets, protecting habitats, and biosecurity on farms.
[bio22-s7.10.5] The One Health approach treats human, animal and environmental health as linked, and coordinates doctors, vets and ecologists.
[bio22-s7.11.b] Control measures work best when they are chosen using evidence and when the public is willing to follow them.
[bio22-s7.11.0] Research and public information: understanding a new pathogen's transmission and R₀ tells authorities which measures will work, so effort is not wasted on measures that won't help.
[bio22-s7.11.1] Clear public information increases vaccine uptake and cooperation and counters misinformation (false or misleading claims). There is more on this in Evaluating evidence.
[bio22-s7.12.b] Understanding how new diseases arise and spread benefits health, the economy, food supplies, the environment and ordinary citizens.
[bio22-s7.12.0] Health: early recognition and control save lives and prevent disability.
[bio22-s7.12.1] Knowing the factors lets health systems prepare, with stockpiles, hospital capacity and vaccine platforms, before the next outbreak.
[bio22-s7.12.2] Protecting vulnerable groups depends on high immunity in everyone else, which means those who can't be vaccinated are shielded by those who can.
[bio22-s7.12.3] Economic: epidemics close businesses, schools and travel, and cost health systems heavily, as COVID-19 did from 2020.
[bio22-s7.12.4] Animal and plant diseases cause culling, lost exports and crop losses, e.g. the 2001 foot-and-mouth epidemic in Britain. Prevention and early control usually cost far less than a large outbreak.
[bio22-s7.12.5] Food security and the environment: plant and animal diseases threaten the food supply, especially where people depend on one crop. The Irish Great Famine shows the human cost, and there is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio22-s7.12.6] Diseases of wild species threaten biodiversity, e.g. chytrid fungus in amphibians, and ash dieback removing a native tree species and the organisms that depend on it.
[bio22-s7.12.7] Informed citizens: understanding how diseases spread, and why vaccines and antibiotics must be used carefully, lets people make informed choices, follow public-health advice and judge claims in the media.
[bio22-s7.13.b] A full discussion gives arguments for, then the costs and tensions of control measures, then a judgement. Examiners expect both sides.
[bio22-s7.13.0] Global cooperation: pathogens ignore borders, so a disease anywhere is a risk everywhere. Sharing data, genome sequences and vaccines between countries benefits all.
[bio22-s7.13.1] Tensions and costs: control measures limit personal freedom, such as quarantine, travel bans and lockdowns, and their social and economic costs, such as lost income and education, isolation and poor mental health, fall unequally.
[bio22-s7.13.2] Culling destroys farmers' livelihoods and healthy animals, vaccine requirements raise questions of consent, and poorer countries have less access to vaccines and drugs.
[bio22-s7.13.3] Acting early on uncertain evidence may later look like over-reaction, while waiting for certainty lets a disease spread. This is the precautionary principle, and there is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio22-s7.13.4] Judgement: knowledge of emerging diseases matters because it allows targeted, proportionate measures, which cost less and do less harm than either doing nothing or applying blanket restrictions.
[bio22-s7.13.5] The more is known about a disease's factors, the more precisely its spread can be limited.

CANDIDATES bio22-s6: Emergence and spread of infectious disease
[bio22-s6.0.b] Disease specialists use precise words for how common a disease is and how fast it is changing. You need these terms to describe any outbreak, and they appear throughout the rest of this subsection.
[bio22-s6.0.0] An emerging infectious disease is one that has newly appeared in a population, or one that was known but is rapidly increasing in incidence (the number of new cases) or in geographic range.
[bio22-s6.0.1] A re-emerging disease is one that had declined and is increasing again, e.g. measles where vaccination rates fall, or TB with drug-resistant strains.
[bio22-s6.0.2] Endemic means always present at a fairly steady level in a region, so the number of cases is predictable from year to year.
[bio22-s6.0.3] An epidemic, or outbreak, is a sudden increase in cases above the expected level in a region.
[bio22-s6.0.4] A pandemic is an epidemic that spreads across several continents or worldwide, e.g. COVID-19, which the World Health Organization declared a pandemic on 11 March 2020.
[bio22-s6.1.b] Some terms describe where a pathogen (a disease-causing organism) lives between outbreaks and who can pass it on. These explain why some diseases are so hard to get rid of.
[bio22-s6.1.0] A zoonosis is an infectious disease that passes from animals to humans, e.g. avian influenza or rabies.
[bio22-s6.1.1] Most emerging human diseases are zoonoses, often from wild animals, because people have little immunity to pathogens they have never met. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.1.2] A reservoir is a host population in which a pathogen persists and from which it infects others, e.g. badgers for bovine TB in Ireland, or wild birds for avian influenza.
[bio22-s6.1.3] A carrier is an infected individual who shows no symptoms but can pass on the pathogen, so nobody knows to avoid them.
[bio22-s6.2.b] A pathogen can only cause an outbreak if it can get from one host to the next. There are several routes, and each has typical diseases that use it, in both animals and plants.
[bio22-s6.2.0] Airborne droplets and aerosols (tiny particles hanging in the air) from coughs, sneezes and breath spread influenza, TB, measles and COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.2.1] Wind-borne spores spread ash dieback, cereal rusts and potato blight in plants, because spores are light enough to be carried between fields.
[bio22-s6.2.2] Direct contact, touch and skin to skin, spreads ringworm and athlete's foot.
[bio22-s6.2.3] Indirect contact via contaminated objects and surfaces (fomites), hands, tools and clothing spreads disease. In plants, contact with hands and tools spreads tobacco mosaic virus.
[bio22-s6.2.4] Contaminated food and water, the faecal–oral route (pathogens in faeces reach the mouth), spreads cholera, Salmonella food poisoning and amoebic dysentery.
[bio22-s6.2.5] Body fluids spread HIV and hepatitis B through sexual contact, blood (shared needles and unscreened transfusions) and mother to baby.
[bio22-s6.2.6] Vectors are animals that carry pathogens between hosts: mosquitoes (malaria), ticks (Lyme disease), tsetse flies (sleeping sickness), elm bark beetles (Dutch elm disease), aphids (many plant viruses, e.g. barley yellow dwarf virus) and biting midges (bluetongue virus of sheep and cattle).
[bio22-s6.2.7] In plants, rain splash, soil, infected seed, tubers and cuttings also spread disease. Vegetative propagation (growing new plants from parts of old ones) passes viruses and blight to the next crop, and grafting does too.
[bio22-s6.3.b] Diseases spread when infected organisms, or the things they touch, move. Over short distances this happens through everyday contact, and over a whole country it follows the trade and travel routes of animals and people.
[bio22-s6.3.0] Local spread occurs within a household, school, hospital or farm, between neighbouring fields by wind and rain splash, and between neighbouring farms by animals straying, shared equipment, vehicles and people's boots.
[bio22-s6.3.1] Regional spread occurs when farm animals move through marts (livestock markets) and between farms, when plants and animals are traded within a country or the EU, when wild animals move seasonally, and when people commute and holiday.
[bio22-s6.3.2] In the 2001 foot-and-mouth disease epidemic in Britain, infected sheep were moved through markets across the country before the disease was detected, spreading it to many farms.
[bio22-s6.3.3] Control of that epidemic needed a ban on animal movement and the culling of millions of animals, which shows how costly it is once movement has carried a disease widely.
[bio22-s6.4.b] Modern travel and trade mean a pathogen no longer stays where it started. A disease can reach another continent before anyone knows it is there.
[bio22-s6.4.0] Air travel can carry an infected person to any continent within a day, faster than many diseases take to show symptoms, so travellers can carry infection without knowing it.
[bio22-s6.4.1] COVID-19 reached every continent within months of its first identification in Wuhan, China, in December 2019.
[bio22-s6.4.2] International trade moves pathogens and vectors in plants, wood, soil, live animals and goods. Ash dieback spread through Europe partly in imported young ash plants and was found in Ireland in 2012.
[bio22-s6.4.3] The second, more aggressive epidemic of Dutch elm disease reached Britain from the late 1960s in imported elm logs.
[bio22-s6.4.4] The Asian tiger mosquito (Aedes albopictus), a vector of several viruses, has spread to many countries in used tyres and plants.
[bio22-s6.4.5] Migrating wild birds carry avian influenza between continents, because infected birds fly long distances without any border controls.
[bio22-s6.5.b] Trade in live animals can move a disease into places where the local species have never met it. Amphibians show how serious this can be.
[bio22-s6.5.0] The pet and wildlife trade spread chytrid fungus (Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis) around the world in the trade in amphibians.
[bio22-s6.5.1] Chytrid is linked to the decline of hundreds of amphibian species and the extinction of some.
[bio22-s6.6.b] Emergence and spread depend on several interrelated factors, and the first is persistence. The longer a host carries a pathogen in a way that can pass on, the more chances it has to infect others.
[bio22-s6.6.0] The longer a host is infectious, the more others it can infect.
[bio22-s6.6.1] Persistence includes long infectious periods, carriers without symptoms, latent infections (the pathogen is present but inactive) that reactivate, and reservoir hosts.
[bio22-s6.6.2] HIV may cause no symptoms for years while the person can still pass it on.
[bio22-s6.6.3] Herpes viruses stay latent in nerve cells for life, so the person is never rid of the virus. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.6.4] TB bacteria can stay dormant (inactive) in the lungs for years and become active later.
[bio22-s6.7.b] Persistence shows up in each kind of host, from a single healthy-looking person to wildlife and crops.
[bio22-s6.7.0] Mary Mallon, known as Typhoid Mary, was born in Co. Tyrone in 1869 and worked as a cook in New York. She carried the typhoid bacterium, Salmonella Typhi, without symptoms and infected many of the families she cooked for.
[bio22-s6.7.1] Health authorities isolated her for most of the rest of her life, because as a carrier she could not be identified by illness.
[bio22-s6.7.2] Bovine TB persists in Irish cattle partly because badgers act as a wildlife reservoir that reinfects herds, so clearing the disease from cattle alone is not enough.
[bio22-s6.7.3] Plant viruses persist in perennial plants (which live for years), weeds and tubers, so infected seed potatoes carry viruses into the next year's crop.
[bio22-s6.7.4] Phytophthora infestans, the cause of potato blight, survives the winter in infected tubers left in the ground or in dumps.
[bio22-s6.8.b] A mutation is a random change in a pathogen's genetic material. Pathogens that reproduce quickly produce huge numbers of changed copies, so some will spread better than the original.
[bio22-s6.8.0] Pathogens with short generation times and large populations produce many mutants, because each new copy is another chance for a change.
[bio22-s6.8.1] RNA viruses mutate fastest because their copying enzyme doesn't proofread (check for copying errors). There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio22-s6.8.2] Antigenic drift: small mutations gradually change a virus's surface antigens (the molecules the immune system recognises), so memory cells and antibodies from earlier infections or vaccines no longer fit well.
[bio22-s6.8.3] Antigenic drift is why influenza returns every winter and why flu vaccines are updated each year.
[bio22-s6.8.4] Mutations likewise produced new SARS-CoV-2 variants, such as Delta and Omicron, that spread faster or partly escaped immunity, causing new waves of COVID-19.
[bio22-s6.9.b] Mutations can also change what a pathogen is able to do, giving it a new host or defeating a crop's defences.
[bio22-s6.9.0] Antigenic shift (influenza): if two different flu viruses infect the same cell, e.g. in a pig, their RNA segments can mix to form a new strain with surface antigens that no human population has met.
[bio22-s6.9.1] Antigenic shift has caused flu pandemics, e.g. the 2009 H1N1 (swine flu) pandemic, because with no existing immunity the new strain spreads easily.
[bio22-s6.9.2] Mutations can let a pathogen infect a new host species (spillover, e.g. a bird virus gaining the ability to infect mammals), become more transmissible, or overcome resistance bred into crops.
[bio22-s6.9.3] The Ug99 strain of wheat stem rust, identified in Uganda in 1999, overcame resistance genes used in most of the world's wheat varieties.
[bio22-s6.10.b] Resistance means a pathogen or pest survives a drug or chemical that used to kill it. It arises by natural selection (survival of the individuals best suited to their conditions), which happens in four steps. There is more on this in The theory of evolution by natural selection.
[bio22-s6.10.0] 1. Random mutations, or genes passed between bacteria on plasmids (small rings of DNA), make a few individuals resistant.
[bio22-s6.10.1] 2. The antibiotic or chemical kills the susceptible individuals.
[bio22-s6.10.2] 3. The resistant survivors reproduce with less competition.
[bio22-s6.10.3] 4. The next generation is mostly resistant. The chemical doesn't cause the mutation; it selects it.
[bio22-s6.11.b] Resistance arises by natural selection when a drug or chemical kills susceptible individuals, and some practices make it spread faster. The examples cover people, crops and livestock.
[bio22-s6.11.0] Overuse and misuse of antibiotics speed up resistance: use for viral infections (which antibiotics cannot treat), not finishing courses, and use in farm animals.
[bio22-s6.11.1] Poor infection control in hospitals and heavy repeated use of one pesticide also speed up resistance.
[bio22-s6.11.2] MRSA (methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus) is resistant to many antibiotics and spreads in hospitals.
[bio22-s6.11.3] Multidrug-resistant TB, malaria parasites resistant to some antimalarial drugs, and mosquitoes resistant to insecticides, which weakens malaria control.
[bio22-s6.11.4] Fungi that cause crop diseases are resistant to some fungicides, and parasitic worms of sheep and cattle are resistant to some anthelmintics (drugs that kill worms).
[bio22-s6.11.5] Resistant pathogens persist longer in hosts and spread further, linking factors 1 and 3.
[bio22-s6.12.b] A pathogen needs susceptible hosts, meaning individuals with no immunity to it. How many of these there are in a population strongly affects how far and how fast an outbreak goes.
[bio22-s6.12.0] A pathogen spreads only if it can reach susceptible (non-immune) hosts. The higher the proportion of immune individuals, the fewer susceptible hosts there are and the slower the spread. This is herd immunity. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio22-s6.12.1] A pathogen new to a population meets no immunity at all, so it spreads fast and can be very severe, e.g. COVID-19 in 2020, before vaccines.
[bio22-s6.12.2] Smallpox and measles, brought by Europeans to the Americas from the late 1400s, killed very large numbers of Indigenous people, who had never been exposed to them.
[bio22-s6.12.3] Immunity falls when vaccination rates drop, as when measles outbreaks return.
[bio22-s6.12.4] Immunity also falls when protection from vaccination or infection wanes over time, and where many people have weakened immune systems, e.g. from untreated HIV or malnutrition, or the very young and very old.
[bio22-s6.13.b] Plants have no antibody-based immunity like animals, but the same principle applies. What matters is whether any individuals in the crop can resist the pathogen.
[bio22-s6.13.0] In plants, the equivalent is genetic uniformity. A monoculture (a crop of one genetically identical variety) has no resistant individuals, so a pathogen that infects one plant can infect them all.
[bio22-s6.13.1] In Ireland in the 1840s, most of the poor depended on one potato variety, the Lumper, grown from tubers (genetically identical clones) and susceptible to blight.
[bio22-s6.13.2] When Phytophthora infestans arrived in 1845, crops failed across the country in successive years. About one million people died and over a million emigrated in the Great Famine.
[bio22-s6.14.b] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more other hosts they meet, so the further a pathogen travels. Mobility applies to people, livestock, wildlife and vectors.
[bio22-s6.14.0] The more infected hosts move and mix, the more contacts each has and the further the pathogen spreads. The routes are those in local, regional and global spread above.
[bio22-s6.14.1] Crowding in cities, refugee camps, festivals and mass gatherings, travel and migration, and the movement of livestock all increase spread.
[bio22-s6.14.2] Migration of wild animals, such as birds, also moves pathogens into new areas.
[bio22-s6.14.3] Vector mobility: climate warming lets vectors survive further north and for longer each year, carrying disease into new regions.
[bio22-s6.14.4] Bluetongue, a midge-borne virus of sheep and cattle, spread into northern Europe from 2006.
[bio22-s6.14.5] Reducing mobility reduces spread: animal movement bans in foot-and-mouth outbreaks, travel restrictions and quarantine in human epidemics, and import controls on plants. There is more on this in Emerging diseases and society.
[bio22-s6.15.b] To compare outbreaks and predict whether one will grow, scientists use a single number, R₀. It measures how contagious a pathogen is when nobody has immunity.
[bio22-s6.15.0] The basic reproduction number, R₀ ('R nought'), is the average number of new (secondary) infections caused by one infected individual in a population where everyone is susceptible, with no immunity and no control measures.
[bio22-s6.15.1] R₀ greater than 1: each case causes more than one new case on average, so numbers grow and an epidemic can occur.
[bio22-s6.15.2] R₀ equal to 1: numbers stay steady, which is endemic.
[bio22-s6.15.3] R₀ less than 1: each case causes fewer than one new case, so the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.16.b] Each infected person passes the pathogen to R₀ others, so cases multiply by R₀ every generation of infection (one round of people infecting the next). Working through numbers shows why R₀ above or below 1 matters.
[bio22-s6.16.0] Growth example: with R₀ = 2, one case leads to 2, then 4, then 8, then 16 new cases per generation of infection. After 10 generations, one case has led to 2¹⁰ = 1,024 new cases in that generation.
[bio22-s6.16.1] Decline example: with R₀ = 0.5, 100 cases give 50, then 25, then about 12, and the outbreak fades.
[bio22-s6.16.2] Diagram: a branching tree from one case, giving 2, then 4, then 8 cases (R₀ = 2), beside a single chain with one case per generation (R₀ = 1). Label each generation.
[bio22-s6.17.b] R₀ is not a fixed property of a pathogen. It combines features of the pathogen, the host and the environment, which are the factors already covered.
[bio22-s6.17.0] R₀ depends on how long a host is infectious (persistence), so a longer infectious period raises R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.1] R₀ depends on how many contacts an infected host has per day (mobility and crowding), so more contacts raise R₀.
[bio22-s6.17.2] R₀ depends on the chance that a contact passes on the infection (the transmission route, and mutations that make a pathogen more transmissible).
[bio22-s6.17.3] So R₀ is not fixed for a disease: it differs between populations and settings.
[bio22-s6.18.b] In real outbreaks some people are already immune or control measures are in place. The effective reproduction number measures spread under those real conditions.
[bio22-s6.18.0] The effective reproduction number, R (sometimes written Rt), is the average number of new infections per case at a given time, in a population where some people are immune or control measures are in place.
[bio22-s6.18.1] Approximately, R = R₀ × the proportion still susceptible. For example, R₀ = 3 with half the population immune gives R = 3 × 0.5 = 1.5.
[bio22-s6.18.2] Control aims to bring R below 1, so that each case infects fewer than one other person and the outbreak dies out.
[bio22-s6.19.b] If R₀ tells us how contagious a disease is, we can work out how many people must be immune to stop it spreading. The more contagious the disease, the higher that proportion.
[bio22-s6.19.0] Herd-immunity threshold: to stop sustained spread, the proportion immune must be at least 1 − 1/R₀.
[bio22-s6.19.1] Examples: R₀ = 2 needs 1 − ½ = 50% immune; R₀ = 4 needs 1 − ¼ = 75% immune; R₀ = 10 needs 90% immune.
[bio22-s6.19.2] Measles is one of the most contagious diseases, with R₀ usually quoted as 12–18, so about 95% of people must be immune.
[bio22-s6.19.3] The World Health Organization therefore recommends at least 95% coverage with two doses of measles vaccine.
[bio22-s6.20.b] R₀ comes from a simple model, and a model is only as good as its assumptions. Real outbreaks differ in important ways.
[bio22-s6.20.0] The model's assumptions are that everyone is equally likely to meet everyone else and that R₀ stays constant. There is more on this in Models.
[bio22-s6.20.1] Real populations are clustered, behaviour changes during outbreaks and new variants change R₀, so estimates are updated as data come in.
[bio22-s6.21.b] The six factors do not work one at a time. Real outbreaks happen when several combine, and other changes in how we live bring pathogens and new hosts into contact.
[bio22-s6.21.0] The factors act together, not one at a time. A mutation that makes a virus more transmissible raises R₀, and a population with low immunity and high mobility then lets it spread widely.
[bio22-s6.21.1] Long persistence in carriers lets a pathogen reach new areas undetected, and resistance to drugs lets infections last longer, raising R₀ further.
[bio22-s6.21.2] Plant example: a genetically uniform crop (low immunity), a pathogen spread by wind and trade (mobility) and survival in tubers (persistence) together explain the speed of the potato famine.
[bio22-s6.21.3] Other drivers increase contact between pathogens and new hosts: clearing forests and expanding farms into wild areas (more contact with wild animals), intensive farming of large numbers of genetically similar animals kept close together, climate change (vectors' ranges), poverty, poor sanitation, and conflict, which weakens health systems.
