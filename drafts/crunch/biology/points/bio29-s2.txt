TARGET bio29-s2: Circulation
[bio29-s2.0.b] Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to get rid of wastes. The circulatory system is the transport network that does this for all of them.
[bio29-s2.0.0] The human circulatory system has three parts: the heart, a pump; the blood vessels, a closed network of tubes made up of arteries, capillaries and veins; and the blood, the transport medium that carries the materials.
[bio29-s2.0.1] It is a closed system: blood stays inside the vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across the thin walls of the capillaries (the tiniest vessels, which pass right alongside the cells).
[bio29-s2.0.2] In a large, many-celled animal, diffusion (the slow, random spreading of molecules) alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut and kidneys) and cells deep in the body.
[bio29-s2.0.3] The circulatory system solves this by carrying materials quickly by mass flow (the whole liquid moving together), so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
[bio29-s2.0.4] Materials transported: oxygen (lungs to all cells); carbon dioxide (cells to lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine to liver to cells); urea (liver to kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands to target organs); water and salts.
[bio29-s2.0.5] Also transported: antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets (which seal wounds); and heat, carried from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin.
[bio29-s2.1.b] Humans have two circuits joined at the heart rather than one loop. Blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and is then sent out to the rest of the body.
[bio29-s2.1.0] Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. This does not mean two hearts; it is one heart with two sides.
[bio29-s2.1.1] The pulmonary circuit (pulmonary means 'of the lungs') runs heart to lungs to heart. The systemic circuit runs heart to body organs to heart.
[bio29-s2.1.2] The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit. There is more on the chambers in Investigation: the heart.
[bio29-s2.1.3] Full route of a red blood cell: vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, semilunar valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valve, aorta, artery, arteriole, capillaries of an organ, venule, vein, vena cava.
[bio29-s2.1.4] Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation. Heart as four boxes, lungs above, body organs below. Add arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae.
[bio29-s2.1.5] Diagram labels: right and left atrium and ventricle, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta, venae cavae, pulmonary circuit, systemic circuit. Shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue.
[bio29-s2.2.b] The pulmonary circuit has one job: to send blood that has given up its oxygen to the lungs to collect more. It starts at the right side of the heart.
[bio29-s2.2.0] 1. Deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) is pumped by the right ventricle (the lower right chamber, which pumps blood out of the heart) into the pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s2.2.1] 2. The pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung, so both lungs receive blood.
[bio29-s2.2.2] 3. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂, because oxygen diffuses in from the air in the alveoli (air sacs) and CO₂ diffuses out. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio29-s2.2.3] 4. Oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium (the upper left chamber, which receives blood coming into the heart).
[bio29-s2.3.b] The systemic circuit takes oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to every organ and brings it back. Its steps follow on from the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.3.0] 1. Oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve (the one-way valve between the left atrium and left ventricle) to the left ventricle.
[bio29-s2.3.1] 2. The left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, the body's largest artery.
[bio29-s2.3.2] 3. Arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ, where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes.
[bio29-s2.3.3] 4. Deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae (the large veins bringing blood from the upper and lower body) and into the right atrium.
[bio29-s2.3.4] 5. It passes through the tricuspid valve (the one-way valve between the right atrium and right ventricle) to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
[bio29-s2.4.b] A double circulation is better than a single loop for an active, warm-blooded animal. Passing the blood through the heart twice solves two problems: lost pressure and the risk of damage to the lungs.
[bio29-s2.4.0] (a) Blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all the body organs quickly.
[bio29-s2.4.1] (b) The two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit, to drive blood through the many organs, and lower in the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.4.2] The lower pulmonary pressure matters because the lung capillaries are thin-walled, so they are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs).
[bio29-s2.4.3] (c) The septum (the wall dividing the two sides of the heart) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated.
[bio29-s2.4.4] Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
[bio29-s2.5.b] Blood reaches each organ along its own named artery and leaves along its own named vein. Knowing these names lets you describe the route to any organ.
[bio29-s2.5.0] A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and is drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and each is usually named after the organ.
[bio29-s2.5.1] Lungs (pulmonary pathway): the pulmonary artery, from the right ventricle, carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins, to the left atrium, carry oxygenated blood. In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s2.5.2] Head and brain: the carotid arteries, in the neck, supply it, and the jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava. Arms: the subclavian arteries and veins. Legs: the iliac arteries and veins.
[bio29-s2.5.3] Kidneys: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered, and the renal veins return filtered blood, low in urea, to the inferior vena cava. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio29-s2.5.4] Heart muscle: the coronary arteries and cardiac veins, which supply the heart's own muscle with blood.
[bio29-s2.5.5] Liver and gut: the liver receives blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut). Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
[bio29-s2.5.6] Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels showing the heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
[bio29-s2.6.b] Most veins take blood straight back towards the heart. The vein from the gut is unusual because it detours through the liver first, so the liver gets the first chance to deal with what has just been absorbed.
[bio29-s2.6.0] A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. Blood therefore passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
[bio29-s2.6.1] The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the capillaries in the villi (the tiny finger-like folds of the small intestine wall), and from the walls of the stomach and large intestine.
[bio29-s2.6.2] It carries this blood to a second set of capillaries, the sinusoids, in the liver. After a meal this blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
[bio29-s2.6.3] Absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio29-s2.6.4] The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood, because it has already passed through the gut capillaries, so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery.
[bio29-s2.7.b] The circulatory system works as the delivery service for every other system. Each system either puts materials into the blood or takes materials out of it, and you can model these exchanges.
[bio29-s2.7.0] Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂. The circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and carries CO₂ back, so without circulation the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
[bio29-s2.7.1] Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals, small vessels in the villi) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells. The blood also supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
[bio29-s2.7.2] Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it. The kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume.
[bio29-s2.7.3] Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs, for example insulin from the pancreas to the liver and ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys. Adrenaline, carried in the blood, increases heart rate.
[bio29-s2.7.4] Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds.
[bio29-s2.7.5] Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells. Active muscles receive extra blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, and contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping to return blood to the heart.
[bio29-s2.7.6] Nervous system: the medulla oblongata (the part of the brain that controls automatic functions) adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles (small arteries), so blood is sent where it is needed. The brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
[bio29-s2.7.7] Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs, and widening or narrowing of the skin arterioles controls heat loss.
[bio29-s2.8.b] A model is a simplified picture that shows how the systems connect. A flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre makes it clear which materials travel where.
[bio29-s2.8.0] Diagram: a labelled flow diagram modelling these interactions, with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain. Label each arrow with the materials transferred: O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones and heat.
[bio29-s2.8.1] One limitation of the model is that it shows what moves but not the structure of the vessels or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
[bio29-s2.8.2] Worked example: during exercise, muscles respire faster, so breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s2.8.3] In the same example, heart rate rises and the arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them, while the arterioles to the gut narrow. Heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

CANDIDATES bio29-s1: Investigation: the heart
[bio29-s1.0.b] The heart is a muscular pump that never stops, and its structure is built around one job: moving blood. It is divided into four chambers, two upper and two lower, split into a left and a right side by a central wall.
[bio29-s1.0.0] Position and size: the heart is a hollow, muscular organ about the size of a clenched fist, in the thorax (the chest) between the two lungs and behind the sternum (breastbone), with its pointed lower end (the apex) tilted to the left.
[bio29-s1.0.1] The heart is enclosed in the pericardium, a double membrane with fluid between its layers. The fluid reduces friction as the heart beats, and the membrane prevents the heart from over-stretching.
[bio29-s1.0.2] Atria (singular atrium): the two upper chambers receive blood returning to the heart from the veins and pump it the short distance into the ventricles below, so their walls are thin. Each has an ear-like flap on the outside, the auricle.
[bio29-s1.0.3] Ventricles: the two lower chambers pump blood out of the heart into the arteries (the vessels that carry blood away from the heart), so their walls are thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.0.4] The left ventricle wall is much thicker than the right, because the left pumps blood all round the body (the systemic circuit) against high resistance. The right pumps blood only to the nearby lungs (the pulmonary circuit) at lower pressure, which protects the delicate lung capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels).
[bio29-s1.0.5] Septum: the thick wall of muscle dividing the heart into left and right sides. It keeps oxygenated blood (left side) separate from deoxygenated blood (right side), so the blood pumped to the body carries as much oxygen as possible.
[bio29-s1.0.6] In other words, the heart is two separate pumps side by side: the right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs, and the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.b] Blood has to arrive at the heart and leave it by separate routes. Veins bring blood in to the atria, and arteries carry it out from the ventricles.
[bio29-s1.1.0] Veins entering the heart are thin-walled and bring blood to the atria. Arteries carry blood away from the heart and veins carry it towards it; this is not about oxygen, which is why one artery and one set of veins break the usual pattern below.
[bio29-s1.1.1] The venae cavae bring deoxygenated blood into the right atrium: the superior (anterior) vena cava from the head and arms, and the inferior (posterior) vena cava from the rest of the body.
[bio29-s1.1.2] The pulmonary veins, four in humans, bring oxygenated blood from the lungs into the left atrium.
[bio29-s1.1.3] Arteries leaving the heart are thick-walled and carry blood away from the ventricles. The pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle and divides to take deoxygenated blood to the two lungs.
[bio29-s1.1.4] The aorta, the largest artery, leaves the left ventricle, arches over the heart and takes oxygenated blood to the body.
[bio29-s1.1.5] The coronary arteries branch from the base of the aorta to supply the heart muscle itself with blood. There is more on this in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.2.b] Blood pressure inside the heart changes sharply as the muscle squeezes, and blood would simply flow back where it came from without help. Valves are flaps of tissue that keep blood flowing in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.2.0] Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and its ventricle: the tricuspid valve, with three flaps, on the right, and the bicuspid (mitral) valve, with two flaps, on the left. A reminder is tRIcuspid on the RIght.
[bio29-s1.2.1] AV valves let blood pass from atrium to ventricle and close when the ventricle contracts, so blood is not pushed back into the atrium.
[bio29-s1.2.2] The flaps of the AV valves are attached by tough cords, the chordae tendineae (heart strings), to small papillary muscles on the ventricle wall. These hold the flaps and stop them turning inside out into the atria under the high pressure when the ventricles contract.
[bio29-s1.2.3] Semilunar valves lie at the base of the pulmonary artery and of the aorta. Each has three half-moon-shaped pockets.
[bio29-s1.2.4] Blood pushed out by the ventricle flattens the pockets against the artery wall. When the ventricle relaxes, blood falling back fills the pockets, which meet and close the opening, so blood cannot flow back into the ventricle.
[bio29-s1.3.b] The heart wall is made of a special muscle found nowhere else in the body. Its features suit it to beating without rest for a whole lifetime.
[bio29-s1.3.0] Cardiac muscle tissue is found only in the heart wall. Its cells are striated (striped) and branched, and are joined end to end by intercalated discs.
[bio29-s1.3.1] Intercalated discs let electrical impulses pass quickly from cell to cell, so the muscle contracts as a coordinated unit.
[bio29-s1.3.2] Each cell has one nucleus and very many mitochondria, which supply ATP (the cell's energy currency) by aerobic respiration, so the heart beats throughout life without tiring (fatigue).
[bio29-s1.3.3] Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts by itself without needing a nerve impulse. There is more on the pacemaker that starts each beat in Heartbeat.
[bio29-s1.3.4] Diagram: a vertical section through the heart viewed from the front, so that the heart's left side is on the right of the page. Label the right and left atria, the right and left ventricles (left wall thicker), the septum, the tricuspid and bicuspid valves, the chordae tendineae, the papillary muscles, the semilunar valves, the superior and inferior venae cavae, the pulmonary artery, the pulmonary veins and the aorta. Add arrows for the direction of blood flow, and shade or label the oxygenated (left) and deoxygenated (right) sides.
[bio29-s1.3.5] Diagram: cardiac muscle as seen under the microscope, from a prepared slide or micrograph, showing branched, striated cells with a central nucleus and intercalated discs. Label the striations, nucleus, intercalated disc and branch.
[bio29-s1.4.b] A dissection is an investigation, so it needs an aim, hypotheses to test, controlled variables and both kinds of data. The heart you cut open is a model for the human heart.
[bio29-s1.4.0] Aim: to identify the external and internal structures of a mammalian heart by dissection, and to relate each structure to its function, supporting conclusions with primary data (observations and measurements) and secondary data (diagrams, models, micrographs and images of the human heart).
[bio29-s1.4.1] Hypothesis to test: the wall of the left ventricle is thicker than the wall of the right ventricle, because it pumps blood around the whole body.
[bio29-s1.4.2] Hypothesis to test: arteries leaving the heart have thicker walls than veins entering it.
[bio29-s1.4.3] Hypothesis to test: the valves allow blood to flow in one direction only.
[bio29-s1.4.4] Variables for the measured part (wall thickness): the independent variable (the one you change) is the chamber (left ventricle, right ventricle or atrium); the dependent variable (the one you measure) is the wall thickness in mm.
[bio29-s1.4.5] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): measure at the same level, about halfway between the base and the apex, with the same ruler or calipers, on the same heart, with the cut made at right angles to the wall.
[bio29-s1.4.6] Repeat each measurement three times at different points and calculate the mean. Pool class results from several hearts to check repeatability.
[bio29-s1.4.7] Secondary data: a labelled diagram or model of the human heart to compare with the dissected heart; micrographs or a prepared slide of cardiac muscle; published images or measurements of human hearts. The dissected heart is a model for the human heart, because mammalian hearts have the same four chambers, valves and vessels.
[bio29-s1.4.8] Equipment: a fresh or thawed sheep's, pig's or ox's heart from a butcher, ideally with the vessels left long; dissecting board or tray; scalpel; sharp dissecting scissors; forceps; a blunt seeker or probe; a glass rod or drinking straw; ruler or calipers; wash bottle or beaker of water; disposable gloves; lab coat or apron; safety glasses; paper towels; disinfectant; a labelled diagram of the heart; a camera or phone to record observations.
[bio29-s1.5.b] Start with protection and with the outside of the heart. Identifying the front surface and the vessels first tells you which side is which before you cut anything.
[bio29-s1.5.0] 1. Put on gloves, lab coat and safety glasses. Rinse the heart under cold running water to remove blood clots, and dry it with paper towel.
[bio29-s1.5.1] 2. Identify the front (ventral) surface: it has a coronary artery running diagonally across it, and the pulmonary artery leaves from the front. Place the heart with the front facing you, so the heart's left side is on your right. Squeeze both sides: the left ventricle feels firmer and thicker.
[bio29-s1.5.2] 3. Examine the outside. Identify the atria (soft, ear-like flaps at the top), the ventricles (firm, at the bottom), the apex, the coronary arteries and fat on the surface, and the vessels at the top.
[bio29-s1.5.3] Arteries (aorta and pulmonary artery) have thick, rubbery walls and stay open; veins (venae cavae and pulmonary veins) have thin, floppy walls and collapse. Draw and label what you see, and photograph it.
[bio29-s1.5.4] 4. Find which vessel joins which chamber: push a blunt probe or glass rod gently into each vessel to see which chamber it enters.
[bio29-s1.5.5] The vena cava leads to the right atrium; the pulmonary artery leaves the right ventricle; the pulmonary veins lead to the left atrium; the aorta leaves the left ventricle.
[bio29-s1.5.6] Butchers often trim the vessels short, so use the diagram (secondary data) to identify any that are missing.
[bio29-s1.6.b] Now cut the heart open to look at the chambers, valves and wall thickness. Work on the right side first, then the left, then compare the walls in cross-section.
[bio29-s1.6.0] 5. Open the right side. Using scissors, cut from the right atrium down the outside wall of the right ventricle towards the apex, and open the chambers.
[bio29-s1.6.1] Observe the tricuspid valve (three flaps), the chordae tendineae and the papillary muscles. Pull gently on a cord to see how it holds a flap. 6. Push the probe from the right ventricle up into the pulmonary artery, then cut along the artery to open it, and observe the three pockets of the semilunar valve at its base.
[bio29-s1.6.2] 7. Open the left side in the same way: cut through the left atrium and down the left ventricle. Observe the bicuspid valve (two flaps) and its chordae tendineae. Follow the probe from the left ventricle into the aorta, cut it open and observe its semilunar valve. Look just above the valve pockets for the small openings of the coronary arteries.
[bio29-s1.6.3] 8. Valve test (optional): before opening the arteries, hold the heart upright and pour water from a wash bottle into the aorta or pulmonary artery from above. The semilunar pockets fill and close, so the water does not pass down into the ventricle, showing that the valves allow one-way flow.
[bio29-s1.6.4] Similarly, water poured into a ventricle through the atrium lifts the AV valve flaps up towards a closed position.
[bio29-s1.6.5] 9. Cut a transverse section (straight across) across both ventricles about halfway between the base and the apex. Observe the septum between them.
[bio29-s1.6.6] Measure the wall thickness of the left ventricle, the right ventricle and the septum with a ruler, following the variables and repeats above. Compare with the thickness of an atrium wall.
[bio29-s1.6.7] 10. Examine a prepared slide of cardiac muscle under the light microscope, or a micrograph, and draw it. 11. Record observations in a table and on labelled drawings. Dispose of the heart and clean up as described under Safety and ethics.
[bio29-s1.7.b] A dissection uses sharp blades and raw animal tissue, so each hazard has a specific precaution. There is also an ethical side to using animal material.
[bio29-s1.7.0] Scalpels and scissors are very sharp: cut on the dissecting board, away from your body and fingers, and never towards another person. Hold the tissue with forceps, not fingers, near the blade, carry sharp instruments pointing down, and report any cut at once.
[bio29-s1.7.1] Raw meat can carry bacteria, such as Salmonella, that cause food poisoning. Wear gloves, do not touch your face, never eat or drink in the laboratory, keep the heart on the tray, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap afterwards.
[bio29-s1.7.2] Disinfect the board, instruments and bench after the dissection. Wrap the heart and remove it as directed by the teacher; it is not to be left in an open bin.
[bio29-s1.7.3] Wear safety glasses, as fluid can spray when cutting or when pouring water through the vessels.
[bio29-s1.7.4] Ethics: use a heart from an animal already killed for food, a by-product of the food industry, so that no animal is killed for the dissection, and treat the tissue with respect.
[bio29-s1.7.5] A student who objects can use a model, a virtual dissection or videos as secondary data, in line with the 3Rs of animal use. There is more on this in Science, scientists and society.
[bio29-s1.8.b] Your results come from two sources: primary data, which you collect yourself from the dissected heart, and secondary data, which comes from diagrams, models and micrographs. Together they support the conclusions.
[bio29-s1.8.0] Expected observations: four chambers; atria thin-walled and small, ventricles thick-walled; the left ventricle wall clearly thicker than the right, typically several times thicker in measurements; the septum thick and muscular.
[bio29-s1.8.1] Valves: the tricuspid valve with three flaps on the right and the bicuspid valve with two on the left, both held by chordae tendineae to papillary muscles; three-pocket semilunar valves at the base of the aorta and pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s1.8.2] Vessels: the aorta with a much thicker, more elastic wall than the venae cavae; the coronary arteries on the surface, with their openings just above the aortic valve.
[bio29-s1.8.3] Results table to record: the structure; where it is found; its description (colour, texture, thickness, number of flaps); the measured wall thickness in mm, with three readings and the mean; and its function.
[bio29-s1.8.4] Secondary data: the dissected heart matches the human heart diagram in chambers, valves and vessels. Differences seen are due to the butcher's trimming, which may remove the atria or vessels, or to species size.
[bio29-s1.8.5] Micrographs show the branched, striated cardiac muscle cells and intercalated discs, which cannot be seen with the naked eye.
[bio29-s1.9.b] The conclusion links each structure you found to its function, and states whether each hypothesis was supported by the evidence.
[bio29-s1.9.0] The left ventricle has the thickest wall because it must generate the highest pressure to pump blood all round the body; the right ventricle pumps only to the lungs.
[bio29-s1.9.1] Atria have thin walls because they pump blood only into the ventricles below them.
[bio29-s1.9.2] The valves, the AV valves with their chordae tendineae and the semilunar valves, allow blood to flow only from atria to ventricles to arteries, never backwards. The water test supports this.
[bio29-s1.9.3] The septum separates oxygenated and deoxygenated blood.
[bio29-s1.9.4] Arteries have thick walls to withstand high pressure, and veins have thin walls. There is more on this in Blood vessels.
[bio29-s1.9.5] For each hypothesis in the planning section, state whether it is supported, citing the measurements and observations.
[bio29-s1.10.b] A dissected animal heart is not a living human heart, and the measurements are rough. Knowing the limits shows how far the conclusions can be trusted and how to improve them.
[bio29-s1.10.0] Butchers often remove the atria or cut the vessels very short, so some structures may be missing or hard to identify. Vessels and valves may be damaged by cutting.
[bio29-s1.10.1] Wall-thickness measurements vary with where and at what angle the cut is made, and soft tissue compresses under the ruler. Measure at the same level at right angles, repeat three times and use the mean.
[bio29-s1.10.2] Heart muscle relaxes and blood clots after death, and freezing and thawing change the texture, so the chambers are not in their living shape.
[bio29-s1.10.3] A sheep's or pig's heart is a model: its size and some features differ from a human heart, for example the number of pulmonary vein openings, so conclusions about humans need secondary data.
[bio29-s1.10.4] One heart per group is a small sample; pooling class data improves repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio29-s5: Blood vessels
[bio29-s5.0.b] Human blood never leaves a set of tubes called blood vessels, which is why the system is described as closed. The vessels differ in structure, and the differences make sense once you know the circuit and the three-layered wall that most of them share.
[bio29-s5.0.0] Blood flows in a closed circuit: heart, arteries, arterioles (the smallest arteries), capillaries, venules (the smallest veins), veins, and back to the heart.
[bio29-s5.0.1] Arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry blood towards the heart. Capillaries link them inside the organs, and it is across capillary walls that materials are exchanged with the cells.
[bio29-s5.0.2] The walls of arteries and veins have three layers. The inner layer is the endothelium, one cell thick and smooth, which reduces friction as blood slides past. The middle layer is smooth muscle and elastic fibres.
[bio29-s5.0.3] The outer layer is tough connective tissue containing collagen fibres, which protects the vessel and stops it over-stretching. The space in the centre, through which blood flows, is the lumen.
[bio29-s5.0.4] Capillaries have only the endothelium, with no muscle, elastic or connective layers. This is why they can act as exchange surfaces, as the capillary block below shows.
[bio29-s5.1.b] Blood leaves the heart in surges at high pressure, and arteries have to carry it to the organs without bursting. Each feature of an artery's structure helps it cope with that pressure and keep the blood moving.
[bio29-s5.1.0] Role: arteries carry blood away from the heart to the organs, at high pressure. All arteries carry oxygenated blood except the pulmonary artery, which takes deoxygenated blood to the lungs.
[bio29-s5.1.1] Thick wall, with a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic fibres, to withstand the high pressure of blood pumped from the ventricles (the heart's lower pumping chambers) without bursting.
[bio29-s5.1.2] Elastic fibres let the wall stretch as each surge of blood enters, then recoil between beats. This pushes the blood on, keeps the pressure up between beats and smooths the flow. The stretching is felt as the pulse.
[bio29-s5.1.3] Narrow lumen relative to the thickness of the wall, which helps keep the pressure high. In section, an artery keeps a round shape, because its thick wall holds it open.
[bio29-s5.1.4] No valves, except the semilunar valves (pocket-shaped valves) where the aorta and pulmonary artery leave the heart. The high pressure keeps blood moving forwards, so valves are not needed along the length.
[bio29-s5.1.5] Blood flows fast and in spurts, and a cut artery spurts bright red blood. Bright red is the colour of oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s5.1.6] Arterioles are the smallest arteries, leading into the capillary networks. Their walls have a high proportion of smooth muscle, which contracts to narrow them (vasoconstriction) or relaxes to widen them (vasodilation).
[bio29-s5.1.7] By narrowing or widening, arterioles control how much blood flows to each organ, for example more to the muscles in exercise and more to the skin when hot.
[bio29-s5.2.b] By the time blood has passed through the capillaries its pressure is low, so veins must return it to the heart without a strong push behind it. Their structure reduces resistance, and valves and the squeezing of nearby muscles keep blood moving one way.
[bio29-s5.2.0] Role: veins carry blood from the organs back towards the heart, at low pressure. All veins carry deoxygenated blood except the pulmonary veins, which bring oxygenated blood from the lungs.
[bio29-s5.2.1] Thin wall, with a thin middle layer and few elastic fibres, because the pressure is low and there is no surge to withstand. The wall is easily squashed, so in section a vein often looks flattened or irregular.
[bio29-s5.2.2] Wide lumen, which reduces resistance to the slow-moving blood, so it can flow with little pressure behind it.
[bio29-s5.2.3] Valves: pocket-shaped, or semilunar, valves are found especially in the veins of the limbs and let blood flow only towards the heart. When blood starts to flow backwards, it fills the pockets, which close the vein.
[bio29-s5.2.4] Skeletal muscle pump: contracting skeletal muscles squeeze the veins that run between them, pushing blood along, and the valves prevent backflow. This is why standing still for a long time makes blood pool in the legs.
[bio29-s5.2.5] Breathing movements lower the pressure in the thorax (the chest cavity) during inhalation, which draws blood towards the heart. Veins next to arteries are also squeezed by the arterial pulse.
[bio29-s5.2.6] Venules are small veins that collect blood from capillary networks and join to form veins.
[bio29-s5.2.7] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a vein showing an open valve, with blood flowing towards the heart, and a closed valve, with its pockets filled and blocking backflow. Add arrows, and label the wall, lumen and valve.
[bio29-s5.3.b] Capillaries are where the circulatory system does its real work, because this is where materials pass between the blood and the cells. Everything about their structure makes that exchange quick and gives it time to happen.
[bio29-s5.3.0] Role: the exchange of materials between the blood and the body cells. Capillaries form dense networks, called capillary beds, in every tissue, so that no cell is far from one.
[bio29-s5.3.1] The wall is only one cell thick, consisting of endothelium only, with no muscle or elastic layers, so the diffusion distance is very short.
[bio29-s5.3.2] The lumen is very narrow, only wide enough for red blood cells to pass in single file, which brings them close to the wall.
[bio29-s5.3.3] Capillaries are so numerous that their total cross-section is large, so blood flows slowly through them, giving time for exchange.
[bio29-s5.3.4] Leaky: there are small gaps between the endothelial cells, so plasma fluid and small dissolved molecules can pass out, and white blood cells can squeeze through. Red blood cells and large plasma proteins cannot.
[bio29-s5.3.5] Pressure falls as blood flows through the capillaries, so blood leaving them enters the venules at low pressure. That is why veins have no pulse.
[bio29-s5.4.b] Cells do not touch the blood directly. Instead, part of the plasma leaks out of the capillaries and bathes the cells, and exchange takes place through this fluid.
[bio29-s5.4.0] At the arterial end of a capillary bed (the end nearer the arteriole), the blood pressure is higher and forces plasma out through the capillary wall into the spaces between the cells. Plasma is water with dissolved glucose, amino acids, salts and oxygen.
[bio29-s5.4.1] This fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes every cell and forms the cells' internal environment. Red blood cells and plasma proteins stay in the capillary because they are too large to pass through the gaps.
[bio29-s5.4.2] Substances pass between tissue fluid and cells by diffusion and active transport. Oxygen and nutrients enter the cells; CO₂, urea and other wastes leave them.
[bio29-s5.4.3] At the venous end the pressure is lower, and the plasma proteins left in the blood draw water back in by osmosis. So most of the tissue fluid, now carrying CO₂ and wastes, returns to the capillary.
[bio29-s5.4.4] The small amount of excess fluid is drained away by lymph vessels and returned to the blood.
[bio29-s5.4.5] Diagram: a capillary bed between an arteriole and a venule, surrounded by body cells. Add arrows for fluid leaving at the arterial end and returning at the venous end, and for O₂ and nutrients entering the cells and CO₂ and wastes leaving them.
[bio29-s5.5.b] Each vessel's structure matches its job, so the clearest way to revise is to compare the three feature by feature. Learn the pairing of each feature with its reason, because exam questions ask you to relate structure to role.
[bio29-s5.5.0] Direction of flow: arteries carry blood away from the heart; veins carry it towards the heart; capillaries link arterioles to venules within an organ.
[bio29-s5.5.1] Pressure: high and pulsing in arteries; falling through the capillaries; low and steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.2] Wall: thick, muscular and elastic in arteries to withstand pressure; thin, with little muscle and elastic tissue, in veins; one cell thick, endothelium only, in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.3] Lumen: narrow relative to the wall in arteries; wide in veins; just wide enough for one red blood cell in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.4] Valves: none in arteries, apart from at the heart; present in veins; none in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.5] Speed of flow: fast in arteries; slow in capillaries; slow but steady in veins.
[bio29-s5.5.6] Oxygen content: arteries usually carry oxygenated blood and veins deoxygenated blood, except in the pulmonary circuit, and capillaries are where exchange happens. This is not how the vessels are defined; they are defined by direction of flow.
[bio29-s5.5.7] Exchange: no exchange with the tissues through the thick walls of arteries and veins; all exchange happens in capillaries.
[bio29-s5.5.8] Diagram: transverse sections of an artery (round, thick wall, narrow lumen), a vein (thin wall, same three layers, wide lumen, often flattened) and a capillary (ring of one or two endothelial cells round one red blood cell). Label each layer and the lumen.
