TARGET bio32-s2: Surveys
[bio32-s2.0.b] A survey is a planned way of finding out what lives in a habitat (the place where a community of organisms lives). There are two kinds: one asks 'which species?' and the other asks 'how many?'.
[bio32-s2.0.0] A qualitative survey records which species are present in the habitat (a species list), without measuring how many of each, so it gives the 'what' but not the 'how much'.
[bio32-s2.0.1] A qualitative survey is built from all the observations and collections made during the fieldwork. There is more on this in Collection and identification.
[bio32-s2.0.2] A qualitative survey can add a subjective estimate of abundance (how many there are) using a scale such as DAFOR: Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare, judged by eye for each species.
[bio32-s2.0.3] The DAFOR scale is quick, but different observers give different grades, so it isn't objective (it depends on the person's opinion, not on a measurement).
[bio32-s2.0.4] A quantitative survey measures how many of each species there are, as numbers, percentages or densities: percentage frequency, percentage cover, density per m², or an estimate of population size.
[bio32-s2.0.5] Quantitative data can be compared between areas and analysed mathematically, so it can be used, for example, to calculate a diversity index (a single number describing how varied a community is), such as Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio32-s2.0.6] Distribution means where in the habitat a species is found; abundance means how many there are. Surveys measure both.
[bio32-s2.1.b] Counting every organism in a habitat is almost never possible, so samples (small parts of the habitat) are taken and the results used to estimate the whole. How the samples are chosen decides whether the estimate can be trusted.
[bio32-s2.1.0] A sample must be representative, meaning it reflects the whole habitat: it must be large enough and chosen without bias (without favouring particular spots).
[bio32-s2.1.1] Random sampling is used when the habitat looks uniform (the same all over), so that every point has an equal chance of being chosen.
[bio32-s2.1.2] 1. Lay two long tape measures at right angles along two edges of the study area to form the axes of a grid.
[bio32-s2.1.3] 2. Generate pairs of random numbers (with a calculator, a phone app or random-number tables) as coordinates, for example (7, 23) means 7 m along one tape and 23 m along the other.
[bio32-s2.1.4] 3. Walk to each coordinate and place the quadrat (the square sampling frame) there, with its corner on the point.
[bio32-s2.1.5] Throwing a quadrat over the shoulder is not truly random and is unsafe, so don't use it.
[bio32-s2.2.b] Random sampling suits an even habitat, but conditions often change from one side of a habitat to the other. Then samples are placed in an orderly way, and enough of them must be taken to give a reliable result.
[bio32-s2.2.0] Systematic sampling places samples at regular intervals, for example a quadrat every 2 m along a line. It is used where conditions change across the habitat (a gradient, for example from a hedge into open field), to show how species change along it; this is a transect.
[bio32-s2.2.1] The more samples, the more reliable the estimate, because chance differences between individual spots average out.
[bio32-s2.2.2] Take quadrats one at a time and keep a running mean (or a running total of species found).
[bio32-s2.2.3] When adding more quadrats no longer changes the mean (or adds new species), enough have been taken. In a grassland this is usually at least 10 to 20 quadrats.
[bio32-s2.3.b] A quadrat is the basic tool for sampling plants. Different designs suit different measurements, and a few rules keep the counting fair.
[bio32-s2.3.0] A quadrat is a square frame of known area used to sample plants and slow-moving or attached animals (ones that can't escape or move while you count). School grassland quadrats are usually 0.5 m × 0.5 m (0.25 m²) or 1 m × 1 m (1 m²).
[bio32-s2.3.1] A gridded quadrat is divided by strings or wires into 100 equal small squares (10 × 10), each 1% of the area, which makes percentages easy to read off.
[bio32-s2.3.2] A point quadrat (point frame) is a frame on legs holding a row of usually 10 long pins (needles) that can be lowered vertically through the vegetation. Each pin that touches a species counts as one 'hit' for that species.
[bio32-s2.3.3] Rule 1: lay the quadrat flat on the ground at the chosen point without moving it towards or away from any plant, so you don't choose which plants fall inside.
[bio32-s2.3.4] Rule 2: use the same size of quadrat throughout, so counts from different quadrats can be compared.
[bio32-s2.3.5] Rule 3: decide in advance how to count plants on the edge (for example count a plant only if more than half of it is inside, or count only those on the top and left edges), and apply the rule every time so no plant is counted twice or missed.
[bio32-s2.3.6] Diagram: a gridded quadrat (100 squares) placed at a random coordinate on a grid laid out with two tape measures at right angles; and a point quadrat with 10 pins, with one pin touching a leaf (a hit).
[bio32-s2.4.b] Percentage frequency tells you how widespread a species is across the samples. It asks only 'is it there or not?' in each quadrat.
[bio32-s2.4.0] Percentage frequency is the percentage of all the quadrats sampled in which a species is present: percentage frequency = (number of quadrats containing the species ÷ total number of quadrats) × 100.
[bio32-s2.4.1] Only presence or absence is recorded in each quadrat, so it is quick and objective, and works for any plant (or attached animal).
[bio32-s2.4.2] It doesn't show how many there are: one daisy or a hundred in a quadrat both count as 'present'.
[bio32-s2.4.3] It depends on quadrat size, because a larger quadrat is more likely to contain the species.
[bio32-s2.4.4] Worked example (sample data): 20 quadrats were placed at random. Daisy was present in 12, dandelion in 5 and white clover in 16.
[bio32-s2.4.5] Daisy: 12 ÷ 20 × 100 = 60%. Dandelion: 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%. White clover: 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80%.
[bio32-s2.4.6] Results are shown in a table and as a bar chart with one bar per species, with percentage frequency on the y-axis.
[bio32-s2.5.b] Percentage cover is the percentage of the ground area of a quadrat covered by a species, viewed from directly above. It measures how much space a species takes up, not how many individuals there are.
[bio32-s2.5.0] Percentage cover is used for plants that are hard to count as individuals, such as grasses, clover and mosses, which spread to form mats.
[bio32-s2.5.1] Gridded-quadrat method: count the small squares in which the species covers more than half the square; each square is 1%. Repeat in each quadrat and find the mean percentage cover.
[bio32-s2.5.2] Worked example (sample data): in five quadrats white clover covered 30, 45, 25, 40 and 35 squares; total = 175; mean percentage cover = 175 ÷ 5 = 35%.
[bio32-s2.5.3] Point-quadrat method: lower each pin and record every species it touches. Percentage cover = (number of hits on the species ÷ total number of pins lowered) × 100.
[bio32-s2.5.4] Worked example (sample data): the 10-pin frame was placed at 10 random points, so 100 pins were lowered; grass was touched by 72 pins and white clover by 23.
[bio32-s2.5.5] Grass cover = 72 ÷ 100 × 100 = 72%; clover cover = 23%.
[bio32-s2.5.6] The total cover of all species in a quadrat can be more than 100%, because plants overlap in layers (for example clover leaves above grass).
[bio32-s2.5.7] Percentage cover estimated by eye is subjective. The grid and point methods are more objective, and the point method is the most objective because a pin either touches or doesn't.
[bio32-s2.6.b] Density is the number of individuals per unit area, usually per m². Once you know the density, you can scale up to estimate how many plants live in the whole habitat.
[bio32-s2.6.0] Count the individuals of a species that can be counted separately (for example daisy or dandelion rosettes) in each quadrat. Density = total number counted ÷ total area sampled.
[bio32-s2.6.1] Estimated population = density × total area of the habitat.
[bio32-s2.6.2] The area of the habitat is found by measuring its length and width with tape measures or a trundle wheel (a measuring wheel you roll along the ground).
[bio32-s2.6.3] Worked example (sample data): five quadrats of 0.25 m² contained 4, 7, 3, 6 and 5 daisies. Total = 25 daisies in 5 × 0.25 = 1.25 m².
[bio32-s2.6.4] Density = 25 ÷ 1.25 = 20 daisies per m² (or: mean per quadrat 5 ÷ 0.25 m² = 20 per m²).
[bio32-s2.6.5] The field is 50 m × 40 m = 2000 m², so the estimated population = 20 × 2000 = 40 000 daisies.
[bio32-s2.6.6] Counting individuals is the most informative measure but is slow, and impossible for grasses and other spreading plants, which need percentage cover instead.
[bio32-s2.7.b] A transect is a line across a habitat along which samples are taken at regular intervals. It is the tool for showing how species change when conditions change gradually.
[bio32-s2.7.0] Transects are used where there is a gradient in conditions, to show how the distribution of species changes along it: for example from under a hedge or tree into open grassland, across a trampled path, from a wet hollow to drier ground, or up a rocky shore.
[bio32-s2.7.1] Line transect, step 1: stretch a rope or tape measure tightly across the area at ground level.
[bio32-s2.7.2] Line transect, step 2: at regular intervals (for example every 1 m), record every species touching the line (or directly below it).
[bio32-s2.7.3] Line transect, step 3: record the distance for each species.
[bio32-s2.7.4] A line transect shows where each species occurs (distribution) but not how many, because it only records presence at each point.
[bio32-s2.7.5] A transect is a systematic, not random, sample: it is chosen to cross the gradient.
[bio32-s2.7.6] Several parallel transects give more reliable results, because one line might happen to cross an unusual patch.
[bio32-s2.8.b] A line transect only tells you where species are. A belt transect adds quadrats along the line, so it also tells you how abundant each species is.
[bio32-s2.8.0] Belt transect: quadrats are placed along the line, either side by side all the way (continuous belt transect) or at regular intervals (interrupted belt transect, for example a quadrat every 2 m).
[bio32-s2.8.1] In each quadrat of a belt transect, percentage cover, frequency or numbers are recorded, so a belt transect shows abundance as well as distribution.
[bio32-s2.8.2] Abiotic factors (non-living conditions such as light or soil moisture) are measured at each quadrat to relate them to the species. There is more on this in Abiotic factors.
[bio32-s2.8.3] Diagram: a belt transect from a hedge into open grassland: tape measure from the hedge, quadrats at 2 m intervals, with distances labelled.
[bio32-s2.9.b] Mobile animals move out of a quadrat before you can count them. Instead you catch, mark and release some, then use the proportion of marked animals in a second catch to work out the total population.
[bio32-s2.9.0] Mobile animals can't be counted in quadrats, so their population is estimated by capture–mark–recapture.
[bio32-s2.9.1] 1. Catch a sample with a suitable method (for example pitfall traps, sunken containers, for ground beetles or woodlice).
[bio32-s2.9.2] 2. Count them (M) and mark each harmlessly, for example with a small dot of non-toxic, quick-drying, waterproof paint on the underside of a woodlouse or the wing case of a beetle.
[bio32-s2.9.3] 3. Release them where they were caught and leave enough time (for example 24 hours) for them to mix back into the population.
[bio32-s2.9.4] 4. Catch a second sample in the same way and place; count the total caught (C) and the number that are marked (R).
[bio32-s2.9.5] Estimated population size: N = (M × C) ÷ R. This works because the fraction of the second sample that is marked (R ÷ C) is assumed to equal the fraction of the whole population that is marked (M ÷ N).
[bio32-s2.9.6] Worked example (sample data): 30 woodlice were caught, marked and released. Next day 40 were caught, 8 of them marked. N = (30 × 40) ÷ 8 = 1200 ÷ 8 = 150 woodlice.
[bio32-s2.9.7] Second example (sample data): M = 48, C = 50, R = 12; N = 2400 ÷ 12 = 200.
[bio32-s2.10.b] The capture–recapture formula only gives a good estimate if certain conditions hold, and it is easy to mix up the letters. When an exact population isn't needed, a simpler count can be enough.
[bio32-s2.10.0] N must be larger than both M and C, because the population includes every marked animal and every animal caught in the second sample. If it isn't, you have made an error.
[bio32-s2.10.1] R is the number of marked animals in the second sample, not the number marked in the first sample.
[bio32-s2.10.2] Assumption: there are no births, deaths, immigration (animals arriving) or emigration (animals leaving) between the two samples, so the population stays the same size.
[bio32-s2.10.3] Assumption: marked animals mix fully and randomly with the rest, and marked and unmarked animals are equally likely to be caught.
[bio32-s2.10.4] Assumption: the mark doesn't harm the animal, make it easier for predators to see, or wash or rub off.
[bio32-s2.10.5] If marks are lost, R is too small and N is overestimated, because fewer marked animals are recognised in the second sample. The assumptions are rarely fully met in practice.
[bio32-s2.10.6] For comparisons only, a relative count is often enough: for example the number of grasshoppers caught per ten sweeps of a sweep net, or the number of beetles per pitfall trap per day, compared between two areas using the same method.
[bio32-s2.11.b] In ecology a model is a simplified representation of a habitat that describes it and can be used to explain or predict. Here the model is a labelled map with data, built from the primary data (results you collected yourself).
[bio32-s2.11.0] Size: measure the length and width (or the sides of an irregular area) with long tape measures or a trundle wheel, and calculate the area, for example 50 m × 40 m = 2000 m².
[bio32-s2.11.1] Map: draw a sketch map to scale, with a north arrow, showing the boundary, hedges, trees, paths, gates, wet and dry areas, any slope (and which way it faces), the positions of the quadrats, transects and traps, and where the main species were found.
[bio32-s2.11.2] Species: the species list from the qualitative survey, with the quantitative results (percentage frequency, cover, density, population estimates) for the main species.
[bio32-s2.11.3] Abiotic factors: the mean values measured across the habitat (for example light intensity, soil pH, soil water content, air and soil temperature, aspect and slope), with differences between areas marked on the map.
[bio32-s2.11.4] Biotic factors (the effects of living things): the feeding and symbiotic relationships observed, shown as food chains or a food web drawn from the species found.
[bio32-s2.11.5] Biotic factors also include grazing, trampling and human management such as mowing or fertilising.
[bio32-s2.11.6] Using the model: it can explain why species are where they are (for example rushes only in the wet hollow) and predict the effect of a change (for example if mowing stopped, taller grasses would shade out daisies).
[bio32-s2.11.7] Limits of the model: it is a snapshot of one season and of the parts sampled, so it may not hold at other times of year or in unsampled areas.
[bio32-s2.12.b] Raw counts are hard to interpret. Tables and graphs show patterns in species abundance and distribution at a glance.
[bio32-s2.12.0] Record results in tables with headings and units: quadrat number or distance along the transect, species, and the measure used.
[bio32-s2.12.1] Bar charts compare species, for example the percentage frequency of each.
[bio32-s2.12.2] For a transect, plot each species' abundance against distance, or draw a kite diagram.
[bio32-s2.12.3] In a kite diagram, for each species a band is centred on a horizontal line; its width at each point along the transect is proportional to the species' abundance there, so the bands show at a glance where each species is common.
[bio32-s2.12.4] Diagram: a kite diagram for two species along a 10 m transect from a hedge, with distance on the x-axis.
[bio32-s2.13.b] A survey can be set up as a fair test, with an aim, a hypothesis (a testable prediction), and variables. Field studies differ from lab experiments because conditions can't be fully controlled.
[bio32-s2.13.0] Aim: to estimate the abundance and distribution of named species (for example daisy, white clover, grass and woodlice) in a grassland.
[bio32-s2.13.1] Hypothesis (example): 'Daisies are more abundant in the short-mown part of the field than in the long-grass part.'
[bio32-s2.13.2] Independent variable (the one you change or compare) when two areas are compared: the area (for example mown vs unmown).
[bio32-s2.13.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): the abundance of the species (density, percentage cover or frequency).
[bio32-s2.13.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the quadrat size and type, the number of quadrats, the method of placing them (random coordinates), the counting rule, the observer, the date and the weather.
[bio32-s2.13.5] There is no true control in a field study, because the two areas can't be made identical in every other way.
[bio32-s2.13.6] Conclusion: state the measured abundance of each species and compare the areas using the means.
[bio32-s2.13.7] Say whether the results support the hypothesis. A difference shows correlation with the area, not proof of the cause.
[bio32-s2.14.b] No survey is perfect. Knowing where errors come from lets you say how reliable your results are and how to improve them.
[bio32-s2.14.0] Too few samples, or samples not placed at random (choosing 'typical' spots), give an unrepresentative estimate; use random coordinates and enough quadrats.
[bio32-s2.14.1] Misidentification of species, especially grasses and small invertebrates, is a source of error.
[bio32-s2.14.2] Estimates of percentage cover by eye are subjective, and inconsistent edge rules change counts.
[bio32-s2.14.3] Results vary with the season and the time of day (plants flower and animals are active at different times), so a single survey is a snapshot.
[bio32-s2.14.4] The assumptions of capture–recapture are rarely fully met.
[bio32-s2.14.5] Trampling during the survey damages the plants being sampled; avoid trampling the quadrat sites before they are sampled.
[bio32-s2.14.6] Reliability improves by repeating the survey (more quadrats, more transects, several dates) and calculating means, because repeats reduce the effect of chance.

CANDIDATES bio31-s1: Biosphere, ecosystems, habitats and biodiversity
[bio31-s1.0.b] Ecology looks at how living things fit into the world around them. To talk about it clearly, ecologists sort life into levels, each one bigger than the last, and each has its own precise name.
[bio31-s1.0.0] Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment. The word comes from the Greek oikos, meaning house or home, because it is about the place where organisms live and how they get on there.
[bio31-s1.0.1] A population is all the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time, e.g. all the daisies in a field, or all the red squirrels in a woodland. Members can interbreed because they are one species.
[bio31-s1.0.2] A community is all the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time, e.g. all the plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms of a woodland. It is the living (biotic) part of an ecosystem only, with no soil, water or air.
[bio31-s1.0.3] The ecological levels, smallest to largest, are: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere. Each level includes the one before it, and from the ecosystem upward the non-living environment is included too.
[bio31-s1.0.4] These levels continue the levels of organisation inside a body (cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism): the organism is where the two sets of levels meet, and ecology then builds upward from it.
[bio31-s1.0.5] Diagram: nested boxes or a stepped sequence. Show one rabbit (organism), a group of rabbits (population), rabbits with grass, foxes and other species (community), the community with soil, water, air and light (ecosystem), and the Earth's living layer (biosphere). Label each level.
[bio31-s1.1.b] The biosphere is the largest ecological level: the whole of the Earth's life taken together. Its limits and the way matter and energy move through it explain why life depends on the Sun.
[bio31-s1.1.0] The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists: the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water (fresh water and oceans). It is the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.1.1] The biosphere is a thin layer compared with the size of the Earth. Life is found from the deep ocean floor to a few kilometres up in the air, in birds, spores and bacteria carried on the wind.
[bio31-s1.1.2] Most life is concentrated in a narrow zone near the surface, because that is where light, liquid water and suitable temperatures are all present.
[bio31-s1.1.3] The biosphere is a single, closed system for matter, because atoms are recycled and not added. It is an open system for energy, because energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer, The carbon cycle and The nitrogen cycle.
[bio31-s1.2.b] An ecosystem is a community together with the non-living world it lives in. The living and non-living parts affect each other, which is why you have to study both.
[bio31-s1.2.0] An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment in a particular area. It has living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts that affect each other.
[bio31-s1.2.1] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment and the effects organisms have on each other: food supply, predation (one animal hunting and eating another), competition, disease, parasites, pollinators and decomposers.
[bio31-s1.2.2] Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions of the environment: light, temperature, water, oxygen, soil type and pH, minerals, wind, slope and aspect (the direction a slope faces). There is more on this in Abiotic factors within Investigating an Ecosystem, and in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio31-s1.2.3] Ecosystems have no fixed size: a rock pool, a hedgerow, a lake, a raised bog or an ocean can each be treated as an ecosystem. Their boundaries are often gradual, e.g. a woodland edge merging into grassland.
[bio31-s1.2.4] Every ecosystem needs a continual input of energy, almost always sunlight captured by producers (such as plants) in photosynthesis. Its matter is recycled by decomposers (such as fungi and bacteria), so the ecosystem keeps running.
[bio31-s1.2.5] Producers, consumers and decomposers each have a different role in an ecosystem. Consumers are the animals that get their energy by eating other organisms. The roles of all three are taught in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio31-s1.2.6] Irish land ecosystems include grassland, the most widespread land cover in Ireland, mostly farmed pasture and meadow. Woodland includes the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park. Peatlands have raised bogs in the midlands and blanket bogs in the uplands and the west.
[bio31-s1.2.7] Irish water and coastal ecosystems include freshwater, such as lakes like Lough Derg, a lake on the River Shannon, and rivers. Coastal and marine ecosystems include rocky shores, sandy shores and sand dunes, salt marshes and the open sea.
[bio31-s1.3.b] An ecosystem is too big to describe where one particular animal lives. Ecologists therefore talk about habitats, and smaller microhabitats, to say exactly where an organism is found.
[bio31-s1.3.0] A habitat is the place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs. A woodlouse's habitat is damp leaf litter, a limpet's is the rocky shore, and a frog's is a pond and the damp vegetation around it.
[bio31-s1.3.1] A microhabitat is a small area within a habitat with its own conditions, e.g. the underside of a stone on a rocky shore, a rock pool, a rotting log, or the bark of a tree. Shade, moisture and temperature there can differ greatly from the surroundings.
[bio31-s1.3.2] One ecosystem contains many habitats: a woodland contains the canopy, the trunk, the leaf litter and the soil, each with different species. A habitat is the organism's 'address'; its role there is its niche, which is dealt with in The niche.
[bio31-s1.4.b] Biodiversity is more than a count of species. It describes how much variety there is in life, and it can be measured at three different levels, from genes up to whole habitats.
[bio31-s1.4.0] Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels. It is usually described at three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem (habitat) diversity.
[bio31-s1.4.1] Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (different versions of a gene) within a species or population, e.g. the different breeds of cattle, or the variation in disease resistance among wild ash trees. It allows a population to adapt by natural selection when conditions change.
[bio31-s1.4.2] Low genetic diversity left the Irish potato crop of the 1840s vulnerable, because it was largely one variety, so when blight (a disease) arrived it destroyed it.
[bio31-s1.4.3] Species diversity is the variety of species in an area. It has two parts: species richness, the number of different species present, and species evenness, how evenly the individuals are spread among those species.
[bio31-s1.4.4] Two areas with the same species can differ in diversity if one is dominated by a single species. The Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness into one number, is Higher Level only. There is more on this in Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio31-s1.4.5] Ecosystem (habitat) diversity is the variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region, e.g. Ireland's bogs, woodlands, grasslands, lakes, rivers and coasts. A region with many habitats supports more species, because each habitat suits different organisms.
[bio31-s1.5.b] Life is not spread evenly across the Earth. Some places hold far more species than others, and Ireland is a good local example of a place with fewer.
[bio31-s1.5.0] Biodiversity is generally higher in warm, wet, stable environments and lower in cold or extreme ones. Tropical rainforests and coral reefs are the most species-rich ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.5.1] Ireland has fewer species than Britain or mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age, before many species had recolonised it from the south.
[bio31-s1.5.2] In brief, biodiversity provides food, medicines and materials, supports processes such as pollination and nutrient cycling, and makes ecosystems more stable. There is more on this, and on its loss and conservation, in Biodiversity loss and conservation.

CANDIDATES bio32-s1: Collection and identification
[bio32-s1.0.b] An ecological investigation studies living things in their natural surroundings, and here the surroundings are a grassland habitat. The first job is to find out which organisms live there, because every later measurement is built on that list.
[bio32-s1.0.0] The aim is to find out which organisms live in a chosen grassland habitat (the place where a community of organisms lives), to collect and identify them, and so to build a species list. The surveys, abiotic measurements and feeding-relationship work are all based on that list.
[bio32-s1.0.1] Data gathered first-hand in the field, by your own counting, measuring and observing, are called primary data. Secondary data would come from books or other people’s work.
[bio32-s1.0.2] 1. A preliminary visit to choose and map the study area (the mapping is done in the Surveys note).
[bio32-s1.0.3] 2. Observe, collect and identify the organisms, which is the stage covered here.
[bio32-s1.0.4] 3. A qualitative survey (which species are present, giving a species list) and a quantitative survey (how many of each) are carried out in the Surveys note.
[bio32-s1.0.5] 4. Measure abiotic factors (non-living factors such as light and temperature) and relate them to the distribution and abundance of a species, as in the Abiotic factors note.
[bio32-s1.0.6] 5. Work out feeding and symbiotic relationships (close partnerships between different species), as in the Biotic factors and relationships note.
[bio32-s1.0.7] 6. Present the results and draw conclusions.
[bio32-s1.1.b] Before collecting anything you visit the site once to plan, because what you can find depends on the place, the time of year and the gear you bring.
[bio32-s1.1.0] On the preliminary visit, walk over the site and note its boundaries and main features: hedges, trees, paths, wet patches, slope and gate. These features create different microhabitats (small areas with their own conditions) that you will need to sample.
[bio32-s1.1.1] Get permission from the landowner, check access and hazards, and choose suitable dates, so that the work is legal and safe before anyone arrives with equipment.
[bio32-s1.1.2] Organisms change with the season, so collect over several visits if possible, for example late spring to early autumn, when most plants flower and most invertebrates (animals without backbones) are active.
[bio32-s1.1.3] Collecting equipment: pitfall traps (jars, trowel, flat stones or lids), pooter, sweep net, beating tray and stick, Tullgren funnel, collecting jars and labelled specimen tubes, and white trays on which small animals show up clearly.
[bio32-s1.1.4] Identification and recording equipment: hand lens, identification keys and field guides, camera or phone, notebook and pencil, and labels.
[bio32-s1.1.5] Protection and measuring equipment: gloves, tape measures, and quadrats (square frames used in the Surveys note).
[bio32-s1.2.b] When you compare two areas or two times, the comparison is only fair if the collecting effort is the same each time. That means deciding what you change, what you measure and what you keep constant.
[bio32-s1.2.0] The independent variable (the one you choose to change) is the area or time sampled.
[bio32-s1.2.1] The dependent variable (the one you measure) is the kinds and numbers of organisms caught.
[bio32-s1.2.2] The controlled variables (kept the same) are the type and number of traps, the trapping time, the number of sweeps, the time of day and the weather, so that the collections are a fair comparison.
[bio32-s1.2.3] No true control is possible in a field study, because the conditions in the field can’t be held constant. This is why you control as many variables as you can and repeat the collection.
[bio32-s1.3.b] Not every organism can or should be caught. Large, fast or delicate organisms are studied by looking, and animals that hide are detected from the traces they leave.
[bio32-s1.3.0] Direct observation (looking at the organism itself) is used for organisms that are large, fast or that should not be caught, such as birds, rabbits, foxes and butterflies, and for all plants, which are identified where they grow and not dug up.
[bio32-s1.3.1] Use binoculars for birds and mammals at a distance, and a hand lens (about ×10) for small features such as the hairs or veins of a leaf.
[bio32-s1.3.2] Indirect signs show animals that are rarely seen: droppings, tracks in mud, burrows, nests, feathers, molehills and wormcasts.
[bio32-s1.3.3] Feeding signs are indirect signs too: nibbled leaves, holes in leaves made by slugs or caterpillars, and broken snail shells beside a stone used by a song thrush.
[bio32-s1.3.4] Record each observation at once, so nothing is forgotten: the organism (or ‘unknown species A’ with a sketch or photograph until it is identified) and where it was found (its microhabitat, for example under a stone or on a dandelion leaf).
[bio32-s1.3.5] Also record the time, the weather and what the organism was doing, such as feeding on what.
[bio32-s1.3.6] Photographs record organisms without removing them and can be checked later against keys, so record the location and date with each image.
[bio32-s1.4.b] Many grassland invertebrates (animals without backbones) walk over the soil surface and are hard to spot. A pitfall trap catches them as they travel, with no effort from the collector.
[bio32-s1.4.0] A pitfall trap is a jar or plastic cup sunk into a hole in the ground so that its rim is exactly level with the soil surface. Animals walking across the ground fall in and can’t climb the smooth sides.
[bio32-s1.4.1] A flat stone or lid raised on small stones covers the trap, which keeps out rain and stops birds and other predators taking the catch. Bait, such as a small piece of meat or fruit, can be added.
[bio32-s1.4.2] Pitfall traps catch ground-dwelling (crawling) invertebrates: ground beetles, spiders, woodlice, slugs, ants and millipedes.
[bio32-s1.4.3] Check traps at least every 24 hours, ideally early morning, because predators such as ground beetles and spiders will eat the other animals in the trap.
[bio32-s1.4.4] Remove all traps at the end of the study so that no animal is trapped later.
[bio32-s1.4.5] Diagram: a pitfall trap in section: jar in the soil with rim level with the surface, raised flat stone cover on small supports, bait, and animals inside. Label soil surface, jar, cover, bait.
[bio32-s1.5.b] Tiny animals living in soil and leaf litter (the layer of dead leaves on the ground) are too small to pick out by hand. A Tullgren funnel gets them to leave the sample by themselves.
[bio32-s1.5.0] A Tullgren funnel extracts small animals from a sample of soil or leaf litter.
[bio32-s1.5.1] The sample sits on a wire mesh (gauze) in a funnel under a lamp, a light bulb about 10–20 cm above it.
[bio32-s1.5.2] The heat and light dry the sample from the top, so the animals move down away from the heat, light and dryness, through the mesh and into a collecting beaker below. The lamp drives the animals away; it does not attract them.
[bio32-s1.5.3] The beaker holds damp tissue paper to keep the animals alive, or ethanol if they are to be preserved for identification. Leave the funnel for several hours to a day.
[bio32-s1.5.4] A Tullgren funnel catches mites, springtails, small beetles and their larvae, and small worms.
[bio32-s1.5.5] The mesh must be fine enough to hold back the soil, and too hot a lamp kills the animals before they leave.
[bio32-s1.5.6] Diagram: a Tullgren funnel: lamp, soil or leaf-litter sample, wire gauze, funnel, collecting beaker with damp tissue (or ethanol), and an arrow showing animals moving down away from the heat and light.
[bio32-s1.6.b] Earthworms and mammals need their own methods, because earthworms burrow too deeply for pitfall traps and mammals need careful, humane handling.
[bio32-s1.6.0] Earthworms are collected by digging a measured block of soil and sorting it by hand on a white tray, or by watering a marked area with a mustard-powder solution, which irritates them so that they come to the surface.
[bio32-s1.6.1] Rinse earthworms in clean water at once.
[bio32-s1.6.2] A small-mammal trap is a baited box with dry bedding and food inside. The door closes behind the animal, which is unharmed.
[bio32-s1.6.3] Small-mammal traps are used only under teacher supervision. Check them at least every few hours and release the animal where it was caught.
[bio32-s1.6.4] Some mammal species are protected by law, so trapping may need a licence. Mammals in grassland are more often recorded by observation and signs, because trapping them is restricted.
[bio32-s1.7.b] Many invertebrates live on stems and leaves rather than on the ground. Two methods knock them off the plants so they can be counted.
[bio32-s1.7.0] A sweep net is a strong net with a fine cloth bag on a stout frame and handle. Sweep it quickly through long grass and low plants in a series of side-to-side arcs while walking slowly, so that animals on the plants are knocked into the bag.
[bio32-s1.7.1] After sweeping, fold the bag over to close it, then empty it onto a white tray or into a jar.
[bio32-s1.7.2] A sweep net catches grasshoppers, aphids, flies, beetles, spiders, caterpillars and leafhoppers.
[bio32-s1.7.3] Use a fixed number of sweeps (for example ten) in each area so that catches can be compared.
[bio32-s1.7.4] A sweep net doesn’t work in wet grass, which soaks the bag and damages the animals.
[bio32-s1.7.5] A beating tray is a white sheet or tray (or an upturned umbrella) held under a branch of a hedge, shrub or tree at the grassland edge. The branch is struck sharply with a stick a set number of times.
[bio32-s1.7.6] Animals living on the branch fall onto the tray, where they are easy to see against the white. A beating tray catches caterpillars, beetles, spiders, ladybirds and shield bugs, which are then collected with a pooter.
[bio32-s1.8.b] Small, fast animals are hard to pick up without crushing them. A pooter lets you suck them gently into a container.
[bio32-s1.8.0] A pooter is a clear container (jar or tube) closed by a stopper through which two tubes pass.
[bio32-s1.8.1] The user places the end of the long inlet tube next to a small animal and sucks on the mouthpiece tube. The animal is drawn up the inlet tube into the container.
[bio32-s1.8.2] A piece of fine gauze (muslin) over the inner end of the mouthpiece tube stops the animal or debris being sucked into the user’s mouth.
[bio32-s1.8.3] A pooter is used for small, fast-moving animals (small insects, spiders, springtails) from a beating tray, sweep net, the ground or bark.
[bio32-s1.8.4] Empty it often, because animals inside can harm each other.
[bio32-s1.8.5] Diagram: a pooter: container, stopper, inlet tube, mouthpiece tube, gauze over the inner end of the mouthpiece tube, arrows for the air flow and the animal entering.
[bio32-s1.9.b] Flying insects escape from ground traps and sweep nets, so they need methods of their own. One works by day with a net and one by night with a lamp.
[bio32-s1.9.0] A butterfly net, a light, fine-meshed net on a long handle, catches flying insects such as butterflies and bees. Hold the net closed with a twist of the bag and release the insect after identification.
[bio32-s1.9.1] Bees and wasps can sting, so many are identified by observation instead of being caught.
[bio32-s1.9.2] A light trap (moth trap) is a bright lamp, often ultraviolet, above a box with egg trays inside, left on overnight.
[bio32-s1.9.3] Night-flying insects, mainly moths, are attracted to the light, fall into the box and settle in the trays. They are identified and released next morning, away from birds, which would otherwise eat them.
[bio32-s1.10.b] Once animals are caught, they must be kept alive, named and recorded quickly, because the record is the data the whole investigation depends on.
[bio32-s1.10.0] Place animals in labelled jars or specimen tubes with a little vegetation, keep them cool and shaded, and identify them as soon as possible on a white tray with a hand lens.
[bio32-s1.10.1] Record for each catch the name (or ‘species A’), the number caught, the method and place of capture, and the date and time. These are the raw primary data used in the Surveys note.
[bio32-s1.10.2] Return every animal to the place where it was caught, after identification.
[bio32-s1.10.3] Collect plants only when they are common and can’t be identified where they grow, taking a single leaf or flower rather than the whole plant.
[bio32-s1.11.b] Once you have an organism, you need its name. An identification key gets you there by asking a short series of questions about what you can see.
[bio32-s1.11.0] An identification key is a tool for naming an unknown organism by working through a series of questions or statements about its visible features.
[bio32-s1.11.1] A dichotomous key (‘dichotomous’ means dividing in two) gives a pair of contrasting statements at each step, called a couplet.
[bio32-s1.11.2] The user chooses the statement that fits the organism, and follows the number or name it leads to, until a name is reached.
[bio32-s1.11.3] Good keys use features that are easy to see and don’t vary between individuals or with age or season: number of legs, number of body parts, wings present or absent, shell present or absent, leaf shape and arrangement, flower colour and number of petals.
[bio32-s1.11.4] Good keys avoid size, which varies with age, and vague words such as ‘large’, because two users might judge them differently.
[bio32-s1.12.b] Here is a key to five grassland invertebrates, written as numbered couplets. Work through it with an animal in mind, then see how such a key is built.
[bio32-s1.12.0] 1. Legs present: go to 2. No legs: go to 4.
[bio32-s1.12.1] 2. Three pairs of legs (6 legs) and body in three parts (head, thorax, abdomen): insect (for example a ground beetle). More than 6 legs: go to 3.
[bio32-s1.12.2] 3. Four pairs of legs (8 legs) and body in two parts: spider. Seven pairs of legs (14 legs) and body flattened and covered with overlapping plates: woodlouse.
[bio32-s1.12.3] 4. Body made of many ring-like segments: earthworm. Body not segmented, soft and slimy: go to 5.
[bio32-s1.12.4] 5. Shell present: snail. No shell: slug. Using the key: an animal with 14 legs goes 1, then 2, then 3, then woodlouse.
[bio32-s1.12.5] To make a key, list the organisms, then choose a feature that splits them into two groups.
[bio32-s1.12.6] Keep splitting each group with a new feature until each organism stands alone, then check the key by keying out each organism in turn to see that it reaches the right name.
[bio32-s1.12.7] A tree diagram (branching chart) of the same splits is another way to show a key.
[bio32-s1.13.b] Keys are not the only help. Several other aids check or speed up an identification, and you still need a plan for organisms you can’t name.
[bio32-s1.13.0] Illustrated field guides and identification charts (for example of wild flowers, grasses, insects and birds) show pictures to compare with your specimen. You can also compare it with labelled specimens or photographs, or ask an expert such as a teacher or naturalist.
[bio32-s1.13.1] Image-recognition apps on a phone are an aid, but their suggestions must be checked against a key or guide, because they are often wrong for small invertebrates.
[bio32-s1.13.2] A hand lens or a stereo (dissecting) microscope shows small features that decide between similar species.
[bio32-s1.13.3] Identify each organism to species if possible. Where that isn’t possible, name the group (for example ‘a ground beetle’) and give it a code (species A, B …) used consistently throughout the study, so that it can still be counted in the surveys.
[bio32-s1.14.b] Knowing what to expect makes identification quicker. These are the plants and animals typically found in an Irish grassland.
[bio32-s1.14.0] Typical plants: grasses (for example perennial ryegrass in pasture), daisy, dandelion, creeping buttercup, white clover, ribwort plantain and, in damp patches, rushes.
[bio32-s1.14.1] Daisy and dandelion grow as flat rosettes of leaves close to the ground, which helps them survive mowing and grazing.
[bio32-s1.14.2] Typical invertebrates: earthworms, slugs, snails, ground beetles, spiders, woodlice (in damp places under stones or logs), ants, aphids, grasshoppers, ladybirds, caterpillars, butterflies and bumblebees.
[bio32-s1.14.3] Typical vertebrates (animals with backbones): rabbits, the Irish hare, foxes, badgers, hedgehogs, wood mice, pygmy shrews, and birds such as the song thrush, blackbird and kestrel.
[bio32-s1.15.b] The methods above suit grassland. In other habitats the organisms and conditions differ, so you choose different methods to match.
[bio32-s1.15.0] Woodland: beating trays under branches and pitfall traps in the leaf litter are the main methods. Leaf-litter samples go in a Tullgren funnel, trees are identified by leaf, bark and fruit, and the layers (canopy, shrub, herb and ground layers) are recorded separately.
[bio32-s1.15.1] Rocky shore: animals are mostly collected by hand from under seaweed, in rock pools and under stones (turned back the right way up), or simply counted where they are, as many are attached (barnacles, limpets, mussels). Seaweeds are identified with a key.
[bio32-s1.15.2] Rocky shore work is done at low tide, ideally a spring low tide. Check tide tables first, and start at the lowest point as the tide goes out.
[bio32-s1.15.3] Freshwater (stream or pond): a pond net is swept through the water and vegetation.
[bio32-s1.15.4] In a stream, kick sampling is used: the net is held on the bed just downstream while the stones upstream are disturbed with the feet for a fixed time, so that animals are washed into the net.
[bio32-s1.15.5] The catch from kick sampling is emptied into a white tray of water and identified, for example mayfly, stonefly and caddis fly larvae, freshwater shrimps, snails and leeches.
[bio32-s1.15.6] A plankton net (very fine mesh) is towed slowly through the water to collect microscopic organisms.
[bio32-s1.15.7] Freshwater animals are kept in water from the same habitat and returned quickly.
[bio32-s1.16.b] Fieldwork takes you away from the classroom, so you must plan for risks on land and near water.
[bio32-s1.16.0] Work in groups, never alone, and tell someone where you are going. Wear suitable clothes and footwear for the weather.
[bio32-s1.16.1] Take care near livestock, electric fences, nettles and brambles. Wear gloves when handling soil, leaf litter and traps, cover cuts, and wash hands before eating.
[bio32-s1.16.2] Check for ticks after working in long grass, because ticks can carry the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.
[bio32-s1.16.3] Be aware of anyone with an allergy to bee or wasp stings.
[bio32-s1.16.4] Near water, never work alone, and check the depth and the current before entering a stream.
[bio32-s1.16.5] At the shore, know the tide times, watch for incoming tides and take care on wet, slippery rocks covered with seaweed.
[bio32-s1.16.6] Cover cuts and wash hands after contact with fresh water, which can carry the bacteria that cause leptospirosis (Weil’s disease), spread in the urine of rats.
[bio32-s1.17.b] Collecting can damage the habitat you are studying. A code of conduct protects the habitat and its organisms.
[bio32-s1.17.0] Get permission from the landowner, close gates, and keep disturbance and trampling to a minimum.
[bio32-s1.17.1] Replace stones and logs the way they were found, because animals beneath them depend on the damp, dark conditions.
[bio32-s1.17.2] Handle animals gently and return them to where they were caught.
[bio32-s1.17.3] Don’t pick or uproot plants unnecessarily, and never collect protected or rare species.
[bio32-s1.17.4] Remove all traps and litter at the end of the study.
[bio32-s1.18.b] A species list from one afternoon is never complete. Each method, and each visit, has built-in limits that you should recognise and reduce.
[bio32-s1.18.0] Each collection method catches only some kinds of organism: pitfall traps catch only animals that walk on the ground, and sweep nets only animals on tall plants. Several methods must be used to build a complete species list.
[bio32-s1.18.1] Catches depend on the weather, the time of day and the season. Many animals are nocturnal or are active only in warm, dry or damp conditions, so one visit misses many species.
[bio32-s1.18.2] Repeat collections on several days and at different times, under similar conditions when comparing areas.
[bio32-s1.18.3] Animals in traps may escape or be eaten by others before they are counted, so check traps often. Birds may take the catch from uncovered traps.
[bio32-s1.18.4] Misidentification, especially of small invertebrates and of grasses that are not in flower, is a source of error. Check identifications with a second key or guide, or with the teacher.
[bio32-s1.18.5] Disturbance by the collectors (trampling, noise) changes animals’ behaviour and damages the habitat.
