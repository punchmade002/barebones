TARGET bio31-s3: Investigation: human activity and species diversity
[bio31-s3.0.b] This outcome asks you to judge data about how people change the number and mix of species in a habitat. First you need to know which human activities matter, what primary and secondary data are, and how diversity is measured.
[bio31-s3.0.0] Human activities that affect species diversity include trampling, grazing and mowing, ploughing and reseeding, fertiliser and pesticide use, pollution of water and air, drainage, building, forestry, and the introduction of invasive species (non-native species that spread and out-compete native ones).
[bio31-s3.0.1] Most of these reduce diversity, either by removing habitat or by favouring a few tolerant species that out-survive the rest.
[bio31-s3.0.2] Some activities can keep diversity high. Light grazing, or cutting a meadow once a year late in summer, stops a few vigorous species taking over, so weaker species still get light and space.
[bio31-s3.0.3] Primary data are collected by the student in the field. Secondary data come from someone else, such as EPA water-quality reports or a published bird survey. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio31-s3.0.4] To evaluate data means to judge what the data show, how reliable and valid they are, and what conclusions they can and cannot support.
[bio31-s3.0.5] Species richness is the number of species in a sample. Abundance is the number of individuals of each species, or the percentage cover for plants (the percentage of the ground each species covers).
[bio31-s3.0.6] At Higher Level you also use the Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness (how equally the individuals are shared between the species). There is more on this in Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio31-s3.1.b] Walking on grass damages it, and a worn path is an easy place to test whether human activity lowers plant diversity. A good plan states a clear aim and hypothesis, and identifies every variable.
[bio31-s3.1.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of trampling on the species diversity of plants in a grassland.
[bio31-s3.1.1] Hypothesis: grassland on a heavily trampled path has fewer plant species than nearby untrampled grassland. The null hypothesis (the statement to be tested against) is that there is no difference.
[bio31-s3.1.2] The independent variable (the one you change or compare) is the amount of trampling, either on the path compared with away from it, or the distance from the centre of the path.
[bio31-s3.1.3] The dependent variables (the ones you measure) are the number of plant species per quadrat, and the percentage cover or number of each species.
[bio31-s3.1.4] Controlled variables are kept the same so they cannot affect the result: the quadrat size (e.g. 0.5 m × 0.5 m), the number of quadrats in each area, the same field (so the same soil type, aspect, slope and management), the same day and observer, and the same identification key.
[bio31-s3.1.5] Control: the untrampled grassland beside the path serves as the comparison area, showing the diversity without trampling. The two areas must be alike except for trampling, otherwise any difference could have another cause.
[bio31-s3.1.6] Equipment: a quadrat (a square frame, e.g. 0.5 m × 0.5 m, often divided into 100 small squares to estimate percentage cover), two measuring tapes, a random-number table or app, an identification key or chart for grassland plants, a recording sheet and a pencil.
[bio31-s3.1.7] Optional equipment: a soil-compaction measure, e.g. how far a metal rod or pencil pushes into the soil under the same force, and a soil thermometer.
[bio31-s3.2.b] The method has to give an unbiased, repeatable sample from both areas. Follow the steps in order.
[bio31-s3.2.0] 1. Choose a grassland with a well-used path and similar untrampled grass beside it, so the only major difference is trampling.
[bio31-s3.2.1] 2. In each area, lay two tapes at right angles as axes, and use random numbers as coordinates to place each quadrat, so that the sample is not biased by choosing interesting spots. Random sampling is detailed in Surveys.
[bio31-s3.2.2] 3. Place at least 10 quadrats in each area. A larger sample makes the means more reliable.
[bio31-s3.2.3] 4. In each quadrat, identify every plant species with the key and record the number of species. Estimate the percentage cover of each species, or count individuals, e.g. daisy and plantain rosettes.
[bio31-s3.2.4] 5. Optionally, measure soil compaction at each quadrat.
[bio31-s3.2.5] 6. Calculate the mean number of species per quadrat for each area, and the mean percentage cover of each species.
[bio31-s3.2.6] 7. Repeat on another day, or combine class results, to check repeatability.
[bio31-s3.2.7] Alternative design: a line or belt transect (a line or strip sampled at set intervals) running at right angles across the path, sampling a quadrat every metre, shows how diversity changes with distance from the path centre.
[bio31-s3.3.b] Fieldwork has risks for you and for the habitat, so a plan must deal with both.
[bio31-s3.3.0] Safety: wash hands after fieldwork; wear suitable clothing and footwear; watch for traffic, livestock, electric fences and stinging nettles; get the landowner's permission; and work in pairs.
[bio31-s3.3.1] Ethics: do not uproot plants (identify them in place), and leave the site as you found it, so your study does not itself damage the diversity you are measuring.
[bio31-s3.4.b] Once the quadrats are counted, the results are summarised as means and ranges. The sample results below show a clear drop in diversity on the path.
[bio31-s3.4.0] Sample data (invented for practice, not real results): number of plant species in each of 10 quadrats. Untrampled grassland: 7, 8, 6, 9, 7, 6, 8, 7, 8, 6 (total 72, mean 7.2, range 6–9).
[bio31-s3.4.1] Sample data for the path (invented for practice): number of plant species in each of 10 quadrats. Path: 3, 2, 2, 4, 3, 1, 3, 2, 3, 2 (total 25, mean 2.5, range 1–4).
[bio31-s3.4.2] Present the results as a bar chart of the mean species per quadrat for the two areas. The independent variable is categoric (it falls into groups, path or untrampled), so a bar chart is used. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio31-s3.4.3] Diagram: bar chart of mean species per quadrat, path vs untrampled, with labelled axes.
[bio31-s3.5.b] The data show fewer species on the path. Explaining why, and what the data can prove, is what earns the marks.
[bio31-s3.5.0] Expected pattern: fewer species on the path. The path is dominated by a few trampling-tolerant plants, and bare soil is more common on it.
[bio31-s3.5.1] Greater plantain and daisy form low, flat rosettes of leaves pressed to the ground, so they are not crushed or broken. Perennial ryegrass has tough, flexible leaves and grows from the base. Taller, more delicate plants are found only away from the path.
[bio31-s3.5.2] Explanation: trampling crushes and breaks shoots and kills seedlings. It also compacts the soil, squeezing out air spaces, so roots get less oxygen and water drains poorly or runs off.
[bio31-s3.5.3] Only species adapted to these conditions survive, so richness and evenness fall.
[bio31-s3.5.4] Conclusion: the results support the hypothesis. The mean number of species was much lower on the path (2.5) than in untrampled grassland (7.2), and the ranges do not overlap, so the difference is unlikely to be due to chance.
[bio31-s3.5.5] The link between trampling and lower diversity is likely to be causal, because the areas were otherwise alike.
[bio31-s3.6.b] Sewage and farm runoff add organic matter to rivers, and the animals living on the river bed show the effect. Here you compare a site above the pollution with one below it.
[bio31-s3.6.0] Aim: to compare the diversity of freshwater invertebrates (animals without backbones) upstream and downstream of a source of organic pollution, e.g. a sewage-treatment outfall or a farm drain.
[bio31-s3.6.1] The independent variable is the site (upstream or downstream). The dependent variables are the number of invertebrate groups, and the presence and number of indicator species.
[bio31-s3.6.2] Controlled variables: the same stream, similar depth, flow rate and stony bed at both sites, the same sampling time (e.g. 1 minute of kick-sampling), the same net, and the same day. This means pollution is the main thing that differs.
[bio31-s3.7.b] Kick-sampling collects the animals living among the stones of a river bed. The order of the steps matters, and so does staying safe near water.
[bio31-s3.7.0] 1. Sample upstream first, so that you do not carry disturbed material downstream.
[bio31-s3.7.1] 2. At each site, hold a net on the river bed facing upstream and kick the stones for a fixed time, so that dislodged animals flow into the net. Kick-sampling is detailed in Collection and identification.
[bio31-s3.7.2] 3. Empty the net into a white tray of river water, so the animals show up clearly and stay alive.
[bio31-s3.7.3] 4. Identify and count the animals with a key.
[bio31-s3.7.4] 5. Measure dissolved oxygen and water temperature at each site.
[bio31-s3.7.5] 6. Repeat three times at each site.
[bio31-s3.7.6] 7. Return the animals to the river.
[bio31-s3.7.7] Safety: never work alone; wear waders or wellingtons and stay in shallow water; avoid fast or deep water; cover cuts and wash hands, because of the risk of infections such as Weil's disease, which is spread in rat urine; and do no fieldwork in flood.
[bio31-s3.8.b] Some animals can only live in clean water and others cope with pollution, so the animals present tell you the condition of the water. This lets you judge pollution from a living sample.
[bio31-s3.8.0] Indicator species are organisms whose presence, absence or abundance shows the condition of the environment.
[bio31-s3.8.1] Clean, well-oxygenated water: stonefly nymphs and many mayfly nymphs, which are pollution-sensitive.
[bio31-s3.8.2] Moderately polluted water: freshwater shrimps, caddis fly larvae and water lice (water hoglouse).
[bio31-s3.8.3] Badly polluted, low-oxygen water: bloodworms (red midge larvae), sludge worms (Tubifex) and rat-tailed maggots, which are pollution-tolerant.
[bio31-s3.8.4] Expected results: upstream, many groups are present, including stonefly and mayfly nymphs. Downstream there are fewer groups, dominated by large numbers of tolerant species such as sludge worms and bloodworms.
[bio31-s3.8.5] Explanation: decomposing organic matter is broken down by bacteria, whose aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen) uses up dissolved oxygen. Sensitive species die or leave.
[bio31-s3.8.6] Tolerant species, with less competition and plenty of food, become very numerous. Diversity falls even though the total number of animals may rise.
[bio31-s3.9.b] Your own sampling is primary data. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) publishes secondary data on the same rivers, which you can use to check your results.
[bio31-s3.9.0] The EPA rates Irish rivers with a biotic index (a score based on the living things present) called the Q-value, from Q5 (high quality, unpolluted) to Q1 (bad quality, seriously polluted).
[bio31-s3.9.1] The Q-value is based mainly on the proportions of pollution-sensitive and pollution-tolerant invertebrates, so it uses the same indicator species you counted.
[bio31-s3.9.2] Students can compare their primary data with the EPA Q-values published for the same river.
[bio31-s3.10.b] To evaluate a field study, ask two questions. Can you trust the numbers (reliability), and do they really measure the effect you claim (validity)?
[bio31-s3.10.0] Reliability: was the sample large enough (number of quadrats or kick samples)? Were the results repeatable on repeated sampling? Do class results agree (reproducibility)? A wide range or an anomalous quadrat (one that does not fit the pattern) reduces confidence. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio31-s3.10.1] Validity: was only the independent variable different between the sites? Confounding variables (other differences that could explain the result) could explain the difference instead.
[bio31-s3.10.2] The path might also be drier, more shaded or on a slope, and the downstream site might also be deeper or slower, or have a different bed. Measuring abiotic factors (non-living conditions) at each site helps to rule these out.
[bio31-s3.10.3] Sampling: random placement avoids bias; the quadrat size must suit the plants; and sampling at one time of year misses seasonal species.
[bio31-s3.10.4] Identification errors: similar species, such as grasses or young invertebrates, may be misidentified, so richness may be under- or over-counted. Percentage cover estimates are subjective, because different observers judge them differently.
[bio31-s3.11.b] A field comparison can show that two things go together, but showing that one causes the other needs more. Secondary data also need checking before you trust them.
[bio31-s3.11.0] Correlation and causation: a field comparison shows an association. It supports a cause only if the sites are otherwise alike and a mechanism explains it, e.g. soil compaction or oxygen depletion. There is more on this in Producing and analysing data.
[bio31-s3.11.1] Secondary data, e.g. bird surveys, EPA reports or news articles: check who collected them and why, the sample size and methods, whether the same method was used every year so that trends are comparable, whether raw data are available, and whether the source might be biased.
[bio31-s3.11.2] Improvements: more quadrats or samples, and several paths or streams, so the result is not just one place.
[bio31-s3.11.3] Improvements: measure abiotic factors at each sample point, and sample in different seasons.
[bio31-s3.11.4] Improvements: use the Simpson's Diversity Index to include evenness (Higher Level).
[bio31-s3.12.b] Interpreting counts means looking at both the number of groups and the number of animals. The sample data below show why more animals does not mean more diversity.
[bio31-s3.12.0] Worked example (sample data, invented for practice, not real results): kick samples from an upstream and a downstream site.
[bio31-s3.12.1] Upstream: stonefly nymphs 6, mayfly nymphs 14, caddis fly larvae 5, freshwater shrimps 9, sludge worms 0. This gives 4 groups present and 34 animals.
[bio31-s3.12.2] Downstream: stonefly nymphs 0, mayfly nymphs 0, caddis fly larvae 1, freshwater shrimps 2, sludge worms 41. This gives 3 groups present and 44 animals.
[bio31-s3.12.3] Interpretation: downstream has more animals but fewer groups, and it is dominated by one tolerant group, so diversity is lower. The loss of stonefly and mayfly nymphs indicates low oxygen.
[bio31-s3.12.4] Calculate percentages to compare the samples: sludge worms are 41/44 = 93% of the downstream sample.

CANDIDATES bio31-s1: Biosphere, ecosystems, habitats and biodiversity
[bio31-s1.0.b] Ecology looks at how living things fit into the world around them. To talk about it clearly, ecologists sort life into levels, each one bigger than the last, and each has its own precise name.
[bio31-s1.0.0] Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment. The word comes from the Greek oikos, meaning house or home, because it is about the place where organisms live and how they get on there.
[bio31-s1.0.1] A population is all the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time, e.g. all the daisies in a field, or all the red squirrels in a woodland. Members can interbreed because they are one species.
[bio31-s1.0.2] A community is all the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time, e.g. all the plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms of a woodland. It is the living (biotic) part of an ecosystem only, with no soil, water or air.
[bio31-s1.0.3] The ecological levels, smallest to largest, are: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere. Each level includes the one before it, and from the ecosystem upward the non-living environment is included too.
[bio31-s1.0.4] These levels continue the levels of organisation inside a body (cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism): the organism is where the two sets of levels meet, and ecology then builds upward from it.
[bio31-s1.0.5] Diagram: nested boxes or a stepped sequence. Show one rabbit (organism), a group of rabbits (population), rabbits with grass, foxes and other species (community), the community with soil, water, air and light (ecosystem), and the Earth's living layer (biosphere). Label each level.
[bio31-s1.1.b] The biosphere is the largest ecological level: the whole of the Earth's life taken together. Its limits and the way matter and energy move through it explain why life depends on the Sun.
[bio31-s1.1.0] The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists: the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water (fresh water and oceans). It is the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.1.1] The biosphere is a thin layer compared with the size of the Earth. Life is found from the deep ocean floor to a few kilometres up in the air, in birds, spores and bacteria carried on the wind.
[bio31-s1.1.2] Most life is concentrated in a narrow zone near the surface, because that is where light, liquid water and suitable temperatures are all present.
[bio31-s1.1.3] The biosphere is a single, closed system for matter, because atoms are recycled and not added. It is an open system for energy, because energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer, The carbon cycle and The nitrogen cycle.
[bio31-s1.2.b] An ecosystem is a community together with the non-living world it lives in. The living and non-living parts affect each other, which is why you have to study both.
[bio31-s1.2.0] An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment in a particular area. It has living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts that affect each other.
[bio31-s1.2.1] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment and the effects organisms have on each other: food supply, predation (one animal hunting and eating another), competition, disease, parasites, pollinators and decomposers.
[bio31-s1.2.2] Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions of the environment: light, temperature, water, oxygen, soil type and pH, minerals, wind, slope and aspect (the direction a slope faces). There is more on this in Abiotic factors within Investigating an Ecosystem, and in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio31-s1.2.3] Ecosystems have no fixed size: a rock pool, a hedgerow, a lake, a raised bog or an ocean can each be treated as an ecosystem. Their boundaries are often gradual, e.g. a woodland edge merging into grassland.
[bio31-s1.2.4] Every ecosystem needs a continual input of energy, almost always sunlight captured by producers (such as plants) in photosynthesis. Its matter is recycled by decomposers (such as fungi and bacteria), so the ecosystem keeps running.
[bio31-s1.2.5] Producers, consumers and decomposers each have a different role in an ecosystem. Consumers are the animals that get their energy by eating other organisms. The roles of all three are taught in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio31-s1.2.6] Irish land ecosystems include grassland, the most widespread land cover in Ireland, mostly farmed pasture and meadow. Woodland includes the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park. Peatlands have raised bogs in the midlands and blanket bogs in the uplands and the west.
[bio31-s1.2.7] Irish water and coastal ecosystems include freshwater, such as lakes like Lough Derg, a lake on the River Shannon, and rivers. Coastal and marine ecosystems include rocky shores, sandy shores and sand dunes, salt marshes and the open sea.
[bio31-s1.3.b] An ecosystem is too big to describe where one particular animal lives. Ecologists therefore talk about habitats, and smaller microhabitats, to say exactly where an organism is found.
[bio31-s1.3.0] A habitat is the place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs. A woodlouse's habitat is damp leaf litter, a limpet's is the rocky shore, and a frog's is a pond and the damp vegetation around it.
[bio31-s1.3.1] A microhabitat is a small area within a habitat with its own conditions, e.g. the underside of a stone on a rocky shore, a rock pool, a rotting log, or the bark of a tree. Shade, moisture and temperature there can differ greatly from the surroundings.
[bio31-s1.3.2] One ecosystem contains many habitats: a woodland contains the canopy, the trunk, the leaf litter and the soil, each with different species. A habitat is the organism's 'address'; its role there is its niche, which is dealt with in The niche.
[bio31-s1.4.b] Biodiversity is more than a count of species. It describes how much variety there is in life, and it can be measured at three different levels, from genes up to whole habitats.
[bio31-s1.4.0] Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels. It is usually described at three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem (habitat) diversity.
[bio31-s1.4.1] Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (different versions of a gene) within a species or population, e.g. the different breeds of cattle, or the variation in disease resistance among wild ash trees. It allows a population to adapt by natural selection when conditions change.
[bio31-s1.4.2] Low genetic diversity left the Irish potato crop of the 1840s vulnerable, because it was largely one variety, so when blight (a disease) arrived it destroyed it.
[bio31-s1.4.3] Species diversity is the variety of species in an area. It has two parts: species richness, the number of different species present, and species evenness, how evenly the individuals are spread among those species.
[bio31-s1.4.4] Two areas with the same species can differ in diversity if one is dominated by a single species. The Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness into one number, is Higher Level only. There is more on this in Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio31-s1.4.5] Ecosystem (habitat) diversity is the variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region, e.g. Ireland's bogs, woodlands, grasslands, lakes, rivers and coasts. A region with many habitats supports more species, because each habitat suits different organisms.
[bio31-s1.5.b] Life is not spread evenly across the Earth. Some places hold far more species than others, and Ireland is a good local example of a place with fewer.
[bio31-s1.5.0] Biodiversity is generally higher in warm, wet, stable environments and lower in cold or extreme ones. Tropical rainforests and coral reefs are the most species-rich ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.5.1] Ireland has fewer species than Britain or mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age, before many species had recolonised it from the south.
[bio31-s1.5.2] In brief, biodiversity provides food, medicines and materials, supports processes such as pollination and nutrient cycling, and makes ecosystems more stable. There is more on this, and on its loss and conservation, in Biodiversity loss and conservation.

CANDIDATES bio32-s2: Surveys
[bio32-s2.0.b] A survey is a planned way of finding out what lives in a habitat (the place where a community of organisms lives). There are two kinds: one asks 'which species?' and the other asks 'how many?'.
[bio32-s2.0.0] A qualitative survey records which species are present in the habitat (a species list), without measuring how many of each, so it gives the 'what' but not the 'how much'.
[bio32-s2.0.1] A qualitative survey is built from all the observations and collections made during the fieldwork. There is more on this in Collection and identification.
[bio32-s2.0.2] A qualitative survey can add a subjective estimate of abundance (how many there are) using a scale such as DAFOR: Dominant, Abundant, Frequent, Occasional, Rare, judged by eye for each species.
[bio32-s2.0.3] The DAFOR scale is quick, but different observers give different grades, so it isn't objective (it depends on the person's opinion, not on a measurement).
[bio32-s2.0.4] A quantitative survey measures how many of each species there are, as numbers, percentages or densities: percentage frequency, percentage cover, density per m², or an estimate of population size.
[bio32-s2.0.5] Quantitative data can be compared between areas and analysed mathematically, so it can be used, for example, to calculate a diversity index (a single number describing how varied a community is), such as Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio32-s2.0.6] Distribution means where in the habitat a species is found; abundance means how many there are. Surveys measure both.
[bio32-s2.1.b] Counting every organism in a habitat is almost never possible, so samples (small parts of the habitat) are taken and the results used to estimate the whole. How the samples are chosen decides whether the estimate can be trusted.
[bio32-s2.1.0] A sample must be representative, meaning it reflects the whole habitat: it must be large enough and chosen without bias (without favouring particular spots).
[bio32-s2.1.1] Random sampling is used when the habitat looks uniform (the same all over), so that every point has an equal chance of being chosen.
[bio32-s2.1.2] 1. Lay two long tape measures at right angles along two edges of the study area to form the axes of a grid.
[bio32-s2.1.3] 2. Generate pairs of random numbers (with a calculator, a phone app or random-number tables) as coordinates, for example (7, 23) means 7 m along one tape and 23 m along the other.
[bio32-s2.1.4] 3. Walk to each coordinate and place the quadrat (the square sampling frame) there, with its corner on the point.
[bio32-s2.1.5] Throwing a quadrat over the shoulder is not truly random and is unsafe, so don't use it.
[bio32-s2.2.b] Random sampling suits an even habitat, but conditions often change from one side of a habitat to the other. Then samples are placed in an orderly way, and enough of them must be taken to give a reliable result.
[bio32-s2.2.0] Systematic sampling places samples at regular intervals, for example a quadrat every 2 m along a line. It is used where conditions change across the habitat (a gradient, for example from a hedge into open field), to show how species change along it; this is a transect.
[bio32-s2.2.1] The more samples, the more reliable the estimate, because chance differences between individual spots average out.
[bio32-s2.2.2] Take quadrats one at a time and keep a running mean (or a running total of species found).
[bio32-s2.2.3] When adding more quadrats no longer changes the mean (or adds new species), enough have been taken. In a grassland this is usually at least 10 to 20 quadrats.
[bio32-s2.3.b] A quadrat is the basic tool for sampling plants. Different designs suit different measurements, and a few rules keep the counting fair.
[bio32-s2.3.0] A quadrat is a square frame of known area used to sample plants and slow-moving or attached animals (ones that can't escape or move while you count). School grassland quadrats are usually 0.5 m × 0.5 m (0.25 m²) or 1 m × 1 m (1 m²).
[bio32-s2.3.1] A gridded quadrat is divided by strings or wires into 100 equal small squares (10 × 10), each 1% of the area, which makes percentages easy to read off.
[bio32-s2.3.2] A point quadrat (point frame) is a frame on legs holding a row of usually 10 long pins (needles) that can be lowered vertically through the vegetation. Each pin that touches a species counts as one 'hit' for that species.
[bio32-s2.3.3] Rule 1: lay the quadrat flat on the ground at the chosen point without moving it towards or away from any plant, so you don't choose which plants fall inside.
[bio32-s2.3.4] Rule 2: use the same size of quadrat throughout, so counts from different quadrats can be compared.
[bio32-s2.3.5] Rule 3: decide in advance how to count plants on the edge (for example count a plant only if more than half of it is inside, or count only those on the top and left edges), and apply the rule every time so no plant is counted twice or missed.
[bio32-s2.3.6] Diagram: a gridded quadrat (100 squares) placed at a random coordinate on a grid laid out with two tape measures at right angles; and a point quadrat with 10 pins, with one pin touching a leaf (a hit).
[bio32-s2.4.b] Percentage frequency tells you how widespread a species is across the samples. It asks only 'is it there or not?' in each quadrat.
[bio32-s2.4.0] Percentage frequency is the percentage of all the quadrats sampled in which a species is present: percentage frequency = (number of quadrats containing the species ÷ total number of quadrats) × 100.
[bio32-s2.4.1] Only presence or absence is recorded in each quadrat, so it is quick and objective, and works for any plant (or attached animal).
[bio32-s2.4.2] It doesn't show how many there are: one daisy or a hundred in a quadrat both count as 'present'.
[bio32-s2.4.3] It depends on quadrat size, because a larger quadrat is more likely to contain the species.
[bio32-s2.4.4] Worked example (sample data): 20 quadrats were placed at random. Daisy was present in 12, dandelion in 5 and white clover in 16.
[bio32-s2.4.5] Daisy: 12 ÷ 20 × 100 = 60%. Dandelion: 5 ÷ 20 × 100 = 25%. White clover: 16 ÷ 20 × 100 = 80%.
[bio32-s2.4.6] Results are shown in a table and as a bar chart with one bar per species, with percentage frequency on the y-axis.
[bio32-s2.5.b] Percentage cover is the percentage of the ground area of a quadrat covered by a species, viewed from directly above. It measures how much space a species takes up, not how many individuals there are.
[bio32-s2.5.0] Percentage cover is used for plants that are hard to count as individuals, such as grasses, clover and mosses, which spread to form mats.
[bio32-s2.5.1] Gridded-quadrat method: count the small squares in which the species covers more than half the square; each square is 1%. Repeat in each quadrat and find the mean percentage cover.
[bio32-s2.5.2] Worked example (sample data): in five quadrats white clover covered 30, 45, 25, 40 and 35 squares; total = 175; mean percentage cover = 175 ÷ 5 = 35%.
[bio32-s2.5.3] Point-quadrat method: lower each pin and record every species it touches. Percentage cover = (number of hits on the species ÷ total number of pins lowered) × 100.
[bio32-s2.5.4] Worked example (sample data): the 10-pin frame was placed at 10 random points, so 100 pins were lowered; grass was touched by 72 pins and white clover by 23.
[bio32-s2.5.5] Grass cover = 72 ÷ 100 × 100 = 72%; clover cover = 23%.
[bio32-s2.5.6] The total cover of all species in a quadrat can be more than 100%, because plants overlap in layers (for example clover leaves above grass).
[bio32-s2.5.7] Percentage cover estimated by eye is subjective. The grid and point methods are more objective, and the point method is the most objective because a pin either touches or doesn't.
[bio32-s2.6.b] Density is the number of individuals per unit area, usually per m². Once you know the density, you can scale up to estimate how many plants live in the whole habitat.
[bio32-s2.6.0] Count the individuals of a species that can be counted separately (for example daisy or dandelion rosettes) in each quadrat. Density = total number counted ÷ total area sampled.
[bio32-s2.6.1] Estimated population = density × total area of the habitat.
[bio32-s2.6.2] The area of the habitat is found by measuring its length and width with tape measures or a trundle wheel (a measuring wheel you roll along the ground).
[bio32-s2.6.3] Worked example (sample data): five quadrats of 0.25 m² contained 4, 7, 3, 6 and 5 daisies. Total = 25 daisies in 5 × 0.25 = 1.25 m².
[bio32-s2.6.4] Density = 25 ÷ 1.25 = 20 daisies per m² (or: mean per quadrat 5 ÷ 0.25 m² = 20 per m²).
[bio32-s2.6.5] The field is 50 m × 40 m = 2000 m², so the estimated population = 20 × 2000 = 40 000 daisies.
[bio32-s2.6.6] Counting individuals is the most informative measure but is slow, and impossible for grasses and other spreading plants, which need percentage cover instead.
[bio32-s2.7.b] A transect is a line across a habitat along which samples are taken at regular intervals. It is the tool for showing how species change when conditions change gradually.
[bio32-s2.7.0] Transects are used where there is a gradient in conditions, to show how the distribution of species changes along it: for example from under a hedge or tree into open grassland, across a trampled path, from a wet hollow to drier ground, or up a rocky shore.
[bio32-s2.7.1] Line transect, step 1: stretch a rope or tape measure tightly across the area at ground level.
[bio32-s2.7.2] Line transect, step 2: at regular intervals (for example every 1 m), record every species touching the line (or directly below it).
[bio32-s2.7.3] Line transect, step 3: record the distance for each species.
[bio32-s2.7.4] A line transect shows where each species occurs (distribution) but not how many, because it only records presence at each point.
[bio32-s2.7.5] A transect is a systematic, not random, sample: it is chosen to cross the gradient.
[bio32-s2.7.6] Several parallel transects give more reliable results, because one line might happen to cross an unusual patch.
[bio32-s2.8.b] A line transect only tells you where species are. A belt transect adds quadrats along the line, so it also tells you how abundant each species is.
[bio32-s2.8.0] Belt transect: quadrats are placed along the line, either side by side all the way (continuous belt transect) or at regular intervals (interrupted belt transect, for example a quadrat every 2 m).
[bio32-s2.8.1] In each quadrat of a belt transect, percentage cover, frequency or numbers are recorded, so a belt transect shows abundance as well as distribution.
[bio32-s2.8.2] Abiotic factors (non-living conditions such as light or soil moisture) are measured at each quadrat to relate them to the species. There is more on this in Abiotic factors.
[bio32-s2.8.3] Diagram: a belt transect from a hedge into open grassland: tape measure from the hedge, quadrats at 2 m intervals, with distances labelled.
[bio32-s2.9.b] Mobile animals move out of a quadrat before you can count them. Instead you catch, mark and release some, then use the proportion of marked animals in a second catch to work out the total population.
[bio32-s2.9.0] Mobile animals can't be counted in quadrats, so their population is estimated by capture–mark–recapture.
[bio32-s2.9.1] 1. Catch a sample with a suitable method (for example pitfall traps, sunken containers, for ground beetles or woodlice).
[bio32-s2.9.2] 2. Count them (M) and mark each harmlessly, for example with a small dot of non-toxic, quick-drying, waterproof paint on the underside of a woodlouse or the wing case of a beetle.
[bio32-s2.9.3] 3. Release them where they were caught and leave enough time (for example 24 hours) for them to mix back into the population.
[bio32-s2.9.4] 4. Catch a second sample in the same way and place; count the total caught (C) and the number that are marked (R).
[bio32-s2.9.5] Estimated population size: N = (M × C) ÷ R. This works because the fraction of the second sample that is marked (R ÷ C) is assumed to equal the fraction of the whole population that is marked (M ÷ N).
[bio32-s2.9.6] Worked example (sample data): 30 woodlice were caught, marked and released. Next day 40 were caught, 8 of them marked. N = (30 × 40) ÷ 8 = 1200 ÷ 8 = 150 woodlice.
[bio32-s2.9.7] Second example (sample data): M = 48, C = 50, R = 12; N = 2400 ÷ 12 = 200.
[bio32-s2.10.b] The capture–recapture formula only gives a good estimate if certain conditions hold, and it is easy to mix up the letters. When an exact population isn't needed, a simpler count can be enough.
[bio32-s2.10.0] N must be larger than both M and C, because the population includes every marked animal and every animal caught in the second sample. If it isn't, you have made an error.
[bio32-s2.10.1] R is the number of marked animals in the second sample, not the number marked in the first sample.
[bio32-s2.10.2] Assumption: there are no births, deaths, immigration (animals arriving) or emigration (animals leaving) between the two samples, so the population stays the same size.
[bio32-s2.10.3] Assumption: marked animals mix fully and randomly with the rest, and marked and unmarked animals are equally likely to be caught.
[bio32-s2.10.4] Assumption: the mark doesn't harm the animal, make it easier for predators to see, or wash or rub off.
[bio32-s2.10.5] If marks are lost, R is too small and N is overestimated, because fewer marked animals are recognised in the second sample. The assumptions are rarely fully met in practice.
[bio32-s2.10.6] For comparisons only, a relative count is often enough: for example the number of grasshoppers caught per ten sweeps of a sweep net, or the number of beetles per pitfall trap per day, compared between two areas using the same method.
[bio32-s2.11.b] In ecology a model is a simplified representation of a habitat that describes it and can be used to explain or predict. Here the model is a labelled map with data, built from the primary data (results you collected yourself).
[bio32-s2.11.0] Size: measure the length and width (or the sides of an irregular area) with long tape measures or a trundle wheel, and calculate the area, for example 50 m × 40 m = 2000 m².
[bio32-s2.11.1] Map: draw a sketch map to scale, with a north arrow, showing the boundary, hedges, trees, paths, gates, wet and dry areas, any slope (and which way it faces), the positions of the quadrats, transects and traps, and where the main species were found.
[bio32-s2.11.2] Species: the species list from the qualitative survey, with the quantitative results (percentage frequency, cover, density, population estimates) for the main species.
[bio32-s2.11.3] Abiotic factors: the mean values measured across the habitat (for example light intensity, soil pH, soil water content, air and soil temperature, aspect and slope), with differences between areas marked on the map.
[bio32-s2.11.4] Biotic factors (the effects of living things): the feeding and symbiotic relationships observed, shown as food chains or a food web drawn from the species found.
[bio32-s2.11.5] Biotic factors also include grazing, trampling and human management such as mowing or fertilising.
[bio32-s2.11.6] Using the model: it can explain why species are where they are (for example rushes only in the wet hollow) and predict the effect of a change (for example if mowing stopped, taller grasses would shade out daisies).
[bio32-s2.11.7] Limits of the model: it is a snapshot of one season and of the parts sampled, so it may not hold at other times of year or in unsampled areas.
[bio32-s2.12.b] Raw counts are hard to interpret. Tables and graphs show patterns in species abundance and distribution at a glance.
[bio32-s2.12.0] Record results in tables with headings and units: quadrat number or distance along the transect, species, and the measure used.
[bio32-s2.12.1] Bar charts compare species, for example the percentage frequency of each.
[bio32-s2.12.2] For a transect, plot each species' abundance against distance, or draw a kite diagram.
[bio32-s2.12.3] In a kite diagram, for each species a band is centred on a horizontal line; its width at each point along the transect is proportional to the species' abundance there, so the bands show at a glance where each species is common.
[bio32-s2.12.4] Diagram: a kite diagram for two species along a 10 m transect from a hedge, with distance on the x-axis.
[bio32-s2.13.b] A survey can be set up as a fair test, with an aim, a hypothesis (a testable prediction), and variables. Field studies differ from lab experiments because conditions can't be fully controlled.
[bio32-s2.13.0] Aim: to estimate the abundance and distribution of named species (for example daisy, white clover, grass and woodlice) in a grassland.
[bio32-s2.13.1] Hypothesis (example): 'Daisies are more abundant in the short-mown part of the field than in the long-grass part.'
[bio32-s2.13.2] Independent variable (the one you change or compare) when two areas are compared: the area (for example mown vs unmown).
[bio32-s2.13.3] Dependent variable (the one you measure): the abundance of the species (density, percentage cover or frequency).
[bio32-s2.13.4] Controlled variables (kept the same): the quadrat size and type, the number of quadrats, the method of placing them (random coordinates), the counting rule, the observer, the date and the weather.
[bio32-s2.13.5] There is no true control in a field study, because the two areas can't be made identical in every other way.
[bio32-s2.13.6] Conclusion: state the measured abundance of each species and compare the areas using the means.
[bio32-s2.13.7] Say whether the results support the hypothesis. A difference shows correlation with the area, not proof of the cause.
[bio32-s2.14.b] No survey is perfect. Knowing where errors come from lets you say how reliable your results are and how to improve them.
[bio32-s2.14.0] Too few samples, or samples not placed at random (choosing 'typical' spots), give an unrepresentative estimate; use random coordinates and enough quadrats.
[bio32-s2.14.1] Misidentification of species, especially grasses and small invertebrates, is a source of error.
[bio32-s2.14.2] Estimates of percentage cover by eye are subjective, and inconsistent edge rules change counts.
[bio32-s2.14.3] Results vary with the season and the time of day (plants flower and animals are active at different times), so a single survey is a snapshot.
[bio32-s2.14.4] The assumptions of capture–recapture are rarely fully met.
[bio32-s2.14.5] Trampling during the survey damages the plants being sampled; avoid trampling the quadrat sites before they are sampled.
[bio32-s2.14.6] Reliability improves by repeating the survey (more quadrats, more transects, several dates) and calculating means, because repeats reduce the effect of chance.

CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.
