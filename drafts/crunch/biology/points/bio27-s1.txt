TARGET bio27-s1: The digestive system
[bio27-s1.0.b] Humans can't make their own food, so every meal has to be taken in, broken down and moved into the body before cells can use it. This happens in four stages: ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.
[bio27-s1.0.0] Humans are heterotrophs, meaning they obtain food from other organisms rather than making it. They are also omnivores: they eat both plant and animal material.
[bio27-s1.0.1] Ingestion is taking food into the body through the mouth, which is where every meal enters the gut.
[bio27-s1.0.2] Digestion is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small, soluble molecules that can be absorbed. It is both mechanical (physical breakdown into smaller pieces) and chemical (hydrolysis, the splitting of large molecules by enzymes).
[bio27-s1.0.3] Digestion is necessary because large molecules such as starch, proteins and fats cannot pass through the wall of the gut or through cell membranes. It converts them to small soluble molecules (glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol) that can be absorbed into the blood.
[bio27-s1.0.4] Cells then use the absorbed molecules to build the body's own molecules or for respiration. This use of absorbed molecules is called assimilation: for example, amino acids are built into proteins and glucose is respired to release energy.
[bio27-s1.0.5] Absorption is the passage of the small soluble products of digestion, together with water, vitamins and minerals, through the wall of the gut into the blood or lymph. It happens mainly in the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.0.6] Egestion is the removal of undigested and unabsorbed material, mainly dietary fibre, as faeces through the anus. This material is left over because the body cannot digest or absorb it.
[bio27-s1.0.7] Egestion is not excretion. Faeces were never part of the body's metabolism (the chemical reactions inside cells), except for bile pigments, whereas excretion removes metabolic waste made by cells.
[bio27-s1.1.b] The alimentary canal, or gut, is the pathway food follows through the body. Its muscular wall and lining are built to move food along and to protect the gut itself.
[bio27-s1.1.0] The alimentary canal, or gut, is a continuous muscular tube from the mouth to the anus. Food passes in order through the mouth, pharynx (throat), oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anus.
[bio27-s1.1.1] The gut wall contains two layers of smooth muscle, circular and longitudinal, which move food along by peristalsis (waves of muscle contraction that push food onwards).
[bio27-s1.1.2] The inner lining of the gut makes mucus. Mucus lubricates the food so it slides along, and protects the lining from digestive enzymes and acid, which would otherwise damage the gut's own cells.
[bio27-s1.1.3] Sphincters are rings of muscle that control movement between parts of the gut, for example at the entrance and exit of the stomach (the exit one is the pyloric sphincter) and at the anus.
[bio27-s1.2.b] The first three organs prepare food and carry it onwards. The mouth breaks it up and starts starch digestion, the oesophagus transports it, and the stomach stores and churns it while starting protein digestion.
[bio27-s1.2.0] The mouth is where food is ingested and chewed by the teeth, then mixed with saliva from three pairs of salivary glands. The tongue shapes it into a ball, the bolus, and pushes it back for swallowing. Starch digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.1] The oesophagus, or gullet, is a muscular tube that carries food by peristalsis from the pharynx, down through the chest behind the trachea (windpipe) and through the diaphragm (the sheet of muscle below the lungs), to the stomach. No digestion or absorption happens in it.
[bio27-s1.2.2] During swallowing the epiglottis (a flap) covers the entrance to the trachea, so food goes down the oesophagus and not into the lungs.
[bio27-s1.2.3] The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag in the upper left of the abdomen, just below the diaphragm. It stores food for a few hours, which means a meal can be eaten quickly and released to the intestine gradually.
[bio27-s1.2.4] The stomach churns food with gastric juice (acid and a protease, an enzyme that digests protein) into a thick liquid called chyme. The acid kills most bacteria in food, and protein digestion begins here.
[bio27-s1.2.5] The pyloric sphincter at the stomach's exit releases chyme a little at a time into the small intestine.
[bio27-s1.3.b] After the stomach, chyme enters the small intestine, where digestion is finished and most absorption happens. What is left passes to the large intestine, which recovers water and stores the waste until it leaves the body.
[bio27-s1.3.0] The small intestine is the longest part of the gut, coiled in the abdomen. Its first part, the duodenum, receives bile from the liver and pancreatic juice from the pancreas, and most chemical digestion happens there.
[bio27-s1.3.1] The later parts, the jejunum and the ileum, complete digestion and absorb most digested food and water through millions of villi (tiny finger-like projections of the lining that give a large surface for absorption).
[bio27-s1.3.2] The large intestine is wider but shorter than the small intestine; "large" refers to its width, not its length. Its parts are the caecum, with the appendix attached, the colon and the rectum.
[bio27-s1.3.3] The appendix is a small dead-end tube with no digestive role in humans, so food is not digested in it.
[bio27-s1.3.4] The colon absorbs water and some minerals from the remaining material, and is home to many bacteria. It is not an excretory organ, because what leaves is egested, not excreted.
[bio27-s1.3.5] The rectum is the last part of the large intestine, which stores faeces until egestion, so waste does not have to leave the body continuously.
[bio27-s1.3.6] The anus is the opening at the end of the gut, closed by sphincter muscles. The outer sphincter is under voluntary control, so egestion can be delayed until it is convenient.
[bio27-s1.4.b] Some organs help digestion without food ever entering them. They make secretions that reach the gut through ducts (narrow tubes), so they are called associated organs.
[bio27-s1.4.0] The salivary glands are three pairs, around the mouth and below the jaw, releasing saliva into the mouth through ducts. Saliva contains water, mucus and the enzyme salivary amylase.
[bio27-s1.4.1] The liver is the largest internal organ, in the upper right of the abdomen just below the diaphragm. Its digestive role is to make bile.
[bio27-s1.4.2] The liver also receives the absorbed products of digestion through the hepatic portal vein (the vein carrying blood from the gut to the liver), stores glycogen and deaminates amino acids (removes their nitrogen-containing part).
[bio27-s1.4.3] The gallbladder is a small sac under the liver that stores and concentrates bile. It squeezes bile through the bile duct into the duodenum when fatty food arrives there. It makes no bile itself.
[bio27-s1.4.4] The pancreas is a soft, pinkish gland behind the stomach, lying in the curve of the duodenum. It makes pancreatic juice, which contains enzymes for starch, protein and fat and sodium hydrogencarbonate, and this flows through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum.
[bio27-s1.4.5] The pancreas is also an endocrine gland (a ductless gland that releases hormones into the blood) making insulin, so it has both ducted (exocrine) and ductless (endocrine) roles.
[bio27-s1.4.6] The liver, gallbladder and pancreas are associated organs: food never passes through them, and their secretions reach the gut through ducts. Food does pass through the mouth, stomach and intestines.

[bio27-s1.6.b] Following one mouthful from start to finish pulls the stages of nutrition together. Each part of the gut has its own main job.
[bio27-s1.6.0] Mouth: ingestion; mechanical and chemical digestion begin.
[bio27-s1.6.1] Oesophagus: transport of food to the stomach, because the mouth and stomach are too far apart for food to move unaided.
[bio27-s1.6.2] Stomach: storage and churning; protein digestion begins.
[bio27-s1.6.3] Duodenum: most chemical digestion, with bile and pancreatic juice.
[bio27-s1.6.4] Ileum: digestion is completed and absorption takes place.
[bio27-s1.6.5] Colon: absorption of water, so the remaining waste becomes more solid.
[bio27-s1.6.6] Rectum: storage of faeces.
[bio27-s1.6.7] Anus: egestion, the removal of faeces from the body.
