TARGET bio3-s1: Evaluating evidence
[bio3-s1.0.b] Every news story, advert or social media post about science makes a claim, and a claim is only as good as the evidence behind it. To judge that evidence you ask three questions: is it relevant, is it accurate, and is it biased?
[bio3-s1.0.0] Evidence is the information (observations, measurements, results of studies) used to support or refute a claim, which means to back it up or to count against it.
[bio3-s1.0.1] A claim is a statement that something is true, e.g. “vitamin C prevents colds”. The claim is what you are testing; the evidence is what you test it with.
[bio3-s1.0.2] Relevance asks whether the evidence actually addresses this claim. Evidence about a different organism, population, dose or situation may not apply, so a study in mice, or on cells in a dish, does not show the same effect in humans.
[bio3-s1.0.3] A study of elite athletes may not apply to the general population, because their bodies and training are unusual. A study of a pure chemical may not apply to a food that contains only a little of it.
[bio3-s1.0.4] Accuracy asks whether the evidence is correct and reported correctly. Check whether it comes from a reliable method, whether it has been reproduced (repeated by others with the same result), and whether the numbers in a report match the original study.
[bio3-s1.0.5] Also check whether the conclusion goes further than the data allow. A report can quote accurate numbers and still claim more than those numbers show.
[bio3-s1.0.6] Bias asks whether the evidence, or the way it is presented, has been pushed in one direction. The types of bias in sources are funding, conflict of interest, cherry-picking, publication bias, confirmation bias, sampling bias and political bias. Here you apply them to the evidence behind an argument.
[bio3-s1.1.b] Not all evidence is equally convincing. Scientists rank the types of evidence by how well each can rule out chance and other explanations, so you can say how far a claim is supported. The list runs from weakest to strongest.
[bio3-s1.1.0] Anecdote or personal testimony (“it worked for me”) is the weakest. One person’s experience can be due to chance, the placebo effect (feeling better because you expect to), natural recovery or other causes. It can prompt a proper study, but it is not evidence that a treatment works.
[bio3-s1.1.1] Expert opinion without data, or a single case report (a description of one patient), is weak. It is useful for raising a question that can then be tested.
[bio3-s1.1.2] Laboratory studies on cells or animals are useful for finding mechanisms (how something works), but they may not apply to humans, so the result is relevant only with caution.
[bio3-s1.1.3] Observational studies of large groups of people, e.g. following thousands of people and comparing smokers with non-smokers, are good for finding links (correlations). They cannot on their own show cause and effect, because other factors may differ between the groups.
[bio3-s1.1.4] Randomised controlled trials place people randomly in a treatment group or a control group, usually double-blind. Random placement spreads other factors evenly between the groups, so these are the strongest single study for showing that a treatment causes an effect.
[bio3-s1.1.5] Systematic reviews and meta-analyses combine all good-quality studies on a question. They are the strongest overall evidence because they do not rely on one study, which might be a fluke.
[bio3-s1.1.6] Scientific consensus is the agreed view of most experts in a field, reached after many studies, e.g. that human activity is causing climate change and that vaccines do not cause autism.
[bio3-s1.1.7] A single new study rarely overturns a consensus, because it needs to be reproduced first. One surprising result is more likely to be a chance or a flaw than the end of a well-tested idea.
[bio3-s1.2.b] Even real evidence can be misused. These are the mistakes that most often turn a modest result into a dramatic headline, and each one is a point you can raise when evaluating a claim.
[bio3-s1.2.0] Correlation presented as causation: media reports often say that a food, drug or habit “causes” or “prevents” a disease when a study found only a correlation (two things occurring together). A link alone does not show that one produces the other.
[bio3-s1.2.1] In large health studies, experiments on people are often impossible or unethical, so a link is accepted as causal only when several kinds of evidence agree. The cause comes before the effect, and more exposure gives more effect (a dose–response pattern).
[bio3-s1.2.2] The same link must also be found in many independent studies in different populations, and a biological mechanism must explain it.
[bio3-s1.2.3] Smoking and lung cancer met all of these tests, which is why smoking is accepted as a cause of lung cancer.
[bio3-s1.2.4] Small samples: results from few individuals are more likely to be due to chance, because one or two unusual people can change the average.
[bio3-s1.2.5] No control group: without a comparison, an improvement may have happened anyway, so you cannot tell whether the treatment did anything.
[bio3-s1.2.6] Relative and absolute risk: a headline saying a food “doubles the risk” of a disease may describe a rise from 1 in 10,000 to 2 in 10,000. This is an arithmetic illustration, not real data, and the absolute increase is small. Always ask “doubled from what?”
[bio3-s1.2.7] Extrapolating beyond the data: assuming that a trend continues outside the range tested, or that the effect of a high dose applies to a low dose, because nobody tested those conditions.
[bio3-s1.3.b] Media include newspapers, television, radio, websites, podcasts, social media and advertising. Most people learn about science from them rather than from journals, so media reports shape public opinion and decisions on health, the environment and technology. Understanding how reports get distorted helps you spot the problem.
[bio3-s1.3.0] Headlines exaggerate to attract readers or clicks (sensationalism). A single preliminary study may be reported as a breakthrough or a “cure”, and animal or cell results may be reported as if they apply to humans.
[bio3-s1.3.1] Uncertainty and limitations are often left out, which makes results sound firmer than they are. Press releases from universities and companies may themselves exaggerate.
[bio3-s1.3.2] False balance: giving equal time to a fringe view and to the scientific consensus, e.g. one climate scientist debating one climate-change denier, makes the evidence seem evenly split when it is not.
[bio3-s1.3.3] Misinformation is false or misleading information spread without intent to deceive. Disinformation is false information spread deliberately to deceive, e.g. for profit or political gain.
[bio3-s1.3.4] Social media spread both quickly, because posts that provoke emotion are shared more and algorithms show people more of what they already engage with, creating echo chambers (feeds where you mostly meet views you already hold).
[bio3-s1.3.5] Persuasion techniques to recognise: appeal to authority (a celebrity or a doctor speaking outside their expertise); appeal to nature (“natural means safe”, though many natural substances are poisonous, e.g. deadly nightshade); emotive language and images; testimonials instead of data.
[bio3-s1.3.6] Two more techniques: “scientists are hiding the truth” conspiracy claims, which cannot be tested, and scientific-sounding words used to sell a product.
[bio3-s1.3.7] Responsible science reporting does the opposite: it names the study and where it was published, gives the sample size and type of study, states the limitations, separates evidence from opinion, and includes comment from independent experts.
[bio3-s1.4.b] This is a method you can apply to any article, advert or post about science or technology. Work through the steps in order, and finish with a judgement, because “evaluate” means deciding how far the evidence supports the claim and noting its limits.
[bio3-s1.4.0] 1. Identify the claim and who is making it, so you know exactly what must be supported and what the speaker stands to gain.
[bio3-s1.4.1] 2. Find the original evidence: is a study named, and was it peer reviewed? A claim with no traceable source cannot be checked.
[bio3-s1.4.2] 3. Check relevance: who or what was studied, and does it apply to the claim? Mice, cells, elite athletes or a pure chemical may not match the situation in the claim.
[bio3-s1.4.3] 4. Check the strength of the evidence: the type of study, the sample size, whether there was a control group, and whether the result has been reproduced. Anecdotes, small samples and missing controls all weaken it.
[bio3-s1.4.4] 5. Check accuracy: does the report match the study? Is correlation presented as causation? Is the risk given as relative or absolute?
[bio3-s1.4.5] 6. Check bias: who funded the study and the report, what is their purpose, and are other views or limitations mentioned?
[bio3-s1.4.6] 7. Check what other reliable sources and the scientific consensus say, because one report that disagrees with many others needs explaining.
[bio3-s1.4.7] 8. Make a judgement: how far the evidence supports the claim, and what further evidence is needed.
[bio3-s1.5.b] This case shows the checklist at work on a real claim. One weak study, a hidden conflict of interest and wide media coverage combined to damage public health.
[bio3-s1.5.0] The claim: in 1998 Andrew Wakefield and colleagues published a paper in The Lancet on 12 children, suggesting a link between the MMR (measles, mumps and rubella) vaccine and autism. The paper suggested a link; later evidence showed there is none.
[bio3-s1.5.1] Relevance and strength: the sample was tiny and had no control group, so there was nothing to compare against. At most, the paper showed a coincidence in timing, not a cause.
[bio3-s1.5.2] Bias: Wakefield had an undisclosed conflict of interest. He had been paid by lawyers acting for parents who wanted to sue vaccine manufacturers.
[bio3-s1.5.3] Media: the claim was reported widely, often with false balance, so the public saw a fringe view presented as an equal to the consensus.
[bio3-s1.5.4] Investigation: the journalist Brian Deer found that the children’s medical details had been misrepresented. The Lancet retracted (formally withdrew) the paper in 2010, and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register in 2010.
[bio3-s1.5.5] Later evidence: large studies found no link, e.g. a 2019 Danish study of over 650,000 children.
[bio3-s1.5.6] Effect: vaccination rates fell in the UK and Ireland and in other countries, and measles outbreaks followed.
[bio3-s1.5.7] The case shows how one weak study, amplified by the media, can harm public health long after it has been disproved. The paper was published in a peer-reviewed journal, so peer review alone did not prevent the harm.
[bio3-s1.6.b] Claims about technology, like claims about medicine, can be tested with the same checklist. A company selling a product has a reason to make it sound better than it is.
[bio3-s1.6.0] Technology claims need the same checklist. For example, a claim that a new app “detects cancer”, or that a gene test “predicts your ideal diet”, should be tested by asking what evidence shows that it works, in whom, how accurately, and who profits from it.

CANDIDATES bio1-s3: Recognising bias and evaluating sources
[bio1-s3.0.b] Before you can judge information, you need words for where it comes from and how it can go wrong. The terms below are easy to mix up, so learn each one precisely.
[bio1-s3.0.0] A source is anything that provides information: a journal article, a textbook, a website, a news report, a government or agency report, a company leaflet, a video or a social media post.
[bio1-s3.0.1] A primary source is the original report of research by the people who did it, e.g. a research paper in a peer-reviewed journal (one checked by independent experts before publication).
[bio1-s3.0.2] A secondary source summarises or interprets primary sources, e.g. a review article, a textbook, a news report or a documentary. A tertiary source compiles secondary sources, e.g. an encyclopaedia.
[bio1-s3.0.3] Primary data are data collected by yourself; secondary data are data collected by somebody else, e.g. a table of national vaccination rates, a population survey, or the results in a published paper.
[bio1-s3.0.4] A research paper is a primary source, but its data are secondary data to a student who uses them, because the student did not collect them. So primary and secondary sources are not the same as primary and secondary data.
[bio1-s3.0.5] Bias is a one-sided influence that pushes information or its interpretation away from the truth in a consistent direction. A biased source is not necessarily false, but it selects, presents or interprets information to favour one view.
[bio1-s3.1.b] Good research is a planned process, not a quick search. This is the research that the Biology in Practice Investigation requires, and each step below makes the next one more reliable.
[bio1-s3.1.0] 1. Define the issue and a focused research question, e.g. “Does eating ultra-processed food raise the risk of type 2 diabetes?” rather than “Is food bad for you?”. A narrow question is easier to search for and to answer.
[bio1-s3.1.1] 2. Choose keywords, including scientific terms and synonyms, and search suitable places: the school or public library; scientific search engines and databases, e.g. Google Scholar, and PubMed for medical research; the websites of scientific and public bodies; and textbooks.
[bio1-s3.1.2] 3. Gather several different types of source, including some primary sources and some secondary data, and sources that take different views, so that you do not see only one side.
[bio1-s3.1.3] 4. Record each source as you use it, so that it can be referenced (credited in a standard written form) and checked later. There is more on this in Communicating results.
[bio1-s3.1.4] 5. Evaluate each source with the criteria for evaluating a source, noting what it lacks and any bias.
[bio1-s3.1.5] 6. Cross-check, or corroborate, important claims in independent sources. A claim found in several independent reliable sources is more trustworthy than one found in a single source, because one source could be mistaken or biased.
[bio1-s3.1.6] 7. Separate facts, evidence and opinions, and summarise in your own words. Reference every source: copying another’s words or ideas without acknowledgement is plagiarism.
[bio1-s3.2.b] To decide how far to trust a source, ask the same set of questions of every one. Each criterion below has a question to ask and a sign that tells you whether the answer is good or poor.
[bio1-s3.2.0] Authority: who wrote it, and are they qualified and named? Good sign: named scientists at a university, research institute or public health body. Poor sign: an anonymous author, or a celebrity or influencer with no expertise.
[bio1-s3.2.1] Peer review and publisher: has it been checked by experts before publication? A peer-reviewed journal article is more reliable than a blog or press release, because errors are more likely to have been caught. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio1-s3.2.2] Currency: when was it published or updated? Biology moves quickly, so older sources may be out of date, e.g. a source describing a five-kingdom classification. Older sources are still useful for historical background.
[bio1-s3.2.3] Purpose: why was it written, to inform, to teach, to persuade, to sell or to entertain? A source written to sell or persuade is more likely to be biased, because it has a reason to present only one view.
[bio1-s3.2.4] Evidence and references: does it give data, describe how they were collected, and reference its sources so that they can be checked? Claims such as “studies show” or “scientists say” with no reference are a warning sign, because you cannot check them.
[bio1-s3.2.5] Relevance: does it address the research question, for the right organism, population or place? A study on mice, for example, may not apply to humans.
[bio1-s3.2.6] Accuracy and consistency: does it agree with other reliable sources and with established science? Are there factual errors, or emotive (feeling-driven) or exaggerated language?
[bio1-s3.2.7] A memory aid used in schools and libraries is the CRAAP test: Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, Purpose.
[bio1-s3.3.b] Even a source that looks professional may leave out the information you need to judge it. Missing detail matters because without it you cannot tell whether a result is valid or can be compared with other work.
[bio1-s3.3.0] Missing method: without knowing how data were collected, a reader cannot judge whether they are valid or repeat the work.
[bio1-s3.3.1] Missing sample size, or a very small sample: a result from a handful of people or plants may be due to chance rather than a real effect.
[bio1-s3.3.2] Missing units, dates, location or definitions, e.g. what counts as “obese” or “a heavy drinker”, so data cannot be compared with other data.
[bio1-s3.3.3] Averages or percentages alone, without the range or the raw data (the original unprocessed measurements), can hide great variation or very small numbers. For example, “50% improved” could mean 1 of 2 patients.
[bio1-s3.3.4] No control or comparison group, which means you cannot tell whether the result was caused by the treatment. A source may also give no information on who funded the work, and state no limitations.
[bio1-s3.3.5] Simplified summaries, such as news reports, infographics and social media posts, often leave out the uncertainty, the limitations and the conditions under which a result applies.
[bio1-s3.4.b] Secondary data are data someone else collected, and students often rely on them in research. They are powerful but come with limits, so you need to weigh both sides and know what to ask.
[bio1-s3.4.0] Advantages: secondary data can cover far larger samples, longer periods and more places than a student could collect, e.g. national health statistics, long-term climate records and genome databases.
[bio1-s3.4.1] They are also quick and cheap to obtain, and they allow results to be compared with others.
[bio1-s3.4.2] Limitations: they were collected for someone else’s purpose, so they may not exactly fit your question. You do not control how they were collected or how carefully.
[bio1-s3.4.3] Methods, definitions or units may be unclear or may change over time, which makes comparison unreliable. The data may be out of date, incomplete, or already processed by averaging or selection, and the collector may be biased.
[bio1-s3.4.4] Questions to ask of secondary data: who collected them and why; how, when and where they were collected; how big the sample was; whether the method is described; whether the data are raw or processed; and whether other sources give similar data.
[bio1-s3.5.b] Bias can enter a source in several ways, from who paid for it to which data were chosen. Knowing the named types lets you spot them in a real advert or article.
[bio1-s3.5.0] Funding or commercial bias: a source paid for by a company or individual that gains from a particular conclusion may favour that conclusion, e.g. research on sugary drinks funded by a drinks manufacturer, or a supplement company’s website reporting only its own favourable trials.
[bio1-s3.5.1] A conflict of interest is a situation in which a person’s financial or personal interests could influence their judgement. Reliable journals require authors to declare them, so readers can take them into account.
[bio1-s3.5.2] Selection bias, or cherry-picking: choosing only the data or studies that support a view and ignoring the rest, e.g. quoting one cold winter as evidence against climate warming.
[bio1-s3.5.3] Publication bias: studies with positive or exciting results are more likely to be published than those finding no effect, so the published literature can overstate how well a treatment works.
[bio1-s3.5.4] Confirmation bias: the tendency of authors, and of readers, to look for, interpret and remember information that confirms what they already believe.
[bio1-s3.5.5] Sampling bias: a sample that is not representative of the population, e.g. a diet survey carried out only among gym members, or a drug trialled only on young men and then recommended for everyone.
[bio1-s3.5.6] Political, ideological or cultural bias: a source from a campaign group, political party or lobby group presents the evidence that suits its cause. This can apply to groups on either side of an issue, e.g. both for and against genetically modified crops.
[bio1-s3.5.7] Sensationalism means exaggerating a result to attract readers or clicks.
[bio1-s3.5.8] Recognising bias: check who produced and who paid for the source; look for one-sided presentation, emotive language, no mention of limitations or opposing evidence, and claims not supported by references. A biased source can still contain accurate facts, so check them against independent sources rather than simply dismissing the source.
[bio1-s3.6.b] Some kinds of source are checked more carefully than others, so type is a useful first guide to reliability. It is only a guide, though, because each individual source still has to be judged.
[bio1-s3.6.0] Usually most reliable: peer-reviewed research papers and systematic reviews; reports of national and international scientific and public health bodies, e.g. the World Health Organization (WHO), the Health Service Executive (HSE, Ireland’s public health service) and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, Ireland); and up-to-date textbooks.
[bio1-s3.6.1] Usually least reliable: anonymous websites, social media posts, advertisements and personal blogs, because they are rarely checked by experts and may aim to sell or persuade.
[bio1-s3.6.2] Each source must still be judged on the criteria, not just on its type. A reliable body can make mistakes, and a blog can be accurate.

CANDIDATES bio2-s4: Producing and analysing data
[bio2-s4.0.b] Every investigation produces data, and the type of data decides how you can record, process and graph it. Biologists sort data by what it describes, how it behaves and who collected it.
[bio2-s4.0.0] Qualitative data are descriptions or categories that are not numerical, e.g. a positive Benedict’s test for a reducing sugar, which turns from blue to brick-red through green, yellow and orange; the presence or absence of a species; or “cloudy” or “clear”.
[bio2-s4.0.1] Quantitative data are numerical measurements or counts with units, e.g. volume of oxygen in cm³, mass in g, or number of daisies per quadrat (a square frame laid on the ground to sample a small area).
[bio2-s4.0.2] Quantitative data are more objective (less dependent on the observer’s judgement) and can be analysed mathematically, so they are preferred where possible. A qualitative result can often be made quantitative, e.g. a colorimeter (an instrument that measures how much light a solution absorbs) reading instead of a colour.
[bio2-s4.0.3] Continuous data can take any value in a range, including decimals, e.g. mass, temperature or time.
[bio2-s4.0.4] Discrete data are whole-number counts, because you cannot have half a seed, e.g. number of seeds or number of stomata (the pores in a leaf).
[bio2-s4.0.5] Categoric data are named groups, e.g. eye colour or type of habitat.
[bio2-s4.0.6] Primary data are collected by the investigator. Secondary data are collected by somebody else, e.g. published results, databases or other groups’ results.
[bio2-s4.0.7] Selecting data means choosing the data that are relevant to the question and of good enough quality. Secondary data can extend or check primary data, for example by adding values you could not measure yourself. There is more on judging a secondary source in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio2-s4.1.b] A results table is where raw data first go, so a clear layout makes the later calculations and graph easy. A few fixed conventions apply every time.
[bio2-s4.1.0] The independent variable (the factor you change) goes in the first column. The dependent variable (the factor you measure) goes in the following columns, with a column for each repeat and a column for the mean.
[bio2-s4.1.1] Each column heading gives the quantity and its unit, e.g. “Temperature (°C)” or “Volume of oxygen (cm³)”. Units go in the heading, not after every number, which keeps the table uncluttered.
[bio2-s4.1.2] Record every reading to the same number of decimal places, matching the resolution of the instrument (the smallest change it can show). A balance reading to 0.01 g gives 2.45 g, not 2.4 g or 2.453 g.
[bio2-s4.1.3] List the values of the independent variable in order, so the pattern can be seen down the column.
[bio2-s4.1.4] Record raw results straight into the table during the investigation, including any that look wrong. Writing them down later from memory risks errors, and a result that looks wrong may turn out to matter.
[bio2-s4.2.b] Raw readings are processed into a few summary numbers before they are graphed. The mean gives the typical value of repeats, and the range shows how closely the repeats agree. The worked numbers here are checked and illustrative.
[bio2-s4.2.0] Mean: add the values and divide by the number of values. Anomalous values (ones that do not fit the pattern) are left out of the mean, and the fact is stated.
[bio2-s4.2.1] Worked example for the mean: the final masses of four replicate (repeat) potato cylinders are 2.45 g, 2.44 g, 2.46 g and 2.10 g.
[bio2-s4.2.2] The value 2.10 g is anomalous, so the mean of the other three is (2.45 + 2.44 + 2.46) ÷ 3 = 7.35 ÷ 3 = 2.45 g.
[bio2-s4.2.3] Range: the largest value minus the smallest. It shows the spread of repeat readings, and a small range means the readings are precise (close to each other).
[bio2-s4.2.4] Worked example for the range: 2.46 − 2.44 = 0.02 g, a small spread, so the readings are precise.
[bio2-s4.2.5] The range of the independent variable is the lowest to highest value tested, e.g. 0 °C to 60 °C. It tells the reader which conditions the results cover.
[bio2-s4.3.b] Two further calculations let you compare results fairly. Percentage change deals with samples that started at different sizes, and rate deals with changes that took different times.
[bio2-s4.3.0] Percentage change = (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100. It allows samples that started with different masses to be compared, because each change is expressed relative to its own starting value.
[bio2-s4.3.1] Worked example for percentage change: a cylinder with an initial mass of 2.50 g and a final mass of 2.45 g has a percentage change of (2.45 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −2.0%.
[bio2-s4.3.2] A negative percentage change is a loss, and a positive value is a gain.
[bio2-s4.3.3] Rate = amount of change ÷ time. For example, 24 cm³ of oxygen in 4 minutes is a rate of 6 cm³ per minute.
[bio2-s4.3.4] When the time taken for something to happen is measured, e.g. the time for starch to disappear, the rate can be calculated as 1 ÷ time. A time of 50 s gives 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s⁻¹.
[bio2-s4.3.5] A shorter time means a faster rate, because 1 ÷ time gets larger as the time gets smaller.
[bio2-s4.4.b] Different data suit different graphs. The choice depends on whether the variables are continuous, discrete or categoric (see the previous block’s data types) and on what you want to show.
[bio2-s4.4.0] Line graph: used when both variables are continuous, e.g. rate of enzyme activity against temperature.
[bio2-s4.4.1] Bar chart: used when the independent variable is categoric or discrete, e.g. the number of a species in three habitats. Bars are separate and of equal width, because the categories are distinct groups.
[bio2-s4.4.2] Histogram: shows the frequency (how many) of a continuous variable divided into classes, e.g. the number of students in each height range. Bars touch, because the classes run on from one to the next.
[bio2-s4.4.3] Scatter graph: used to look for a correlation (a link) between two measured variables, e.g. light intensity and the number of a plant species in quadrats.
[bio2-s4.4.4] Pie chart: shows proportions of a whole, e.g. the percentage of each blood group in a class.
[bio2-s4.5.b] Once the graph type is chosen, marks come from drawing it carefully. Axes, scales, plotting and the line each follow a rule.
[bio2-s4.5.0] The independent variable goes on the horizontal (x) axis and the dependent variable on the vertical (y) axis. Label both with the quantity and unit.
[bio2-s4.5.1] Use even, linear scales that use more than half of the grid in each direction, so the graph is large enough to read. The scale need not start at zero if the data do not come near zero, but this must be shown clearly.
[bio2-s4.5.2] Plot points accurately as small crosses or circled dots, and plot the means rather than every repeat.
[bio2-s4.5.3] Draw a line or smooth curve of best fit that shows the trend, with points balanced on either side. Where no trend line can be assumed, join the points with straight ruled lines.
[bio2-s4.5.4] Do not force the line through an anomalous point, because one unreliable reading would distort the trend.
[bio2-s4.5.5] Give the graph a title in the form “the effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable]”.
[bio2-s4.5.6] Diagram: a line graph of rate of enzyme activity against temperature. Labels: axes with units, plotted points, smooth curve rising to a peak (optimum) then falling, one circled anomalous point off the curve.
[bio2-s4.6.b] A graph is only useful once you can say what it shows. Describing a trend, naming its key features and classifying the relationship between the variables are the first steps in analysing data.
[bio2-s4.6.0] Describe the trend in words and quote data, e.g. “the rate increased from 2 to 8 cm³ per minute as temperature rose from 10 °C to 40 °C, then fell to zero by 60 °C”. Using figures makes the description specific.
[bio2-s4.6.1] Name key features of a graph: a peak or optimum (the value giving the highest result), a plateau (levelling off), and the point where a line crosses zero.
[bio2-s4.6.2] A positive correlation means that as one variable increases, the other increases. A negative correlation means that as one increases, the other decreases. No correlation means there is no pattern.
[bio2-s4.6.3] A straight line through the origin shows that the variables are directly proportional, so doubling one doubles the other.
[bio2-s4.6.4] Diagram: three small scatter graphs side by side. Labels: positive correlation (points rise), negative correlation (points fall), no correlation (points scattered), with both axes marked.
[bio2-s4.7.b] A graph also lets you find values that were not measured and work out how fast something is changing. Each use has its own limits.
[bio2-s4.7.0] Interpolation is reading a value from the graph between plotted points. It is fairly reliable, because the line is supported by data on both sides.
[bio2-s4.7.1] Extrapolation is extending the line beyond the data range to predict a value. It is less reliable, because the pattern may not continue, e.g. an enzyme’s rate does not keep rising with temperature.
[bio2-s4.7.2] The gradient (steepness) of a line graph of amount against time is the rate. A steeper line means a faster rate.
[bio2-s4.7.3] A line levelling off means the reaction is slowing, e.g. as substrate (the substance the enzyme acts on) is used up.
[bio2-s4.8.b] Finding a pattern between two variables is not the same as finding out why. A correlation is a starting point, and several different explanations can lie behind it.
[bio2-s4.8.0] A correlation shows that two variables change together. It does not show that one causes the other.
[bio2-s4.8.1] Other explanations of a correlation are chance, especially with a small sample; a third variable (a confounding variable) that affects both; or a cause that runs the other way.
[bio2-s4.8.2] Illustrative example: people who carry cigarette lighters are more likely to develop lung cancer, but the lighter does not cause cancer. Smoking, the third variable, causes both, because smokers carry lighters and smoking causes lung cancer.
[bio2-s4.8.3] Evidence that one variable causes a change in another comes from a controlled experiment (a fair test with a control, where only the factor being tested changes), together with a biological mechanism that explains it.
[bio2-s4.8.4] Surveys and secondary data alone usually show correlation, because they cannot control other variables. There is more on the strategies in Questioning and predicting.
[bio2-s4.9.b] Real data rarely come out perfectly, and sometimes one result sits well away from the rest. You need to recognise these results and know exactly how to treat them.
[bio2-s4.9.0] An anomalous result (outlier) does not fit the pattern of the other results. It is far from the other repeats at the same value, or off the line of best fit on a graph.
[bio2-s4.9.1] Possible causes are a mistake in measuring or recording, an uncontrolled variable, faulty equipment, or an unusual biological sample.
[bio2-s4.9.2] Check the reading and the calculation, and repeat that measurement if possible. This shows whether it was a simple mistake.
[bio2-s4.9.3] Leave the anomalous result out of the mean and the line of best fit, but keep it in the results table and report it with a possible reason. Hiding it would be dishonest. There is more on this integrity in Designing and conducting investigations.
[bio2-s4.9.4] A result is not anomalous just because it disagrees with the hypothesis. If repeats confirm it, it may show the hypothesis is wrong or reveal something new.
[bio2-s4.10.b] The conclusion is where the data answer the question the investigation set out to test. A good conclusion states the finding, backs it with evidence, explains it and admits its limits.
[bio2-s4.10.0] A conclusion states the relationship found between the independent and dependent variables, e.g. “As sucrose concentration increased, the percentage change in mass of the potato cylinders decreased.”
[bio2-s4.10.1] A conclusion is justified with evidence: quote values or describe the trend in the graph, and say whether the data support or do not support the hypothesis. Never say that the data prove it.
[bio2-s4.10.2] A conclusion explains the result using biological knowledge, e.g. water left the cells by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane) into the more concentrated solutions.
[bio2-s4.10.3] A conclusion is limited to what was tested: the range of the independent variable, the organism or tissue used, and the conditions. It does not claim more than the data show.
[bio2-s4.10.4] Confidence in a conclusion depends on whether the test was fair and valid, the sample size, the number of repeats, the spread of the data, the number of anomalies, and whether the results are repeatable and reproducible. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s4.10.5] A conclusion is valid when it is supported by valid, reliable data.
