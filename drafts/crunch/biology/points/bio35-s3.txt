TARGET bio35-s3: Generating a DNA profile
[bio35-s3.0.b] A DNA profile is made by turning a tiny biological sample into a pattern of bands that can be compared between people. The process always follows the same five steps, and each step has its own reagents or equipment.
[bio35-s3.0.0] The steps are: 1. Collect a sample, because every cell with a nucleus carries the person's DNA.
[bio35-s3.0.1] 2. Extract the DNA, which means freeing it from the cells and separating it from everything else.
[bio35-s3.0.2] 3. Amplify (copy) the chosen repeat regions by PCR (the polymerase chain reaction), so there is enough DNA to detect.
[bio35-s3.0.3] 4. Separate the copies by size by gel electrophoresis.
[bio35-s3.0.4] 5. Make the bands visible and analyse (compare) the pattern.
[bio35-s3.0.5] In the earlier method, step 3 was replaced by cutting the DNA with restriction enzymes (enzymes that cut DNA at specific sequences). It is taught in the restriction enzymes block below.
[bio35-s3.0.6] Diagram: a flow diagram of the five steps, with the key reagent or equipment at each: sample (swab, blood) → extraction (detergent, salt, protease, cold ethanol) → PCR (primers, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, thermal cycler) → gel electrophoresis (agarose gel, wells, electric current) → stained bands compared.
[bio35-s3.1.b] DNA can be taken from any cell that has a nucleus, because the nucleus is where a human cell keeps its DNA. Extraction then frees the DNA from the cell in three steps, each aimed at removing one barrier.
[bio35-s3.1.0] Sources are any cells with a nucleus, e.g. white blood cells (mature red blood cells have no nucleus, so they contain no nuclear DNA), cheek cells from a mouth swab, skin cells, hair roots (not the cut hair shaft, which has no usable nucleus), saliva and semen.
[bio35-s3.1.1] Samples are collected with gloves and stored sealed and cold to avoid contamination (DNA from the collector or other sources getting in) and breakdown of the DNA.
[bio35-s3.1.2] Extraction, step 1 (lysis, meaning breaking open the cells): a detergent breaks down the phospholipid membranes (the cell membrane and the nuclear envelope), releasing the DNA.
[bio35-s3.1.3] Step 2: a protease enzyme digests proteins, including the histones (the proteins the DNA is wound around in a chromosome) bound to the DNA, leaving the DNA free.
[bio35-s3.1.4] Step 3: DNA is insoluble in cold ethanol, so it comes out of solution (precipitates) and can be collected. Salt helps the DNA strands clump together.
[bio35-s3.1.5] Forensic laboratories use the same principles (break cells, remove protein, purify DNA) with purified kits, because crime-scene samples are tiny and need careful handling.
[bio35-s3.2.b] The school version of extraction uses onion, a plant tissue, and everyday chemicals. The first five steps break the tissue down and protect the DNA from damage.
[bio35-s3.2.0] 1. Chop the onion finely, to break cell walls and give a large surface area.
[bio35-s3.2.1] 2. Add it to a beaker containing a solution of washing-up liquid (detergent) and table salt.
[bio35-s3.2.2] 3. Stand the beaker in a water bath at 60 °C for 15 minutes. The heat helps the detergent work and denatures enzymes that would break down the DNA; longer heating would start to break the DNA itself.
[bio35-s3.2.3] 4. Cool the beaker in an ice-water bath for 5 minutes, slowing any enzyme breakdown of the DNA.
[bio35-s3.2.4] 5. Blend in a food processor for no more than a few seconds, because longer blending would break the long DNA strands.
[bio35-s3.2.5] Kiwi or strawberry also work in place of onion.
[bio35-s3.3.b] The last four steps remove the debris, digest the proteins and then bring the DNA out of solution so it can be seen. Each reagent has one job.
[bio35-s3.3.0] 6. Filter through coffee filter paper (or several layers of muslin) to remove cell debris, collecting the liquid filtrate in a test tube.
[bio35-s3.3.1] 7. Add a few drops of protease to digest proteins.
[bio35-s3.3.2] 8. Pour ice-cold ethanol gently down the side of the tilted test tube so it forms a layer on top of the filtrate.
[bio35-s3.3.3] 9. DNA appears at the boundary as white, stringy threads that can be wound onto a glass rod.
[bio35-s3.3.4] In short: detergent breaks the membranes, salt clumps the DNA, protease removes proteins and cold ethanol precipitates the DNA.
[bio35-s3.3.5] Safety for this practical: ethanol is highly flammable (no flames nearby); take care with blender blades and hot water; wear safety glasses.
[bio35-s3.4.b] A crime-scene sample may hold only a few cells, far too little DNA to see on a gel. PCR solves this by copying a chosen stretch of DNA many times over.
[bio35-s3.4.0] PCR makes millions to billions of copies of a chosen short region of DNA (here, the STR regions, short repeated sequences that differ between people) from a tiny sample, e.g. a single hair root or a spot of blood, so that there is enough DNA to see on a gel.
[bio35-s3.4.1] PCR is DNA replication carried out in a tube. It copies only the chosen region, not the whole genome.
[bio35-s3.4.2] The reaction mixture contains the extracted DNA (the template, the DNA to be copied).
[bio35-s3.4.3] It also contains primers, which are short single-stranded pieces of DNA made to be complementary to the sequences on either side of the region to be copied. This is why only that region is copied: the primers decide which region.
[bio35-s3.4.4] DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides onto an existing strand, so a primer is needed to start each new strand.
[bio35-s3.4.5] It also contains a supply of the four DNA nucleotides (A, T, C, G), the building blocks of the new strands, a heat-stable DNA polymerase (Taq polymerase), and a buffer solution.
[bio35-s3.4.6] The tubes are placed in a thermal cycler, a machine that changes the temperature automatically.
[bio35-s3.5.b] PCR involves repeated heating to a very high temperature, which would destroy most enzymes. The solution was an enzyme from a heat-loving organism.
[bio35-s3.5.0] Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium found in hot springs (first found in Yellowstone National Park, USA).
[bio35-s3.5.1] Its structure holds its shape at high temperatures, so it is not denatured (permanently unfolded) at 95 °C and works best at about 72 °C.
[bio35-s3.5.2] A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first heating step, and fresh enzyme would have to be added every cycle.
[bio35-s3.5.3] This is why PCR can be run automatically in a thermal cycler: the same enzyme survives every cycle.
[bio35-s3.6.b] Each PCR cycle has three temperature steps in a fixed order: hot, cool, then warm. Each step does one job in copying the DNA.
[bio35-s3.6.0] Step 1, denaturation: heat to about 95 °C. The hydrogen bonds between the complementary bases break and the two strands of each DNA molecule separate. Heat does here what helicase (the enzyme that unwinds DNA) does in cells.
[bio35-s3.6.1] Step 2, annealing: cool to about 55 °C (usually between 50 and 65 °C, depending on the primers). The primers bind (anneal) by complementary base pairing to their matching sequences on each single strand.
[bio35-s3.6.2] Step 3, extension: heat to about 72 °C, the optimum temperature of Taq polymerase. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to the end of each primer, complementary to the template strand, building a new strand.
[bio35-s3.6.3] Each double-stranded region becomes two.
[bio35-s3.6.4] Diagram: the PCR cycle as three stages in a circle: DNA at 95 °C → separated strands; primers binding at 55 °C; extension at 72 °C → two copies; “cycle repeats, doubling the DNA each time” (1 → 2 → 4 → 8). Labels: template strand, primer, nucleotides, Taq polymerase, new strand, three temperatures.
[bio35-s3.7.b] Because every cycle doubles the DNA, the number of copies grows very quickly. This is what makes a tiny sample usable.
[bio35-s3.7.0] Each cycle takes a few minutes and doubles the number of copies of the target region, so after n cycles one molecule gives 2ⁿ copies.
[bio35-s3.7.1] 10 cycles give 1,024 copies (2¹⁰); 30 cycles give 2³⁰, about 1 billion (1,073,741,824).
[bio35-s3.7.2] A run is usually 25–35 cycles and takes a few hours.
[bio35-s3.7.3] Kary Mullis invented PCR in 1983 and shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1993.
[bio35-s3.7.4] Using Taq polymerase, an enzyme discovered in basic research on bacteria of hot springs, made PCR quick and automatic.
[bio35-s3.7.5] Other uses of PCR: diagnosing infections by detecting a pathogen’s DNA or RNA (e.g. PCR tests for COVID-19), detecting alleles that cause genetic diseases, and preparing DNA for sequencing (reading the order of bases).
[bio35-s3.8.b] Before PCR was used, DNA profiles were made by cutting the DNA rather than copying it. This is the method of Alec Jeffreys, who first developed DNA profiling.
[bio35-s3.8.0] In Alec Jeffreys’ original method, the extracted DNA was cut with restriction enzymes, which cut at specific recognition sequences on either side of the repeat regions.
[bio35-s3.8.1] Because people have different numbers of repeats between the cutting sites, the fragments differed in length between people, and these were separated by gel electrophoresis.
[bio35-s3.8.2] This needed much more DNA than PCR-based profiling, which has replaced it.
[bio35-s3.9.b] Gel electrophoresis separates DNA fragments by size using an electric current. Before the current is switched on, the gel, the buffer and the samples must be set up the right way round.
[bio35-s3.9.0] The gel is usually agarose, a jelly made from a polysaccharide extracted from seaweed; it is full of tiny pores that act like a sieve.
[bio35-s3.9.1] The gel is made with a row of small holes (wells) near one end and placed in a tank of buffer solution, which conducts electricity, with the wells at the negative electrode (cathode) end.
[bio35-s3.9.2] Each DNA sample is mixed with a loading dye, which is dense so the sample sinks into the well, and coloured so that the progress of the run can be seen.
[bio35-s3.9.3] Each sample is placed in its own well with a micropipette (a tool for measuring tiny volumes).
[bio35-s3.9.4] One well holds a DNA ladder (size marker): a mixture of fragments of known lengths, which lets you work out the size of the other fragments.
[bio35-s3.10.b] Once the current is on, the DNA moves and sorts itself by size. Because DNA is colourless, the bands then need to be made visible.
[bio35-s3.10.0] Running: when the current is switched on, the negatively charged DNA (its phosphate groups carry the charge) moves through the gel towards the positive electrode (anode).
[bio35-s3.10.1] Small fragments pass through the pores more easily and move faster, so after a set time fragments of different lengths are separated into bands, the smallest furthest from the wells.
[bio35-s3.10.2] The current is switched off before the loading dye reaches the end of the gel, so that no fragments run off the gel.
[bio35-s3.10.3] Making the bands visible: DNA is colourless, so the gel is stained. In schools a safe stain such as methylene blue is used, showing blue bands under white light.
[bio35-s3.10.4] Research laboratories use fluorescent stains that glow under ultraviolet light. The older stain ethidium bromide is a mutagen (it can cause mutations) and must be handled with gloves.
[bio35-s3.10.5] Modern forensic profiling uses primers labelled with fluorescent dyes and separates the fragments in a thin capillary tube in a machine, which reads the result as peaks.
[bio35-s3.10.6] Diagram: an electrophoresis tank, side and top views: gel in buffer, wells at the negative (black) end, positive (red) electrode at the other, power pack, ladder and sample lanes, arrow showing DNA direction. Labels: agarose gel, well, buffer solution, cathode (−), anode (+), DNA ladder, bands, “smaller fragments travel further”.
[bio35-s3.11.b] The final step compares the patterns of bands to decide whether DNA samples match.
[bio35-s3.11.0] The band positions (or peak sizes) of the samples are compared with each other and with the ladder, which gives the size of each fragment.
[bio35-s3.11.1] Matching at every locus (position on the DNA) indicates the same person.
[bio35-s3.11.2] Inheritance of one band per locus from each parent is used in paternity cases, because a child's DNA is a mix of the mother's and the father's.
[bio35-s3.11.3] There is more on this in Investigation: DNA-profile patterns.

CANDIDATES bio35-s2: DNA profiling
[bio35-s2.0.b] Every person's DNA is slightly different, and DNA profiling turns those differences into a pattern that can be compared between samples. It is used to link people to evidence, to each other or to a remains sample.
[bio35-s2.0.0] DNA profiling (also called DNA fingerprinting) is a method of making a pattern of bands (or peaks) from a sample of a person's DNA, so that the DNA of different samples can be compared.
[bio35-s2.0.1] Except for identical twins, the pattern is unique to each person, which is why it can identify an individual.
[bio35-s2.0.2] Identical twins come from one zygote (the single cell formed at fertilisation), so they have the same DNA and the same profile. Non-identical twins and other siblings do not share a profile.
[bio35-s2.0.3] How a profile is made, in outline: 1. DNA is extracted from cells. 2. Many copies of the repeat regions are made by PCR, so there is enough to detect (in older methods the DNA was cut with restriction enzymes).
[bio35-s2.0.4] 3. The fragments are separated by size by gel electrophoresis, so that different lengths form different bands. 4. The pattern is analysed and compared. There is more on each step in Generating a DNA profile (Higher Level).
[bio35-s2.1.b] A profile does not read a person's genes. It looks at stretches of DNA that do not code for protein, where short sequences are repeated, because the number of repeats differs so much from person to person.
[bio35-s2.1.0] DNA profiling is based on non-coding DNA (DNA that does not code for a protein): about 98% of human DNA is non-coding.
[bio35-s2.1.1] Parts of non-coding DNA contain short base sequences repeated many times one after another, for example short tandem repeats (STRs), a short sequence, often four bases (e.g. GATA), is repeated end to end, e.g. GATAGATAGATA…
[bio35-s2.1.2] The number of repeats at a particular place on a chromosome (a locus) varies greatly between people, so the length of that piece of DNA varies too. This length difference is what a profile detects.
[bio35-s2.1.3] Profiling is therefore not a test of a person's genes, their whole genome, their appearance or their health.
[bio35-s2.2.b] Because we inherit one of each chromosome pair from each parent, every person has two copies of each locus. This is what gives the pattern its structure and lets it be used for family relationships.
[bio35-s2.2.0] Each person has two copies of every locus, one on the chromosome inherited from the mother and one on the chromosome inherited from the father.
[bio35-s2.2.1] Each copy can have a different number of repeats (two alleles, giving two bands), or the same number (giving one band, twice as strong because both copies run to the same position).
[bio35-s2.2.2] So a child's profile at each locus has one band from the mother and one from the father. A child has half its bands from each parent, not most from the father.
[bio35-s2.3.b] One locus on its own would match many people by chance. Testing many loci together makes a full match very strong evidence.
[bio35-s2.3.0] A profile tests many loci at once; modern forensic profiles use more than ten.
[bio35-s2.3.1] The chance that two unrelated people match at every locus is extremely small, which is why a full match is strong evidence of identity.
[bio35-s2.3.2] The exception is identical twins, who share the same DNA and so the same profile at every locus.
[bio35-s2.4.b] In gel electrophoresis, DNA fragments are pulled through a gel by an electric field and sorted by size. Knowing which way they move and which sizes go furthest lets you read a profile.
[bio35-s2.4.0] On a gel, each band is a group of DNA fragments of the same length.
[bio35-s2.4.1] DNA is negatively charged (because of its phosphate groups), so in an electric field it moves through the gel towards the positive electrode, away from the wells (the small slots) where samples are loaded.
[bio35-s2.4.2] Shorter fragments (fewer repeats) move faster and travel further from the wells, because they pass through the gel more easily; longer fragments (more repeats) stay nearer the wells. So band position shows fragment length.
[bio35-s2.4.3] Comparing band positions with a ladder of fragments of known length (a size marker) lets lengths be read.
[bio35-s2.4.4] In modern laboratories the fragments are labelled with fluorescent dyes and separated by machine. The result is printed as a graph of peaks, each peak being one allele labelled with its number of repeats, rather than as bands. The rules for matching are the same.
[bio35-s2.4.5] Diagram: a gel with wells at the top (negative electrode) and the positive electrode at the bottom; lanes for a ladder, a crime-scene sample and three suspects; one suspect's bands match the crime-scene lane exactly. Label wells, lanes, bands, "shorter fragments travel further", + and − electrodes.
[bio35-s2.5.b] Once the bands are on the gel, you compare lanes. The rule for a match is strict: everything must line up.
[bio35-s2.5.0] Two samples match only if every band is in the same position.
[bio35-s2.5.1] A single band that differs, at any locus, shows that they did not come from the same person, because the same DNA would give identical bands; this is an exclusion.
[bio35-s2.5.2] Paternity uses the same logic: every band in a child's profile must come from the mother or the father, so a man whose profile lacks a band the child must have inherited from its father is excluded as the father.
[bio35-s2.6.b] DNA profiling is a recent method, and its first uses showed that it can both convict and clear people.
[bio35-s2.6.0] DNA profiling was invented by Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester, England, in 1984.
[bio35-s2.6.1] It was first used to convict a murderer, Colin Pitchfork, in 1988, which showed that it works as evidence in court.
[bio35-s2.6.2] The same investigation first cleared an innocent suspect, who had confessed to one of the killings but whose DNA did not match.
[bio35-s2.7.b] The two best-known uses compare a person's profile with other evidence or other people. In crime it links or excludes suspects, and in families it tests who is related to whom.
[bio35-s2.7.0] Forensic science (crime): DNA from blood, semen, saliva, skin cells or hair roots left at a crime scene is compared with a suspect's DNA (taken from a mouth swab).
[bio35-s2.7.1] A match links the suspect to the scene; a mismatch excludes them, because a single differing band shows the DNA came from someone else.
[bio35-s2.7.2] Profiles are stored in national DNA databases so that new crime-scene samples can be compared with them, which can identify a suspect.
[bio35-s2.7.3] Old crime-scene samples can be retested, solving "cold cases" (unsolved old crimes) and freeing people wrongly convicted.
[bio35-s2.7.4] Paternity and other family relationships: DNA profiling is also used to establish maternity, to reunite separated families, and in immigration cases where family relationships must be proven.
[bio35-s2.8.b] When a body is unrecognisable or old, a profile can still identify it by comparing with relatives or personal items. When nuclear DNA is not usable, other DNA can trace ancestry.
[bio35-s2.8.0] Identifying human remains, e.g. victims of disasters, wars and accidents, or missing persons, by comparing their DNA with that of relatives or with DNA from their personal items (e.g. a toothbrush), which carry the person's own DNA.
[bio35-s2.8.1] Mitochondrial DNA and Y-chromosome profiles trace family lines when nuclear DNA is too damaged or no close relative is available. Mitochondrial DNA is passed down the female line and the Y chromosome down the male line, so each follows one line of ancestors.
[bio35-s2.8.2] Example: the skeleton found under a car park in Leicester in 2012 was confirmed in 2013 to be King Richard III, partly by matching its mitochondrial DNA to living descendants of his sister's female line.
[bio35-s2.8.3] There is more on these two kinds of inheritance in Nuclear and non-nuclear inheritance.
[bio35-s2.9.b] DNA profiling is not only for people. It is also applied to wildlife, farm and breeding animals, food and medicine.
[bio35-s2.9.0] Animals, plants and conservation: identifying illegally traded wildlife products (e.g. ivory, meat from protected species), and checking the pedigree (parentage) of animals used for breeding.
[bio35-s2.9.1] It also helps keep breeding programmes for endangered species from inbreeding (mating between close relatives).
[bio35-s2.9.2] Food: DNA tests identify the species in food, so they show fraud when the food is not what the label says. Species tests check for a species' DNA, not an individual's profile.
[bio35-s2.9.3] Example: in January 2013 the Food Safety Authority of Ireland found horse DNA in beef burgers on sale in Ireland, which revealed a Europe-wide horsemeat scandal.
[bio35-s2.9.4] Medicine: DNA-based tests also help match donors and patients for bone-marrow transplants, and identify the strain of a disease-causing microorganism in an outbreak.
[bio35-s2.10.b] The two are easily confused because both use DNA. They answer different questions.
[bio35-s2.10.0] DNA profiling compares band patterns of mostly non-coding DNA to identify a person or a relationship; it tells nothing about health.
[bio35-s2.10.1] Genetic screening (DNA testing) looks at particular genes to find whether a person has, or carries, an allele linked to a disease. There is more on this in Bioinformatics: genome databases and, for screening of newborns, in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio35-s2.11.b] DNA evidence is powerful but not perfect. Problems can arise before, during and after the test.
[bio35-s2.11.0] Contamination of a sample with another person's DNA (e.g. by investigators) can give a false link, so samples must be collected with gloves, masks and sealed bags.
[bio35-s2.11.1] DNA can be degraded by heat, moisture, bacteria or time, giving incomplete profiles that are harder to interpret.
[bio35-s2.11.2] A match shows that the DNA came from that person (or an identical twin), not when or how it got there; DNA can be transferred innocently, e.g. by touch or on shared objects.
[bio35-s2.11.3] So DNA evidence must be weighed with other evidence; it does not prove guilt alone.
[bio35-s2.11.4] Mistakes in labelling or interpretation, and the human judgement needed for mixed samples from several people, can lead to errors, for example if a sample is attributed to the wrong person.

CANDIDATES bio13-s2: Enzyme structure and specificity
[bio13-s2.0.b] An enzyme is a protein, and the way its chain is folded decides what it can do. Folding builds a small pocket called the active site (the region where the substrate, the molecule the enzyme acts on, binds and is converted).
[bio13-s2.0.0] An enzyme's three-dimensional shape is set by the sequence of amino acids in its polypeptide chain (its primary structure), and that sequence is set by its gene, so the gene ultimately decides the shape.
[bio13-s2.0.1] The chain coils and folds (secondary structure), then folds further into a precise globular (rounded) shape (tertiary structure); some enzymes are made of more than one polypeptide (quaternary structure).
[bio13-s2.0.2] The active site is formed by folding: it is made of a small number of amino acids, often far apart in the chain, that folding brings together into a pocket or cleft.
[bio13-s2.0.3] Most of the rest of the enzyme holds these few active-site amino acids in exactly the right positions, so the whole molecule matters even though only a small part touches the substrate.
[bio13-s2.0.4] The R groups (side groups, the part of each amino acid that differs from the others) of the active-site amino acids give the site its shape and its pattern of charges and of water-attracting and water-repelling regions.
[bio13-s2.0.5] The R groups bind the substrate by weak attractions (hydrogen bonds and ionic attractions between opposite charges), and some take part directly in the reaction.
[bio13-s2.1.b] An enzyme acts only on particular substrates, a property called specificity. It comes from the active site's shape and chemistry, and that shape is only as stable as the bonds that hold the protein folded.
[bio13-s2.1.0] Specificity depends on two things: the shape of the active site (only a substrate of complementary shape fits) and its chemistry (only a substrate with matching charges and groups is held in the right position).
[bio13-s2.1.1] A molecule of similar shape but with the wrong charges is not catalysed, because its groups are not held in the correct position.
[bio13-s2.1.2] The shape is held by hydrogen bonds, by ionic bonds between charged R groups, and by attractions between water-repelling R groups; these are all weak bonds.
[bio13-s2.1.3] Disulfide (sulfur) bridges between cysteine units (cysteine is an amino acid that contains sulfur) are strong covalent bonds that also hold the shape.
[bio13-s2.1.4] Because most of these bonds are weak, the shape is easily changed, which is why enzymes are sensitive to conditions.
[bio13-s2.1.5] Diagram: a folded polypeptide chain drawn as a looping ribbon, with three or four amino acids (circles) from distant parts of the chain meeting to form a labelled active site; label polypeptide chain, R groups of active-site amino acids, active site, a hydrogen bond and a disulfide bridge.
[bio13-s2.2.b] An enzyme works only while its active site keeps its exact shape. Anything that alters the shape of the protein, or the amino acids that make it, changes how well it works.
[bio13-s2.2.0] Activity depends on the active site keeping its shape.
[bio13-s2.2.1] Heat makes the enzyme molecule vibrate until weak bonds break; a change in pH changes the charges on R groups (by adding or removing hydrogen ions), breaking ionic and hydrogen bonds.
[bio13-s2.2.2] Either way the active site loses its shape, the substrate no longer fits, and the enzyme is denatured (permanently changed in shape and unable to work). The effects on rate, and the investigations, are in “Investigation: rate of enzyme activity”.
[bio13-s2.2.3] A change in a single amino acid can change the shape or charge of the active site, so a mutation in the gene can make an enzyme work less well or not at all.
[bio13-s2.2.4] Example: in phenylketonuria the enzyme that converts phenylalanine is faulty, so the conversion does not happen properly.
[bio13-s2.2.5] Some enzymes need a cofactor, such as a metal ion, held in or near the active site to complete its shape or chemistry; without it the enzyme is inactive.
[bio13-s2.3.b] Enzymes differ in how narrowly they choose a substrate. Some enzymes also have structures that keep their shape in conditions that would ruin most others.
[bio13-s2.3.0] Some enzymes act on only one substrate (absolute specificity): for example, urease acts only on urea, breaking it into ammonia and carbon dioxide.
[bio13-s2.3.1] Others act on a group of substrates that share the same type of bond (group specificity): for example, lipase acts on many different triglycerides, and proteases such as pepsin break peptide bonds in many different proteins.
[bio13-s2.3.2] A rigid lock-and-key active site does not explain group specificity well, because one fixed shape cannot fit many different substrates; the induced-fit model does.
[bio13-s2.3.3] Enzymes that work in extreme conditions have structures that hold their shape there: for example, Taq polymerase, from the hot-spring bacterium Thermus aquaticus, stays active at temperatures that denature human enzymes.
[bio13-s2.3.4] Pepsin keeps its shape in the strong acid of the stomach, where most enzymes are denatured.
[bio13-s2.4.b] The induced-fit model describes an active site that is flexible, not a fixed mould. It explains how an enzyme grips its substrate, strains it and releases the products, then is ready to go again.
[bio13-s2.4.0] The induced-fit model says the active site is flexible, not rigid. Before binding, its shape is close to, but not exactly, complementary to the substrate; binding of the substrate induces (causes) a change in the enzyme's shape that produces a close fit.
[bio13-s2.4.1] 1. The substrate collides with the enzyme and starts to bind to the active site through weak attractions to its R groups.
[bio13-s2.4.2] 2. Binding causes the enzyme to change shape: the active site moulds itself around the substrate, like a glove taking the shape of a hand, forming a tightly fitting enzyme–substrate complex.
[bio13-s2.4.3] 3. The change in shape puts strain on particular bonds in the substrate, or brings reacting groups of two substrates close together in the correct orientation.
[bio13-s2.4.4] 3 (continued). The R groups of the active site may lend or take hydrogen ions or electrons, which lowers the activation energy (the energy needed to start the reaction), and the substrate is converted to products.
[bio13-s2.4.5] 4. The products no longer fit the active site closely, so they are released.
[bio13-s2.4.6] 5. The enzyme returns to its original shape, unchanged and ready to bind another substrate molecule.
[bio13-s2.5.b] The earlier lock-and-key model treated the active site as rigid. Induced fit replaced it because it explains observations that a rigid site cannot.
[bio13-s2.5.0] Example: the enzyme hexokinase (used in the first stage of respiration) closes around its substrate glucose like a clamp when glucose binds; structural studies of enzymes showed this change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.1] Induced fit explains why some enzymes act on several similar substrates: a flexible site can mould around each of them.
[bio13-s2.5.2] Induced fit explains how binding itself helps catalysis: the shape change strains the substrate's bonds, lowering the activation energy.
[bio13-s2.5.3] Induced fit explains why molecules of similar shape may bind but not react: they do not induce the correct change of shape.
[bio13-s2.5.4] Lock and key remains a useful simple model of specificity.
[bio13-s2.5.5] Diagram: induced fit in four labelled stages: (1) an enzyme whose active site does not quite match the approaching substrate; (2) substrate binding and the active site changing shape around it, forming the enzyme–substrate complex, with arrows showing the change.
[bio13-s2.5.6] Diagram (continued): (3) products formed in the active site; (4) products released and the enzyme back in its original shape. Label enzyme, active site, substrate, change in shape, enzyme–substrate complex and products.
[bio13-s2.5.7] Diagram (contrast): include a one-line sketch of lock and key, with a rigid active site that already fits the substrate exactly.
[bio13-s2.6.b] The two models differ in whether the active site can move. Setting them side by side shows why induced fit explains more.
[bio13-s2.6.0] Active site: rigid, with a fixed shape that exactly matches the substrate (lock and key); flexible, changing shape when the substrate binds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.1] Fit: an exact fit before binding (lock and key); a close fit only after binding (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.2] Role of binding: it holds the substrate only (lock and key); the change of shape itself helps the reaction by straining bonds (induced fit).
[bio13-s2.6.3] Specificity explained: absolute specificity only (lock and key); both absolute and group specificity (induced fit), because a flexible site can mould around several similar substrates.

CANDIDATES bio17-s1: Modelling DNA replication and information flow
[bio17-s1.0.b] Before a cell divides it must copy all of its DNA, a process called replication, so that each new cell gets a complete set of genetic instructions. Knowing where, when and how fast this happens is the starting point for the model.
[bio17-s1.0.0] Replication takes place in the nucleus (the compartment that holds the DNA) during the S phase of interphase, the stage of the cell cycle between divisions when the cell copies its DNA. There is more on this in The cell cycle.
[bio17-s1.0.1] In eukaryotes (cells with a nucleus) replication begins at many points along each chromosome at once, so the very long DNA molecules are copied quickly.
[bio17-s1.0.2] Bacteria start replication at one point on their circular chromosome, because their DNA is a single, much smaller loop.
[bio17-s1.0.3] Replication needs energy, which is supplied by ATP (the cell's energy-carrying molecule) and by the nucleotides themselves as they are added.
[bio17-s1.1.b] Replication uses each old strand of DNA as a pattern for a new one, and two key enzymes (proteins that speed up a chemical reaction) do the main jobs. Learn the stages in order and name the enzyme that does each job.
[bio17-s1.1.0] 1. The enzyme DNA helicase unwinds the double helix and separates the two strands by breaking the hydrogen bonds (the weak links) between complementary bases. This opens a Y-shaped replication fork where copying happens.
[bio17-s1.1.1] 2. Each exposed strand acts as a template, a pattern to copy. Free DNA nucleotides, which are made in the cell and are present in the nucleus, pair with the exposed bases by complementary base pairing (A–T, C–G).
[bio17-s1.1.2] 3. The enzyme DNA polymerase joins each new nucleotide to the growing strand, forming the sugar–phosphate backbone of the new strand. It adds nucleotides only where they pair correctly with the template, so the copy matches the original.
[bio17-s1.1.3] 4. DNA polymerase also proofreads: it checks each new base pair and removes and replaces most wrongly paired nucleotides. Other repair enzymes correct many of the rest.
[bio17-s1.1.4] A mistake that is not corrected becomes a permanent change in the base sequence, a point mutation (a change to one base in the DNA). There is more on this in Mutations.
[bio17-s1.1.5] 5. Each new DNA molecule, made of one template strand plus its new complementary strand, twists into a double helix. The two new molecules are identical to each other and to the original.
[bio17-s1.1.6] The two new molecules remain joined as two sister chromatids (identical copies of a chromosome) at a centromere, the narrow point where they are held together, until they separate at anaphase of mitosis.
[bio17-s1.1.7] Diagram: a replication fork, labelled: parent (original) DNA double helix, DNA helicase at the fork, hydrogen bonds being broken, the two template strands, free nucleotides, DNA polymerase adding nucleotides, the new complementary strands, and the two daughter molecules, each with one old strand (dark) and one new strand (light).
[bio17-s1.2.b] Once the base-pairing structure of DNA was known, there was more than one way the copying could work. Scientists had to decide between three models, and the one that proved correct is called semi-conservative.
[bio17-s1.2.0] Semi-conservative replication: each new DNA molecule contains one strand from the original molecule (conserved, meaning kept) and one newly made strand. This is why it is called half-conserving.
[bio17-s1.2.1] Watson and Crick predicted this in 1953 from the base-pairing model, because each strand can act as a template for its partner. There is more on this in Models.
[bio17-s1.2.2] Conservative replication: the original double helix stays intact, and a completely new molecule is made.
[bio17-s1.2.3] Dispersive replication: each new molecule is a patchwork of old and new pieces in both strands, so old and new DNA are mixed along each strand.
[bio17-s1.3.b] In 1958 Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl tested the three models by labelling DNA so that old and new strands could be told apart by their weight. Learn the method and the results, because you may be asked to interpret them.
[bio17-s1.3.0] 1. They grew the bacterium E. coli for many generations in a medium whose only nitrogen was the heavy isotope ¹⁵N (a heavier form of nitrogen), so all its DNA was “heavy”.
[bio17-s1.3.1] 2. They moved the bacteria to a medium containing only normal, light ¹⁴N, so any new DNA strand would be built from light nitrogen.
[bio17-s1.3.2] 3. After each generation they extracted the DNA and spun it at high speed in a caesium chloride solution, which forms a density gradient, so DNA settles as a band at a level that matches its density.
[bio17-s1.3.3] Before the transfer there is one heavy band, because all the DNA is made with ¹⁵N.
[bio17-s1.3.4] After one generation there is one band of intermediate density, so conservative replication, which would give one heavy and one light band, is ruled out.
[bio17-s1.3.5] After two generations there are one intermediate band and one light band, of equal size. This rules out dispersive replication, which would give a single band at every generation, becoming lighter each time.
[bio17-s1.3.6] Only the semi-conservative model fits both results.
[bio17-s1.3.7] Diagram: three centrifuge tubes side by side (generation 0, 1, 2) showing band positions heavy; intermediate; intermediate and light; with a strand diagram under each showing heavy strands dark and light strands pale.
[bio17-s1.4.b] The semi-conservative model is precise enough to make exact predictions about base sequences and numbers of nucleotides. Because A always pairs with T and C with G, you can work these out step by step.
[bio17-s1.4.0] Predicting daughter molecules: given a parent molecule with strands ATGCCA and TACGGT, the strands separate.
[bio17-s1.4.1] ATGCCA is the template for a new TACGGT, and TACGGT is the template for a new ATGCCA, because each base can only pair with its partner.
[bio17-s1.4.2] The two daughter molecules are identical to the parent, each with one old strand and one new strand.
[bio17-s1.4.3] Counting nucleotides: copying a DNA molecule of 1,000 base pairs makes two new strands of 1,000 nucleotides each, so 2,000 free nucleotides are used.
[bio17-s1.4.4] If 30% of the parent's bases are adenine, then 30% of the free nucleotides used are A and 30% are T.
[bio17-s1.4.5] The remaining free nucleotides are 20% C and 20% G, because A = T and C = G in double-stranded DNA. There is more on this in Nucleotides, DNA and RNA.
[bio17-s1.5.b] Two more skills use the model: working out how many molecules exist after several rounds of replication, and building a hands-on model to show how copying works. A good model also has limits that you should be able to name.
[bio17-s1.5.0] Generations: one DNA molecule replicated three times gives 2³ = 8 molecules, because the number of molecules doubles each time.
[bio17-s1.5.1] In a Meselson–Stahl-type experiment only 2 of these 8 still contain an original heavy strand, because each original strand is kept whole and never broken up.
[bio17-s1.5.2] Physical model: build a short double strand from paper or plastic nucleotide pieces in four colours, one per base, that fit only their correct partners.
[bio17-s1.5.3] Separate the strands, add free pieces to each, and compare the two products with the original. Use two shades for old and new pieces to show semi-conservation.
[bio17-s1.5.4] Strength of the physical model: it shows templates, base pairing and identical products.
[bio17-s1.5.5] Limitations of the physical model: it is flat and short, and does not show the helix, the enzymes, the energy needed, or the speed and accuracy of copying in cells. There is more on this in Models.
[bio17-s1.6.b] A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for one protein. The instructions are not used directly: they are first copied into a molecule of RNA, and that copy is then used to build the protein.
[bio17-s1.6.0] Genetic information flows from DNA to mRNA to protein: DNA → transcription → mRNA → translation → protein. Replication, by contrast, copies DNA to DNA.
[bio17-s1.6.1] Francis Crick called this one-way flow from nucleic acid to protein the “central dogma” of molecular biology.
[bio17-s1.6.2] Transcription: in the nucleus, the base sequence of one gene is copied into a molecule of messenger RNA (mRNA), using one DNA strand as the template.
[bio17-s1.6.3] The mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore (a small channel in the nuclear envelope) and travels to a ribosome in the cytoplasm, either free or attached to rough endoplasmic reticulum. The DNA stays in the nucleus.
[bio17-s1.6.4] Translation: at the ribosome (the structure that builds proteins), the mRNA's codons (groups of three bases) are read, and transfer RNA molecules bring the matching amino acids.
[bio17-s1.6.5] The amino acids are joined in the order set by the codons to make a specific polypeptide (a chain of amino acids), which folds into a functional protein. There is more on this in Transcription and translation.
[bio17-s1.6.6] Diagram: a eukaryotic cell showing a gene on DNA in the nucleus → transcription → mRNA passing through a nuclear pore → ribosome in the cytoplasm → translation → polypeptide folding into a protein. Label: nucleus, DNA, gene, mRNA, nuclear pore, ribosome, tRNA bringing amino acid, polypeptide.
[bio17-s1.7.b] The ribosome works from an mRNA copy of a gene rather than from the DNA itself, for three reasons. There is also one well-known exception to the usual direction of flow.
[bio17-s1.7.0] The DNA is kept safe in the nucleus and is not damaged by being used directly.
[bio17-s1.7.1] Many mRNA copies can be made from one gene, and each is read by many ribosomes, so a lot of protein can be made quickly.
[bio17-s1.7.2] mRNA is broken down after use, so the cell can switch protein production on and off.
[bio17-s1.7.3] Exception: retroviruses such as HIV carry RNA and use the enzyme reverse transcriptase to make DNA from their RNA, so information flows from RNA to DNA. There is more on this in Viruses: replication.
[bio17-s1.7.4] Information never flows from protein back to nucleic acid.
