TARGET bio35-s5: Bioinformatics: genome databases
[bio35-s5.0.b] Modern genetics produces far more data than anyone can read. Bioinformatics is the answer: using computers to handle biological information, and genome databases are where that information is kept.
[bio35-s5.0.0] Bioinformatics is the use of computers and software to store, search, compare and analyse biological information, especially DNA, RNA and protein sequences.
[bio35-s5.0.1] A genome database is an online store of genome sequences and information about genes, their variants (alleles, the different versions of a gene) and their effects, which anyone can search.
[bio35-s5.0.2] Computers are essential because one human genome is about 3 billion base pairs, and sequencing machines (which read out the order of bases in DNA) produce data on many genomes quickly. No person could read or compare this by eye.
[bio35-s5.0.3] Computers compare sequences in seconds and store them permanently, so nothing is lost between studies.
[bio35-s5.0.4] Computers also let scientists anywhere share sequences, much of it freely, as the Human Genome Project did.
[bio35-s5.1.b] Once sequences are stored, software can look for similarities between them. These patterns tell scientists how species are related and what unknown genes do.
[bio35-s5.1.0] Software lines up (aligns) sequences so that matching stretches sit side by side, which finds similarities such as genes shared by different species.
[bio35-s5.1.1] Shared genes show evolutionary relationships, because species that share more of a gene's sequence are more closely related.
[bio35-s5.1.2] Shared genes also help work out what a newly found gene does, since a gene similar to a known gene in another species often has a similar job.
[bio35-s5.1.3] The NCBI tool BLAST takes a sequence typed or pasted in and finds the most similar sequences in the database, for example to identify the species or gene a DNA sample came from.
[bio35-s5.2.b] Computers can also look for differences. Comparing one person's DNA with a standard shows exactly where it departs from the norm, and the database shows whether that difference matters.
[bio35-s5.2.0] A person's sequence is compared with a reference genome (a standard sequence used as the point of comparison), and the computer lists every difference, called a variant.
[bio35-s5.2.1] Most variants are harmless. The database shows which variants have been recorded in people with a disease, so a disease-causing allele can be recognised.
[bio35-s5.2.2] Worked example: reference beta-globin gene … CCT GAG GAG …; patient … CCT GTG GAG …. The computer flags one substitution, A to T in the codon GAG → GTG, which is the sickle-cell allele.
[bio35-s5.2.3] In the sickle-cell allele, glutamic acid is replaced by valine in the beta-globin protein.
[bio35-s5.2.4] Databases show the DNA strand with the same sequence as the mRNA, so GTG here matches GUG in the mRNA, the copy of the gene that is read to build the protein.
[bio35-s5.3.b] Searching large amounts of genetic data is useful beyond the lab. It changes how diseases are diagnosed, treated and tracked.
[bio35-s5.3.0] Rare genetic diseases can be diagnosed quickly by comparing a patient's genome with databases, instead of testing one gene after another.
[bio35-s5.3.1] Carrier testing and genetic counselling use the databases to identify people who carry a disease-causing allele without having the disease.
[bio35-s5.3.2] Finding which genes are mutated in a tumour helps doctors choose a treatment.
[bio35-s5.3.3] Databases help track the spread and evolution of pathogens (disease-causing organisms).
[bio35-s5.3.4] They also help find genes that raise the risk of common diseases.
[bio35-s5.4.b] Three databases are used in this subsection. Each was built for a different purpose, so it helps to know what each one holds.
[bio35-s5.4.0] OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man, omim.org) is a catalogue of human genes and genetic disorders and the relationships between them. It is written and updated by scientists at Johns Hopkins University in the USA.
[bio35-s5.4.1] OMIM was begun by the geneticist Victor McKusick. Each entry has a six-digit MIM number, and searching is free on the website.
[bio35-s5.4.2] NCBI (the National Center for Biotechnology Information, ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) is part of the US National Library of Medicine.
[bio35-s5.4.3] NCBI hosts many linked databases: GenBank (DNA sequences), Gene (one record per gene, with its location and a summary of its function), ClinVar (variants found in patients and their classification), and the BLAST tool.
[bio35-s5.4.4] A search box at the top of the NCBI home page searches all of them, or one chosen database.
[bio35-s5.4.5] Ensembl (ensembl.org) is a genome browser, a site that displays genomes as maps. It is run by the European Bioinformatics Institute (EMBL-EBI) near Cambridge, UK.
[bio35-s5.4.6] Ensembl shows the genomes of humans and many other animals as maps on which genes, their structure and known variants can be viewed and compared between species.
[bio35-s5.5.b] Aim: to use a genome database to find the gene and the alleles responsible for two named genetic diseases, cystic fibrosis and Huntington's disease, and record the gene, its chromosome location, the pattern of inheritance and the disease-causing allele. The first steps find the disease entry.
[bio35-s5.5.0] 1. Go to omim.org and type the disease name (for example "cystic fibrosis") in the search box.
[bio35-s5.5.1] 2. The results list entries, each with a MIM number and a title. A symbol before the number shows the type of entry: # means a description of a disease or other phenotype (an observable trait) whose molecular basis, the gene, is known; * means a gene.
[bio35-s5.5.2] 3. Open the disease entry, the # entry. Cystic fibrosis is #219700 and Huntington's disease is #143100.
[bio35-s5.5.3] 4. Near the top of the disease entry, a table of phenotype–gene relationships gives the chromosome location (cytogenetic band), the phenotype, and the pattern of inheritance, for example "AR" for autosomal recessive or "AD" for autosomal dominant.
[bio35-s5.5.4] The same table names the gene responsible, with a link to the gene's own (*) entry.
[bio35-s5.6.b] With the disease entry found, the remaining steps pull out the alleles, record them and check them. The last point shows how to read a chromosome location.
[bio35-s5.6.0] 5. Read the text sections of the entry (for example clinical features, inheritance, molecular genetics) for a description of the disease and of the mutations that cause it.
[bio35-s5.6.1] 6. Follow the link to the gene entry. Its section on allelic variants lists selected disease-causing alleles, each with a description of the change in the DNA and the protein.
[bio35-s5.6.2] 7. Record the findings in a table with these columns: disease; gene; chromosome location; inheritance; disease-causing allele; protein affected.
[bio35-s5.6.3] 8. Repeat for the second disease.
[bio35-s5.6.4] 9. Check one result in a second database, for example by searching the gene name in NCBI Gene or Ensembl, to confirm the location. This is a check of reliability, because two independent sources agreeing makes the result more trustworthy.
[bio35-s5.6.5] Reading a chromosome location: 7q31.2 means chromosome 7, the long arm (q), band 31.2; 4p16.3 means chromosome 4, the short arm (p), band 16.3.
[bio35-s5.7.b] The results table has one row per disease, giving the gene, location, inheritance and disease-causing allele. Written out, it looks like this.
[bio35-s5.7.0] Cystic fibrosis: gene CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator), chromosome 7 (7q31.2); autosomal recessive.
[bio35-s5.7.1] The commonest cystic fibrosis allele is F508del, a deletion of three bases that removes one phenylalanine from the CFTR protein. Many hundreds of other disease-causing alleles are listed.
[bio35-s5.7.2] The protein affected in cystic fibrosis is CFTR, a channel that moves chloride ions across cell membranes.
[bio35-s5.7.3] Huntington's disease: gene HTT (huntingtin), chromosome 4 (4p16.3); autosomal dominant.
[bio35-s5.7.4] The Huntington's allele has too many repeats of the three bases CAG near the start of the gene: 40 or more repeats cause the disease, 36 to 39 may or may not, and unaffected people usually have 26 or fewer.
[bio35-s5.7.5] Each CAG codes for the amino acid glutamine, so the huntingtin protein carries an abnormally long chain of glutamines, which damages nerve cells in the brain.
[bio35-s5.7.6] Third example (optional, may be given as a row in the table): sickle-cell anaemia; gene HBB (beta-globin), chromosome 11 (11p15.4); autosomal recessive.
[bio35-s5.7.7] The sickle-cell allele is one base substitution changing glutamic acid to valine in beta-globin (haemoglobin S).
[bio35-s5.8.b] OMIM is one source. NCBI and Ensembl let you look up the same gene again, which is how you check a result.
[bio35-s5.8.0] NCBI: choose the Gene database, search the gene name with "human" (for example "CFTR human"), and open the human gene record.
[bio35-s5.8.1] The NCBI gene record gives the official name, the chromosome location, a summary of what the gene does, and links to OMIM and ClinVar.
[bio35-s5.8.2] In ClinVar, a search for the gene lists the variants reported in patients, each with a classification: pathogenic, likely pathogenic, uncertain significance, likely benign or benign.
[bio35-s5.8.3] A disease-causing allele is one classified as pathogenic, which is why not every variant in the database counts as one.
[bio35-s5.8.4] Ensembl: choose the human genome, search for the gene name, and open the gene page.
[bio35-s5.8.5] The Ensembl gene page shows the gene's position on the chromosome, its coding and non-coding sections, and a table of known variants with their consequences.
[bio35-s5.8.6] The same gene can be viewed in other species to see how similar it is.
[bio35-s5.9.b] The results show what databases do well. They also show why a database result is only part of the picture.
[bio35-s5.9.0] Each disease is caused by alleles of a single gene at a known chromosome location.
[bio35-s5.9.1] Cystic fibrosis is recessive, so a person needs two disease-causing alleles, while Huntington's disease is dominant, so one is enough.
[bio35-s5.9.2] Databases record many different disease-causing alleles of the same gene.
[bio35-s5.9.3] Many variants are of "uncertain significance", meaning it is not yet known whether they cause disease.
[bio35-s5.9.4] Most common diseases, such as heart disease and type 2 diabetes, involve many genes and the environment, so no single allele "causes" them.
[bio35-s5.9.5] Databases contain far more data from people of European ancestry than from other populations, so variants in other groups are less well understood.
[bio35-s5.9.6] Databases are for research and professionals, so a result should be interpreted with a doctor or genetic counsellor. There is more on the privacy of genetic data in Ethics of genetic technologies.

CANDIDATES bio35-s4: DNA sequencing
[bio35-s4.0.b] DNA is a long chain of four bases, and the order of those bases carries the genetic information. Sequencing is the technique that reads that order directly.
[bio35-s4.0.0] DNA sequencing is working out the exact order of the bases (A, T, C, G) along a piece of DNA, so the result is a string of letters such as TACCGTAG.
[bio35-s4.0.1] DNA profiling only compares fragment lengths, giving a banding pattern; sequencing reads every base, so it gives far more detailed information.
[bio35-s4.0.2] The human genome (all of a person's DNA) contains about 3 billion base pairs and about 20,000 protein-coding genes, which shows how large a job reading it is.
[bio35-s4.0.3] The Human Genome Project (1990–2003) produced the first reference sequence, a standard human sequence that others could be compared with. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio35-s4.0.4] The first complete, gap-free human sequence was published in 2022. It included the repeated regions that had been impossible to read before.
[bio35-s4.1.b] The standard method copies the DNA many times and stops each copy at a different point, so the copies form a ladder of fragments. Reading the ladder in order gives the sequence. This is the chain-termination method, and the steps below run from preparation to reading.
[bio35-s4.1.0] The method was developed by Frederick Sanger in 1977, and is called chain-termination or Sanger sequencing.
[bio35-s4.1.1] Sanger won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry twice: in 1958 for working out the amino-acid sequence of insulin, and in 1980 (shared) for DNA sequencing.
[bio35-s4.1.2] 1. Many copies of the DNA to be sequenced are made (e.g. by PCR, which copies DNA in a machine) and separated into single strands. Each single strand is the template, the strand that will be copied.
[bio35-s4.1.3] 2. The template is mixed with a primer (a short piece of DNA that binds at the start of the region to be read) and DNA polymerase (the enzyme that builds new DNA).
[bio35-s4.1.4] Also added are a large supply of the four normal nucleotides, and a small amount of each of four modified “terminator” nucleotides.
[bio35-s4.1.5] A terminator nucleotide can be added to a growing strand like a normal one, but it lacks the chemical group needed to attach the next nucleotide, so once it is added the strand can grow no further.
[bio35-s4.1.6] 3. DNA polymerase builds new strands complementary to the template. At each position it usually adds a normal nucleotide, but occasionally, by chance, it adds a terminator, and that strand stops there.
[bio35-s4.1.7] Because there are millions of template copies, strands stop at every possible position, so every length of fragment is made.
[bio35-s4.1.8] 4. The result is a collection of DNA fragments of every length, each one nucleotide shorter than the next, and each ending in a terminator that shows which base is at its last position.
[bio35-s4.2.b] The fragments now need to be sorted by length and their end bases identified. Once they are lined up from shortest to longest, the sequence can be read off in order.
[bio35-s4.2.0] 5. The terminators are identified. In Sanger’s original method four separate reactions were run, each with only one kind of terminator (A, T, C or G), and loaded in four lanes of a gel, so the lane tells you the base.
[bio35-s4.2.1] In the modern automated method each of the four terminators carries a different coloured fluorescent dye, so one reaction is enough and a laser reads the colour of each fragment.
[bio35-s4.2.2] 6. The fragments are separated by gel electrophoresis (in a thin capillary tube in modern machines). The gel must separate fragments that differ by a single nucleotide in length, and the shortest move furthest.
[bio35-s4.2.3] 7. The sequence is read from the shortest fragment to the longest, which on a gel means from the bottom (furthest from the wells) upwards. The last base of each successive fragment gives the next base of the new strand.
[bio35-s4.2.4] The bands give the new strand, which is copied from the template. The template’s sequence is therefore the complement of what you read.
[bio35-s4.2.5] Diagram: four-lane gel, lanes labelled A, C, G, T at the top, with the wells at the top at the negative electrode; label wells, lanes, bands, + and − electrodes, shortest and longest fragment; arrow “read from bottom (shortest) to top”, sequence beside it. Beside it, a short template with growing strands, each stopped one base later.
[bio35-s4.3.b] Exam questions give you a picture of a gel and ask for the base sequence. The method is a fixed routine: start at the bottom and work upwards, one band at a time.
[bio35-s4.3.0] 1. Find the band nearest the bottom (furthest from the wells), because it is the shortest fragment. Its lane gives the first base of the new strand.
[bio35-s4.3.1] 2. Move up one band at a time, whichever lane it is in, writing down the lane letter each time.
[bio35-s4.3.2] 3. Stop at the top band, which is the longest fragment and gives the last base.
[bio35-s4.3.3] 4. To give the template, write the complementary base for each (A↔T, C↔G), because the new strand was built to match the template.
[bio35-s4.4.b] Here is the method applied to a gel with eight bands, followed by the same information as it appears on an automated machine.
[bio35-s4.4.0] A gel has bands at eight heights. From the bottom up, the bands are in lanes T, A, C, C, G, T, A, G.
[bio35-s4.4.1] So the new strand reads TACCGTAG, and the template strand is ATGGCATC, found by swapping each base for its complement.
[bio35-s4.4.2] Lane by lane: lane A has bands at positions 2 and 7 from the bottom; lane C at 3 and 4; lane G at 5 and 8; lane T at 1 and 6.
[bio35-s4.4.3] The shortest fragment (position 1) is one nucleotide longer than the primer; each band above is one nucleotide longer than the one below, so the position number is the fragment’s length in steps.
[bio35-s4.4.4] Automated output: the machine prints a trace of coloured peaks, one colour per base, in order of fragment length, with the base letter above each peak. It is read left to right in the same way, because the shortest fragments come first.
[bio35-s4.5.b] The original method reads one stretch of DNA at a time and is slow. Newer methods do the same job on a huge scale, which has changed how much DNA can be read.
[bio35-s4.5.0] Newer (next-generation) methods sequence millions of short DNA fragments at the same time, then use computers to assemble the overlapping fragments into the full sequence.
[bio35-s4.5.1] They are far faster and cheaper than the original method, so whole genomes are now sequenced routinely.
[bio35-s4.5.2] The huge amount of data is stored and analysed in genome databases. There is more on this in Bioinformatics: genome databases.
[bio35-s4.6.b] Knowing the exact base order of DNA is useful in medicine, research and public health. Each use below depends on comparing a sequence with another one.
[bio35-s4.6.0] Finding point mutations (changes to a single base) and diagnosing genetic diseases, because the sequence shows exactly which base has changed.
[bio35-s4.6.1] Choosing cancer drugs by sequencing a tumour’s DNA, so that treatment can match the changes found in that tumour.
[bio35-s4.6.2] Comparing the DNA of different species to work out evolutionary relationships and classification, since more similar sequences suggest closer relatives.
[bio35-s4.6.3] Identifying and tracking pathogens (disease-causing organisms): the genome of the virus that causes COVID-19 was sequenced and shared online in January 2020.

CANDIDATES bio17-s3: Mutations
[bio17-s3.0.b] DNA is copied every time a cell divides, and the copying is not perfect. A mutation is what is left when a change in the DNA is not put right, and it matters because it is the raw material for new traits.
[bio17-s3.0.0] A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA: either in the base sequence of a gene, called a point or gene mutation, or in the structure of a chromosome, called a chromosomal mutation.
[bio17-s3.0.1] A mutation that changes a gene produces a new allele (a different version of that gene).
[bio17-s3.0.2] Mutations arise spontaneously, mainly as uncorrected errors in base pairing during DNA replication (the copying of DNA before a cell divides), when a wrong base is paired and nobody fixes it.
[bio17-s3.0.3] Mutagens (agents that increase the rate of mutation) include ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays, radioactive substances) and ultraviolet light, which damage DNA directly.
[bio17-s3.0.4] Other mutagens are chemicals such as those in tobacco smoke, and some viruses.
[bio17-s3.0.5] Repair enzymes correct most damage, so only the errors that escape repair become permanent mutations. There is more on mutagens and cancer in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.b] Whether a mutation matters to a family depends on which cell it happens in. What it does to the organism depends on whether it changes a protein, and for evolution it matters because mutation creates new alleles.
[bio17-s3.1.0] Body-cell (somatic) mutations affect only the individual and the cells descended from the mutated cell, so they are not inherited; they can lead to cancer.
[bio17-s3.1.1] Mutations in cells that form gametes (germ-line mutations) can be passed to offspring, and are then present in every cell of the child because every cell grows from the fertilised egg.
[bio17-s3.1.2] Most mutations are neutral, either because they fall in non-coding DNA (DNA that does not code for a protein) or because they are silent, meaning the protein is not changed.
[bio17-s3.1.3] Many mutations are harmful, because they change or stop a protein.
[bio17-s3.1.4] A few mutations are beneficial in a particular environment, for example lactase persistence (being able to digest milk sugar as an adult).
[bio17-s3.1.5] Mutations giving antibiotic resistance help bacteria survive antibiotics, so they are beneficial to the bacteria in the presence of the drug.
[bio17-s3.1.6] Mutation is the only source of new alleles, and so the ultimate source of variation for natural selection to act on. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio17-s3.2.b] A point mutation is a tiny change at one spot in a gene. Because the genetic code is read in codons (groups of three bases, each coding for one amino acid), the effect depends on exactly what changes. The simplest case is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.2.0] A point mutation is a change in the nucleotide sequence at one point in a gene, usually of one or a few base pairs. This includes small deletions and insertions, not only substitutions.
[bio17-s3.2.1] It occurs when a wrong nucleotide pairs during replication and is not corrected, or when a nucleotide is added or missed out.
[bio17-s3.2.2] At the next replication the change is copied into both strands, so it becomes permanent.
[bio17-s3.2.3] In a substitution one base is replaced by another, so only one codon changes.
[bio17-s3.2.4] Silent substitution: the new codon codes for the same amino acid, because the code is degenerate (several codons can code for the same amino acid), so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.2.5] Missense substitution: the new codon codes for a different amino acid, which may or may not change the protein's shape and function.
[bio17-s3.2.6] Nonsense substitution: the codon becomes a stop codon (a signal to end translation), so the polypeptide is cut short and usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.b] Adding or losing bases is more dangerous than swapping one, because it can shift the way the whole rest of the gene is read.
[bio17-s3.3.0] Insertion or deletion of one base, or of any number of bases that is not a multiple of three, is a frameshift: every codon after the change is read differently, because the code is read in threes without gaps from the start codon.
[bio17-s3.3.1] After a frameshift many amino acids are wrong, and a stop codon often appears early or is lost, so the protein usually does not work.
[bio17-s3.3.2] A frameshift near the start of a gene is more damaging than one near the end, because more of the protein is changed.
[bio17-s3.3.3] Insertion or deletion of three bases adds or removes one amino acid without a frameshift, so the rest of the protein is normal, but even one missing amino acid can change the folding, as in cystic fibrosis.
[bio17-s3.4.b] To model a mutation, write the mRNA in codons, apply the change, then read each new codon with the codon table. Start with the normal mRNA AUG UUU GGC AAA UAA, which codes for methionine, phenylalanine, glycine and lysine, then stops.
[bio17-s3.4.0] Substitution, silent: GGC changed to GGA; still glycine, so the protein is unchanged.
[bio17-s3.4.1] Substitution, missense: UUU changed to UCU; serine instead of phenylalanine.
[bio17-s3.4.2] Substitution, nonsense: AAA changed to UAA, a stop codon; the chain ends after glycine.
[bio17-s3.4.3] Insertion of G after AUG: AUG GUU UGG CAA AUA A… gives methionine, valine, tryptophan, glutamine, isoleucine…; every amino acid after the start is changed and the original stop codon is lost from the reading frame.
[bio17-s3.4.4] Deletion of the first U of UUU: AUG UUG GCA AAU AA… gives methionine, leucine, alanine, asparagine…; this is a frameshift, because the groups of three have moved along.
[bio17-s3.4.5] Deletion of the whole codon UUU: AUG GGC AAA UAA gives methionine, glycine, lysine; one amino acid is missing and the rest is unchanged, so there is no frameshift.
[bio17-s3.4.6] Word analogy for revision: THE CAT ATE THE RAT; substitution THE CAT ATE THE HAT changes one word; insertion THE CXA TAT ETH ERA T gives nonsense words after the change; deletion THE ATA TET HER AT shifts every word after it.
[bio17-s3.5.b] Sickle-cell anaemia shows how a change in a single base can alter a protein and so a whole disease. It is a substitution.
[bio17-s3.5.0] Sickle-cell anaemia is a substitution in the gene for beta-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin, on chromosome 11.
[bio17-s3.5.1] One base change alters the codon for the sixth amino acid from GAG to GUG in mRNA (CTC to CAC in the DNA strand being read), so valine replaces glutamic acid.
[bio17-s3.5.2] The changed haemoglobin, haemoglobin S, molecules stick together into long fibres when oxygen is low, distorting red blood cells into a rigid sickle shape.
[bio17-s3.5.3] Sickled cells block small blood vessels, causing pain and organ damage, and are destroyed quickly, causing anaemia.
[bio17-s3.5.4] Sickle-cell is recessive: people with two copies have the disease; people with one copy (sickle-cell trait) are usually healthy and are more resistant to malaria.
[bio17-s3.5.5] This is why the sickle-cell allele is common in parts of Africa where malaria is common, an example of natural selection.
[bio17-s3.6.b] Cystic fibrosis is the example of a point mutation that is a small deletion. It shows that losing even one amino acid can ruin a protein.
[bio17-s3.6.0] Cystic fibrosis is caused by mutations in the CFTR gene on chromosome 7, which codes for a chloride-ion channel protein in cell membranes.
[bio17-s3.6.1] The most common cystic fibrosis mutation, F508del (delta F508), is a deletion of three bases, which removes one amino acid, phenylalanine, at position 508 of the protein.
[bio17-s3.6.2] The faulty protein folds wrongly, is broken down by the cell and does not reach the membrane, giving thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas.
[bio17-s3.6.3] Cystic fibrosis is recessive, and Ireland has the highest rate of cystic fibrosis in the world.
[bio17-s3.6.4] Newer CFTR modulator drugs help the faulty protein fold and work, which shows how knowing the mutation leads to treatment.
[bio17-s3.6.5] Cystic fibrosis is one of the conditions tested for in the newborn heel-prick screening offered to every baby in Ireland. There is more on this in Prenatal and postnatal care.
[bio17-s3.7.b] A chromosomal mutation changes a large piece of a chromosome, so many genes are affected at once, unlike a point mutation. A letter model makes the four types easy to follow.
[bio17-s3.7.0] A chromosomal mutation is a change in the structure of one or more chromosomes that affects many genes at once.
[bio17-s3.7.1] It occurs when chromosomes break, for example from ionising radiation or chemicals, or from errors when chromosomes are copied or separated in meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes), and the broken pieces are lost or rejoin in the wrong place.
[bio17-s3.7.2] Large changes can be seen under the light microscope in a karyotype (a picture of a cell's chromosomes).
[bio17-s3.7.3] Model: write a chromosome as A B C • D E F G H, where the letters are genes or groups of genes and • is the centromere (the constricted point where the two halves of a chromosome are joined).
[bio17-s3.7.4] A different, non-homologous chromosome (one that is not its partner in a pair) is written M N O • P Q R.
[bio17-s3.8.b] Two of the four types change how many copies of a section there are, one by losing it and one by repeating it.
[bio17-s3.8.0] Deletion: a section breaks off and is lost, so its genes are missing: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E H, with F and G lost.
[bio17-s3.8.1] Deletion is usually harmful, because a whole set of genes is missing.
[bio17-s3.8.2] Duplication: a section is copied twice, so its genes are repeated: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D E F E F G H.
[bio17-s3.8.3] Extra copies can upset the balance of gene products (the proteins the genes make); over evolutionary time, duplicated genes can mutate to take on new functions.
[bio17-s3.9.b] The other two types move genes without necessarily losing any. An inversion turns a section around in the same chromosome; a translocation moves a section to another chromosome.
[bio17-s3.9.0] Inversion: a section breaks out, turns through 180 degrees and rejoins, so its genes are in reverse order: A B C • D E F G H becomes A B C • D G F E H.
[bio17-s3.9.1] No genes are lost, so an inversion often has no effect on the person, unless a break falls inside a gene; it can cause problems in meiosis, leading to gametes with missing or extra genes.
[bio17-s3.9.2] Translocation: a section of one chromosome breaks off and joins a different, non-homologous chromosome; in a reciprocal translocation two chromosomes swap sections.
[bio17-s3.9.3] In a reciprocal translocation, A B C • D E F G H and M N O • P Q R become A B C • D E F Q R and M N O • P G H.
[bio17-s3.9.4] If no genes are lost the person may be healthy, but a break can join two genes into a new hybrid (fusion) gene, and carriers can produce gametes with unbalanced sets of genes.
[bio17-s3.9.5] Diagram: each of the four mutations drawn as a chromosome with lettered bands and a centromere, before and after, with the translocation shown between two chromosomes of different sizes.
[bio17-s3.10.b] Each structural type has a real disorder that illustrates it.
[bio17-s3.10.0] Deletion: cri du chat syndrome (French for “cry of the cat”) is caused by deletion of part of the short arm of chromosome 5.
[bio17-s3.10.1] Babies with cri du chat have a high-pitched cry like a cat's, a small head and low birth weight, and later intellectual disability and delayed development.
[bio17-s3.10.2] Duplication: Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A is caused by duplication of a small section of chromosome 17 containing the PMP22 gene.
[bio17-s3.10.3] The extra copy disrupts the insulating myelin sheath of nerves in the limbs, causing gradual weakness of the muscles of the feet and lower legs.
[bio17-s3.10.4] Inversion: an inversion within the gene for blood-clotting factor VIII on the X chromosome breaks the gene and causes nearly half of all cases of severe haemophilia A.
[bio17-s3.10.5] By contrast, a common inversion in chromosome 9 usually has no effect, because no genes are broken. There is more on haemophilia as a sex-linked trait in Sex-linked traits.
[bio17-s3.11.b] The Philadelphia chromosome shows how swapping pieces between chromosomes can create a gene that causes cancer.
[bio17-s3.11.0] The Philadelphia chromosome is found in chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML), a cancer of white blood cells.
[bio17-s3.11.1] A reciprocal translocation between chromosomes 9 and 22, in a bone-marrow stem cell, joins part of the ABL gene from chromosome 9 to the BCR gene on chromosome 22.
[bio17-s3.11.2] This forms a BCR-ABL fusion gene on a shortened chromosome 22, the Philadelphia chromosome.
[bio17-s3.11.3] The fusion gene codes for an enzyme that is permanently switched on and drives the cell to divide uncontrollably, so it is an oncogene (a gene that causes cancer).
[bio17-s3.11.4] CML is caused by a somatic mutation and is not inherited, because it happens in a body cell and not in a gamete-forming cell.
[bio17-s3.11.5] The Philadelphia chromosome was discovered in Philadelphia, USA, in 1960 by Peter Nowell and David Hungerford; Janet Rowley showed in 1973 that it arises by translocation.
[bio17-s3.11.6] The drug imatinib blocks the BCR-ABL enzyme. There is more on this in Causes, prevention and treatment of cancer.
[bio17-s3.12.b] Some conditions come from having the wrong number of chromosomes, not from a change in structure. They are therefore not examples of deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.0] Some conditions are caused by a change in the number of chromosomes, not in their structure, so they are not deletion, duplication, inversion or translocation.
[bio17-s3.12.1] They usually result from non-disjunction: a homologous pair, or two copies of a chromosome, fails to separate during meiosis, so a gamete has one chromosome too many or too few.
[bio17-s3.12.2] Down syndrome: in about 95% of cases there are three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21), giving 47 chromosomes, after non-disjunction, usually during egg formation.
[bio17-s3.12.3] The risk of Down syndrome rises with the mother's age.
[bio17-s3.12.4] Features of Down syndrome include a characteristic facial appearance, some degree of intellectual disability and a higher risk of heart defects.
[bio17-s3.12.5] A few per cent of Down syndrome cases are caused instead by a translocation that attaches extra chromosome-21 material to another chromosome; only those cases involve a structural change.
[bio17-s3.13.b] Sex chromosomes can also be gained or lost, and a karyotype is the tool that shows number changes and large structural changes.
[bio17-s3.13.0] Turner syndrome is a change in number with 45 chromosomes, X: one X and no second sex chromosome, female.
[bio17-s3.13.1] Klinefelter syndrome is a change in number with 47 chromosomes, XXY, male.
[bio17-s3.13.2] A karyotype is a photograph of a cell's chromosomes, taken during mitosis when they are condensed, and arranged in homologous pairs by size and shape.
[bio17-s3.13.3] A karyotype shows changes in number (for example three chromosome 21s) and large structural changes (for example the shortened chromosome 22).
[bio17-s3.14.b] The two kinds of mutation differ in size, in how they arise, and in how they are detected. Comparing them in pairs is the quickest way to answer a distinguish question.
[bio17-s3.14.0] Scale: a point mutation changes one or a few bases in one gene; a chromosomal mutation changes the arrangement or number of copies of a large section with many genes.
[bio17-s3.14.1] How they arise: point mutations mainly from mispairing during replication; chromosomal mutations from breakage and wrong rejoining of chromosomes, often during meiosis.
[bio17-s3.14.2] Detection: point mutations need DNA sequencing (reading the order of bases), because they are too small to see; large chromosomal mutations can be seen in a karyotype under the light microscope.
[bio17-s3.14.3] Examples: sickle-cell anaemia (substitution) and cystic fibrosis (three-base deletion); cri du chat (deletion), Charcot–Marie–Tooth type 1A (duplication), severe haemophilia A (inversion), and the Philadelphia chromosome in CML (translocation).

CANDIDATES bio11-s5: Monohybrid crosses
[bio11-s5.0.b] A genetic cross predicts what offspring a pair of parents can have. It works only because of how alleles (the different versions of a gene) are shared out when gametes (sex cells) are made and joined.
[bio11-s5.0.0] Each body cell carries two alleles of each gene, one on each chromosome of a homologous pair (two matching chromosomes, one inherited from each parent).
[bio11-s5.0.1] Each gamete carries only one of the two alleles, because meiosis (the cell division that makes gametes) separates the two chromosomes of each homologous pair.
[bio11-s5.0.2] At fertilisation the zygote (the first cell of the new organism) again has two alleles, one from each parent, so the pair is restored.
[bio11-s5.0.3] There is more on this in the subsections on chromosome structure and genes, mitosis and meiosis, and the key concepts of information transfer, where allele, homozygous, heterozygous, pure-breeding and carrier are defined.
[bio11-s5.1.b] Before you can set out any cross you must work out which gametes each parent can make. This depends only on whether the parent is homozygous (two identical alleles) or heterozygous (two different alleles).
[bio11-s5.1.0] A homozygous individual (TT or tt) makes only one type of gamete for that gene: T from TT, or t from tt, because both of its alleles are the same.
[bio11-s5.1.1] A heterozygous individual (Tt) makes two types of gamete, T and t, in equal numbers, half of its gametes each, because the two chromosomes separate at random in meiosis.
[bio11-s5.1.2] True-breeding is another name for pure-breeding (homozygous): crossed with one like itself, it always gives offspring with the same trait.
[bio11-s5.2.b] A monohybrid cross is the simplest kind of genetic cross, because it follows just one characteristic at a time. Labels for the generations let you say clearly which individuals you mean.
[bio11-s5.2.0] A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of one trait, controlled by one gene with two alleles.
[bio11-s5.2.1] Unlinked means the gene is considered on its own, without other genes on the same chromosome affecting the result. There is more on this in Linkage.
[bio11-s5.2.2] P (parental generation) are the parents crossed.
[bio11-s5.2.3] F1 (first filial generation) are their offspring, for example the plants grown from the seeds of the P cross.
[bio11-s5.2.4] F2 (second filial generation) are the offspring of crossing F1 individuals with each other.
[bio11-s5.2.5] “First generation” means the offspring of the cross being modelled, so you only need to work out the offspring of one cross.
[bio11-s5.3.b] Symbols are shorthand for alleles. A key at the start tells the examiner what each symbol means, so your answer can be read without guessing.
[bio11-s5.3.0] Use the first letter of the dominant trait: a capital for the dominant allele and a lower-case letter for the recessive allele, e.g. T = tall, t = dwarf.
[bio11-s5.3.1] Use the letters a question gives, because the marking scheme is written with them.
[bio11-s5.3.2] Avoid letters whose capital and lower case look alike when handwritten, because the examiner cannot tell which allele you mean: C/c, O/o, S/s, W/w, P/p, K/k, V/v, X/x, Z/z.
[bio11-s5.3.3] Always state a key first, e.g. “T = allele for tall, t = allele for dwarf”.
[bio11-s5.4.b] Every line is shown, in this order, because marks are given for each. Working in the same sequence every time also stops you missing a step.
[bio11-s5.4.0] Step 1: the key, so that the examiner knows what each letter means.
[bio11-s5.4.1] Step 2: the parents' phenotypes (the visible traits), e.g. tall × dwarf.
[bio11-s5.4.2] Step 3: the parents' genotypes (the alleles they carry), e.g. TT × tt.
[bio11-s5.4.3] Step 4: the gametes, each different allele each parent can pass on, written once and usually circled (T from TT; t from tt; T and t from Tt).
[bio11-s5.4.4] Step 5: the Punnett square (a grid that combines the gametes), or fertilisation lines joining every gamete of one parent to every gamete of the other.
[bio11-s5.4.5] Step 6: the offspring genotypes, with their ratio, such as 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt.
[bio11-s5.4.6] Step 7: the offspring phenotypes, with their ratio, such as 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
[bio11-s5.5.b] A Punnett square is a grid that shows every possible fertilisation between the gametes of two parents. It makes sure you do not miss a combination.
[bio11-s5.5.0] One parent's gametes go across the top and the other parent's gametes down the side.
[bio11-s5.5.1] Each box takes the allele from its column and the allele from its row, with the dominant allele written first (Tt, not tT), so that identical genotypes are always written the same way.
[bio11-s5.5.2] Each box is one equally likely outcome of fertilisation; with two gametes from each parent, each box is a 1 in 4 (25%) chance.
[bio11-s5.5.3] Diagram: a 2 × 2 Punnett square for Tt × Tt: gametes T and t across the top (labelled “gametes of parent 1”), T and t down the side (labelled “gametes of parent 2”); boxes TT, Tt, Tt, tt; each box labelled with its phenotype (tall, tall, tall, dwarf).
[bio11-s5.6.b] A cross predicts chances, not certainties. Each fertilisation is a separate chance event, so the ratio describes what is expected on average.
[bio11-s5.6.0] A ratio is a prediction of the proportions expected, not an exact count.
[bio11-s5.6.1] Each fertilisation is independent, so in a family where the first child is affected, the chance for the next child is unchanged.
[bio11-s5.6.2] A 3 : 1 ratio is a probability, not a guarantee that a family of four has exactly one affected child.
[bio11-s5.6.3] Ratios can be given as a ratio (3 : 1), a fraction (3/4), a percentage (75%) or a probability (0.75, or “a 3 in 4 chance”).
[bio11-s5.6.4] Observed results come close to the predicted ratio only with large numbers of offspring (e.g. hundreds of pea plants), not in a small human family, because chance has more effect on small samples.
[bio11-s5.7.b] Pea plants: tall (T) is dominant to dwarf (t). Here two heterozygous tall plants are crossed, and every line of the standard layout is shown.
[bio11-s5.7.0] Key: T = tall, t = dwarf.
[bio11-s5.7.1] Parents' phenotypes: tall × tall.
[bio11-s5.7.2] Parents' genotypes: Tt × Tt (both heterozygous, so each makes two types of gamete).
[bio11-s5.7.3] Gametes: T, t × T, t, because each heterozygous parent makes both types.
[bio11-s5.7.4] Punnett square: TT, Tt / Tt, tt.
[bio11-s5.7.5] Offspring genotypes: 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, because Tt arises in two boxes of the square.
[bio11-s5.7.6] Offspring phenotypes: 3 tall : 1 dwarf (75% tall, 25% dwarf), because TT and both Tt boxes are tall.
[bio11-s5.7.7] Of the tall offspring, 1/3 are homozygous (TT) and 2/3 heterozygous (Tt), because the three tall offspring are TT, Tt and Tt.
[bio11-s5.8.b] In the cross above there are two different ratios, one for genotypes and one for phenotypes. They differ because of dominance.
[bio11-s5.8.0] The genotype ratio (1 : 2 : 1) differs from the phenotype ratio (3 : 1) because TT and Tt look the same: the dominant allele masks the recessive one.
[bio11-s5.8.1] A recessive trait reappears in offspring of parents who both show the dominant trait only if both parents are heterozygous, because only then can each parent pass on a recessive allele.
[bio11-s5.9.b] Crossing a true-breeding tall plant with a true-breeding dwarf plant is the simplest case, because each parent makes only one type of gamete.
[bio11-s5.9.0] Parents' phenotypes: true-breeding tall × true-breeding dwarf.
[bio11-s5.9.1] Parents' genotypes: TT × tt (both homozygous, so true-breeding).
[bio11-s5.9.2] Gametes: T × t, because each parent makes only one type.
[bio11-s5.9.3] Offspring genotypes: all Tt (heterozygous), because each gets T from one parent and t from the other.
[bio11-s5.9.4] Offspring phenotypes: all (100%) tall, because the dominant T masks t.
[bio11-s5.9.5] This is Mendel's P → F1 cross; crossing these F1 plants together (Tt × Tt) gives the F2 result of the heterozygous × heterozygous example above, in which the dwarf trait reappears.
[bio11-s5.9.6] Mendel's own figures are in Mendel's Laws.
[bio11-s5.10.b] In this cross a heterozygous tall plant is crossed with a dwarf plant. It gives a different ratio from the first example, because one parent can pass on only the recessive allele.
[bio11-s5.10.0] Parents' phenotypes: tall × dwarf.
[bio11-s5.10.1] Parents' genotypes: Tt × tt (one heterozygous parent, so only the Tt parent makes two types of gamete).
[bio11-s5.10.2] Gametes: T, t × t, because the tt parent can give only t.
[bio11-s5.10.3] Punnett square (2 × 1, or 2 × 2 with t written twice): Tt, tt.
[bio11-s5.10.4] Offspring genotypes: 1 Tt : 1 tt.
[bio11-s5.10.5] Offspring phenotypes: 1 tall : 1 dwarf (50% each), because Tt is tall and tt is dwarf.
[bio11-s5.11.b] With one gene and two alleles there are only three genotypes, TT, Tt and tt, so only six different crosses are possible. Knowing all six lets you recognise any monohybrid cross quickly.
[bio11-s5.11.0] Cross 1: TT × TT → all TT → all tall, because both parents can only pass on T.
[bio11-s5.11.1] Cross 2: TT × Tt → 1 TT : 1 Tt → all tall.
[bio11-s5.11.2] Cross 3: TT × tt → all Tt → all tall, because T masks t.
[bio11-s5.11.3] Cross 4: Tt × Tt → 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt → 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
[bio11-s5.11.4] Cross 5: Tt × tt → 1 Tt : 1 tt → 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
[bio11-s5.11.5] Cross 6: tt × tt → all tt → all dwarf, because both parents can only pass on t.
[bio11-s5.12.b] You can also work from the offspring back to the parents. The ratio of offspring tells you which genotypes the parents must have had.
[bio11-s5.12.0] Offspring in a 3 : 1 ratio mean both parents are heterozygous, because only Tt × Tt gives this ratio.
[bio11-s5.12.1] A 1 : 1 ratio means one parent is heterozygous and the other homozygous recessive, so only one parent makes two kinds of gamete.
[bio11-s5.12.2] Any offspring showing the recessive trait mean each parent carries at least one recessive allele, because an offspring needs two recessive alleles, one from each parent.
[bio11-s5.13.b] An organism showing the dominant trait could be TT or Tt, and you cannot tell by looking. A test cross finds out by crossing it with a recessive organism.
[bio11-s5.13.0] A test cross finds the unknown genotype of an organism showing the dominant trait (TT or Tt) by crossing it with a homozygous recessive (tt) organism.
[bio11-s5.13.1] The tt parent can give only t, so each offspring's phenotype shows directly which allele came from the unknown parent.
[bio11-s5.13.2] If all offspring show the dominant trait (all tall), the unknown parent is almost certainly TT (TT × tt → all Tt).
[bio11-s5.13.3] If any offspring show the recessive trait (about half dwarf), the unknown parent is Tt (Tt × tt → 1 : 1), because it must have passed on a recessive allele.
[bio11-s5.13.4] Many offspring are needed to be confident that a TT result is not chance.
[bio11-s5.14.b] Cystic fibrosis is an inherited disorder that follows the same rules as the pea plants. Two parents who do not have the disease can still have an affected child, if both are carriers.
[bio11-s5.14.0] Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on an autosome (a chromosome that is not a sex chromosome).
[bio11-s5.14.1] Key: F = normal allele, f = cystic fibrosis allele, with the normal allele dominant.
[bio11-s5.14.2] Parents: two unaffected carriers (people who carry the recessive allele but do not have the disease).
[bio11-s5.14.3] Parents' genotypes: Ff × Ff.
[bio11-s5.14.4] Gametes: F, f × F, f, because each carrier makes both types.
[bio11-s5.14.5] Offspring genotypes: 1 FF : 2 Ff : 1 ff.
[bio11-s5.14.6] Offspring phenotypes: 3 unaffected : 1 with cystic fibrosis, because only ff has the disease.
[bio11-s5.14.7] Each child has a 1 in 4 (25%) chance of having cystic fibrosis, a 1 in 2 (50%) chance of being a carrier, and a 1 in 4 chance of being neither.
[bio11-s5.14.8] Why the mutation causes the disease is in Mutations, and its use in genetic counselling is in Benefits and limitations of Mendelian genetics.
