TARGET bio6-s2: Domains of life
[bio6-s2.0.b] Living things are sorted into ranks, from broad groups down to single species. A domain is the highest, broadest rank in the classification of living things, above kingdom. All cellular life is placed in three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya (the specification calls the last one eukaryota).
[bio6-s2.0.0] The system was developed by the American microbiologist Carl Woese. In 1977, with George Fox, he showed that archaea are a separate group from bacteria. In 1990 Woese and colleagues formally proposed the three domains.
[bio6-s2.0.1] Before this, all prokaryotes (cells with no nucleus) were placed in one group, the kingdom "Monera" in the five-kingdom system. Archaea look like bacteria under the microscope, so the difference could only be found from their molecules.
[bio6-s2.0.2] Genetic basis: Woese compared the base sequences of ribosomal RNA (rRNA), specifically the RNA of the small ribosome subunit. Every cell has ribosomes (the structures that build proteins), and the rRNA does the same job in all of them.
[bio6-s2.0.3] rRNA is a good tool for comparing all life because its sequence changes very slowly over evolutionary time, so even distantly related organisms still have sequences that can be lined up and compared.
[bio6-s2.0.4] The more similar two organisms' rRNA sequences are, the more closely related they are. This is how a molecule can be used to work out family relationships between organisms that look nothing alike.
[bio6-s2.0.5] The results showed three deep branches of life, not two (prokaryote and eukaryote). Archaea differ from bacteria about as much as either differs from eukaryotes.
[bio6-s2.0.6] In several genetic features archaea are more similar to eukaryotes than to bacteria, so archaea and eukaryotes share a more recent common ancestor with each other than either does with bacteria.
[bio6-s2.0.7] Diagram: a three-domain tree with one common ancestor at the base; one branch to Bacteria; and a second branch that splits into Archaea and Eukarya. Label the four eukaryotic kingdoms (Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) on the Eukarya branch.
[bio6-s2.1.b] Bacteria are the first prokaryotic domain, and the one most people picture when they think of microbes. They live almost everywhere and do very different jobs.
[bio6-s2.1.0] Bacteria are prokaryotic: they have no nucleus or membrane-bound organelles, so their DNA lies loose in the cell rather than inside a nuclear membrane.
[bio6-s2.1.1] Bacteria have a single circular chromosome without histone proteins (the proteins eukaryotes wind their DNA around), and their cell walls contain peptidoglycan, a strengthening molecule found in bacterial walls.
[bio6-s2.1.2] Bacteria are found almost everywhere. They include decomposers, nitrogen-fixing bacteria, photosynthetic cyanobacteria and pathogens (disease-causing organisms) such as Streptococcus and some strains of Escherichia coli.
[bio6-s2.1.3] Many bacteria are killed by antibiotics, which is why bacterial infections can often be treated with them.
[bio6-s2.2.b] Archaea look like bacteria, but their chemistry is different, and in several ways it is closer to that of eukaryotes. Archaea are not "ancient bacteria" and not a type of bacterium, even though the old name was "archaebacteria".
[bio6-s2.2.0] Archaea are prokaryotic and single-celled, similar in size and shape to bacteria. This is why they were once classified with bacteria.
[bio6-s2.2.1] Their cell walls lack peptidoglycan, and their membrane lipids (the fats that make up the cell membrane) have a different chemical structure from those of all other organisms.
[bio6-s2.2.2] Their DNA is wound around histone-like proteins, and their enzymes for copying DNA and making RNA are more like those of eukaryotes. This is part of the evidence that archaea and eukaryotes are closer relatives.
[bio6-s2.2.3] Most antibiotics that kill bacteria do not affect archaea, because archaea have different cell walls and different molecular machinery for the antibiotics to target.
[bio6-s2.2.4] Many archaea are extremophiles (organisms living in extreme conditions). Thermophiles live in hot springs and deep-sea hydrothermal vents; halophiles live in very salty water such as salt lakes, e.g. Halobacterium.
[bio6-s2.2.5] Methanogens live where there is no oxygen, e.g. in bogs, rice paddies and the digestive systems of cattle, and make methane.
[bio6-s2.2.6] Archaea are also common in ordinary soil and the oceans. Archaea are not restricted to extreme environments, and no archaea are known to cause disease in humans.
[bio6-s2.3.b] The third domain contains every organism whose cells are built on the more complex eukaryotic plan, from single-celled protists to plants, fungi and animals.
[bio6-s2.3.0] Eukarya are all organisms whose cells have a nucleus enclosed by a membrane and membrane-bound organelles (compartments with their own membrane), e.g. mitochondria.
[bio6-s2.3.1] Eukaryotic cells have several linear chromosomes, with DNA wound around histones, unlike the single circular chromosome of bacteria.
[bio6-s2.3.2] Eukarya are unicellular or multicellular. The domain contains the kingdoms Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia, which are the four kingdoms that sit on the Eukarya branch of the tree.
[bio6-s2.4.b] Putting the domains side by side shows what separates them. Two features (peptidoglycan and the type of membrane lipid) set bacteria and archaea apart, while archaea share others, such as histone-like proteins, with eukaryotes.
[bio6-s2.4.0] Nucleus: absent in bacteria, absent in archaea, present in eukaryotes. The two prokaryotic domains therefore keep their DNA unenclosed.
[bio6-s2.4.1] Membrane-bound organelles: absent in bacteria, absent in archaea, present in eukaryotes.
[bio6-s2.4.2] Peptidoglycan in the cell wall: present in bacteria, absent in archaea, absent in eukaryotes. So peptidoglycan is a feature unique to bacteria.
[bio6-s2.4.3] Membrane lipids: bacterial-type in bacteria, a unique archaeal type in archaea, bacterial-type in eukaryotes. Archaea are the odd one out here.
[bio6-s2.4.4] Histones: absent in bacteria, histone-like in archaea, present in eukaryotes. This is one feature in which archaea resemble eukaryotes more than bacteria.
[bio6-s2.4.5] Ribosome size: 70S in bacteria, 70S in archaea, 80S in the cytoplasm of eukaryotes. Ribosomes are the structures that build proteins, and the S number is explained below.
[bio6-s2.4.6] Sensitivity to common antibiotics: many bacteria are affected, archaea are generally not, and eukaryotes are not affected.
[bio6-s2.4.7] Examples: Escherichia coli and Streptococcus for bacteria; Halobacterium and methanogens for archaea; the kingdoms Protista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia for Eukarya.
[bio6-s2.5.b] The ribosome sizes in the comparison (70S and 80S) are written in S units, so you need to know what the S means.
[bio6-s2.5.0] The S number (Svedberg unit) measures how fast a particle settles in a centrifuge (a machine that spins samples at high speed). A larger number means a larger particle, which is why 80S eukaryotic ribosomes are the larger type.
[bio6-s2.6.b] The three domains change how we picture the living world, and they show how classification changes as new evidence arrives.
[bio6-s2.6.0] The biggest division in life is not plant versus animal, or even prokaryote versus eukaryote, but these three lineages. Animals, plants and fungi are just three small branches of one domain.
[bio6-s2.6.1] The domain system is based on genetic evidence, which reflects evolutionary relationships, rather than on appearance. This is why archaea, which look like bacteria, are placed in a separate domain.
[bio6-s2.6.2] It is an example of how new technology (gene sequencing, which reads the order of bases in DNA or RNA) changes classification. There is more on this in Classification.

CANDIDATES bio7-s1: Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells
[bio7-s1.0.b] Biologists sort all cells into two types, depending on whether the DNA is enclosed in a nucleus. The names themselves tell you the difference, and you need the units to talk about how big cells are.
[bio7-s1.0.0] A prokaryotic cell has no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments wrapped in their own membrane). Its DNA lies free in the cytoplasm, the jelly-like fluid that fills the cell. “Pro” means before and “karyon” means kernel or nucleus. Bacteria and archaea are prokaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.1] A eukaryotic cell has its DNA enclosed in a nucleus surrounded by a double membrane (the nuclear envelope), and it has membrane-bound organelles such as mitochondria. “Eu” means true, so eukaryote means “true nucleus”. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are eukaryotes.
[bio7-s1.0.2] Cells are far too small for millimetres to be a handy unit, so their sizes are given in micrometres. 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres) and 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres). Cell sizes are given in µm.
[bio7-s1.1.b] Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells look very different, but both are living cells that must do the same basic jobs. So they share a core set of features.
[bio7-s1.1.0] Both have a cell membrane that controls what enters and leaves the cell, and cytoplasm, the fluid where many of the cell's reactions take place.
[bio7-s1.1.1] Both have ribosomes (the tiny structures that make proteins), and both use DNA as the genetic material, reading it with the same genetic code.
[bio7-s1.1.2] Both respire, releasing energy from food, and both use ATP (the molecule cells use to carry energy) as the energy currency.
[bio7-s1.1.3] These shared features suggest that all cells evolved from a common ancestor, because such an unusual set of identical features is unlikely to have arisen separately.
[bio7-s1.2.b] The most important difference between the two cell types is how the DNA is stored. This affects where the DNA sits, what shape it has and what it is packed with.
[bio7-s1.2.0] Nucleus: prokaryotes have none. Their DNA is concentrated in a region of the cytoplasm called the nucleoid, which has no membrane around it. Eukaryotes have a true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nuclear pores (small channels through the envelope).
[bio7-s1.2.1] Number and shape of chromosomes: prokaryotes have one circular chromosome (a closed loop of DNA). Eukaryotes have several linear chromosomes (DNA with two ends).
[bio7-s1.2.2] Packaging: eukaryotic DNA is wound around histone proteins, which help to pack it. In bacteria the circular chromosome is not wound around histone proteins.
[bio7-s1.2.3] Prokaryotes often also have plasmids, small extra rings of DNA separate from the main chromosome. Plasmids can carry genes such as antibiotic resistance, which is why resistance can spread between bacteria.
[bio7-s1.2.4] Plasmids are found in prokaryotes (and in yeast), not in all eukaryotes, so do not say that eukaryotes have none or that all have them.
[bio7-s1.3.b] Eukaryotic cells divide their work between membrane-bound organelles, whereas prokaryotes have to do everything without them. Ribosomes are the one structure both have, but they differ in size.
[bio7-s1.3.0] Membrane-bound organelles: prokaryotes have none, so no mitochondria, no chloroplasts and no other membrane-bound organelles. Eukaryotes have mitochondria, and plants and algae also have chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.3.1] Without organelles, prokaryotes carry out respiration and, in photosynthetic bacteria, photosynthesis on the cell membrane or on infoldings of it (folds of the membrane that increase its area).
[bio7-s1.3.2] Mitochondria and chloroplasts contain their own small circular DNA and 70S ribosomes, like a prokaryote. This is one more way these organelles resemble prokaryotic cells.
[bio7-s1.3.3] Ribosomes: both cell types have them, but prokaryotic ribosomes are smaller (70S) than those in eukaryotic cytoplasm (80S). The S number is a measure of size.
[bio7-s1.3.4] Many antibiotics work by blocking 70S ribosomes, so they stop bacteria making proteins and harm bacteria but not human cells, which use 80S ribosomes in their cytoplasm.
[bio7-s1.4.b] The remaining differences are about the whole cell: how big it is, what surrounds it, and how the organism is built and reproduces.
[bio7-s1.4.0] Size: prokaryotic cells are small, most about 1–10 µm long. Eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm, so they are roughly ten times larger in length.
[bio7-s1.4.1] Cell wall: almost all prokaryotes have one, made of peptidoglycan in bacteria. Among eukaryotes, plant cells have cellulose walls, fungal cells have chitin walls, and animal cells have none.
[bio7-s1.4.2] Some prokaryotes have extra outer structures: a capsule (slime layer) for protection, and a flagellum, a whip-like tail, for movement. There is more on the full bacterial cell in Microorganisms.
[bio7-s1.4.3] Organisation: prokaryotes are almost all unicellular (made of a single cell) and divide by binary fission, in which one cell splits in two. Eukaryotes can be unicellular or multicellular and divide by mitosis.
[bio7-s1.4.4] Examples: prokaryotes are bacteria and archaea. Eukaryotes are protists, fungi, plants and animals.
[bio7-s1.5.b] How a cell looks in a micrograph (a photograph taken through a microscope) depends on the microscope, because a light microscope cannot show very small structures but an electron microscope can. Knowing what each shows lets you identify a cell.
[bio7-s1.5.0] Under a light microscope at ×400, a bacterium appears as a tiny dot, rod or spiral with no visible internal structure. It is too small (about 1–10 µm) for the light microscope to show its parts.
[bio7-s1.5.1] Under a light microscope, eukaryotic cells show the nucleus (especially when stained), the cytoplasm and the cell membrane as a line. Plant cells also show the cell wall, a large vacuole (a fluid-filled sac) and chloroplasts.
[bio7-s1.5.2] Electron micrographs show what the light microscope cannot. In prokaryotes they show the nucleoid, ribosomes as tiny dots, plasmids and the layers of the wall.
[bio7-s1.5.3] In eukaryotes, electron micrographs show the double nuclear envelope and its pores, the internal membranes of mitochondria and chloroplasts, and ribosomes.
[bio7-s1.5.4] Diagram: side-by-side labelled diagrams of a generalised prokaryotic cell (cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular DNA, plasmid, ribosomes, capsule, flagellum) and a eukaryotic animal cell (cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus, nuclear envelope, nuclear pore, mitochondria, ribosomes), with the eukaryotic cell drawn much larger.
[bio7-s1.6.b] You may be given a micrograph and asked whether the cell is prokaryotic or eukaryotic, and to justify the answer. Work through the same checks in order.
[bio7-s1.6.0] 1. Check for a nucleus. If one is present, the cell is eukaryotic, because only eukaryotes enclose their DNA in a nucleus.
[bio7-s1.6.1] 2. Check the scale bar or stated magnification to estimate size. A cell of about 1–10 µm suggests a prokaryote, since eukaryotic cells are usually about 10–100 µm.
[bio7-s1.6.2] 3. Look for membrane-bound organelles, which only eukaryotes have.
[bio7-s1.6.3] 4. For a eukaryote, look for a cell wall, a large vacuole and chloroplasts, which mean a plant cell, or their absence, which suggests an animal cell. There is more on this in Structures and organelles of animal and plant cells.
