TARGET bio31-s5: Carrying capacity and limiting factors
[bio31-s5.0.b] A population is all the individuals of one species living in one area. Its size rises and falls, and in every habitat there is an upper limit that the environment can support. That limit is the carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.0.0] A population's size changes through four processes. Births (natality) and immigration (individuals arriving) increase it; deaths (mortality) and emigration (individuals leaving) decrease it.
[bio31-s5.0.1] A population grows when births plus immigration exceed deaths plus emigration. If the two sides are equal, the size stays steady.
[bio31-s5.0.2] Every population could grow very fast if nothing stopped it, because organisms produce more offspring than are needed to replace themselves. In reality, resources run short, so growth slows and stops.
[bio31-s5.0.3] The carrying capacity of an environment is the maximum population size of a species that it can support indefinitely, given the food, water, space and other resources available. It is often given the symbol K.
[bio31-s5.0.4] A limiting factor is any factor that restricts the size, growth or distribution of a population. The factor in shortest supply relative to need sets the limit, even if all the others are plentiful, which is the same principle as limiting factors in photosynthesis.
[bio31-s5.0.5] Carrying capacity is set by the limiting factors acting together, not by any one of them alone.
[bio31-s5.0.6] Carrying capacity is not fixed. It changes when conditions change, such as in a wet or dry year, after a new predator or disease arrives, or with habitat loss or restoration. It also differs between species in the same habitat.
[bio31-s5.0.7] If a population rises above carrying capacity, it damages its resources, for example by overgrazing (eating plants faster than they regrow), and then its numbers fall.
[bio31-s5.1.b] Abiotic factors are the non-living parts of the environment. Each of them can cap the number of plants and animals a habitat supports, and the Irish examples below show how.
[bio31-s5.1.0] Water: all organisms need water. On the dry, free-draining sand of coastal sand dunes, water shortage limits which plants can grow and how many.
[bio31-s5.1.1] Marram grass grows on sand dunes because its rolled leaves and deep roots reduce water loss and reach water, so it survives where most plants cannot. In a dry summer, grass growth slows and fewer grazing animals can be supported.
[bio31-s5.1.2] Oxygen: aquatic animals depend on oxygen dissolved in water, which is scarce compared with air. Salmon, trout and stonefly nymphs need well-oxygenated, cool, fast-flowing water, so oxygen limits their numbers in slow, warm or polluted rivers.
[bio31-s5.1.3] Decomposing organic pollution uses up oxygen in a river, which is why polluted water supports fewer oxygen-demanding animals. In waterlogged bog soil, low oxygen limits roots and decomposers, so few plant species can grow there.
[bio31-s5.1.4] Space: organisms need space to attach, shelter, nest or hold a territory (an area an animal defends). On a rocky shore, bare rock is limited, so barnacles, mussels and seaweeds compete for space to attach.
[bio31-s5.1.5] Seabirds nest only on cliffs and islands with suitable ledges. Many songbirds, such as robins, defend territories, so the number of territories in a woodland limits the breeding population.
[bio31-s5.1.6] Temperature affects enzyme activity, growth and survival. Cold winters kill many small birds such as wrens, and low temperatures in the uplands shorten the growing season, limiting plant growth. Warm water holds less dissolved oxygen, so temperature also limits fish.
[bio31-s5.1.7] Soil type, pH, minerals, water content and air content limit plants, and so the animals that feed on them. Acidic, nitrogen-poor bog peat supports only specialised plants such as Sphagnum, heather and sundews.
[bio31-s5.2.b] Soil is one of the factors that limit which plants can grow in a place, and plants are the food base for animals. Light is not on the specification's list of abiotic factors, but it is a key limiting factor for plants.
[bio31-s5.2.0] The thin limestone soils of the Burren, Co. Clare, support lime-loving plants, because only plants suited to thin, lime-rich soil can grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.1] Fertile, well-drained lowland soils support productive grassland, because they are fertile and drain well.
[bio31-s5.2.2] Under a closed woodland canopy (the roof of leaves formed by the treetops) in summer, little light reaches the floor, so few plants grow there.
[bio31-s5.2.3] Woodland plants such as bluebells grow and flower in spring, before the leaves of the trees open, which means they get the light they need while the canopy is still open.
[bio31-s5.3.b] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment: other organisms and the diseases they carry. Food, disease, competition and predation all limit populations, and some of them also limit one another.
[bio31-s5.3.0] Food: the supply of food usually sets the carrying capacity for animals. For grazers it is the amount of grass or browse (leaves and shoots of shrubs and trees).
[bio31-s5.3.1] Seed-eaters such as squirrels and wood mice do better in years when trees produce large seed crops. Birds of prey such as barn owls are limited by the number of small mammals they can catch.
[bio31-s5.3.2] Disease: disease spreads faster in dense populations, so it limits large populations most. Squirrelpox virus, carried by grey squirrels, kills red squirrels.
[bio31-s5.3.3] Myxomatosis, a viral disease, killed most of Ireland's rabbits after it arrived in the 1950s. Ash dieback is killing ash trees.
[bio31-s5.3.4] Competition: organisms competing for the same limited resource, such as food, light, water, space or mates, reduce each other's share, so fewer survive or breed. Competition between members of the same species (intraspecific) and between different species (interspecific) both limit populations.
[bio31-s5.3.5] Grey squirrels outcompete red squirrels for food in broadleaf woods, and on a woodland floor taller plants shade out shorter ones. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio31-s5.3.6] Predation: predators kill and eat prey, keeping prey numbers below what the food supply alone would allow. Foxes take rabbits and the eggs and chicks of ground-nesting birds such as the curlew.
[bio31-s5.3.7] Pine martens prey on grey squirrels, reducing their numbers where martens are common. Predator numbers are in turn limited by the number of prey, so the two populations affect each other.
[bio31-s5.4.b] Predation normally keeps herbivore numbers down. Where a predator is missing, food becomes the main limit, and the habitat can pay the price.
[bio31-s5.4.0] Ireland's deer, the native red deer and the sika deer introduced to Powerscourt, Co. Wicklow, in 1860, have had no natural predator since wolves died out in Ireland more than two centuries ago.
[bio31-s5.4.1] Their numbers are limited mainly by food, so in parts of Co. Wicklow they have risen high enough to browse away tree seedlings and stop woodland regenerating.
[bio31-s5.4.2] Culling (killing selected animals) by people is used to keep deer below the level that damages habitats, so people take over the role the predator once had.
[bio31-s5.5.b] Limiting factors fall into two groups, depending on whether their effect changes with how crowded the population is. This explains why some factors steady a population while others crash it without warning.
[bio31-s5.5.0] Density-dependent factors have a greater effect as the population becomes more crowded. They are food shortage, competition, disease and predation, and are mostly biotic.
[bio31-s5.5.1] They slow growth as a population approaches carrying capacity, so they regulate population size, pulling it back towards K.
[bio31-s5.5.2] Density-independent factors affect a population regardless of its size, for example a severe frost, a flood, a storm, a drought or a fire, and are mostly abiotic.
[bio31-s5.5.3] Density-independent factors can cause sudden crashes at any population size, because a frost kills the same fraction whether the population is small or large.
[bio31-s5.5.4] Human activity changes carrying capacity. Draining wet grassland and bogs removes curlew breeding habitat, lowering its carrying capacity.
[bio31-s5.5.5] Rewetting a bog or planting hedgerows raises carrying capacity for the species that depend on them, and garden feeders increase the food available for some birds.
[bio31-s5.6.b] A grassland field shows how several limiting factors act on one species at once. For rabbits, the field's carrying capacity is the result of all of them together.
[bio31-s5.6.0] Food: grass is the main density-dependent limit. As rabbit numbers rise, the grass is grazed shorter and competition increases.
[bio31-s5.6.1] Predation by foxes, stoats and birds of prey removes many young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.2] Disease: myxomatosis causes crashes when the population is dense, because the virus spreads fastest between crowded animals.
[bio31-s5.6.3] Space: burrows need well-drained soil, for example on banks and slopes, which limits where warrens (burrow systems) can be dug.
[bio31-s5.6.4] Wet winters, an abiotic and density-independent factor, can flood burrows and kill young rabbits.
[bio31-s5.6.5] The carrying capacity of the field for rabbits is the population that these factors together allow it to support over time.
[bio31-s5.6.6] Diagram: a simple graph of a population rising and levelling off at a horizontal dashed line labelled “carrying capacity (K)”, with arrows labelled “limiting factors (environmental resistance)” pressing down on the curve.

CANDIDATES bio31-s1: Biosphere, ecosystems, habitats and biodiversity
[bio31-s1.0.b] Ecology looks at how living things fit into the world around them. To talk about it clearly, ecologists sort life into levels, each one bigger than the last, and each has its own precise name.
[bio31-s1.0.0] Ecology is the study of the interactions between living organisms, and between organisms and their non-living environment. The word comes from the Greek oikos, meaning house or home, because it is about the place where organisms live and how they get on there.
[bio31-s1.0.1] A population is all the members of one species living in a particular area at the same time, e.g. all the daisies in a field, or all the red squirrels in a woodland. Members can interbreed because they are one species.
[bio31-s1.0.2] A community is all the populations of all the different species living and interacting in an area at the same time, e.g. all the plants, animals, fungi and microorganisms of a woodland. It is the living (biotic) part of an ecosystem only, with no soil, water or air.
[bio31-s1.0.3] The ecological levels, smallest to largest, are: organism, population, community, ecosystem, biosphere. Each level includes the one before it, and from the ecosystem upward the non-living environment is included too.
[bio31-s1.0.4] These levels continue the levels of organisation inside a body (cell, tissue, organ, organ system, organism): the organism is where the two sets of levels meet, and ecology then builds upward from it.
[bio31-s1.0.5] Diagram: nested boxes or a stepped sequence. Show one rabbit (organism), a group of rabbits (population), rabbits with grass, foxes and other species (community), the community with soil, water, air and light (ecosystem), and the Earth's living layer (biosphere). Label each level.
[bio31-s1.1.b] The biosphere is the largest ecological level: the whole of the Earth's life taken together. Its limits and the way matter and energy move through it explain why life depends on the Sun.
[bio31-s1.1.0] The biosphere is the part of the Earth where life exists: the lower atmosphere, the land surface and soil, and the water (fresh water and oceans). It is the sum of all the Earth's ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.1.1] The biosphere is a thin layer compared with the size of the Earth. Life is found from the deep ocean floor to a few kilometres up in the air, in birds, spores and bacteria carried on the wind.
[bio31-s1.1.2] Most life is concentrated in a narrow zone near the surface, because that is where light, liquid water and suitable temperatures are all present.
[bio31-s1.1.3] The biosphere is a single, closed system for matter, because atoms are recycled and not added. It is an open system for energy, because energy enters as sunlight and leaves as heat. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer, The carbon cycle and The nitrogen cycle.
[bio31-s1.2.b] An ecosystem is a community together with the non-living world it lives in. The living and non-living parts affect each other, which is why you have to study both.
[bio31-s1.2.0] An ecosystem is a community of organisms interacting with each other and with their non-living (abiotic) environment in a particular area. It has living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic) parts that affect each other.
[bio31-s1.2.1] Biotic factors are the living parts of the environment and the effects organisms have on each other: food supply, predation (one animal hunting and eating another), competition, disease, parasites, pollinators and decomposers.
[bio31-s1.2.2] Abiotic factors are the non-living physical and chemical conditions of the environment: light, temperature, water, oxygen, soil type and pH, minerals, wind, slope and aspect (the direction a slope faces). There is more on this in Abiotic factors within Investigating an Ecosystem, and in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio31-s1.2.3] Ecosystems have no fixed size: a rock pool, a hedgerow, a lake, a raised bog or an ocean can each be treated as an ecosystem. Their boundaries are often gradual, e.g. a woodland edge merging into grassland.
[bio31-s1.2.4] Every ecosystem needs a continual input of energy, almost always sunlight captured by producers (such as plants) in photosynthesis. Its matter is recycled by decomposers (such as fungi and bacteria), so the ecosystem keeps running.
[bio31-s1.2.5] Producers, consumers and decomposers each have a different role in an ecosystem. Consumers are the animals that get their energy by eating other organisms. The roles of all three are taught in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio31-s1.2.6] Irish land ecosystems include grassland, the most widespread land cover in Ireland, mostly farmed pasture and meadow. Woodland includes the native oak woodlands of Killarney National Park. Peatlands have raised bogs in the midlands and blanket bogs in the uplands and the west.
[bio31-s1.2.7] Irish water and coastal ecosystems include freshwater, such as lakes like Lough Derg, a lake on the River Shannon, and rivers. Coastal and marine ecosystems include rocky shores, sandy shores and sand dunes, salt marshes and the open sea.
[bio31-s1.3.b] An ecosystem is too big to describe where one particular animal lives. Ecologists therefore talk about habitats, and smaller microhabitats, to say exactly where an organism is found.
[bio31-s1.3.0] A habitat is the place where an organism lives, which provides the food, shelter and conditions it needs. A woodlouse's habitat is damp leaf litter, a limpet's is the rocky shore, and a frog's is a pond and the damp vegetation around it.
[bio31-s1.3.1] A microhabitat is a small area within a habitat with its own conditions, e.g. the underside of a stone on a rocky shore, a rock pool, a rotting log, or the bark of a tree. Shade, moisture and temperature there can differ greatly from the surroundings.
[bio31-s1.3.2] One ecosystem contains many habitats: a woodland contains the canopy, the trunk, the leaf litter and the soil, each with different species. A habitat is the organism's 'address'; its role there is its niche, which is dealt with in The niche.
[bio31-s1.4.b] Biodiversity is more than a count of species. It describes how much variety there is in life, and it can be measured at three different levels, from genes up to whole habitats.
[bio31-s1.4.0] Biodiversity (biological diversity) is the variety of life in an area, or on Earth, at all its levels. It is usually described at three levels: genetic diversity, species diversity and ecosystem (habitat) diversity.
[bio31-s1.4.1] Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles (different versions of a gene) within a species or population, e.g. the different breeds of cattle, or the variation in disease resistance among wild ash trees. It allows a population to adapt by natural selection when conditions change.
[bio31-s1.4.2] Low genetic diversity left the Irish potato crop of the 1840s vulnerable, because it was largely one variety, so when blight (a disease) arrived it destroyed it.
[bio31-s1.4.3] Species diversity is the variety of species in an area. It has two parts: species richness, the number of different species present, and species evenness, how evenly the individuals are spread among those species.
[bio31-s1.4.4] Two areas with the same species can differ in diversity if one is dominated by a single species. The Simpson's Diversity Index, which combines richness and evenness into one number, is Higher Level only. There is more on this in Simpson's Diversity Index.
[bio31-s1.4.5] Ecosystem (habitat) diversity is the variety of different ecosystems and habitats in a region, e.g. Ireland's bogs, woodlands, grasslands, lakes, rivers and coasts. A region with many habitats supports more species, because each habitat suits different organisms.
[bio31-s1.5.b] Life is not spread evenly across the Earth. Some places hold far more species than others, and Ireland is a good local example of a place with fewer.
[bio31-s1.5.0] Biodiversity is generally higher in warm, wet, stable environments and lower in cold or extreme ones. Tropical rainforests and coral reefs are the most species-rich ecosystems.
[bio31-s1.5.1] Ireland has fewer species than Britain or mainland Europe, partly because it became an island after the last ice age, before many species had recolonised it from the south.
[bio31-s1.5.2] In brief, biodiversity provides food, medicines and materials, supports processes such as pollination and nutrient cycling, and makes ecosystems more stable. There is more on this, and on its loss and conservation, in Biodiversity loss and conservation.

CANDIDATES bio32-s3: Abiotic factors
[bio32-s3.0.b] Abiotic factors are the non-living features of an environment, such as light, temperature and soil. To explain why a species lives where it does, you must measure them reliably, and a few rules apply whichever factor you measure. There is more on abiotic factors as limiting factors in Carrying capacity and limiting factors.
[bio32-s3.0.0] The specification groups abiotic factors as air (climatic) factors, ground (topographic) factors, aquatic (water) factors and edaphic (soil) factors. “Topographic” means the shape of the land, such as slope and aspect, and “edaphic” means to do with the soil.
[bio32-s3.0.1] The specification names water, oxygen, space, temperature, soil, aspect, wind speed and slope, so you need an instrument and a method for each of these.
[bio32-s3.0.2] Measure abiotic factors at the same places as the quadrats (square frames that mark out a sample area) or transect points (sampling points along a line), and at the same time as the survey, so that each measurement can be linked to the species found there.
[bio32-s3.0.3] Many factors (light, temperature, wind, humidity) change during the day and with the weather. Take all readings within a short period under similar conditions, and record the time and weather, so that readings from different places can be compared fairly.
[bio32-s3.0.4] Repeat each reading (e.g. three times) and use the mean, which reduces the effect of one odd reading.
[bio32-s3.0.5] Electronic probes connected to a data logger (a device that stores readings automatically) can record factors such as temperature, light intensity, humidity, pH and dissolved oxygen continuously over hours or days, and at several points at once. This removes reading errors and shows changes over time.
[bio32-s3.0.6] Calibrate meters before use, which means checking them against a known standard, e.g. a pH meter with buffer solutions of known pH, so that its readings are accurate.
[bio32-s3.1.b] Air factors describe the climate around the organisms. Light and temperature both affect plant growth and animal activity, and each has its own instrument.
[bio32-s3.1.0] Light intensity is the energy for photosynthesis (how plants make food), so it affects plant growth and which plants can grow in shade; it also affects animal activity.
[bio32-s3.1.1] Light intensity is measured with a light meter (lux meter), in lux.
[bio32-s3.1.2] Hold the light sensor horizontally, facing up, at the same height each time (e.g. at ground level or at the top of the vegetation), keep your shadow off it, and take readings quickly, as light changes with cloud cover.
[bio32-s3.1.3] Air temperature controls the rate of enzyme reactions (the chemical reactions in cells), so it affects the activity and growth of plants and of cold-blooded animals, whose body temperature follows their surroundings.
[bio32-s3.1.4] Air temperature is measured with a thermometer or temperature probe, in °C, held in the shade at a set height above the ground (e.g. 1 m) so that direct sunlight doesn’t heat it and give a false high reading.
[bio32-s3.1.5] A maximum–minimum thermometer left on site records the highest and lowest temperatures over a period, e.g. 24 hours, so you do not have to be there at the hottest and coldest moments.
[bio32-s3.2.b] Moving air, water vapour and rain all change how quickly organisms lose or gain water. Each is measured with a different instrument.
[bio32-s3.2.0] Wind speed increases water loss by evaporation and transpiration (loss of water vapour from leaves), cools organisms, can damage tall plants, and makes flying difficult for insects.
[bio32-s3.2.1] Wind speed is measured with an anemometer (a cup anemometer or a hand-held digital one), in metres per second (m/s) or km/h, held at a set height facing into the wind.
[bio32-s3.2.2] Take the mean of several readings, as wind gusts. Wind direction is found with a wind vane or a compass.
[bio32-s3.2.3] Humidity (water in the air) affects water loss from plants and from animals with moist skins; slugs and woodlice need humid places because they dry out quickly.
[bio32-s3.2.4] Humidity is measured with a hygrometer or humidity probe, as relative humidity (%).
[bio32-s3.2.5] Rainfall (water supply) is measured with a rain gauge: a funnel draining into a measuring cylinder, set in an open place so that nothing shelters it, in mm, read at the same time each day.
[bio32-s3.3.b] The shape of the land decides how much water drains away and how much sunlight a place gets. Space decides how many organisms can fit in a habitat. Slope, aspect and space are all measured directly in the field.
[bio32-s3.3.0] Slope affects drainage: water runs off steep slopes, so they are drier and soils are thinner, while hollows at the bottom collect water. Slope also affects the amount of sunlight received.
[bio32-s3.3.1] Slope is measured with a clinometer (an instrument for measuring angles), in degrees. Two people of the same eye height (or two poles marked at the same height) stand up and down the slope a set distance apart.
[bio32-s3.3.2] One person sights the other’s eye level (or the mark) through the clinometer and reads the angle.
[bio32-s3.3.3] Aspect is the compass direction a slope faces, measured with a compass, because direction decides how much sun the slope gets.
[bio32-s3.3.4] In Ireland (northern hemisphere) a south-facing slope receives more direct sunlight than a north-facing one, so it is warmer and drier and plants there start growing earlier in spring.
[bio32-s3.3.5] Space is the room available for organisms to live, grow, find shelter, nest or attach.
[bio32-s3.3.6] Measure the area of the habitat (length × width with tapes or a trundle wheel), and the percentage of bare ground in each quadrat (squares of a gridded quadrat with no plants), which shows the space available for new seedlings.
[bio32-s3.3.7] Density (individuals per m², from surveys) shows how crowded a species is. Shortage of space is a cause of competition. There is more on this in Biotic factors and relationships.
[bio32-s3.4.b] Soil factors are called edaphic. Soil temperature and pH both help decide which plants and soil organisms can live in a place, and each is measured on a small soil sample or with a probe.
[bio32-s3.4.0] Soil temperature affects root growth, germination and the activity of soil organisms and decomposers (organisms that break down dead material).
[bio32-s3.4.1] Push a soil thermometer or temperature probe into the soil to a set depth (e.g. 10 cm), leave it for a minute or two to settle, and read it in °C.
[bio32-s3.4.2] Soil pH affects which plants grow (by changing which minerals are available) and which soil organisms live there; earthworms are scarce in acid soils.
[bio32-s3.4.3] Soil pH method, step 1: take a small soil sample from a set depth.
[bio32-s3.4.4] Soil pH method, step 2: put about 1 cm depth of soil in a test tube, add about the same amount of barium sulfate (which makes the clay particles settle, so that the liquid clears), then distilled water and a few drops of universal indicator.
[bio32-s3.4.5] Soil pH method, step 3: stopper and shake, then leave to settle.
[bio32-s3.4.6] Soil pH method, step 4: compare the colour of the liquid with the universal indicator colour chart to read the pH.
[bio32-s3.4.7] Alternatively, mix the soil with distilled water and use a calibrated pH meter, rinsing the probe in distilled water between samples. Use distilled water, as tap water has its own pH and would change the result.
[bio32-s3.5.b] Soil water decides which plants and soil animals can live in a place. It is measured by drying a soil sample and finding how much mass it loses.
[bio32-s3.5.0] Soil water content affects which plants grow (rushes grow in wet soil) and soil animals (earthworms need moist soil); waterlogged soil lacks air.
[bio32-s3.5.1] Soil water content method, step 1: weigh an empty evaporating dish.
[bio32-s3.5.2] Soil water content method, step 2: add a soil sample and weigh again, to find the fresh mass of soil by subtraction.
[bio32-s3.5.3] Soil water content method, step 3: heat in an oven at about 100–105 °C (hot enough to evaporate water, not to burn humus) for 24 hours.
[bio32-s3.5.4] Soil water content method, step 4: cool in a desiccator (so the soil doesn’t reabsorb water from the air) and reweigh.
[bio32-s3.5.5] Soil water content method, step 5: repeat the heating, cooling and weighing until the mass stays the same (constant mass), showing that all the water has gone.
[bio32-s3.6.b] The water lost on drying is the water the soil held, so the calculation compares the mass before and after heating.
[bio32-s3.6.0] Percentage water = (fresh mass − dry mass) ÷ fresh mass × 100. It is worked out from the fresh mass, which is the mass before drying.
[bio32-s3.6.1] Worked example: fresh soil 50.0 g; dry soil 38.0 g at constant mass. Water = 12.0 g. Percentage water = 12.0 ÷ 50.0 × 100 = 24%.
[bio32-s3.6.2] A soil-moisture probe (meter) gives a quick reading in the field, but less accurately than drying the soil.
[bio32-s3.7.b] Soil is a mixture of solid particles and air spaces. Measuring the air content tells you how well roots and soil animals can breathe.
[bio32-s3.7.0] Soil air content matters because roots and soil animals need oxygen for respiration. Air spaces are larger in sandy soils and smaller in clay and waterlogged soils.
[bio32-s3.7.1] Soil air content method, step 1: find the volume of an empty can by filling it with water from a graduated cylinder.
[bio32-s3.7.2] Soil air content method, step 2: push the can, open end down, into the soil until it is full, dig it out and level the soil with the rim.
[bio32-s3.7.3] Soil air content method, step 3: put a volume of water equal to the can’s volume in a large graduated cylinder.
[bio32-s3.7.4] Soil air content method, step 4: add the soil, and stir with a rod to release the trapped air.
[bio32-s3.7.5] Soil air content method, step 5: read the new level.
[bio32-s3.7.6] Volume of air = (volume of water + volume of soil) − final reading. Percentage air = volume of air ÷ volume of soil × 100.
[bio32-s3.7.7] Worked example: can volume 100 cm³; 100 cm³ water; reading after adding soil and stirring 185 cm³. Air = 200 − 185 = 15 cm³; percentage air = 15 ÷ 100 × 100 = 15%.
[bio32-s3.8.b] Humus is the organic part of soil. Because it burns away when heated strongly and the mineral part does not, the loss in mass measures how much humus there was.
[bio32-s3.8.0] Humus is the dark, partly decomposed remains of dead organisms in soil. It holds water and minerals and feeds decomposers.
[bio32-s3.8.1] Humus method, step 1: weigh a crucible (a small heat-proof pot).
[bio32-s3.8.2] Humus method, step 2: add dry soil from the water-content method and weigh it.
[bio32-s3.8.3] Humus method, step 3: heat it strongly over a Bunsen burner (lid partly on) until the humus burns away (about 15 minutes).
[bio32-s3.8.4] Humus method, step 4: cool in a desiccator and reweigh.
[bio32-s3.8.5] Humus method, step 5: repeat until constant mass, so that you know all the humus has burned away.
[bio32-s3.8.6] Percentage humus = loss in mass ÷ mass of dry soil × 100, so it uses the dry mass because the water has already been removed.
[bio32-s3.8.7] Worked example: dry soil 38.0 g; after burning, 34.2 g. Loss = 3.8 g; percentage humus = 3.8 ÷ 38.0 × 100 = 10% of the dry soil.
[bio32-s3.9.b] Soil texture describes the sizes of the particles in the soil. A simple settling test separates them into layers.
[bio32-s3.9.0] Soil texture method: shake soil with water in a graduated cylinder and leave it to settle.
[bio32-s3.9.1] The particles settle in layers, largest at the bottom: stones and gravel, then sand, silt and clay, with humus floating on top.
[bio32-s3.9.2] The proportions of the layers show the soil type, such as sandy or clay soil.
[bio32-s3.10.b] In water, temperature, dissolved oxygen and pH are measured. Temperature matters for oxygen because cold water holds more of it.
[bio32-s3.10.0] Water temperature is measured with a thermometer or probe in °C, at a set depth. It affects how much oxygen water holds (cold water holds more) and the activity of aquatic organisms.
[bio32-s3.10.1] Dissolved oxygen is measured with an oxygen meter (probe), in mg per litre, or with a chemical test kit.
[bio32-s3.10.2] Animals such as stonefly larvae need high oxygen. Low oxygen is a sign of organic pollution, because decomposers use up the oxygen as they break down the waste.
[bio32-s3.10.3] Water pH is measured with a pH meter or universal indicator paper, as for soil.
[bio32-s3.11.b] Moving water, cloudy water and salty water each create different conditions for organisms. Rate of flow is measured with a simple timed float.
[bio32-s3.11.0] Rate of flow (current speed): mark two points a set distance apart along a straight stretch of stream (e.g. 10 m); time a float (e.g. an orange or a dog biscuit) from one point to the other.
[bio32-s3.11.1] Repeat the timing three times and take the mean time; speed = distance ÷ time.
[bio32-s3.11.2] Worked example: mean time 25 s over 10 m: 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 m/s.
[bio32-s3.11.3] Fast flow carries away animals that can’t cling on and keeps the water well oxygenated.
[bio32-s3.11.4] Turbidity (cloudiness) is measured with a Secchi disc, a black-and-white disc on a marked rope, which is lowered until it is no longer visible; the depth is recorded.
[bio32-s3.11.5] Cloudy water lets less light reach water plants, so they photosynthesise less.
[bio32-s3.11.6] Salinity (saltiness, in seawater, rock pools and estuaries) is measured with a conductivity or salinity meter. Rock pools become saltier as water evaporates in sunshine.
[bio32-s3.12.b] You must be able to investigate quantitatively (using numbers) how an abiotic factor affects the distribution (where) and abundance (how many) of a species. The standard example is light and daisies on a belt transect (a strip across a habitat, sampled with quadrats at set intervals).
[bio32-s3.12.0] Aim: to investigate quantitatively how light intensity affects the distribution and abundance of the daisy in a grassland, along a belt transect from under a hedge or tree into the open field.
[bio32-s3.12.1] Hypothesis (a testable prediction): “The number of daisies per quadrat increases as light intensity increases.”
[bio32-s3.12.2] Reason for the hypothesis: the daisy is a low-growing rosette plant that needs plenty of light for photosynthesis, and is shaded out where light is low.
[bio32-s3.12.3] Independent variable: light intensity. It varies naturally along the transect, so it is measured, not set by the student.
[bio32-s3.12.4] Dependent variable: the abundance of daisies (number per quadrat, or percentage cover).
[bio32-s3.12.5] Controlled variables: quadrat size, the interval between quadrats, the counting rule, the height and direction of the light sensor, the time of day (all readings within a short period) and the weather.
[bio32-s3.13.b] A field study cannot hold everything else constant. This limits what the results can prove, so it is worth knowing before you plan the method.
[bio32-s3.13.0] No true control is possible: other factors also change along the transect, such as soil water content, leaf litter, trampling, and competition from taller plants near the hedge. Measure these too where possible, so that their effect can be considered.
[bio32-s3.13.1] This is why a field investigation shows a correlation (two things changing together), not proof of cause.
[bio32-s3.13.2] Equipment: two long tape measures (one for the transect), a gridded quadrat, a light meter, pegs, a recording sheet, and a soil-moisture probe or soil-sampling equipment for the second factor.
[bio32-s3.13.3] Other suitable pairs of factor and species: soil water content and rushes (more in wet ground); trampling or soil compaction across a path and plantain or grass cover; soil pH and a named plant; height above low tide on a rocky shore and the cover of each seaweed.
[bio32-s3.14.b] The method lays quadrats at regular intervals along the transect, then counts daisies and measures light at each one.
[bio32-s3.14.0] Step 1: stretch a tape measure from the base of the hedge (0 m) straight out into the open field, e.g. for 10 m.
[bio32-s3.14.1] Step 2: place a gridded quadrat at 0 m and then at every 2 m along the tape (an interrupted belt transect, because it samples at intervals rather than along the whole line).
[bio32-s3.14.2] Step 3: in each quadrat, count the daisy rosettes (or the squares in which daisy covers more than half, for percentage cover).
[bio32-s3.14.3] Step 4: at each quadrat, hold the light meter at ground level, facing up, and take three readings in quick succession; record the mean in lux.
[bio32-s3.14.4] Do all the light readings within a few minutes, ideally under even cloud, because light changes quickly when clouds move.
[bio32-s3.15.b] Repeating the transect and recording results in a table make the data reliable and ready to graph. Fieldwork also carries small risks to manage.
[bio32-s3.15.0] Step 5: measure the second factor (e.g. soil water content) at each quadrat, so that its effect can be compared with that of light.
[bio32-s3.15.1] Step 6: repeat the whole transect at least twice more, parallel to the first and a few metres away, and calculate the mean count at each distance.
[bio32-s3.15.2] Step 7: record the results in a table, and plot a scatter graph of the number of daisies (y-axis) against light intensity (x-axis), or plot both against distance from the hedge.
[bio32-s3.15.3] Safety: take care at hedges with thorns, nettles and brambles; wear gloves when handling soil; wash hands afterwards; avoid trampling the quadrat sites before they are sampled.
[bio32-s3.15.4] Diagram: a belt transect running from a hedge into open grassland with quadrats every 2 m and a light meter at one quadrat. Label the hedge, 0 m, the tape, the quadrats, the light meter and the distances.
[bio32-s3.16.b] Plotting the data shows whether daisy numbers rise with light. The sample data below are illustrative, an example of what results might look like, not real measurements.
[bio32-s3.16.0] Sample data: distance 0 m: light 800 lux, 0 daisies; 2 m: 2500 lux, 1 daisy; 4 m: 6000 lux, 4 daisies; 6 m: 9500 lux, 9 daisies; 8 m: 12 000 lux, 12 daisies; 10 m: 12 500 lux, 11 daisies.
[bio32-s3.16.1] Expected result: daisies are absent or few in deep shade close to the hedge, and become more abundant as light intensity increases into the open field.
[bio32-s3.16.2] Daisies level off once light no longer limits them; other factors, such as space or competition with grass, then limit them.
[bio32-s3.16.3] The scatter graph shows a positive correlation: as light intensity rises, so does the number of daisies.
[bio32-s3.17.b] A conclusion must say whether the data support the hypothesis and how far they can be trusted. Naming the sources of error shows you understand the limits of fieldwork.
[bio32-s3.17.0] Conclusion: the results support the hypothesis that daisy abundance increases with light intensity along this transect.
[bio32-s3.17.1] Because soil water and leaf litter also changed, the result shows a correlation, not proof that light alone is the cause.
[bio32-s3.17.2] A laboratory experiment growing daisies at different light intensities, with other factors controlled, could test the cause.
[bio32-s3.17.3] Sources of error: light intensity changes rapidly with cloud cover, so readings at different quadrats may not be comparable (take readings quickly, or use data loggers at several points at once).
[bio32-s3.17.4] Other sources of error: a single transect may not be representative (use several); counting errors and inconsistent edge rules; misidentification of small daisy rosettes; other factors varying along the transect; the survey is a snapshot of one day and season.
[bio32-s3.17.5] Primary data are the student’s own counts and measurements. They can be compared with secondary data (e.g. published descriptions of where daisies grow) when drawing conclusions.
[bio32-s3.18.b] Each habitat has its own key abiotic factors. A rocky shore, a woodland and a freshwater stream show how different factors dominate.
[bio32-s3.18.0] Rocky shore: the main factor is the time spent out of water at low tide (exposure), which rises with height up the shore and brings drying, temperature change and, in rock pools, salinity change.
[bio32-s3.18.1] This produces zonation, bands of species at different heights. Brown seaweeds (wracks) on Irish shores are zoned from the top down: channelled wrack (highest, survives longest out of water), spiral wrack, bladder wrack and knotted wrack (middle shore), serrated wrack (lower shore), and kelps (at and below the low-tide mark).
[bio32-s3.18.2] Zonation on a rocky shore is measured with a belt transect from low to high water, with height measured using a ranging pole and level.
[bio32-s3.18.3] Woodland: light intensity under the canopy (lowest in summer when trees are in leaf), humidity, leaf-litter depth, soil humus and pH are the key factors.
[bio32-s3.18.4] Many woodland floor plants (e.g. bluebells) grow and flower in spring before the canopy closes, while enough light still reaches the ground.
[bio32-s3.18.5] Freshwater: dissolved oxygen, temperature, rate of flow and pH are the key factors.
[bio32-s3.18.6] Fast, cold streams are rich in oxygen and support stonefly and mayfly larvae; still or polluted water low in oxygen supports fewer species.
