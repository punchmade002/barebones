TARGET bio2-s7: The Biology in Practice Investigation
[bio2-s7.0.b] Leaving Certificate Biology is not assessed by a single exam. Alongside the written paper there is a second component in which you do your own scientific investigation, so it is worth knowing how the two fit together.
[bio2-s7.0.0] Leaving Certificate Biology has two assessment components: a written examination worth 60%, taken at Higher and Ordinary level, and an additional assessment component, the Biology in Practice Investigation, worth 40%.
[bio2-s7.0.1] The investigation is based on one common brief (the same task for all students) and is worth 200 marks. It is assessed at the level at which you take the written paper, so only the written examination has two levels.
[bio2-s7.0.2] Both components are set and examined by the State Examinations Commission (SEC), the body that runs the state exams. The investigation is not coursework marked by your own teacher.
[bio2-s7.0.3] You complete the work during the course and, in Year 2 (sixth year), submit to the SEC evidence of your ability to conduct scientific research on a particular issue and to use appropriate primary data (data you collect yourself) to investigate aspects of that issue.
[bio2-s7.0.4] The specification envisages that the investigation will take up to 20 hours to complete. Further details are given in the Guidelines for the Biology in Practice Investigation.
[bio2-s7.1.b] The investigation is there to let you show what you have learned across the whole course, not to test one topic. It also builds the habits of a working scientist.
[bio2-s7.1.0] It gives you an opportunity to display evidence of your learning throughout the course, in particular the learning in the Unifying Strand (Nature of Science): the outcomes in this chapter and in Scientific Knowledge, Science in Society and Biological Reasoning.
[bio2-s7.1.1] It is designed to be integrated into the flow of teaching and learning, to be motivating and relevant, and to show the relevance of biology to students’ lives, so it uses real issues rather than abstract ones.
[bio2-s7.1.2] It also draws together the learning outcomes and the cross-cutting themes of Health, Sustainability and Technology, which run through every strand of the course.
[bio2-s7.1.3] It lets you pursue your own interests in biology, make your own investigative decisions, gain a depth of conceptual understanding and self-regulate your learning (plan, monitor and adjust it yourself).
[bio2-s7.1.4] The specification stresses that research and experimentation are “more about engaging with and learning from the process, rather than seeking a perfect answer”. This is why an experiment that does not go to plan can still be valuable.
[bio2-s7.1.5] You should give an authentic account of how your investigative work unfolded, discuss and explain the outcomes, and say how you might revise aspects of the process. There is more on this in Reflecting on investigations.
[bio2-s7.2.b] Every year the SEC publishes a brief, the document that tells you exactly what the investigation must cover. It is the starting point for everything you do.
[bio2-s7.2.0] The SEC publishes an Investigation Brief every year, in term two of Year 1 (fifth year) of the course, so you have time to plan; the brief for the 2027 examination was issued in January 2026.
[bio2-s7.2.1] The 2027 brief is set on membranes, osmosis and food preservation.
[bio2-s7.2.2] The brief sets out the specific requirements of the investigation for that year, so it is the final word on what you must do.
[bio2-s7.2.3] It allows you to develop your thinking and ideas on areas related to the brief that you would like to pursue.
[bio2-s7.2.4] It helps teachers and students plan, and includes stimulus material (articles, data or images) to set a context for the investigation.
[bio2-s7.2.5] It allows you to develop an investigative log (a running record of your work and decisions) that you can draw on as you complete the investigation. There is more on this in Reflecting on investigations.
[bio2-s7.3.b] The work has two parts, followed by an evidence-based argument and a submitted report. Using both research and an experiment is what makes it more than just a lab write-up.
[bio2-s7.3.0] Part 1 is scientific research on an issue related to the brief, so you start by reading, not experimenting. You gather, process and evaluate information from secondary sources (work done by others, such as articles and reports), and what you learn may help to inform your experimental work.
[bio2-s7.3.1] Evaluating how reliable those sources are, including checking for bias, matters because a weak source weakens your whole argument. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio2-s7.3.2] Part 2 is an experiment related to an issue within the brief, which gives you primary data of your own. You generate a hypothesis (a testable prediction), plan and design the experiment, carry it out and gather primary data, then analyse the data and form conclusions.
[bio2-s7.3.3] The skills for Part 2 are in Questioning and predicting, Designing and conducting investigations, Objectivity and data quality and Producing and analysing data.
[bio2-s7.3.4] You then develop an evidence-based argument in response to the brief, drawing on both your research and your experiment, so each part supports your case, because research gives the background and the experiment gives your own evidence.
[bio2-s7.3.5] On completion you submit a report of the investigation in Year 2, in a format prescribed by the SEC. General report-writing skills are in Communicating results, but the SEC sets the actual format, so follow its instructions rather than your own layout.
[bio2-s7.3.6] Schools have a high degree of autonomy in planning and organising the completion of the investigation. A separate document, Guidelines for the Biology in Practice Investigation, gives guidance on its organisation, implementation and oversight.
[bio2-s7.4.b] The specification sums up what the investigation asks of you in four requirements. They are also the reasons the descriptors of quality look the way they do.
[bio2-s7.4.0] It requires you to consider issues related to real-world applications of biology, such as how biological ideas are used in health, sustainability or technology.
[bio2-s7.4.1] It requires you to demonstrate investigative skills, such as the planning, practical and analysis skills used to answer a scientific question.
[bio2-s7.4.2] It requires you to relate your investigative work to the work of scientists in society, for example by asking how professionals would tackle the same question.
[bio2-s7.4.3] It requires you to communicate your findings appropriately and effectively.
[bio2-s7.5.b] The SEC brief sets the report's headings, limits and format, and allocates the 200 marks in four blocks of 50. The descriptors of quality that follow describe the standard expected within them.
[bio2-s7.5.0] Criterion A, 50 marks: title and introduction, research question and hypothesis, background research and evaluation of secondary data, and references.
[bio2-s7.5.1] Criterion B, 50 marks: designing and planning, and conducting the experiment, including safety, fairness, accuracy and choice of equipment.
[bio2-s7.5.2] Criterion C, 50 marks: primary data and its presentation, data analysis, and conclusions.
[bio2-s7.5.3] Criterion D, 50 marks: scientific literacy, so it rewards good writing and thinking throughout (communication, coherence, relevance and a reflective approach), which is judged across the whole report rather than in one section.
[bio2-s7.6.b] Besides the marks, the 2027 brief fixes exactly how the report must look and how it is handed in. Following these rules is part of doing the task properly.
[bio2-s7.6.0] The report uses seven numbered headings, each on a new page: Title and Introduction; Background Research; Designing and Planning; Conducting the Experiment; Data and Data Analysis; Conclusions; References.
[bio2-s7.6.1] The report body is no more than 1,500 words, so you must be concise (references, data tables, formulae, equations and labels are not counted), with no more than 20 images and no video.
[bio2-s7.6.2] Text is Arial and black, with 14-point bold headings and 12-point body text at 1.5 line spacing, portrait pages, 20 mm margins with nothing in them, and page numbers at the bottom centre.
[bio2-s7.6.3] The experiment and primary data collection are carried out under the direct supervision of the biology teacher, and every source, including any use of AI, is referenced.
[bio2-s7.6.4] The completed SEC cover page goes first. The report is converted to PDF, printed and proof-read, then given to the teacher by Friday 26 February 2027 with Authentication Form P.2 signed by the student, teacher and principal.
[bio2-s7.7.b] The SEC judges the quality of your work against four descriptors, each with a high, moderate and low level. The first is about how well you understand the biology behind your investigation.
[bio2-s7.7.0] High: you engage thoroughly with the concepts being investigated; explain clearly and accurately, using appropriate means, the biological phenomena involved; and, where applicable, pose a testable hypothesis that is underpinned by biological theory and clearly describe the purpose of the investigation.
[bio2-s7.7.1] Moderate: you show good engagement with the concepts; describe the biological phenomena involved; and, where applicable, pose a testable hypothesis and outline the purpose of the investigation.
[bio2-s7.7.2] Low: you show limited engagement with the concepts; outline the biological phenomena involved; and are provided with a testable hypothesis to investigate rather than making your own.
[bio2-s7.8.b] The second descriptor is about how well you planned and carried out the experiment, because good data can only come from a good design.
[bio2-s7.8.0] High: you use clear, thorough and appropriate investigative design and methods to collect primary data, leading to high quality data, presentation and analysis, because good data can only come from a good method.
[bio2-s7.8.1] Moderate: you use clear and appropriate design and methods, leading to good quality data, presentation and analysis.
[bio2-s7.8.2] Low: your design and methods are unclear, leading to limited data, presentation and analysis.
[bio2-s7.9.b] The third descriptor is about what you do with your results: whether your conclusions follow from the data and whether you recognise the weaknesses in your own design.
[bio2-s7.9.0] High: you draw valid conclusions that are justified by the data and relate to any hypotheses made, and thoroughly evaluate the investigation, acknowledging sources of error in the investigative design, so your evaluation must be honest and specific.
[bio2-s7.9.1] Moderate: you draw conclusions that relate to any hypotheses made, and identify potential sources of error in the investigative design.
[bio2-s7.9.2] Low: you draw limited conclusions, and potential sources of error in the investigative design are not identified.
[bio2-s7.10.b] The fourth descriptor looks beyond your own experiment. It asks how your findings connect to what scientists do and to wider issues in society.
[bio2-s7.10.0] High: you offer a considered reflection that locates the outcomes of the investigation within broader issues relating to the work of a scientist or science and society.
[bio2-s7.10.1] Moderate: you reflect on how the outcomes relate to the work of a scientist or science and society.
[bio2-s7.10.2] Low: you make limited links between the outcomes and the work of a scientist or science and society, so your findings stay isolated from the wider picture.
[bio2-s7.11.b] This is study advice drawn from the wording of the descriptors, not an SEC rule. Reading the four descriptors side by side shows what moves work from one level to the next.
[bio2-s7.11.0] The differences between levels are in the verbs and qualifiers: “explain clearly and accurately” against “describe” against “outline”; “thoroughly evaluate” against “identify” against not identifying errors; and “considered reflection” within “broader issues” against “limited links”.
[bio2-s7.11.1] Posing your own testable hypothesis, grounded in biological theory, is a feature of the high and moderate levels. Being given a hypothesis is described at the low level.
[bio2-s7.11.2] Acknowledging and evaluating sources of error in the design is expected for high achievement. A perfect result is not required, but an honest evaluation is, because it shows you understand how reliable your results are. This fits the specification’s stress on learning from the process.
[bio2-s7.11.3] The fourth descriptor needs explicit links between your own findings and the work of scientists or wider issues in science and society, for example how scientists would investigate the same issue on a larger scale, or the health, sustainability or technology issue in the brief.

CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.

CANDIDATES bio2-s4: Producing and analysing data
[bio2-s4.0.b] Every investigation produces data, and the type of data decides how you can record, process and graph it. Biologists sort data by what it describes, how it behaves and who collected it.
[bio2-s4.0.0] Qualitative data are descriptions or categories that are not numerical, e.g. a positive Benedict’s test for a reducing sugar, which turns from blue to brick-red through green, yellow and orange; the presence or absence of a species; or “cloudy” or “clear”.
[bio2-s4.0.1] Quantitative data are numerical measurements or counts with units, e.g. volume of oxygen in cm³, mass in g, or number of daisies per quadrat (a square frame laid on the ground to sample a small area).
[bio2-s4.0.2] Quantitative data are more objective (less dependent on the observer’s judgement) and can be analysed mathematically, so they are preferred where possible. A qualitative result can often be made quantitative, e.g. a colorimeter (an instrument that measures how much light a solution absorbs) reading instead of a colour.
[bio2-s4.0.3] Continuous data can take any value in a range, including decimals, e.g. mass, temperature or time.
[bio2-s4.0.4] Discrete data are whole-number counts, because you cannot have half a seed, e.g. number of seeds or number of stomata (the pores in a leaf).
[bio2-s4.0.5] Categoric data are named groups, e.g. eye colour or type of habitat.
[bio2-s4.0.6] Primary data are collected by the investigator. Secondary data are collected by somebody else, e.g. published results, databases or other groups’ results.
[bio2-s4.0.7] Selecting data means choosing the data that are relevant to the question and of good enough quality. Secondary data can extend or check primary data, for example by adding values you could not measure yourself. There is more on judging a secondary source in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio2-s4.1.b] A results table is where raw data first go, so a clear layout makes the later calculations and graph easy. A few fixed conventions apply every time.
[bio2-s4.1.0] The independent variable (the factor you change) goes in the first column. The dependent variable (the factor you measure) goes in the following columns, with a column for each repeat and a column for the mean.
[bio2-s4.1.1] Each column heading gives the quantity and its unit, e.g. “Temperature (°C)” or “Volume of oxygen (cm³)”. Units go in the heading, not after every number, which keeps the table uncluttered.
[bio2-s4.1.2] Record every reading to the same number of decimal places, matching the resolution of the instrument (the smallest change it can show). A balance reading to 0.01 g gives 2.45 g, not 2.4 g or 2.453 g.
[bio2-s4.1.3] List the values of the independent variable in order, so the pattern can be seen down the column.
[bio2-s4.1.4] Record raw results straight into the table during the investigation, including any that look wrong. Writing them down later from memory risks errors, and a result that looks wrong may turn out to matter.
[bio2-s4.2.b] Raw readings are processed into a few summary numbers before they are graphed. The mean gives the typical value of repeats, and the range shows how closely the repeats agree. The worked numbers here are checked and illustrative.
[bio2-s4.2.0] Mean: add the values and divide by the number of values. Anomalous values (ones that do not fit the pattern) are left out of the mean, and the fact is stated.
[bio2-s4.2.1] Worked example for the mean: the final masses of four replicate (repeat) potato cylinders are 2.45 g, 2.44 g, 2.46 g and 2.10 g.
[bio2-s4.2.2] The value 2.10 g is anomalous, so the mean of the other three is (2.45 + 2.44 + 2.46) ÷ 3 = 7.35 ÷ 3 = 2.45 g.
[bio2-s4.2.3] Range: the largest value minus the smallest. It shows the spread of repeat readings, and a small range means the readings are precise (close to each other).
[bio2-s4.2.4] Worked example for the range: 2.46 − 2.44 = 0.02 g, a small spread, so the readings are precise.
[bio2-s4.2.5] The range of the independent variable is the lowest to highest value tested, e.g. 0 °C to 60 °C. It tells the reader which conditions the results cover.
[bio2-s4.3.b] Two further calculations let you compare results fairly. Percentage change deals with samples that started at different sizes, and rate deals with changes that took different times.
[bio2-s4.3.0] Percentage change = (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100. It allows samples that started with different masses to be compared, because each change is expressed relative to its own starting value.
[bio2-s4.3.1] Worked example for percentage change: a cylinder with an initial mass of 2.50 g and a final mass of 2.45 g has a percentage change of (2.45 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −2.0%.
[bio2-s4.3.2] A negative percentage change is a loss, and a positive value is a gain.
[bio2-s4.3.3] Rate = amount of change ÷ time. For example, 24 cm³ of oxygen in 4 minutes is a rate of 6 cm³ per minute.
[bio2-s4.3.4] When the time taken for something to happen is measured, e.g. the time for starch to disappear, the rate can be calculated as 1 ÷ time. A time of 50 s gives 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s⁻¹.
[bio2-s4.3.5] A shorter time means a faster rate, because 1 ÷ time gets larger as the time gets smaller.
[bio2-s4.4.b] Different data suit different graphs. The choice depends on whether the variables are continuous, discrete or categoric (see the previous block’s data types) and on what you want to show.
[bio2-s4.4.0] Line graph: used when both variables are continuous, e.g. rate of enzyme activity against temperature.
[bio2-s4.4.1] Bar chart: used when the independent variable is categoric or discrete, e.g. the number of a species in three habitats. Bars are separate and of equal width, because the categories are distinct groups.
[bio2-s4.4.2] Histogram: shows the frequency (how many) of a continuous variable divided into classes, e.g. the number of students in each height range. Bars touch, because the classes run on from one to the next.
[bio2-s4.4.3] Scatter graph: used to look for a correlation (a link) between two measured variables, e.g. light intensity and the number of a plant species in quadrats.
[bio2-s4.4.4] Pie chart: shows proportions of a whole, e.g. the percentage of each blood group in a class.
[bio2-s4.5.b] Once the graph type is chosen, marks come from drawing it carefully. Axes, scales, plotting and the line each follow a rule.
[bio2-s4.5.0] The independent variable goes on the horizontal (x) axis and the dependent variable on the vertical (y) axis. Label both with the quantity and unit.
[bio2-s4.5.1] Use even, linear scales that use more than half of the grid in each direction, so the graph is large enough to read. The scale need not start at zero if the data do not come near zero, but this must be shown clearly.
[bio2-s4.5.2] Plot points accurately as small crosses or circled dots, and plot the means rather than every repeat.
[bio2-s4.5.3] Draw a line or smooth curve of best fit that shows the trend, with points balanced on either side. Where no trend line can be assumed, join the points with straight ruled lines.
[bio2-s4.5.4] Do not force the line through an anomalous point, because one unreliable reading would distort the trend.
[bio2-s4.5.5] Give the graph a title in the form “the effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable]”.
[bio2-s4.5.6] Diagram: a line graph of rate of enzyme activity against temperature. Labels: axes with units, plotted points, smooth curve rising to a peak (optimum) then falling, one circled anomalous point off the curve.
[bio2-s4.6.b] A graph is only useful once you can say what it shows. Describing a trend, naming its key features and classifying the relationship between the variables are the first steps in analysing data.
[bio2-s4.6.0] Describe the trend in words and quote data, e.g. “the rate increased from 2 to 8 cm³ per minute as temperature rose from 10 °C to 40 °C, then fell to zero by 60 °C”. Using figures makes the description specific.
[bio2-s4.6.1] Name key features of a graph: a peak or optimum (the value giving the highest result), a plateau (levelling off), and the point where a line crosses zero.
[bio2-s4.6.2] A positive correlation means that as one variable increases, the other increases. A negative correlation means that as one increases, the other decreases. No correlation means there is no pattern.
[bio2-s4.6.3] A straight line through the origin shows that the variables are directly proportional, so doubling one doubles the other.
[bio2-s4.6.4] Diagram: three small scatter graphs side by side. Labels: positive correlation (points rise), negative correlation (points fall), no correlation (points scattered), with both axes marked.
[bio2-s4.7.b] A graph also lets you find values that were not measured and work out how fast something is changing. Each use has its own limits.
[bio2-s4.7.0] Interpolation is reading a value from the graph between plotted points. It is fairly reliable, because the line is supported by data on both sides.
[bio2-s4.7.1] Extrapolation is extending the line beyond the data range to predict a value. It is less reliable, because the pattern may not continue, e.g. an enzyme’s rate does not keep rising with temperature.
[bio2-s4.7.2] The gradient (steepness) of a line graph of amount against time is the rate. A steeper line means a faster rate.
[bio2-s4.7.3] A line levelling off means the reaction is slowing, e.g. as substrate (the substance the enzyme acts on) is used up.
[bio2-s4.8.b] Finding a pattern between two variables is not the same as finding out why. A correlation is a starting point, and several different explanations can lie behind it.
[bio2-s4.8.0] A correlation shows that two variables change together. It does not show that one causes the other.
[bio2-s4.8.1] Other explanations of a correlation are chance, especially with a small sample; a third variable (a confounding variable) that affects both; or a cause that runs the other way.
[bio2-s4.8.2] Illustrative example: people who carry cigarette lighters are more likely to develop lung cancer, but the lighter does not cause cancer. Smoking, the third variable, causes both, because smokers carry lighters and smoking causes lung cancer.
[bio2-s4.8.3] Evidence that one variable causes a change in another comes from a controlled experiment (a fair test with a control, where only the factor being tested changes), together with a biological mechanism that explains it.
[bio2-s4.8.4] Surveys and secondary data alone usually show correlation, because they cannot control other variables. There is more on the strategies in Questioning and predicting.
[bio2-s4.9.b] Real data rarely come out perfectly, and sometimes one result sits well away from the rest. You need to recognise these results and know exactly how to treat them.
[bio2-s4.9.0] An anomalous result (outlier) does not fit the pattern of the other results. It is far from the other repeats at the same value, or off the line of best fit on a graph.
[bio2-s4.9.1] Possible causes are a mistake in measuring or recording, an uncontrolled variable, faulty equipment, or an unusual biological sample.
[bio2-s4.9.2] Check the reading and the calculation, and repeat that measurement if possible. This shows whether it was a simple mistake.
[bio2-s4.9.3] Leave the anomalous result out of the mean and the line of best fit, but keep it in the results table and report it with a possible reason. Hiding it would be dishonest. There is more on this integrity in Designing and conducting investigations.
[bio2-s4.9.4] A result is not anomalous just because it disagrees with the hypothesis. If repeats confirm it, it may show the hypothesis is wrong or reveal something new.
[bio2-s4.10.b] The conclusion is where the data answer the question the investigation set out to test. A good conclusion states the finding, backs it with evidence, explains it and admits its limits.
[bio2-s4.10.0] A conclusion states the relationship found between the independent and dependent variables, e.g. “As sucrose concentration increased, the percentage change in mass of the potato cylinders decreased.”
[bio2-s4.10.1] A conclusion is justified with evidence: quote values or describe the trend in the graph, and say whether the data support or do not support the hypothesis. Never say that the data prove it.
[bio2-s4.10.2] A conclusion explains the result using biological knowledge, e.g. water left the cells by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane) into the more concentrated solutions.
[bio2-s4.10.3] A conclusion is limited to what was tested: the range of the independent variable, the organism or tissue used, and the conditions. It does not claim more than the data show.
[bio2-s4.10.4] Confidence in a conclusion depends on whether the test was fair and valid, the sample size, the number of repeats, the spread of the data, the number of anomalies, and whether the results are repeatable and reproducible. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s4.10.5] A conclusion is valid when it is supported by valid, reliable data.

CANDIDATES bio2-s6: Communicating results
[bio2-s6.0.b] A report is organised in standard sections so that a reader can follow the investigation and repeat it. Each section answers one question a reader would ask, from why you did it to what you found and how far it can be trusted.
[bio2-s6.0.0] Sections in order: title; aim or question; hypothesis and prediction; background (the biological theory behind the hypothesis, with sources); equipment and materials; method; risk assessment and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s6.0.1] The sections after that, in order: results (tables and graphs); analysis (calculations, patterns and anomalies, meaning results that do not fit the pattern); conclusion; evaluation (limitations, sources of error and improvements); and references.
[bio2-s6.0.2] The method is written as numbered steps with exact quantities, concentrations, times and temperatures, so that someone else can reproduce it. It is usually in the past tense and impersonal style, e.g. “10 cm³ of hydrogen peroxide was added”.
[bio2-s6.0.3] A scientific paper written for other scientists begins with an abstract: a short summary of the aim, method, main results and conclusion. This lets readers decide quickly whether the paper is relevant to them.
[bio2-s6.0.4] Results sections present data and do not explain them, so the reader can judge the evidence before being told what it means. Explanation belongs in the analysis and the conclusion.
[bio2-s6.1.b] The same findings are communicated differently depending on who will read them and why: the audience's background knowledge, what they need to know, and what they will do with it. A format that suits scientists can confuse the public, so you choose the format and the level of detail to match the reader.
[bio2-s6.1.0] Scientists and examiners need a full report or paper. Technical terms are used precisely, and full data, methods, limitations and references are included, so the reader can check and repeat the work.
[bio2-s6.1.1] The general public needs plain language, with technical terms explained or avoided. The main finding and why it matters come first, because these readers will not work through the detail. Use simple graphs or infographics (pictures that present data at a glance).
[bio2-s6.1.2] The general public also needs uncertainty stated honestly, without exaggeration. Suitable formats include a poster, article, presentation, video, infographic and social media post.
[bio2-s6.1.3] Decision-makers, e.g. a school board, a council or a health service, need a short summary of the evidence, its reliability and its implications, with recommendations, because they must decide what to do rather than check the method.
[bio2-s6.1.4] A poster or presentation is selective: a clear title, the question, a brief method, one or two key graphs and the conclusion, with little text. A full report includes everything, which is why it suits readers who need to check the work.
[bio2-s6.2.b] Science only works as communication if every term means the same thing to every reader. Using exact words, symbols and units removes ambiguity and makes your results reproducible.
[bio2-s6.2.0] Use precise scientific terms with their correct meanings, e.g. “independent variable” (the factor you change), “control” (the comparison set-up), “precise” (close repeat values), “accurate” (close to the true value), “reliable” (repeatable) and “anomalous” (not fitting the pattern). There is more on these in the other notes on this chapter.
[bio2-s6.2.1] Use biological terms precisely: write “denatured” (its shape changed so it no longer works) rather than “killed” for an enzyme, since enzymes are not alive. Write “osmosis” rather than “soaking up water”.
[bio2-s6.2.2] Use SI units (the international standard units) and their symbols consistently: g and kg, cm³ and dm³, °C, s and min, and mm and µm. Write a unit with every quantity, because a number without a unit cannot be interpreted.
[bio2-s6.2.3] Write scientific names of organisms in binomial form (two-part names). The genus takes a capital letter and the species a lower-case letter, in italics in print or underlined by hand, e.g. Homo sapiens: genus Homo, species sapiens.
[bio2-s6.2.4] Avoid vague words such as “a lot”, “it worked” or “the results were good”, because they give the reader nothing to check. State what changed, by how much and in which direction.
[bio2-s6.3.b] A representation is the form in which results or ideas are shown, such as a table, graph, diagram or photograph. Each form shows something different well, so the best one depends on the point you want the audience to take away.
[bio2-s6.3.0] A table gives exact values, whereas a graph shows a trend or comparison at a glance. There is more on graph types in Producing and analysing data.
[bio2-s6.3.1] A labelled diagram shows apparatus or a structure. A flow chart shows the steps of a method or process in order. A photograph records a result, e.g. a colour change or colonies on an agar plate (the jelly on which microbes are grown).
[bio2-s6.3.2] A diagram of apparatus is drawn in two dimensions as a cross-section (as if the apparatus were cut through), in pencil. Ruled label lines touch the part labelled and do not cross, so each label is clearly linked to one part.
[bio2-s6.3.3] Biological drawings use clear single lines with no shading, labels to the side, and a title with magnification or scale where relevant, so that the size of the real structure can be worked out.
[bio2-s6.3.4] Every table, graph and figure has a title or caption and is referred to in the text, so the reader knows what it shows and when to look at it.
[bio2-s6.4.b] Readers trust a report only if it is honest and its sources can be traced. Integrity means reporting what you actually found, giving credit for other people's work, and claiming no more than your evidence supports.
[bio2-s6.4.0] Report all results honestly, including anomalies and results that do not support the hypothesis, and state the limitations. Leaving them out would mislead the reader.
[bio2-s6.4.1] Acknowledge every source by citing it in the text and giving a reference list. A reference gives at least the author or organisation, the year, the title and where it was published.
[bio2-s6.4.2] For a website, the reference gives the web address and the date it was accessed, because web pages can change or be removed.
[bio2-s6.4.3] Do not exaggerate findings. A small school investigation cannot show that something is true in general, and communication to the public should not overstate certainty.
[bio2-s6.4.4] Digital tools support communication: spreadsheets record, process and graph data; presentation and word-processing software produce reports and posters; and data loggers (devices that measure and store readings automatically) collect and display data.
[bio2-s6.4.5] The specification's digital technology section names using digital technology to collect, record, analyse and display data, and to develop investigative research, communication and report-writing skills.

CANDIDATES bio3-s2: Science, scientists and society
[bio3-s2.0.b] Scientists add to society in several ways, and the words 'discovery' and 'invention' mean different things. Knowing the difference, and how research is organised and paid for, lets you describe any scientist's contribution accurately.
[bio3-s2.0.0] A discovery is finding something that already exists in nature but was not known, for example the structure of DNA, or that a mould kills bacteria. The thing was always there; the scientist is the first to find and describe it.
[bio3-s2.0.1] An invention is creating a new device, process or product that did not exist before, for example the electron microscope, PCR (a method of copying small samples of DNA) or a vaccine.
[bio3-s2.0.2] Discoveries often lead to inventions. The discovery of penicillin's effect on bacteria led to the invention of methods to mass-produce it, so that it could be given to many patients.
[bio3-s2.0.3] Basic (pure) research aims to understand nature with no particular use in mind. Applied research aims to solve a practical problem, such as finding a cure for a disease.
[bio3-s2.0.4] Many applications come from basic research done decades earlier. For example, the enzymes used in PCR and in DNA profiling (identifying people from their DNA) came from the study of bacteria, long before anyone planned such uses.
[bio3-s2.0.5] Most discoveries are now made by teams, often international, working in universities, hospitals, public research institutes and companies. They are funded by governments, the EU, charities and industry, and who funds research influences which questions are studied. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio3-s2.0.6] Scientists also contribute by advising governments, for example with public health advice in a pandemic (a disease outbreak spreading across many countries). They assess the risks of new technologies, communicate with the public, and warn about problems, as Rachel Carson did in the DDT case study.
[bio3-s2.1.b] To evaluate the impact of a scientific contribution is to judge it using evidence. Four factors give you a checklist: economic, social, sustainability and ethical. Each comes with questions you can ask about any discovery or technology.
[bio3-s2.1.0] Economic means the costs and benefits in money and jobs. Ask who pays for the research and the product, and who profits (companies, through patents). Ask whether it creates jobs or industries.
[bio3-s2.1.1] Economic questions also cover savings and affordability. Ask whether it saves money, for example fewer days lost to illness or higher crop yields, and whether poorer people and countries can afford it.
[bio3-s2.1.2] Social means the effects on people's health, lives and communities, and on how people live. Ask who benefits and who is harmed, and whether it changes life expectancy or quality of life. Ask whether it increases or reduces inequality, and how the public accepts it.
[bio3-s2.1.3] Sustainability means meeting present needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Ask what the effects are on the environment, biodiversity, resources and climate, and whether the benefits are long-lasting.
[bio3-s2.1.4] Sustainability questions also cover side effects. Ask whether it causes problems such as resistance (pests or bacteria no longer killed by a chemical or drug) or pollution.
[bio3-s2.1.5] Ethical means whether something is right or wrong. Ask whether it is fair (justice) and whether it does good and avoids harm. Ask whether people were informed and gave consent, whether animals were used and, if so, whether their use was justified, and who decides.
[bio3-s2.1.6] Ethical questions are answered by reasoning about values, informed by evidence. This means science alone cannot settle them: the facts can show what happens, but people must decide what is acceptable.
[bio3-s2.2.b] Ethical questions in biology are not left to opinion alone. Researchers and doctors use a small set of agreed principles to decide what is acceptable, and these give you the vocabulary for the ethical factor.
[bio3-s2.2.0] The four principles of medical ethics are autonomy (respecting people's right to decide for themselves, through informed consent, which means agreeing after being told the risks and benefits), beneficence (doing good), non-maleficence (doing no harm) and justice (treating people fairly and sharing benefits and burdens fairly).
[bio3-s2.2.1] For animal research, the 3Rs were proposed by Russell and Burch in 1959. Replacement means using non-animal methods where possible, Reduction means using as few animals as possible, and Refinement means minimising pain and distress.
[bio3-s2.2.2] The precautionary principle says that where an action might cause serious harm and the science is uncertain, you should take care or not proceed until there is more evidence. In other words, uncertainty is a reason for caution, not for going ahead.
[bio3-s2.2.3] International research conventions, for example the Declaration of Helsinki, set ethical rules for research across countries. There is more on this in Science as a global enterprise.
[bio3-s2.3.b] Penicillin is an antibiotic (a medicine that kills bacteria), and it began the era of antibiotics. Its story shows a discovery turning into an invention through the work of different scientists, and how its benefits are now at risk.
[bio3-s2.3.0] What happened: in 1928 Alexander Fleming, at St Mary's Hospital in London, noticed that a mould (Penicillium) contaminating a culture plate (a dish in which bacteria are grown) of Staphylococcus bacteria had killed the bacteria around it.
[bio3-s2.3.1] Howard Florey and Ernst Chain, at the University of Oxford, purified penicillin and showed that it cured infections, from 1940. It was mass-produced during the Second World War.
[bio3-s2.3.2] Fleming, Florey and Chain shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945. Fleming did not purify or mass-produce penicillin; Florey and Chain did, so Fleming made the discovery and the Oxford team turned it into a usable drug.
[bio3-s2.3.3] Social impact (positive): bacterial infections that were often fatal, such as infected wounds, pneumonia and sepsis (blood poisoning), became curable. Surgery and childbirth became much safer, and the era of antibiotics began.
[bio3-s2.3.4] Economic impact (positive): penicillin created a large pharmaceutical industry, healthier workers and fewer deaths, and antibiotics are cheap to make.
[bio3-s2.3.5] Sustainability impact (negative): overuse and misuse of antibiotics in medicine and farming have selected for resistant bacteria, such as MRSA, by natural selection. This threatens to make infections untreatable again, and Fleming warned of this in his Nobel lecture in 1945. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio3-s2.3.6] Ethical impact: access is unequal in poorer countries. Using antibiotics to make farm animals grow faster, which has been banned in the EU since 2006, raises resistance for everyone. Prescribing responsibly, known as antibiotic stewardship, is now an ethical duty.
[bio3-s2.3.7] Judgement: one of the most beneficial discoveries in medicine, whose benefits are now threatened by the way it has been used.
[bio3-s2.4.b] Insulin is the hormone that lowers blood glucose, and people with type 1 diabetes cannot make enough of it. This case study shows a discovery that saved lives, a decision to give up profit, and a later invention that changed how the medicine is made.
[bio3-s2.4.0] What happened: in 1921 at the University of Toronto, Frederick Banting and Charles Best extracted insulin from the pancreases of dogs and showed that it lowered blood glucose in diabetic dogs.
[bio3-s2.4.1] In 1922 insulin was first used to treat a person with type 1 diabetes, a 14-year-old boy named Leonard Thompson. Social impact (positive): type 1 diabetes changed from a fatal disease to a manageable condition.
[bio3-s2.4.2] Banting and John Macleod received the Nobel Prize in 1923. The discoverers sold the patent (the legal right to profit from an invention) to the University of Toronto for a token sum so that insulin could be made widely available.
[bio3-s2.4.3] Insulin was extracted from cattle and pig pancreases until 1982, when human insulin made by genetically modified bacteria became the first genetically engineered medicine approved for use. There is more on this in Genetic engineering.
[bio3-s2.4.4] Economic impact: a large global insulin industry has grown. In some countries, notably the USA, high insulin prices mean that some patients cannot afford enough.
[bio3-s2.4.5] Sustainability impact (positive): making insulin in bacteria in fermenters (large tanks where microbes are grown) gives an unlimited, consistent supply without relying on slaughterhouse animals.
[bio3-s2.4.6] Ethical impact: experiments on dogs should be assessed using the 3Rs. Animal-derived insulin raised religious and vegetarian concerns that human insulin from bacteria removes. Fair access is a justice issue.
[bio3-s2.4.7] Judgement: overwhelmingly positive, but access is unequal.
[bio3-s2.5.b] DDT is a chemical that kills insects. It saved lives, but it also did lasting damage to wildlife. This case study shows how a scientist can contribute by warning society about a problem, and how one technology can have major benefits and major harms.
[bio3-s2.5.0] What happened: in 1939 Paul Müller discovered that the chemical DDT is a powerful insecticide. He received the Nobel Prize in 1948. DDT was used to control insects that spread malaria and typhus, and to protect crops.
[bio3-s2.5.1] In 1962 the American biologist Rachel Carson published Silent Spring, presenting evidence that DDT and other pesticides were harming wildlife, especially birds.
[bio3-s2.5.2] DDT is not broken down quickly, so it builds up in food chains and reaches high concentrations in top predators such as birds of prey. There it causes thin eggshells that break. There is more on this in Energy and nutrient transfer.
[bio3-s2.5.3] The USA banned most uses of DDT in 1972. The Stockholm Convention (2001) restricts it worldwide but allows its use to control malaria-carrying mosquitoes.
[bio3-s2.5.4] Social impact (positive): millions of people were protected from malaria and typhus, and food production increased. Economic impact: DDT was cheap and effective, so it raised crop yields, but chemical companies attacked Carson's work.
[bio3-s2.5.5] Sustainability impact (negative): persistence and build-up in food chains harmed birds of prey and other wildlife, and insects evolved resistance to DDT. Silent Spring helped to start the modern environmental movement.
[bio3-s2.5.6] Ethical impact: weighing human lives saved from malaria against harm to wildlife and long-term ecological damage, applying the precautionary principle, and respecting the public's right to be told the risks.
[bio3-s2.5.7] Judgement: the same scientific contribution can have major benefits and major harms, and scientists also contribute by evaluating technologies.
[bio3-s2.6.b] Ivermectin is a drug against parasitic worms, developed with the help of an Irish scientist. It shows how basic research on soil microorganisms can lead to a medicine, and how a company can share a discovery with people who cannot pay for it.
[bio3-s2.6.0] What happened: William Campbell, born in 1930 in Ramelton, Co. Donegal, and a graduate of Trinity College Dublin, worked at the drug company Merck in the USA.
[bio3-s2.6.1] Satoshi Ōmura in Japan isolated a soil bacterium, a Streptomyces species, that produced useful compounds. Campbell's team found that one of them, avermectin, killed parasitic worms, and developed the drug ivermectin from it. Ōmura isolated the bacterium; Campbell did not.
[bio3-s2.6.2] Ivermectin is used against river blindness (onchocerciasis), caused by a parasitic worm spread by the bites of blackflies, and against lymphatic filariasis. Social impact (positive): it has protected many millions of people in Africa and Latin America from river blindness and other parasitic diseases.
[bio3-s2.6.3] Campbell and Ōmura shared the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Tu Youyou, who discovered the antimalarial drug artemisinin.
[bio3-s2.6.4] Economic impact: the drug was first sold as a veterinary drug for livestock and pets, which made it highly profitable. Healthier people can work and attend school.
[bio3-s2.6.5] Ethical impact (positive): since 1987 Merck has donated the human form, Mectizan, free for as long as it is needed, through the Mectizan Donation Program. This is a model of a company sharing a discovery with people who cannot pay.
[bio3-s2.6.6] Sustainability impact (negative): heavy use in livestock has led to resistance in some livestock parasites, and residues in animal dung can harm dung-feeding insects.
[bio3-s2.6.7] Judgement: a discovery from basic research on soil microorganisms, with an enormous health benefit, shared ethically.
[bio3-s2.7.b] Thalidomide shows what can go wrong when a drug is released without enough testing. Unlike the other case studies, its main lesson is a warning, and its legacy is the strict system of drug testing we have today.
[bio3-s2.7.0] What happened: thalidomide was sold from 1957, first in West Germany and then in many countries including Ireland and the UK, as a sedative. It was given to pregnant women for morning sickness.
[bio3-s2.7.1] It had not been tested for effects on the developing foetus. It caused severe birth defects, especially shortened or missing limbs, in more than 10,000 babies before it was withdrawn in 1961.
[bio3-s2.7.2] In the USA, Frances Kelsey, a reviewer at the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), refused to approve it because the safety evidence was inadequate. Her refusal shows why independent review matters.
[bio3-s2.7.3] Social impact (negative): lifelong disability for survivors and their families, and loss of public trust in medicines. Economic impact: compensation costs, and new regulation made developing drugs slower and more expensive.
[bio3-s2.7.4] Ethical impact (negative): patients were harmed by a drug released without adequate testing. Survivors campaigned for decades for compensation and an apology.
[bio3-s2.7.5] Lasting effect: much stricter laws on testing drugs before sale, including testing for effects in pregnancy, and systems to report side effects.
[bio3-s2.7.6] Thalidomide is now used, under strict controls, to treat leprosy complications and a blood cancer, multiple myeloma.
[bio3-s2.7.7] Judgement: shows why scientists and regulators must test new technologies carefully, and why independent review matters.
[bio3-s2.8.b] The syllabus asks you to research a scientist's contribution and present it, not just recall case studies. This is the method to follow, whichever scientist or team you choose.
[bio3-s2.8.0] 1. Choose a scientist or team and their discovery or invention.
[bio3-s2.8.1] 2. Research using reliable sources (sources you can trust, such as scientific journals and established institutions): who the scientists were, the problem, what they did and how, the evidence, and how the work was received. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio3-s2.8.2] 3. Describe the impact under the four factors (economic, social, sustainability and ethical), positive and negative, using specific evidence.
[bio3-s2.8.3] 4. Make and justify a judgement: say whether the overall impact was positive or negative, and give your reasons.
[bio3-s2.8.4] 5. Present the work for the audience, for example as a poster, a slide presentation or a short report, with references so that others can check your sources.
