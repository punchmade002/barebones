TARGET bio11-s2: Key concepts of information transfer
[bio11-s2.0.b] Inheritance starts with a few basic ideas: what counts as one kind of organism, what is passed on, and what carries it. These terms are used throughout genetics.
[bio11-s2.0.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed in natural conditions to produce fertile offspring (offspring that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, the mule, is sterile, so horses and donkeys are different species.
[bio11-s2.0.1] The species definition has limits: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually, because they never interbreed, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding. There is more on how new species form in Evidence for evolution.
[bio11-s2.0.2] Heredity is the passing of characteristics (traits), by means of genes, from parents to offspring, that is, from one generation to the next. A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for a trait.
[bio11-s2.0.3] Genetics is the study of heredity (how traits are passed on) and of variation (the differences between individuals).
[bio11-s2.0.4] A chromosome is the structure that carries genes from one generation to the next. Body cells carry chromosomes in homologous pairs (two matching chromosomes, one from each parent), and each gamete carries one chromosome from each pair.
[bio11-s2.1.b] Parents pass genes on through special sex cells. Following the chromosome number from parent to gametes to zygote shows how each generation gets its genes and why the number stays constant.
[bio11-s2.1.0] A gamete is a sex cell: a sperm or egg in animals, or in flowering plants the male gamete nucleus in the pollen grain and the egg cell in the ovule.
[bio11-s2.1.1] Gametes are haploid, with one set of chromosomes (one from each pair), so a human gamete has 23 chromosomes. They are made by meiosis. Gametes must be haploid because fertilisation adds the two sets together, so otherwise the chromosome number would double every generation.
[bio11-s2.1.2] Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of two gametes, usually from two parents. The offspring receive half their chromosomes from each parent, so they are genetically different from both parents and from each other. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio11-s2.1.3] Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no gametes, so it produces genetically identical offspring, called clones.
[bio11-s2.1.4] Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes), the first cell of the new organism. It restores the diploid number: in humans 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.5] The zygote divides by mitosis, so every body cell carries the same set of chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.6] Diagram: life-cycle flow: parent (2n = 46) → meiosis → gametes (n = 23, sperm and egg) → fertilisation → zygote (2n = 46) → mitosis → offspring; label haploid, diploid, meiosis, fertilisation and mitosis.
[bio11-s2.2.b] A gene is a section of DNA for one trait, such as seed shape. Genes come in slightly different versions, and these versions, called alleles, are what make individuals differ.
[bio11-s2.2.0] An allele is one of the alternative forms of a gene, found at the same locus (position) on homologous chromosomes. Alleles differ slightly in base sequence, as they arise by mutation, so they may produce different versions of the trait.
[bio11-s2.2.1] In peas, the gene for seed shape has an allele for round seeds (R) and one for wrinkled seeds (r).
[bio11-s2.2.2] A diploid organism has two alleles for each autosomal gene (a gene on a non-sex chromosome), one on each homologous chromosome, one inherited from each parent.
[bio11-s2.2.3] A population may have more than two alleles of a gene, but each individual carries only two, because it has only two homologous chromosomes. The human ABO blood-group gene has three alleles (A, B and O) in the population.
[bio11-s2.2.4] Homozygous means having two identical alleles of a gene (RR or rr). A homozygous organism is also called pure-breeding, because it passes on the same allele in every gamete.
[bio11-s2.2.5] Heterozygous means having two different alleles of a gene (Rr). A heterozygous organism is also called a hybrid for that gene.
[bio11-s2.2.6] Diagram: a pair of homologous chromosomes with one gene locus marked, one chromosome carrying R and the other r (heterozygous); a second pair carrying r and r (homozygous); label homologous chromosomes, locus and allele.
[bio11-s2.3.b] Two terms separate what an organism's genes are from what it looks like or does. They are not always the same, because the environment and dominance both play a part.
[bio11-s2.3.0] Genotype is the genetic make-up of an organism: the alleles it has for a particular trait, written with letters, such as RR, Rr or rr.
[bio11-s2.3.1] Phenotype is the observable (physical, chemical or behavioural) characteristics of an organism that result from its genotype, such as round or wrinkled seeds, or blood group A.
[bio11-s2.3.2] Phenotype = genotype + environment. The genotype sets the possible range, and the environment affects how it is expressed.
[bio11-s2.3.3] Identical twins have the same genotype but may differ in mass because of diet and exercise, so the same genes can give different phenotypes.
[bio11-s2.3.4] Hydrangea plants of the same variety have blue flowers in acid soil and pink flowers in alkaline soil, so soil pH changes the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.3.5] A plant with the genes for chlorophyll is pale yellow if grown in the dark, because the environment (no light) stops the pigment forming.
[bio11-s2.3.6] Different genotypes can give the same phenotype: RR and Rr are both round, because one allele is dominant. So the phenotype does not always reveal the genotype. There is more on how to find the genotype (the test cross) in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s2.4.b] When an organism is heterozygous, the two alleles do not always show equally. Dominant and recessive describe which allele is seen in the phenotype and which is hidden.
[bio11-s2.4.0] A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote, masking the effect of the other allele. It is written with a capital letter (R), and a dominant trait shows in both RR and Rr.
[bio11-s2.4.1] A recessive allele is expressed in the phenotype only when no dominant allele is present, that is, only in the homozygous condition (rr). It is written with the lower-case letter (r).
[bio11-s2.4.2] In a heterozygote, a recessive allele is present but hidden, and it can still be passed on to offspring. It does not disappear and is not weaker.
[bio11-s2.4.3] Often the dominant allele codes for a working protein and the recessive allele for a non-working one. One working copy makes enough protein, so Rr looks the same as RR.
[bio11-s2.4.4] In peas, R codes for a working starch-branching enzyme; rr seeds make less starch and wrinkle. There is more on this chain in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio11-s2.4.5] Dominant does not mean common or better. The allele causing Huntington's disease is dominant but rare, and harmful.
[bio11-s2.4.6] Cystic fibrosis and albinism are caused by recessive alleles, so two carriers (heterozygotes) can have an affected child.
[bio11-s2.4.7] A carrier is a heterozygous individual who carries a recessive allele for a condition but does not show it.
[bio11-s2.5.b] Having a gene is not the same as using it. Gene expression is how a cell turns the information in a gene into something that affects the organism.
[bio11-s2.5.0] Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make its product (a protein or a functional RNA), so that the gene affects the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.5.1] A gene that is being used is switched on (expressed); one that is not is switched off.
[bio11-s2.5.2] Every body cell has the same genes, but each cell type expresses only some of them, which is why cells become specialised.
[bio11-s2.5.3] Expression is controlled by regulatory DNA (stretches of DNA that switch genes on or off), by signals such as hormones, and by the environment: some genes are switched on only in response to a stimulus.
[bio11-s2.5.4] There is more on how the environment and behaviour change expression in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s2.6.b] Four more terms extend the basic vocabulary. Each has a short definition and, where there is one, a named example.
[bio11-s2.6.0] Epigenetic expression is a change in gene expression that is not caused by a change in the DNA base sequence, but by chemical tags on the DNA or on the histones (the proteins DNA is wound around) that switch genes on or off.
[bio11-s2.6.1] Epigenetic tags can be caused by the environment and behaviour, can be passed on when cells divide, and some are reversible.
[bio11-s2.6.2] Incomplete dominance means neither allele is dominant over the other, so the heterozygote has a phenotype intermediate between the two homozygotes. For example, red-flowered and white-flowered snapdragons give pink-flowered heterozygotes. This contrasts with complete dominance, where the heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote.
[bio11-s2.6.3] Linkage: genes located on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together, because they do not separate into different gametes. Linked genes do not assort independently.
[bio11-s2.6.4] Sex-linkage: a gene located on a sex chromosome (in humans almost always the X chromosome) is sex-linked, so the trait's inheritance pattern differs between males and females. Examples are red–green colour blindness and haemophilia, which are much more common in males.

CANDIDATES bio11-s1: Chromosome structure and genes
[bio11-s1.0.b] A chromosome is DNA together with protein, and it carries genes in a fixed linear order. Knowing what it is made of, how it looks in a resting and a dividing cell, and how big it is explains why cells need to pack their DNA.
[bio11-s1.0.0] A chromosome is a single, very long molecule of DNA together with proteins. It is found in the nucleus of a eukaryotic cell (a cell with a nucleus) and carries many genes in a fixed linear order along its length.
[bio11-s1.0.1] Chromatin is the name for the DNA and protein material of the chromosomes when the cell is not dividing. The chromosomes are then long, thin and uncoiled, spread through the nucleus, so they cannot be seen as separate threads.
[bio11-s1.0.2] Before a cell divides, the chromatin coils and folds up tightly (condenses), so each chromosome becomes short and thick and is visible under the light microscope when stained. In other words, chromatin and chromosomes are the same material in two states.
[bio11-s1.0.3] Human body cells are diploid (two sets of chromosomes), with 46 chromosomes in 23 pairs: 22 pairs of autosomes (chromosomes that are not sex chromosomes) and one pair of sex chromosomes, XX in females and XY in males. There is more on this in Sex determination.
[bio11-s1.0.4] Homologous chromosomes are the two chromosomes of a pair. They are the same length, have the centromere (the narrowed region of the chromosome) in the same position and carry the same genes at the same positions, with one chromosome of each pair coming from each parent.
[bio11-s1.0.5] The chromosome number is fixed for a species (46 in humans); a different number does not mean a more complex organism, so you cannot judge complexity by counting chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.0.6] The human genome (all the DNA in one set of 23 chromosomes) is about 3 billion base pairs. The DNA in one human body cell, stretched out, would be about 2 m long, yet it fits in a nucleus a few micrometres across, which is why DNA must be packed.
[bio11-s1.0.7] Prokaryotes (bacteria) differ: a bacterial cell has one circular chromosome in the cytoplasm, not wound around histones (the packing proteins of eukaryotes), plus plasmids (small extra rings of DNA).
[bio11-s1.1.b] Histones are the proteins that pack eukaryotic DNA into a small space. Without them, about 2 m of DNA per cell could not fit in the nucleus or be moved safely when the cell divides.
[bio11-s1.1.0] Histones are small proteins around which the DNA of a eukaryotic chromosome is wound. They are positively charged, while the phosphate groups of the DNA backbone are negatively charged, so DNA binds tightly to them.
[bio11-s1.1.1] The DNA wraps nearly twice around a core of eight histone proteins, forming a bead called a nucleosome. Nucleosomes are joined by short stretches of linker DNA, like beads on a string.
[bio11-s1.1.2] The string of nucleosomes coils and loops further, and before division it condenses into the visible chromosome.
[bio11-s1.1.3] Histones do three jobs: they pack a very long DNA molecule into a small nucleus, they protect the DNA and keep it untangled during division, and they help control which genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.4] DNA wound tightly around histones cannot be reached by the enzymes that read genes, so those genes are switched off; loosely packed regions are active, which means their genes can be read.
[bio11-s1.1.5] Chemical changes to histones are one of the epigenetic mechanisms (ways of switching genes on or off without changing the DNA sequence). There is more on this in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s1.1.6] Diagram: DNA double helix wound around histone cores to form nucleosomes, then the coiled chromatin fibre, then a condensed chromosome; label DNA, histone, nucleosome, chromatin and chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.b] Before a cell divides it copies its DNA, so each chromosome ends up as two joined copies. Chromatids and the centromere are the names for those copies and for the point that holds them together.
[bio11-s1.2.0] Before a cell divides, each chromosome's DNA is copied, a process called replication. The two identical copies stay joined together; each copy is a chromatid. There is more on this in DNA replication and mitosis in the cell cycle.
[bio11-s1.2.1] A replicated chromosome therefore consists of two identical sister chromatids, each containing one complete DNA molecule with the same genes and the same alleles (versions of a gene).
[bio11-s1.2.2] The centromere is the constricted (narrowed) region of a chromosome where the two sister chromatids are held together. Spindle fibres (protein threads that move chromosomes in division) attach at the centromere and pull the chromatids apart to opposite poles of the cell.
[bio11-s1.2.3] Once separated, each chromatid is a chromosome in its own right.
[bio11-s1.2.4] The centromere is in a fixed position for each chromosome, dividing it into two arms of equal or different length, so it is not always in the middle. Its position, together with length and banding pattern, is used to identify each chromosome.
[bio11-s1.2.5] Before replication, a chromosome is one DNA molecule and one chromatid. After replication it is still one chromosome but has two chromatids, the X shape usually drawn; the two arms of the X are chromatids, not separate chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.2.6] Homologous chromosomes (a pair, one from each parent) may carry different alleles, whereas sister chromatids are identical copies of each other, so they always carry identical alleles.
[bio11-s1.2.7] Diagram: an unreplicated chromosome (single rod) beside a replicated chromosome (X shape); label chromatid, sister chromatids, centromere, arm, and a gene locus marked as a band at the same position on both chromatids. Optionally add a homologous pair of replicated chromosomes, same locus marked on each, labelled homologous chromosomes.
[bio11-s1.3.b] A gene is the unit of heredity, the piece of information that is passed on from cell to cell and parent to offspring. Genes sit at fixed places on chromosomes and, through the proteins they code for, control traits.
[bio11-s1.3.0] A gene is a section of DNA on a chromosome that codes for a protein. This means a chromosome is not simply a string of genes: genes are sections of its DNA.
[bio11-s1.3.1] Each gene has a fixed position on a particular chromosome, called its locus (plural loci). The locus of a gene is the same in every member of a species.
[bio11-s1.3.2] A gene carries the information to make one protein, although some genes code instead for RNA molecules that are used directly, such as ribosomal RNA and transfer RNA. So not every gene codes for a protein.
[bio11-s1.3.3] Through the protein it codes for, a gene controls a trait (a characteristic of the organism). The gene is copied and passed to daughter cells in division and to offspring in gametes (sex cells).
[bio11-s1.3.4] Humans have about 20,000 protein-coding genes spread across the 23 kinds of chromosome; each chromosome carries hundreds to thousands of genes.
[bio11-s1.3.5] Because homologous chromosomes carry the same genes, a body cell has two copies of each autosomal gene. These may be the same or different versions, called alleles. There is more on this in Key concepts of information transfer.
[bio11-s1.4.b] Most of the DNA in a human cell does not code for protein. Separating the small coding part from the large non-coding part shows that a chromosome is far more than a list of protein recipes.
[bio11-s1.4.0] Coding DNA is DNA whose base sequence is used to make a protein: the protein-coding parts of genes. Only about 1–2% of human DNA is coding DNA.
[bio11-s1.4.1] Non-coding DNA is all DNA that does not code for protein, about 98% of the human genome. It includes several kinds of sequence, listed in the next points.
[bio11-s1.4.2] Regulatory sequences beside genes act as switches, controlling when, where and how much a gene is expressed (used to make its product).
[bio11-s1.4.3] Genes for functional RNA (rRNA and tRNA) are non-coding DNA, as are introns, the non-coding sections within genes, which are copied into mRNA but removed before it is used to make protein.
[bio11-s1.4.4] Repeated sequences, such as the DNA at centromeres and at the ends of chromosomes (telomeres), protect the chromosome and help it divide correctly. Some non-coding DNA has a function that is still unknown.
[bio11-s1.4.5] Non-coding DNA was once called 'junk DNA', but much of it is now known to have functions, especially in regulating genes. Changes in regulatory DNA can change a trait without changing any protein.
[bio11-s1.4.6] Repeated non-coding sequences vary greatly in length between people, which is why they are used in DNA profiling (identifying individuals from their DNA).
[bio11-s1.4.7] Diagram: a stretch of a chromosome's DNA drawn as a line, with two genes shown as boxes (each with a regulatory region at one end and coding sections separated by an intron), and the DNA between genes labelled non-coding; label gene, coding DNA, non-coding DNA, regulatory sequence and intron.

CANDIDATES bio10-s2: Genes, proteins and traits
[bio10-s2.0.b] Three words link what is written in DNA to what an organism looks like and does: gene, protein and trait. Getting each one clear is the first step, because exam answers lose marks when they are mixed up.
[bio10-s2.0.0] A gene is a section of DNA that contains the instructions, or code, for making a particular protein (strictly, a polypeptide, which is a chain of amino acids). A gene is not itself a protein: it is the recipe for one.
[bio10-s2.0.1] Genes are carried on chromosomes (the long DNA molecules in the nucleus), and each chromosome holds many genes, so one chromosome carries the instructions for many different proteins.
[bio10-s2.0.2] A protein is a chain of amino acids folded into a particular shape. About 20 different amino acids are used to make proteins, and different orders of them give different proteins.
[bio10-s2.0.3] A trait is an inherited characteristic of an organism, such as eye colour, blood group, seed shape in peas, or whether a person can make a particular enzyme. The trait is not the protein: it is the visible or measurable result of what the protein does.
[bio10-s2.1.b] A gene's code is turned into a working protein in a fixed chain of events. The order of the code decides the protein's shape, and the shape decides what the protein can do.
[bio10-s2.1.0] The sequence of bases in a gene (the order of the chemical letters along the DNA) determines the sequence of amino acids in the protein it codes for. Change the order of the bases and the order of amino acids can change.
[bio10-s2.1.1] The sequence of amino acids determines how the chain folds, and so the protein's three-dimensional shape. This is why every protein folds into its own shape rather than a random tangle.
[bio10-s2.1.2] The shape determines the protein's function, because other molecules can only fit or bind to the right shape. Examples are the active site of an enzyme (the pocket where the reaction happens) and the binding site of a receptor (the spot that a signal molecule attaches to).
[bio10-s2.1.3] The cell's machinery reads the gene. The code is copied into a messenger molecule, mRNA, which travels from the nucleus to a ribosome (a tiny structure that builds proteins), where the amino acids are joined in the correct order.
[bio10-s2.1.4] Information therefore flows in one direction: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein → trait. There is more on how the copying and joining work in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s2.2.b] Proteins are the link between a gene and a trait because they do most of the work in cells and build much of their structure. Different versions of a gene can therefore give different versions of a trait, and different cells use different genes.
[bio10-s2.2.0] Enzymes (proteins that speed up reactions) control the chemical reactions that make substances such as pigments. If the enzyme is missing or faulty, the substance is not made and the trait changes.
[bio10-s2.2.1] Structural proteins build hair, skin, muscle and connective tissue, for example keratin (hair, nails, skin) and collagen (connective tissue, skin, tendons).
[bio10-s2.2.2] Transport and channel proteins move substances, for example across membranes or around the body. Hormones such as insulin, and receptor proteins, carry signals between cells.
[bio10-s2.2.3] Different versions of a gene, called alleles, have slightly different base sequences. They can give a protein with a different amino acid sequence, a different shape, or no working protein at all, which produces a different version of the trait.
[bio10-s2.2.4] Every body cell contains the same genes, but each cell type uses, or expresses, only some of them. For example, the insulin gene is used to make insulin only in cells of the pancreas, and haemoglobin is made only in developing red blood cells.
[bio10-s2.3.b] Four examples show the whole chain in action, each as gene → protein → trait. In each one, a change to the protein changes the trait that you can see.
[bio10-s2.3.0] Albinism (one form): the gene for the enzyme tyrosinase → tyrosinase is needed to make the dark pigment melanin → people with two non-working copies make little or no melanin, so they have very pale skin, white or very fair hair and light-sensitive eyes.
[bio10-s2.3.1] In albinism the gene codes for the enzyme, not for the pigment: the pigment (melanin) is not a protein, and it is missing only because the enzyme that makes it does not work.
[bio10-s2.3.2] Cystic fibrosis: the CFTR gene → the CFTR protein, a channel in cell membranes that lets chloride ions out of cells → a faulty channel causes thick, sticky mucus in the lungs and pancreas, leading to lung infections and poor digestion.
[bio10-s2.3.3] Haemoglobin: the haemoglobin genes → haemoglobin, the protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen → normal oxygen transport. There is more on how the change arises in DNA Replication, Protein Synthesis and Mutations.
[bio10-s2.3.4] A changed form of one of the haemoglobin genes gives sickle-cell haemoglobin, which makes red blood cells sickle-shaped when oxygen is low. This causes sickle-cell anaemia.
[bio10-s2.3.5] Seed shape in peas (Mendel's trait): the gene for starch-branching enzyme → the enzyme helps convert sugar into starch in the developing seed → seeds with a working copy are round.
[bio10-s2.3.6] Seeds with two non-working copies (rr) make less starch and keep more sugar. They take up more water while developing, and so wrinkle as they dry out.
[bio10-s2.3.7] Diagram: DNA (gene) → mRNA → protein (amino acid chain folded into a shape) → trait, with the albinism example written under it.
[bio10-s2.4.b] The chain from gene to protein to trait is a useful model, but real traits are often more complicated. Genes are not the only thing that shapes an organism.
[bio10-s2.4.0] Many traits are controlled by several genes acting together, such as height and skin colour in humans, so there is no single gene for a trait. Each gene adds a small part of the final result.
[bio10-s2.4.1] The environment also affects many traits. A person's height depends on diet as well as genes, and a plant's height depends on light and nutrients.
[bio10-s2.4.2] The trait, or phenotype (what the organism is actually like), results from genes and environment together. This is why two organisms with the same genes can still look different.
