TARGET bio27-s4: Absorption, transport and storage
[bio27-s4.0.b] Digestion only breaks food into small molecules; those molecules still have to get from the gut into the blood. The small intestine is where this absorption happens, and its structure is built to make it fast.
[bio27-s4.0.0] Most absorption of digested food, and most of the water, takes place in the small intestine, mainly in the ileum (the last section of the small intestine).
[bio27-s4.0.1] The surface area is huge, so many molecules can cross at once. The intestine is very long and its inner wall is folded.
[bio27-s4.0.2] The folds are covered in millions of villi (singular villus), which are tiny finger-like projections. The cells covering each villus have microvilli, microscopic projections of their cell membranes, which increase the area further.
[bio27-s4.0.3] The diffusion distance is short, so substances cross quickly: the lining of each villus (the epithelium) is only one cell thick, and the capillaries (the smallest blood vessels) lie just beneath it.
[bio27-s4.0.4] A steep concentration gradient (a big difference between the gut and the blood) is kept up, because diffusion only works while the gut side has more. A dense network of blood capillaries in each villus constantly carries absorbed products away.
[bio27-s4.0.5] Peristalsis, the waves of muscle contraction along the gut, also helps keep the gradient steep because it keeps moving fresh digested food past the surface.
[bio27-s4.1.b] Each villus contains more than capillaries. It also has a vessel for fats and lining cells equipped to do the work of absorbing, and you need to be able to draw and label all of it.
[bio27-s4.1.0] Each villus has a lacteal, a lymph vessel in its centre, which absorbs fats. Lymph is the clear fluid carried in the lymphatic system, a network of vessels separate from the blood.
[bio27-s4.1.1] The lining cells have many mitochondria to supply ATP (the cell's usable energy) for active transport, which is the movement of substances using energy.
[bio27-s4.1.2] Goblet cells in the lining secrete mucus, which protects and lubricates the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.1.3] Diagram: a longitudinal section of a villus labelled epithelium (one cell thick), microvilli, goblet cell, blood capillary network, arteriole (bringing blood in), venule (leading to the hepatic portal vein), lacteal, and an arrow for the direction of absorption.
[bio27-s4.2.b] Different digestion products take different routes into the body. The route depends on the molecule: water-soluble products go into the blood, while fats go into the lymph.
[bio27-s4.2.0] Glucose and amino acids enter the lining cells of the villus by diffusion and by active transport (using ATP), so they are absorbed even when their concentration in the gut is lower than in the blood. They then pass into the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.1] Fatty acids and glycerol diffuse into the lining cells, because they pass easily through the lipid membrane (the fatty outer layer of the cell), and are recombined there into fats. The fats are coated with protein and pass into the lacteal, not the blood capillaries.
[bio27-s4.2.2] Lymph carries the fats through the lymphatic system, which drains into the blood in a large vein near the heart. Fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) are absorbed with them, because they dissolve in fat.
[bio27-s4.2.3] Water is absorbed by osmosis (movement of water across a membrane towards the higher concentration of solutes), following the absorbed solutes. Most water is absorbed in the small intestine; the large intestine absorbs most of what is left.
[bio27-s4.2.4] Minerals and water-soluble vitamins are absorbed into the blood capillaries by diffusion and active transport. Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio27-s4.3.b] Blood from the gut does not go straight back to the heart. First it is sent to the liver, which checks and processes everything that has just been absorbed.
[bio27-s4.3.0] The blood capillaries of the villi join to form veins that unite as the hepatic portal vein, which carries blood from the small intestine, and the rest of the gut, directly to the liver, not to the heart.
[bio27-s4.3.1] A portal vein carries blood between two capillary networks: it begins in capillaries (in the villi) and ends in capillaries (in the liver). The hepatic portal vein is the example to give.
[bio27-s4.3.2] After a meal its blood is rich in absorbed glucose, amino acids, water-soluble vitamins and minerals; the amounts vary greatly with what has been eaten. It is low in oxygen because it has already passed through the gut wall.
[bio27-s4.3.3] The products go to the liver first because the liver processes and stores them and adjusts what is released to the rest of the body, keeping blood glucose and amino-acid levels steady.
[bio27-s4.3.4] Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the vena cava (the large vein returning blood to the heart). The liver also receives oxygen-rich blood in the hepatic artery. There is more on the full route in Circulation.
[bio27-s4.3.5] Fats do not travel in the hepatic portal vein at first: they reach the blood through the lymph.
[bio27-s4.4.b] Blood glucose rises after a meal and falls between meals. The liver acts as a store, taking glucose in when there is plenty and releasing it when there is not.
[bio27-s4.4.0] After a meal, blood glucose rises. Insulin, a hormone released by the pancreas, causes liver cells to take up glucose and convert it into glycogen, an insoluble storage polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units). Muscle cells also store glycogen.
[bio27-s4.4.1] Between meals, or during exercise, when blood glucose falls, the liver breaks glycogen back down into glucose and releases it into the blood, so the brain and other cells have a steady supply. There is more on this feedback loop in Homeostasis as a stimulus-response model.
[bio27-s4.4.2] The liver can store only a limited amount of glycogen. Glucose beyond this is converted into fat and stored in adipose tissue (fatty tissue).
[bio27-s4.4.3] The liver also stores iron and vitamins A, D and B12.
[bio27-s4.5.b] Amino acids are needed for building proteins, but the body has no store of them. Whatever is surplus is broken down in the liver, and the nitrogen it contains has to be got rid of safely.
[bio27-s4.5.0] Absorbed amino acids are carried to all body cells and used to make proteins for growth and repair, enzymes, hormones and antibodies; this is assimilation (absorbed food becoming part of the body).
[bio27-s4.5.1] Excess amino acids cannot be stored. Step 1: in the liver, the amino group (–NH₂) is removed from each excess amino acid; this is deamination.
[bio27-s4.5.2] Step 2: the amino group forms ammonia, which is very toxic.
[bio27-s4.5.3] Step 3: liver cells immediately convert the ammonia into urea, which is much less toxic, so it can be carried in the blood safely.
[bio27-s4.5.4] Step 4: urea passes into the blood, is carried to the kidneys, and is excreted in urine. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio27-s4.5.5] The rest of the amino acid, the part without nitrogen, is not wasted: it is respired to release energy, or converted into glucose, glycogen or fat.
[bio27-s4.5.6] A very high-protein diet means more deamination and more urea for the kidneys to excrete.
[bio27-s4.6.b] Besides storing glycogen and deaminating amino acids, the liver has several other jobs that you should be able to name.
[bio27-s4.6.0] The liver makes bile, breaks down old red blood cells (forming bile pigments), and breaks down alcohol and many drugs, a process called detoxification because it removes harmful substances.
[bio27-s4.7.b] What the small intestine cannot digest or absorb moves on into the large intestine. Its main jobs are to reclaim water and to form and eliminate the waste.
[bio27-s4.7.0] Material entering the large intestine is watery and contains what could not be digested or absorbed: mainly dietary fibre (cellulose), with dead cells shed from the gut lining, bile pigments, mucus and bacteria.
[bio27-s4.7.1] The colon (the main part of the large intestine) absorbs most of the remaining water, by osmosis, and some mineral ions into the blood, so the contents become semi-solid faeces. This saves water for the body.
[bio27-s4.7.2] Bacteria in the colon (the gut microbiota) feed on undigested material, including some fibre, and make vitamin K and some B vitamins, which are absorbed. There is more on their wider role in health in The microbiome.
[bio27-s4.7.3] Faeces are moved by peristalsis to the rectum (the final section of the gut) and stored there. When the rectum fills, stretch receptors give the urge to egest; the anal sphincters (rings of muscle) relax and the faeces are pushed out through the anus. This removal of undigested waste is called egestion, or elimination.
[bio27-s4.7.4] If faeces move too slowly, too much water is absorbed and they become hard and difficult to pass (constipation); fibre and water prevent this.
[bio27-s4.7.5] If faeces move too fast, as in a gut infection, too little water is absorbed and diarrhoea results, which can cause dangerous dehydration, especially in young children.

CANDIDATES bio27-s3: Chemical breakdown
[bio27-s3.0.b] Food is made of large molecules that are too big to pass into the blood. Chemical digestion cuts them into small units using enzymes (biological catalysts made by the body).
[bio27-s3.0.0] Chemical digestion is the hydrolysis of large food molecules into their small, soluble units by digestive enzymes: each bond is broken by adding a molecule of water, which is what hydrolysis means.
[bio27-s3.0.1] Carbohydrates become simple sugars, proteins become amino acids, and fats become fatty acids and glycerol. These small units are soluble and small enough to be absorbed.
[bio27-s3.0.2] Each digestive enzyme is specific to one type of substrate (the molecule it acts on) and works best at its optimum pH, the pH of the part of the gut where it acts.
[bio27-s3.0.3] The gut changes pH along its length: neutral in the mouth, acid in the stomach and alkaline in the small intestine. This suits the different enzymes working in each place.
[bio27-s3.0.4] Most digestive enzymes are released from glands into the gut cavity, so digestion there is extracellular, meaning it happens outside cells.
[bio27-s3.1.b] Digestion begins in the mouth, where food is mixed with saliva. Saliva moistens the food and also starts the digestion of starch.
[bio27-s3.1.0] Saliva is produced by the three pairs of salivary glands and released into the mouth.
[bio27-s3.1.1] The pH in the mouth is about 7 (neutral to slightly alkaline), which is the optimum for salivary amylase, the enzyme in saliva.
[bio27-s3.1.2] Salivary amylase digests starch to maltose (a sugar made of two glucose units). The product of an amylase is maltose.
[bio27-s3.1.3] Food is in the mouth only briefly, so only a little starch is digested there. Stomach acid denatures (destroys the shape of) the amylase soon after it reaches the stomach.
[bio27-s3.1.4] Saliva also contains water, which dissolves food so that it can be tasted and moistens it, and mucus, which lubricates swallowing.
[bio27-s3.1.5] Saliva also contains lysozyme, an enzyme that kills some bacteria.
[bio27-s3.2.b] In the stomach, food meets a strongly acidic fluid called gastric juice. It begins protein digestion and also protects the body from bacteria that arrive in food.
[bio27-s3.2.0] Gastric juice is produced by gastric glands in the stomach lining and released into the stomach. It contains hydrochloric acid, pepsin (released as inactive pepsinogen) and mucus.
[bio27-s3.2.1] The pH in the stomach is about 2 (strongly acidic), which is the optimum for pepsin.
[bio27-s3.2.2] Hydrochloric acid kills most bacteria in food, which is a defence against infection. It also provides the acid pH that pepsin needs.
[bio27-s3.2.3] Hydrochloric acid converts inactive pepsinogen to active pepsin, and it stops salivary amylase, which cannot work at this pH.
[bio27-s3.2.4] Pepsin is a protease (a protein-digesting enzyme): it hydrolyses peptide bonds inside protein chains, breaking proteins into shorter chains called polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.2.5] The product of a protease is polypeptides. Complete protein digestion needs further enzymes in the small intestine to give amino acids.
[bio27-s3.2.6] Pepsin is made in the inactive form pepsinogen so that it does not digest the gastric gland cells that make it. It becomes active only in the stomach cavity.
[bio27-s3.2.7] A thick layer of mucus protects the stomach lining from the acid and pepsin. If the mucus barrier fails, the lining is damaged, causing a stomach ulcer.
[bio27-s3.3.b] Fat digestion needs help before enzymes can act, and the acid from the stomach has to be dealt with. Bile does both jobs when food enters the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[bio27-s3.3.0] Bile is produced by the liver, stored and concentrated in the gallbladder, and released through the bile duct into the duodenum when chyme (the partly digested, acidic food mixture leaving the stomach), especially fatty chyme, arrives.
[bio27-s3.3.1] Bile contains water, bile salts, bile pigments and sodium hydrogencarbonate. It contains no enzymes.
[bio27-s3.3.2] Bile salts emulsify fats, which means they break large fat droplets into tiny droplets. This is a physical change, not a chemical one. There is more on this in Mechanical breakdown.
[bio27-s3.3.3] Sodium hydrogencarbonate is alkaline and neutralises the acid chyme from the stomach. This raises the pH to suit the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes and stops the action of pepsin.
[bio27-s3.3.4] Bile pigments are excretory wastes from the breakdown of haemoglobin from old red blood cells in the liver. They give faeces their brown colour.
[bio27-s3.4.b] The pancreas adds a second fluid to the duodenum, one that carries most of the enzymes needed to digest starch, protein and fat. Because the duodenum is alkaline, these enzymes work best there.
[bio27-s3.4.0] Pancreatic juice is produced by the pancreas and released through the pancreatic duct into the duodenum. It contains sodium hydrogencarbonate, which neutralises acid, and three enzymes: pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.4.1] Pancreatic amylase digests the remaining starch (the starch not digested in the mouth) to maltose.
[bio27-s3.4.2] Trypsin is a protease, released in an inactive form and activated in the duodenum. It digests proteins and polypeptides into shorter peptides.
[bio27-s3.4.3] Lipase digests fats and oils (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol. The product of a lipase is fatty acids and glycerol, not fatty acids alone.
[bio27-s3.4.4] Lipase acts on the tiny droplets made by emulsification, because they give it a much larger surface to work on.
[bio27-s3.4.5] The pH in the duodenum and small intestine is about 8 (slightly alkaline), because of the sodium hydrogencarbonate in bile and pancreatic juice. This suits pancreatic amylase, trypsin and lipase.
[bio27-s3.5.b] The last stage of digestion is done by enzymes made by the cells lining the small intestine. They finish breaking down the sugars and peptides that earlier enzymes produced.
[bio27-s3.5.0] The cells lining the villi (the tiny finger-like projections of the small intestine) make enzymes that complete digestion.
[bio27-s3.5.1] Many of these enzymes are held in the membranes of the lining cells, so the last step of digestion happens right at the absorbing surface.
[bio27-s3.5.2] Maltase digests maltose to two glucose molecules. Sucrase digests sucrose to glucose and fructose. Lactase digests lactose to glucose and galactose.
[bio27-s3.5.3] Peptidases digest peptides to amino acids.
[bio27-s3.5.4] These enzymes work at a pH of about 8, the same alkaline conditions as the duodenum.
[bio27-s3.5.5] Lactose intolerance: people who stop making lactase after childhood cannot digest lactose, so bacteria in the large intestine ferment it, causing bloating, wind and diarrhoea. There is more on this in Variation and Diet.
[bio27-s3.6.b] This table pulls together where each enzyme is made, where it acts, its pH and its products. Each line has the same pattern so you can compare them quickly.
[bio27-s3.6.0] Salivary amylase – made in salivary glands – acts in mouth – pH about 7 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.1] Pepsin (protease) – made in gastric glands of stomach wall – acts in stomach – pH about 2 – protein → polypeptides.
[bio27-s3.6.2] Pancreatic amylase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – starch → maltose.
[bio27-s3.6.3] Trypsin (protease) – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – proteins and polypeptides → peptides.
[bio27-s3.6.4] Lipase – made in pancreas – acts in duodenum – pH about 8 – fats → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.6.5] Maltase – made in the wall of the small intestine – acts there – pH about 8 – maltose → glucose.
[bio27-s3.6.6] Peptidases – made in the wall of the small intestine – act there – pH about 8 – peptides → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.6.7] Bile salts (not an enzyme) – made in liver, stored in gallbladder – act in duodenum – emulsify fats.
[bio27-s3.7.b] Putting the enzymes in order shows how each nutrient is broken down step by step along the gut. You can then use these flow models to predict what goes wrong when part of the system fails.
[bio27-s3.7.0] Carbohydrate model: starch is digested by salivary amylase in the mouth (pH 7) to maltose. The remaining starch is digested by pancreatic amylase in the duodenum (pH 8) to maltose. Maltose is digested by maltase in the small intestine wall to glucose.
[bio27-s3.7.1] Sucrose and lactose are digested by sucrase and lactase in the small intestine wall. Cellulose is not digested, because humans make no enzyme for it, so it passes on as fibre.
[bio27-s3.7.2] Protein model: protein → (pepsin, stomach, pH 2) → polypeptides → (trypsin, duodenum, pH 8) → shorter peptides → (peptidases, small intestine wall) → amino acids.
[bio27-s3.7.3] Fat model: large fat droplets → (bile salts, duodenum: emulsification, a physical change) → tiny droplets → (lipase from the pancreas, duodenum, pH 8) → fatty acids and glycerol.
[bio27-s3.7.4] Fat is digested only in the small intestine, after emulsification.
[bio27-s3.7.5] Using the models to predict: if the bile duct is blocked, for example by gallstones, fats are poorly emulsified and digested, and undigested fat appears in the faeces.
[bio27-s3.7.6] If the pancreas releases too little juice, as in cystic fibrosis, digestion of all three nutrients is reduced, which is why patients take enzyme capsules. There is more on this in Industrial enzymes.
[bio27-s3.7.7] Diagram: a flow chart with three rows (carbohydrate, protein, fat), each running left to right through mouth, stomach, duodenum and small intestine wall, showing enzyme or bile salts, pH and product at each step; or an alimentary canal (whole gut) outline with enzymes and pH at each site.
[bio27-s3.8.b] Visking tubing is a simple physical model of the small intestine. It shows why food must be digested into small molecules before it can pass into the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.0] Visking (dialysis) tubing is a selectively permeable membrane: it lets small molecules (water, glucose and maltose) through but not large ones (starch). It models the wall of the small intestine, and the water around it models the blood.
[bio27-s3.8.1] Method, step 1: tie one end of two lengths of Visking tubing, after soaking them in water.
[bio27-s3.8.2] Method, step 2: fill tube A with starch solution plus amylase solution. Fill tube B, the control (the comparison with no enzyme), with the same volume of starch solution plus water (or boiled amylase).
[bio27-s3.8.3] Method, step 3: tie the open ends, rinse the outsides, and place each tube in a separate boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C.
[bio27-s3.8.4] Method, step 4: at the start, and again after about 30 minutes, test samples of the water outside each tube with iodine solution (for starch) and with Benedict's solution in a hot water bath (for reducing sugar), using the food tests.
[bio27-s3.8.5] Expected results: the water outside both tubes stays yellow-brown with iodine, so no starch passes out.
[bio27-s3.8.6] After 30 minutes the water outside tube A turns brick red with Benedict's solution, showing reducing sugar, while the water outside tube B stays blue. Inside tube A, a test would show less starch and much reducing sugar.
[bio27-s3.8.7] Conclusion: starch molecules are too large to pass through the membrane. Amylase digests starch to maltose, which is small enough to pass through. This models why food must be digested before it can be absorbed into the blood. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio27-s3.8.8] Safety: wear safety glasses; Benedict's solution is an irritant; take care with hot water; heat the tubes in a water bath, never directly.
[bio27-s3.9.b] A model is useful only if you know where it differs from the real gut. The tubing copies the barrier but little else.
[bio27-s3.9.0] The tubing has no villi or microvilli, so its surface area is far smaller than the gut's.
[bio27-s3.9.1] No blood flows past to carry products away and keep the concentration gradient (the difference in concentration that drives diffusion).
[bio27-s3.9.2] The real gut absorbs some products by active transport (using energy to move molecules), which tubing cannot do.
[bio27-s3.9.3] Only one enzyme and one nutrient are modelled, at a constant pH, whereas the gut uses a sequence of enzymes and pH values.
[bio27-s3.9.4] The tubing does not move food by peristalsis (the waves of muscle contraction that push food along the gut).
[bio27-s3.9.5] Diagram: a boiling tube of distilled water in a water bath at 37 °C, with a knotted length of Visking tubing containing starch and amylase hanging in it; label Visking tubing, starch and amylase, distilled water, water bath at 37 °C; show the control tube beside it.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.

CANDIDATES bio29-s2: Circulation
[bio29-s2.0.b] Every cell in your body needs a steady supply of oxygen and nutrients and a way to get rid of wastes. The circulatory system is the transport network that does this for all of them.
[bio29-s2.0.0] The human circulatory system has three parts: the heart, a pump; the blood vessels, a closed network of tubes made up of arteries, capillaries and veins; and the blood, the transport medium that carries the materials.
[bio29-s2.0.1] It is a closed system: blood stays inside the vessels, and materials leave or enter it only across the thin walls of the capillaries (the tiniest vessels, which pass right alongside the cells).
[bio29-s2.0.2] In a large, many-celled animal, diffusion (the slow, random spreading of molecules) alone is far too slow to move materials between the exchange surfaces (lungs, gut and kidneys) and cells deep in the body.
[bio29-s2.0.3] The circulatory system solves this by carrying materials quickly by mass flow (the whole liquid moving together), so every cell is within a short diffusion distance of a capillary.
[bio29-s2.0.4] Materials transported: oxygen (lungs to all cells); carbon dioxide (cells to lungs); digested nutrients such as glucose, amino acids, vitamins and minerals (small intestine to liver to cells); urea (liver to kidneys); hormones (endocrine glands to target organs); water and salts.
[bio29-s2.0.5] Also transported: antibodies and white blood cells (to sites of infection); clotting factors and platelets (which seal wounds); and heat, carried from active organs such as the liver and muscles to the whole body and the skin.
[bio29-s2.1.b] Humans have two circuits joined at the heart rather than one loop. Blood goes to the lungs, comes back to the heart, and is then sent out to the rest of the body.
[bio29-s2.1.0] Humans have a double circulation: in one complete circuit of the body, blood passes through the heart twice. This does not mean two hearts; it is one heart with two sides.
[bio29-s2.1.1] The pulmonary circuit (pulmonary means 'of the lungs') runs heart to lungs to heart. The systemic circuit runs heart to body organs to heart.
[bio29-s2.1.2] The right side of the heart pumps the pulmonary circuit and the left side pumps the systemic circuit. There is more on the chambers in Investigation: the heart.
[bio29-s2.1.3] Full route of a red blood cell: vena cava, right atrium, tricuspid valve, right ventricle, semilunar valve, pulmonary artery, lung capillaries, pulmonary vein, left atrium, bicuspid valve, left ventricle, semilunar valve, aorta, artery, arteriole, capillaries of an organ, venule, vein, vena cava.
[bio29-s2.1.4] Diagram: a schematic of the double circulation. Heart as four boxes, lungs above, body organs below. Add arrows for the pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta and venae cavae.
[bio29-s2.1.5] Diagram labels: right and left atrium and ventricle, pulmonary artery, pulmonary veins, aorta, venae cavae, pulmonary circuit, systemic circuit. Shade oxygenated blood red and deoxygenated blood blue.
[bio29-s2.2.b] The pulmonary circuit has one job: to send blood that has given up its oxygen to the lungs to collect more. It starts at the right side of the heart.
[bio29-s2.2.0] 1. Deoxygenated blood (blood low in oxygen) is pumped by the right ventricle (the lower right chamber, which pumps blood out of the heart) into the pulmonary artery.
[bio29-s2.2.1] 2. The pulmonary artery divides into a branch to each lung, so both lungs receive blood.
[bio29-s2.2.2] 3. In the lung capillaries the blood picks up oxygen and loses CO₂, because oxygen diffuses in from the air in the alveoli (air sacs) and CO₂ diffuses out. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio29-s2.2.3] 4. Oxygenated blood returns in the pulmonary veins to the left atrium (the upper left chamber, which receives blood coming into the heart).
[bio29-s2.3.b] The systemic circuit takes oxygenated blood from the left side of the heart to every organ and brings it back. Its steps follow on from the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.3.0] 1. Oxygenated blood passes from the left atrium through the bicuspid valve (the one-way valve between the left atrium and left ventricle) to the left ventricle.
[bio29-s2.3.1] 2. The left ventricle pumps it into the aorta, the body's largest artery.
[bio29-s2.3.2] 3. Arteries branching from the aorta carry it to every organ, where it passes through capillaries, giving up oxygen and nutrients and collecting CO₂ and wastes.
[bio29-s2.3.3] 4. Deoxygenated blood returns through veins to the superior and inferior venae cavae (the large veins bringing blood from the upper and lower body) and into the right atrium.
[bio29-s2.3.4] 5. It passes through the tricuspid valve (the one-way valve between the right atrium and right ventricle) to the right ventricle, and the pulmonary circuit begins again.
[bio29-s2.4.b] A double circulation is better than a single loop for an active, warm-blooded animal. Passing the blood through the heart twice solves two problems: lost pressure and the risk of damage to the lungs.
[bio29-s2.4.0] (a) Blood returning from the lungs has lost much of its pressure in the lung capillaries. Returning it to the heart lets it be pumped out again at high pressure, so it reaches all the body organs quickly.
[bio29-s2.4.1] (b) The two circuits can run at different pressures: high in the systemic circuit, to drive blood through the many organs, and lower in the pulmonary circuit.
[bio29-s2.4.2] The lower pulmonary pressure matters because the lung capillaries are thin-walled, so they are not damaged and fluid is not forced into the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs).
[bio29-s2.4.3] (c) The septum (the wall dividing the two sides of the heart) keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, so the blood reaching the organs is fully oxygenated.
[bio29-s2.4.4] Together these deliver oxygen fast enough for the high respiration rate of a warm-blooded, active animal.
[bio29-s2.5.b] Blood reaches each organ along its own named artery and leaves along its own named vein. Knowing these names lets you describe the route to any organ.
[bio29-s2.5.0] A circulation pathway is the route from the heart to one organ and back. Each organ is supplied by an artery that branches from the aorta and is drained by a vein that joins a vena cava, and each is usually named after the organ.
[bio29-s2.5.1] Lungs (pulmonary pathway): the pulmonary artery, from the right ventricle, carries deoxygenated blood; the pulmonary veins, to the left atrium, carry oxygenated blood. In adults these are the only artery carrying deoxygenated blood and the only veins carrying oxygenated blood.
[bio29-s2.5.2] Head and brain: the carotid arteries, in the neck, supply it, and the jugular veins drain it to the superior vena cava. Arms: the subclavian arteries and veins. Legs: the iliac arteries and veins.
[bio29-s2.5.3] Kidneys: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea to be filtered, and the renal veins return filtered blood, low in urea, to the inferior vena cava. There is more on this in The urinary system.
[bio29-s2.5.4] Heart muscle: the coronary arteries and cardiac veins, which supply the heart's own muscle with blood.
[bio29-s2.5.5] Liver and gut: the liver receives blood from two vessels, the hepatic artery (oxygenated blood from the aorta) and the hepatic portal vein (nutrient-rich blood from the gut). Blood leaves the liver in the hepatic vein, which joins the inferior vena cava.
[bio29-s2.5.6] Diagram: a simple plan of the body's main vessels showing the heart, lungs, head, arms, liver, gut, kidneys and legs, with the aorta and venae cavae and each organ's named artery and vein, including the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and hepatic vein.
[bio29-s2.6.b] Most veins take blood straight back towards the heart. The vein from the gut is unusual because it detours through the liver first, so the liver gets the first chance to deal with what has just been absorbed.
[bio29-s2.6.0] A portal system is a vein that carries blood from one capillary network to a second capillary network, instead of straight back to the heart. Blood therefore passes through two sets of capillaries between leaving the heart and returning to it.
[bio29-s2.6.1] The hepatic portal vein collects blood from the capillaries in the villi (the tiny finger-like folds of the small intestine wall), and from the walls of the stomach and large intestine.
[bio29-s2.6.2] It carries this blood to a second set of capillaries, the sinusoids, in the liver. After a meal this blood is rich in glucose, amino acids and other absorbed nutrients.
[bio29-s2.6.3] Absorbed water-soluble nutrients, and any absorbed toxins or drugs, reach the liver before the rest of the body, so the liver can process them and keep the composition of the blood reaching the body steady. There is more on this in Absorption, transport and storage.
[bio29-s2.6.4] The hepatic portal vein carries deoxygenated blood, because it has already passed through the gut capillaries, so the liver gets its oxygen from the hepatic artery.
[bio29-s2.7.b] The circulatory system works as the delivery service for every other system. Each system either puts materials into the blood or takes materials out of it, and you can model these exchanges.
[bio29-s2.7.0] Breathing system: in the lung capillaries the blood takes up oxygen and releases CO₂. The circulatory system carries oxygen to every respiring cell and carries CO₂ back, so without circulation the gas exchanged in the lungs could not reach the tissues.
[bio29-s2.7.1] Digestive system: absorbed nutrients enter the blood capillaries of the villi (fats enter lacteals, small vessels in the villi) and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and then to all cells. The blood also supplies the gut wall with the oxygen it needs for active absorption.
[bio29-s2.7.2] Urinary system: the renal arteries bring blood containing urea, excess water and salts to the kidneys, which filter it. The kidneys regulate the water and salt content of the blood, and so its volume.
[bio29-s2.7.3] Endocrine system: hormones are secreted directly into the blood and carried to target organs, for example insulin from the pancreas to the liver and ADH from the pituitary to the kidneys. Adrenaline, carried in the blood, increases heart rate.
[bio29-s2.7.4] Immune system: blood carries white blood cells and antibodies to sites of infection, and platelets and clotting factors seal wounds.
[bio29-s2.7.5] Musculoskeletal system: red bone marrow makes the blood cells. Active muscles receive extra blood, carrying oxygen and glucose, and contracting skeletal muscles squeeze veins, helping to return blood to the heart.
[bio29-s2.7.6] Nervous system: the medulla oblongata (the part of the brain that controls automatic functions) adjusts the heart rate and the diameter of arterioles (small arteries), so blood is sent where it is needed. The brain itself needs a constant blood supply of oxygen and glucose.
[bio29-s2.7.7] Temperature regulation: blood carries heat from active organs, and widening or narrowing of the skin arterioles controls heat loss.
[bio29-s2.8.b] A model is a simplified picture that shows how the systems connect. A flow diagram with the heart and blood at the centre makes it clear which materials travel where.
[bio29-s2.8.0] Diagram: a labelled flow diagram modelling these interactions, with the heart and blood at the centre and arrows to and from the lungs, gut and liver, kidneys, endocrine glands, muscles and brain. Label each arrow with the materials transferred: O₂, CO₂, nutrients, urea, hormones and heat.
[bio29-s2.8.1] One limitation of the model is that it shows what moves but not the structure of the vessels or the rates of transfer, and it treats each organ as one box.
[bio29-s2.8.2] Worked example: during exercise, muscles respire faster, so breathing rate rises to take in more O₂ and remove more CO₂. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in breathing.
[bio29-s2.8.3] In the same example, heart rate rises and the arterioles to the muscles widen, so more blood reaches them, while the arterioles to the gut narrow. Heat from the muscles is carried to the skin and lost.

CANDIDATES bio8-s3: Metabolic roles and nutritional sources
[bio8-s3.0.b] Carbohydrates do far more than supply energy. Glucose is fuel, a product of photosynthesis and a building block for storage molecules, and other carbohydrates give structure and carry sugar around organisms.
[bio8-s3.0.0] Respiratory and catabolic role (catabolic means breaking molecules down): glucose is the main respiratory substrate, the fuel respiration uses. It is broken down to release energy, which is trapped as ATP, the cell's energy carrier. There is more on this in Respiration.
[bio8-s3.0.1] The brain relies mainly on glucose for its energy, so a steady supply of glucose in the blood matters.
[bio8-s3.0.2] Photosynthetic role: glucose is the product of photosynthesis, the anabolic process (anabolic means building molecules up) in which plants use light energy to make glucose from carbon dioxide and water.
[bio8-s3.0.3] Almost all the carbohydrate in food chains therefore comes from photosynthesis, because plants are the producers that make it first and every consumer depends on them.
[bio8-s3.0.4] Anabolic and storage role: excess glucose is built up into storage polysaccharides (long chains of sugar units), which are starch in plants and glycogen in the liver and muscles of animals.
[bio8-s3.0.5] These stores are broken down again (catabolism) when glucose is needed, so the organism can release it back as fuel.
[bio8-s3.0.6] Structural role: cellulose forms plant cell walls, and chitin forms fungal cell walls and insect exoskeletons (the hard outer body casing). The pentose (five-carbon) sugars ribose and deoxyribose are part of RNA and DNA nucleotides.
[bio8-s3.0.7] Transport role: plants move sugar from the leaves to other parts as sucrose, carried in the phloem (the tissue that transports food in plants).
[bio8-s3.1.b] Lipids (fats, oils and related molecules) are used for energy storage, but their uses go much further. They insulate, protect, build membranes, waterproof surfaces and act as regulators.
[bio8-s3.1.0] Respiratory and catabolic role: fats are respired to release energy, especially when glucose and glycogen run low.
[bio8-s3.1.1] A gram of fat releases about twice as much energy as a gram of carbohydrate or protein, so lipid is a compact long-term energy store.
[bio8-s3.1.2] Storage (anabolic): excess food is built into triglycerides (the main type of fat molecule) and stored as fat in adipose tissue, under the skin and around organs in animals.
[bio8-s3.1.3] Plants store oils in seeds, such as sunflower seeds, and in fruits, such as olives.
[bio8-s3.1.4] Insulation: fat under the skin reduces heat loss, as in the thick blubber of whales and seals. The myelin sheath around nerve fibres is rich in lipid and insulates them.
[bio8-s3.1.5] Protection: fat around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against physical damage.
[bio8-s3.1.6] Structural role: phospholipids form the bilayer (double layer) of all cell membranes, including the membranes inside chloroplasts and mitochondria.
[bio8-s3.1.7] Photosynthetic role: the light-absorbing pigments of photosynthesis are held in the thylakoid membranes of the chloroplast, which are built of phospholipids. Plants also convert some of the glucose made in photosynthesis into oils.
[bio8-s3.2.b] Two further roles of lipids come from their water-repelling nature and from their chemistry as signalling molecules.
[bio8-s3.2.0] Waterproofing: waxes, which are lipids, form the waxy cuticle on leaves, reducing water loss. They also waterproof feathers and fur.
[bio8-s3.2.1] Regulators: steroid hormones such as testosterone, oestrogen and progesterone are lipids, which means not all hormones are proteins.
[bio8-s3.2.2] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed with dietary fat. There is more on this in Vitamins.
[bio8-s3.3.b] Proteins have many different jobs in the body, from speeding up reactions to defence, movement and transport. Each role below has a named protein you can give as an example.
[bio8-s3.3.0] Enzymes: almost all enzymes are proteins. They are biological catalysts that speed up the reactions of metabolism (all the chemical reactions in a cell) without being used up, for example amylase digests starch to maltose.
[bio8-s3.3.1] Hormones: some hormones are proteins, for example insulin, made by the pancreas, which lowers blood glucose. Other hormones are lipids.
[bio8-s3.3.2] Immunity: antibodies are proteins made by lymphocytes (B cells, a type of white blood cell). Each binds to a specific antigen (a marker molecule) on a pathogen (a disease-causing organism), marking it for destruction.
[bio8-s3.3.3] Transport of substances in the body: haemoglobin in red blood cells carries oxygen.
[bio8-s3.3.4] Movement: actin and myosin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they shorten to make the muscle contract.
[bio8-s3.3.5] Energy: protein is respired only when carbohydrate and fat are in short supply.
[bio8-s3.3.6] Excess amino acids (the units proteins are made of) cannot be stored. The liver removes their amino group (deamination), forming urea, which is excreted.
[bio8-s3.3.7] Growth and repair: proteins are the main raw material for new cells and tissues, so children, pregnant women and people recovering from injury need more.
[bio8-s3.4.b] Many proteins sit in the cell membrane (the phospholipid bilayer around every cell) and decide what crosses it and how cells respond to signals. The specification names four kinds.
[bio8-s3.4.0] Membrane proteins are embedded in the phospholipid bilayer. They control what enters and leaves cells and how cells communicate.
[bio8-s3.4.1] Channel proteins form water-filled pores through the membrane that let specific ions or molecules pass by diffusion (movement from high to low concentration), for example the sodium and potassium channels of neurons, which allow nerve impulses.
[bio8-s3.4.2] Transporters (carrier proteins) bind a specific molecule and change shape to move it across the membrane.
[bio8-s3.4.3] Some transporters use energy from ATP to move substances against a concentration gradient (active transport), for example the sodium-potassium pump. There is more on this in Transport Across Membranes.
[bio8-s3.4.4] Receptors are proteins on the cell surface, or inside the cell, with a shape that fits one signalling molecule such as a hormone or neurotransmitter. When the signal binds, the cell responds, for example insulin receptors on liver and muscle cells.
[bio8-s3.4.5] This is why a hormone affects only its target cells: only cells with the matching receptor can respond.
[bio8-s3.4.6] Structural proteins give strength and support. Keratin forms hair, nails and the outer skin, and collagen forms skin, tendons, ligaments, cartilage and bone. Proteins also form the cytoskeleton, the internal scaffold of cells.
[bio8-s3.4.7] Diagram: a section of cell membrane (phospholipid bilayer) showing a channel protein, a carrier protein, and a receptor protein with its signalling molecule. Label each.
[bio8-s3.5.b] Hormones link the metabolic roles of proteins and lipids, because they are made from both. They let one part of the body control the activity of another.
[bio8-s3.5.0] A hormone is a chemical messenger made by an endocrine gland and carried in the blood to target cells, where it regulates their activity. Hormones regulate growth, development, metabolism and reproduction.
[bio8-s3.5.1] Hormones are biomolecules from two groups: proteins, such as insulin, and steroid lipids, such as testosterone and oestrogen. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio8-s3.5.2] Hormones work only on cells with the matching receptor proteins, so only target cells respond.
[bio8-s3.5.3] Plants also make chemical regulators, called plant growth regulators, for example auxin.
[bio8-s3.6.b] Knowing the roles of each biomolecule is only half the story. You also need to name the foods that supply each one.
[bio8-s3.6.0] Carbohydrate sources, sugars: fruit, honey, jam, sweets and fizzy drinks, and lactose (milk sugar) in milk.
[bio8-s3.6.1] Carbohydrate sources, starch: bread, pasta, rice, potatoes and breakfast cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.2] Carbohydrate sources, fibre (cellulose): vegetables, fruit, wholegrain cereals and brown bread, and beans.
[bio8-s3.6.3] Lipid sources, fats (mostly saturated): butter, cream, cheese, lard and fatty meat.
[bio8-s3.6.4] Lipid sources, oils (mostly unsaturated): olive and sunflower oils, nuts and seeds, avocados, and oily fish such as salmon and mackerel.
[bio8-s3.6.5] Protein sources, animal: meat, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Protein sources, plant: beans, peas, lentils, nuts, soya and tofu, and cereals.
[bio8-s3.6.6] Animal proteins contain all the essential amino acids (those the body cannot make). Many single plant foods lack one or more, so vegetarian and vegan diets combine a range of plant proteins, for example beans with cereal.
[bio8-s3.7.b] What we eat affects both our health and the environment, so food sources link back to the roles of each biomolecule.
[bio8-s3.7.0] Diets high in saturated fat are linked to raised blood cholesterol and heart disease.
[bio8-s3.7.1] Diets high in free sugars are linked to tooth decay, obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[bio8-s3.7.2] Producing plant protein generally needs less land and water, and causes lower greenhouse gas emissions, than producing meat. There is more on balanced diets in The Digestive System.
