TARGET bio8-s8: Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively
[bio8-s8.0.b] A food test that only says "reducing sugar present" is qualitative. This investigation goes a step further and compares how much reducing sugar (a sugar that reduces the copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution) different foods contain, by checking each food against glucose solutions of known concentration.
[bio8-s8.0.0] Aim: to compare the concentration of reducing sugar in a range of food samples, by comparing their Benedict's results with those of glucose solutions of known concentration.
[bio8-s8.0.1] Hypothesis, for example: "Grape juice contains a higher concentration of reducing sugar than milk." A prediction that ranks the foods in order is also acceptable.
[bio8-s8.0.2] Principle: the more reducing sugar in a sample, the more blue copper(II) ions are reduced to brick-red copper(I) oxide. The colour therefore moves further along the scale blue, green, yellow, orange, brick red.
[bio8-s8.0.3] More precipitate (solid that forms in the tube) appears as well. There is more on the colour scale in Investigation: food tests.
[bio8-s8.0.4] This works only if Benedict's is in excess, meaning there is more than enough to react with all the sugar. Then the colour keeps changing as sugar concentration rises, right across the range tested.
[bio8-s8.1.b] A fair test changes one thing at a time and keeps everything else the same. Here the standards give a reference scale and the controls show that any colour change really comes from the sugar.
[bio8-s8.1.0] Independent variable (the thing you change): the food sample, and for the standards the known glucose concentration. Dependent variable (the thing you measure): the concentration of reducing sugar, judged from the colour, or measured as absorbance with a colorimeter.
[bio8-s8.1.1] Controlled variables (kept the same, so the test is fair): the mass of food and the volume of water used to make each extract; the volume of sample and of Benedict's solution; the concentration of Benedict's; the water-bath temperature; the heating time; and the time before the colours are compared.
[bio8-s8.1.2] Negative control: a tube of distilled water with Benedict's solution (0%, no reducing sugar) stays blue, which shows the reagent alone does not change colour.
[bio8-s8.1.3] Positive controls: the standard glucose solutions, which are known to contain reducing sugar. They also act as the reference scale that the food tubes are compared against.
[bio8-s8.1.4] Equipment and materials: glucose powder, electronic balance, volumetric flask or graduated cylinder, beakers, distilled water, graduated pipettes or syringes, test tubes and rack, labels, mortar and pestle, filter funnel and paper.
[bio8-s8.1.5] Also needed: hot water bath with thermometer, test-tube holder, timer, Benedict's solution, white tile or card and safety glasses. Optionally a colorimeter with cuvettes and glucose test strips.
[bio8-s8.1.6] Example food samples: grape juice, apple juice, orange juice, honey, milk, onion, a sports drink, and a table-sugar solution. The table-sugar solution is expected to stay blue because sucrose is non-reducing.
[bio8-s8.2.b] To read a food's sugar level you need a scale of solutions whose concentration you know exactly. You make these by diluting (adding water to) a single stock solution, which is a stronger solution you start from.
[bio8-s8.2.0] 1. Make a stock solution of 1% glucose by dissolving 1 g of glucose in distilled water and making it up to 100 cm³.
[bio8-s8.2.1] 2. Dilute the stock with distilled water to make a range of known concentrations, for example 1%, 0.5%, 0.25%, 0.1% and 0% (distilled water only).
[bio8-s8.2.2] Worked dilutions: 5 cm³ of 1% stock plus 5 cm³ of water gives 0.5%, because the same amount of glucose is spread through twice the volume. 2.5 cm³ of stock plus 7.5 cm³ of water gives 0.25%, and 1 cm³ of stock plus 9 cm³ of water gives 0.1%.
[bio8-s8.2.3] 3. Label each tube with its concentration. Any evenly spread range is acceptable; a serial dilution, halving the concentration each time, also works.
[bio8-s8.3.b] The Benedict's test needs a clear liquid, and every food must be prepared in the same way so that differences in colour reflect sugar content and not the preparation.
[bio8-s8.3.0] 1. Weigh the same mass of each solid food, for example 5 g, on an electronic balance.
[bio8-s8.3.1] 2. Crush it with a mortar and pestle in the same volume of distilled water, for example 50 cm³, so that the sugar dissolves out of the food.
[bio8-s8.3.2] 3. Filter to give a clear extract, because solid pieces would cloud the tube and hide the colour.
[bio8-s8.3.3] 4. Dilute liquid foods by the same factor, for example 1 cm³ of juice plus 9 cm³ of water, so that very sweet samples fall within the range of the standards. Record the dilution so the original concentration can be worked out.
[bio8-s8.4.b] All the standards and food extracts are now treated in exactly the same way, so the colours can be compared directly. The standards, once heated, form a colour scale that you match each food against.
[bio8-s8.4.0] 1. Place 2 cm³ of each standard and 2 cm³ of each food extract in separate labelled test tubes.
[bio8-s8.4.1] 2. Add 2 cm³ of Benedict's solution to every tube and mix.
[bio8-s8.4.2] 3. Place all the tubes in the same hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) at the same time, and heat them for exactly the same time, for example 5 minutes.
[bio8-s8.4.3] 4. Remove all the tubes at the same time and place them in a rack in front of a white background, which makes the colours easier to judge.
[bio8-s8.4.4] 5. Arrange the standards in order to form a colour scale. 6. Compare each food tube with the scale and record the standard it matches most closely, or the two it lies between.
[bio8-s8.4.5] 7. Repeat the whole test three times for each food and use the most common or average result, because repeats show whether the result is reliable.
[bio8-s8.4.6] Do not tie an exact colour to each concentration, as the colour depends on the Benedict's volume and heating time.
[bio8-s8.4.7] Diagram: a row of test tubes of the standards after heating, labelled 0%, 0.1%, 0.25%, 0.5% and 1%, colours from blue at 0% through green, yellow and orange to brick red at the highest, and a food tube matched against them.
[bio8-s8.5.b] Judging colour by eye is a matter of opinion. A colorimeter replaces the eye with a measurement, so the result is a number that can be plotted and compared.
[bio8-s8.5.0] A colorimeter measures how much light of a chosen colour passes through a solution (transmission) or is absorbed by it (absorbance). It gives a number instead of a judgement by eye.
[bio8-s8.5.1] Method: after heating, let the precipitate settle, or filter or centrifuge (spin to separate solids) each tube, to leave the remaining blue solution. Zero the colorimeter with a cuvette (a small clear holder for the liquid) of distilled water.
[bio8-s8.5.2] Measure the absorbance of each standard's solution using a red filter, because blue solutions absorb red light.
[bio8-s8.5.3] The more reducing sugar that was present, the more copper(II) ions were used up. The remaining solution is paler blue, so its absorbance is lower and its transmission higher.
[bio8-s8.5.4] Plot a calibration curve (a graph of readings from known solutions): absorbance on the y-axis against known glucose concentration on the x-axis for the standards, with a line or smooth curve of best fit. The curve falls as concentration rises.
[bio8-s8.5.5] Measure the absorbance of each food extract and read its concentration from the curve, then multiply by any dilution factor to give the concentration in the original food.
[bio8-s8.6.b] Glucose test strips are a quick alternative that uses an enzyme instead of Benedict's solution. Because enzymes are specific, the strips behave differently from Benedict's.
[bio8-s8.6.0] A glucose test strip has a pad containing the enzymes glucose oxidase and peroxidase and a dye. When dipped in a solution, the pad changes colour according to the glucose concentration.
[bio8-s8.6.1] The colour is matched to a chart on the container that gives the concentration. Test the standards first to check the strips.
[bio8-s8.6.2] Limitation: the enzyme is specific to glucose, so strips detect glucose only, not other reducing sugars such as fructose, lactose or maltose. Milk, for example, gives a negative or low strip result but a positive Benedict's result, because its sugar is lactose.
[bio8-s8.6.3] Strips have a limited range, so samples may need diluting to bring them within the chart.
[bio8-s8.6.4] Technology and health link: similar strips and electronic glucose meters are used by people with diabetes to monitor their glucose. There is more on this in The Endocrine System.
[bio8-s8.7.b] This investigation uses Benedict's solution, which is harmful if swallowed or splashed in the eyes, along with near-boiling water and food in a lab, so each risk needs a specific precaution.
[bio8-s8.7.0] Wear safety glasses. Benedict's solution is harmful if swallowed or splashed in the eyes; wash splashes off with water.
[bio8-s8.7.1] Heat only in a water bath, never directly in a flame, because the mixture can boil suddenly and spurt. Use a test-tube holder, and take care with near-boiling water and hot tubes.
[bio8-s8.7.2] Do not eat or taste any food in the lab. Note allergies, and clean up spills: sugar solutions make benches sticky and floors slippery.
[bio8-s8.8.b] The results are primary data (measurements you collected yourself), and the conclusion must be built from them. Because the match is made by eye, the answer is a range, not an exact figure.
[bio8-s8.8.0] Record primary data in a table: food sample; colour after heating; matching standard, as an estimated percentage of reducing sugar; repeats; and, with a colorimeter, absorbance and the concentration read from the curve.
[bio8-s8.8.1] Expected pattern: honey and grape juice give orange to brick red, showing high reducing sugar, mostly glucose and fructose. Other fruit juices and sports drinks give yellow to orange.
[bio8-s8.8.2] Milk gives green to yellow, because of its lactose. The table-sugar solution and water stay blue, because sucrose is non-reducing. Actual results depend on the products used, so exact percentages are not given here.
[bio8-s8.8.3] Conclusion: rank the foods by reducing-sugar concentration, give each an estimated range, for example "between 0.25% and 0.5%", state whether the results support the hypothesis, and refer to the primary data.
[bio8-s8.8.4] This method is semi-quantitative: it estimates a range rather than an exact value, because matching colours by eye is a judgement. The colorimeter gives a more precise and objective value.
[bio8-s8.9.b] Every step above can introduce error. For each weakness, state the problem and the matching improvement, and repeating the test is the general safeguard.
[bio8-s8.9.0] Judging colour by eye is subjective. Improve it with a colorimeter, or by having several people judge independently.
[bio8-s8.9.1] Unequal heating, such as tubes put in or taken out at different times, or a water bath that cools, changes the colour. Heat all tubes together for the same time, and check the bath temperature with a thermometer.
[bio8-s8.9.2] If Benedict's is not in excess, all the copper(II) ions are used up and every concentrated sample looks brick red, so they cannot be told apart. Dilute the samples or use more Benedict's.
[bio8-s8.9.3] Coloured or cloudy food extracts affect the colour. Filter them and dilute coloured juices.
[bio8-s8.9.4] The precipitate settles over time, changing the appearance. Compare all the tubes at the same time after heating.
[bio8-s8.9.5] Inaccurate measuring of volumes and masses. Use graduated pipettes or syringes and an electronic balance.
[bio8-s8.9.6] Too few standards give a coarse scale. Use more standards spread over the range, and repeat each test at least three times to check repeatability.

CANDIDATES bio8-s7: Investigation: food tests
[bio8-s7.0.b] Food tests are a fair, planned investigation, not just a set of recipes. Before testing anything you decide what you are trying to find out, what you predict, and what you will change, measure and keep the same.
[bio8-s7.0.0] Aim: to find out which nutrients (starch, reducing sugar, protein and lipid) are present in a range of food samples. The investigation is qualitative, which means you record whether a nutrient is present or absent, from a colour change or other observation, not how much of it there is.
[bio8-s7.0.1] Hypothesis, e.g.: “Potato contains starch but not lipid; milk contains reducing sugar (lactose), protein and lipid.” Students predict the result for each food from what the food is, so the results can later support or contradict the prediction.
[bio8-s7.0.2] Independent variable (the one you change): the food sample tested. Dependent variable (the one you observe): the result of each test, the colour or observation, recorded as present or absent.
[bio8-s7.0.3] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the volume of food solution and of reagent, the concentration of the reagents, the water-bath temperature and heating time for Benedict's, and clean apparatus for every test to avoid carry-over from one sample to the next.
[bio8-s7.0.4] Every test needs two controls. A negative control uses distilled water in place of the food and shows the colour when the nutrient is absent.
[bio8-s7.0.5] A positive control uses a known pure sample, such as starch solution, glucose solution, egg-white (albumen) solution or vegetable oil, and shows the colour when the nutrient is present.
[bio8-s7.0.6] Results for the foods are judged against these controls, so you can tell a real colour change from the reagent's starting colour, and know the test itself is working.
[bio8-s7.1.b] Choose foods that you expect to be rich in each nutrient, and gather the equipment and reagents (the chemicals that react with a nutrient to give a visible result) before you start.
[bio8-s7.1.0] Example food samples: potato, bread, rice or flour (starch); glucose, honey, grapes, fruit juice and milk (reducing sugar); egg white, milk, meat or cheese (protein); butter, vegetable oil, nuts, crisps and cheese (lipid); and table sugar, which gives a negative Benedict's result.
[bio8-s7.1.1] Equipment: test tubes and rack, test-tube holder, droppers, graduated pipettes or syringes (to measure equal volumes), spotting tile, mortar and pestle or knife and chopping board, beaker of distilled water, filter funnel and filter paper.
[bio8-s7.1.2] Also needed: a hot water bath (a beaker of near-boiling water from a kettle, or an electric water bath) with a thermometer, brown paper, labels, safety glasses and gloves.
[bio8-s7.1.3] Reagents: iodine solution (iodine dissolved in potassium iodide), Benedict's solution, biuret reagent (or sodium hydroxide solution and dilute copper sulfate solution) and Sudan III stain.
[bio8-s7.2.b] Most tests work on a liquid, so solid foods must first be turned into a food solution. This preparation is common to all the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.0] 1. Crush or grind a solid food with a little distilled water using a mortar and pestle. Crushing breaks open the cells so the nutrients can mix into the water.
[bio8-s7.2.1] 2. Add more water, stir, and filter or let the solid settle, then use the liquid food solution for the tests.
[bio8-s7.2.2] 3. Test liquid foods such as milk and juice directly, or dilute them.
[bio8-s7.2.3] 4. Label each tube with the food and the test, so the results cannot be mixed up.
[bio8-s7.3.b] Starch is the carbohydrate plants store, and iodine solution is the quickest way to detect it. It needs no heating and gives a result at once.
[bio8-s7.3.0] Method, step 1: place a few drops of food solution, or a small piece of solid food, on a white spotting tile, or place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. The white tile makes the colour easy to see.
[bio8-s7.3.1] Step 2: add a few drops of iodine solution. Step 3: observe the colour at once. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.3.2] Step 4: repeat with water as the negative control and starch solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.3.3] Result: iodine solution is yellow-brown (orange-brown) at the start. It turns blue-black if starch is present and stays yellow-brown if starch is absent.
[bio8-s7.4.b] Some sugars can react chemically with Benedict's solution and others cannot. This test detects the ones that can, and it is the one food test that needs heat.
[bio8-s7.4.0] A reducing sugar is one that can reduce (give electrons to) the blue copper(II) ions in Benedict's solution. Glucose, fructose, galactose, maltose and lactose are reducing sugars; sucrose is not.
[bio8-s7.4.1] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add an equal volume, 2 cm³, of Benedict's solution and mix; the mixture is blue.
[bio8-s7.4.2] Step 3: place the tube in a hot water bath (near boiling, at least about 80 °C) for about 5 minutes. Never heat the tube directly in a Bunsen flame, because the liquid can spurt out.
[bio8-s7.4.3] Step 4: observe the colour. Step 5: run water as the negative control and glucose solution as the positive control, in the same water bath at the same time so the conditions are identical.
[bio8-s7.4.4] Result: if a reducing sugar is present, the blue solution turns green, then yellow, then orange, and finally a brick-red precipitate (solid particles) forms. It stays blue if no reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.5] The reducing sugar reduces blue copper(II) ions to copper(I) oxide, the brick-red precipitate. The colour reached gives a rough idea of how much sugar is present: green for a little, brick red for a lot. There is more on this in Investigation: reducing sugars, quantitatively.
[bio8-s7.4.6] Fehling's test is an alternative: equal volumes of Fehling's solutions A and B are mixed with the sample and heated in a water bath. A blue mixture turns to a brick-red precipitate if a reducing sugar is present.
[bio8-s7.4.7] Extension: sucrose stays blue. To show it is made of reducing sugars, boil a sucrose solution with a few drops of dilute hydrochloric acid to hydrolyse it to glucose and fructose. Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate until fizzing stops, then do the Benedict's test: it now turns brick red.
[bio8-s7.5.b] Biuret detects the bonds that join amino acids together in a protein. Like iodine, it works at room temperature with no heating.
[bio8-s7.5.0] Method, step 1: place 2 cm³ of food solution in a test tube. Step 2: add 2 cm³ of sodium hydroxide solution and mix (or add biuret reagent, which already contains it).
[bio8-s7.5.1] Step 3: add a few drops of dilute copper sulfate solution, one drop at a time, shaking gently. Step 4: leave for a few minutes and observe. No heating is needed.
[bio8-s7.5.2] Step 5: run water as the negative control and egg-white solution as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.5.3] Result: the blue colour turns purple (violet or lilac) if protein is present, and stays blue if protein is absent. The copper ions react with the peptide bonds (the links between amino acids) in the protein, so the test detects peptide bonds, not single amino acids.
[bio8-s7.6.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do not dissolve in water, and two simple tests use this. They are alternatives, so you can use either one.
[bio8-s7.6.0] Grease-spot (brown paper) test, method: rub a small amount of food on a piece of brown paper, or place a drop of liquid food on it. Place a drop of water on another part of the paper as a control.
[bio8-s7.6.1] Leave the paper to dry, or warm it gently away from flames, then hold it up to the light.
[bio8-s7.6.2] Grease-spot result: a translucent (see-through), greasy spot that does not disappear when the paper dries shows that lipid is present. The water spot also looks translucent at first but disappears as it dries, which is why the paper must dry before the result is judged.
[bio8-s7.6.3] Sudan III test, method: place 2 cm³ of food solution, or a little crushed food in 2 cm³ of water, in a test tube. Add a few drops of Sudan III, a red dye that dissolves in lipid but not in water.
[bio8-s7.6.4] Shake gently and leave to stand for a minute. Run water as the negative control and vegetable oil in water as the positive control.
[bio8-s7.6.5] Sudan III result: if lipid is present, a red-stained oily layer forms on top of the water, because lipid is less dense than water and does not mix with it. With no lipid, the dye spreads through the water or settles as red specks, with no red oily layer.
[bio8-s7.7.b] Several reagents here are corrosive, harmful or flammable, and the Benedict's test uses near-boiling water. Safe practice is part of any investigation.
[bio8-s7.7.0] Wear safety glasses throughout. Sodium hydroxide (biuret test) is corrosive and especially harmful to the eyes. Benedict's and copper sulfate solutions are harmful if swallowed or splashed. Wash any splash off the skin with plenty of water.
[bio8-s7.7.1] Heat Benedict's tests only in a water bath, never directly over a flame, because the liquid can boil suddenly and spurt out of the tube. Use a test-tube holder, point the tube away from people, and take care with near-boiling water.
[bio8-s7.7.2] Sudan III stain is usually dissolved in ethanol, an alcohol that is highly flammable, so keep it away from flames. Do not warm brown paper near a flame.
[bio8-s7.7.3] Iodine solution stains skin and clothes; wear gloves if possible.
[bio8-s7.7.4] Never taste or eat food in the laboratory. Be aware of food allergies (e.g. to nuts) among the class. Clean up spills and wash hands after the practical.
[bio8-s7.8.b] Your observations are the primary data (results you collected yourself). A clear table lets you compare every food with the controls and draw a conclusion from the evidence.
[bio8-s7.8.0] Record primary data in a results table: one row per food and one column per test, recording the colour observed and whether the nutrient is present or absent (or + or −). Include the control rows.
[bio8-s7.8.1] Expected results: potato shows starch; glucose, honey and grapes show reducing sugar; table sugar is Benedict's negative, as sucrose is non-reducing; egg white shows protein; vegetable oil and butter show lipid; milk shows reducing sugar, protein and lipid; distilled water is negative for all tests.
[bio8-s7.8.2] Conclusion: state which nutrients each food contains, supported by the results compared with the controls, and say whether this supports the hypothesis. The tests show presence only, not the amount.
[bio8-s7.9.b] Food tests are simple, so small mistakes easily give a wrong result. Knowing the common errors, and how to reduce them, makes the conclusion more reliable.
[bio8-s7.9.0] Colour judgements are subjective, so different people may see different shades. Compare each tube with the controls against a white background, and have two people check the result.
[bio8-s7.9.1] Coloured foods, such as dark fruit juice, can mask a colour change. Dilute or filter them, or use a control of the food without the reagent.
[bio8-s7.9.2] Contamination: reusing a dropper or test tube can carry a nutrient from one sample to another, giving a false positive.
[bio8-s7.9.3] A very small amount of nutrient may not give a visible change, which is a false negative. Some nutrients are inside cells, so the food must be well crushed.
[bio8-s7.9.4] Benedict's detects reducing sugars only, so a food rich in sucrose can give a negative result even though it contains sugar.
[bio8-s7.9.5] Repeat each test, e.g. three times, to check that the results are repeatable.
[bio8-s7.9.6] Diagram: a test tube of food solution with Benedict's solution standing in a beaker of hot water, with a thermometer, labelled.

CANDIDATES bio2-s4: Producing and analysing data
[bio2-s4.0.b] Every investigation produces data, and the type of data decides how you can record, process and graph it. Biologists sort data by what it describes, how it behaves and who collected it.
[bio2-s4.0.0] Qualitative data are descriptions or categories that are not numerical, e.g. a positive Benedict’s test for a reducing sugar, which turns from blue to brick-red through green, yellow and orange; the presence or absence of a species; or “cloudy” or “clear”.
[bio2-s4.0.1] Quantitative data are numerical measurements or counts with units, e.g. volume of oxygen in cm³, mass in g, or number of daisies per quadrat (a square frame laid on the ground to sample a small area).
[bio2-s4.0.2] Quantitative data are more objective (less dependent on the observer’s judgement) and can be analysed mathematically, so they are preferred where possible. A qualitative result can often be made quantitative, e.g. a colorimeter (an instrument that measures how much light a solution absorbs) reading instead of a colour.
[bio2-s4.0.3] Continuous data can take any value in a range, including decimals, e.g. mass, temperature or time.
[bio2-s4.0.4] Discrete data are whole-number counts, because you cannot have half a seed, e.g. number of seeds or number of stomata (the pores in a leaf).
[bio2-s4.0.5] Categoric data are named groups, e.g. eye colour or type of habitat.
[bio2-s4.0.6] Primary data are collected by the investigator. Secondary data are collected by somebody else, e.g. published results, databases or other groups’ results.
[bio2-s4.0.7] Selecting data means choosing the data that are relevant to the question and of good enough quality. Secondary data can extend or check primary data, for example by adding values you could not measure yourself. There is more on judging a secondary source in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio2-s4.1.b] A results table is where raw data first go, so a clear layout makes the later calculations and graph easy. A few fixed conventions apply every time.
[bio2-s4.1.0] The independent variable (the factor you change) goes in the first column. The dependent variable (the factor you measure) goes in the following columns, with a column for each repeat and a column for the mean.
[bio2-s4.1.1] Each column heading gives the quantity and its unit, e.g. “Temperature (°C)” or “Volume of oxygen (cm³)”. Units go in the heading, not after every number, which keeps the table uncluttered.
[bio2-s4.1.2] Record every reading to the same number of decimal places, matching the resolution of the instrument (the smallest change it can show). A balance reading to 0.01 g gives 2.45 g, not 2.4 g or 2.453 g.
[bio2-s4.1.3] List the values of the independent variable in order, so the pattern can be seen down the column.
[bio2-s4.1.4] Record raw results straight into the table during the investigation, including any that look wrong. Writing them down later from memory risks errors, and a result that looks wrong may turn out to matter.
[bio2-s4.2.b] Raw readings are processed into a few summary numbers before they are graphed. The mean gives the typical value of repeats, and the range shows how closely the repeats agree. The worked numbers here are checked and illustrative.
[bio2-s4.2.0] Mean: add the values and divide by the number of values. Anomalous values (ones that do not fit the pattern) are left out of the mean, and the fact is stated.
[bio2-s4.2.1] Worked example for the mean: the final masses of four replicate (repeat) potato cylinders are 2.45 g, 2.44 g, 2.46 g and 2.10 g.
[bio2-s4.2.2] The value 2.10 g is anomalous, so the mean of the other three is (2.45 + 2.44 + 2.46) ÷ 3 = 7.35 ÷ 3 = 2.45 g.
[bio2-s4.2.3] Range: the largest value minus the smallest. It shows the spread of repeat readings, and a small range means the readings are precise (close to each other).
[bio2-s4.2.4] Worked example for the range: 2.46 − 2.44 = 0.02 g, a small spread, so the readings are precise.
[bio2-s4.2.5] The range of the independent variable is the lowest to highest value tested, e.g. 0 °C to 60 °C. It tells the reader which conditions the results cover.
[bio2-s4.3.b] Two further calculations let you compare results fairly. Percentage change deals with samples that started at different sizes, and rate deals with changes that took different times.
[bio2-s4.3.0] Percentage change = (final value − initial value) ÷ initial value × 100. It allows samples that started with different masses to be compared, because each change is expressed relative to its own starting value.
[bio2-s4.3.1] Worked example for percentage change: a cylinder with an initial mass of 2.50 g and a final mass of 2.45 g has a percentage change of (2.45 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = −2.0%.
[bio2-s4.3.2] A negative percentage change is a loss, and a positive value is a gain.
[bio2-s4.3.3] Rate = amount of change ÷ time. For example, 24 cm³ of oxygen in 4 minutes is a rate of 6 cm³ per minute.
[bio2-s4.3.4] When the time taken for something to happen is measured, e.g. the time for starch to disappear, the rate can be calculated as 1 ÷ time. A time of 50 s gives 1 ÷ 50 = 0.02 s⁻¹.
[bio2-s4.3.5] A shorter time means a faster rate, because 1 ÷ time gets larger as the time gets smaller.
[bio2-s4.4.b] Different data suit different graphs. The choice depends on whether the variables are continuous, discrete or categoric (see the previous block’s data types) and on what you want to show.
[bio2-s4.4.0] Line graph: used when both variables are continuous, e.g. rate of enzyme activity against temperature.
[bio2-s4.4.1] Bar chart: used when the independent variable is categoric or discrete, e.g. the number of a species in three habitats. Bars are separate and of equal width, because the categories are distinct groups.
[bio2-s4.4.2] Histogram: shows the frequency (how many) of a continuous variable divided into classes, e.g. the number of students in each height range. Bars touch, because the classes run on from one to the next.
[bio2-s4.4.3] Scatter graph: used to look for a correlation (a link) between two measured variables, e.g. light intensity and the number of a plant species in quadrats.
[bio2-s4.4.4] Pie chart: shows proportions of a whole, e.g. the percentage of each blood group in a class.
[bio2-s4.5.b] Once the graph type is chosen, marks come from drawing it carefully. Axes, scales, plotting and the line each follow a rule.
[bio2-s4.5.0] The independent variable goes on the horizontal (x) axis and the dependent variable on the vertical (y) axis. Label both with the quantity and unit.
[bio2-s4.5.1] Use even, linear scales that use more than half of the grid in each direction, so the graph is large enough to read. The scale need not start at zero if the data do not come near zero, but this must be shown clearly.
[bio2-s4.5.2] Plot points accurately as small crosses or circled dots, and plot the means rather than every repeat.
[bio2-s4.5.3] Draw a line or smooth curve of best fit that shows the trend, with points balanced on either side. Where no trend line can be assumed, join the points with straight ruled lines.
[bio2-s4.5.4] Do not force the line through an anomalous point, because one unreliable reading would distort the trend.
[bio2-s4.5.5] Give the graph a title in the form “the effect of [independent variable] on [dependent variable]”.
[bio2-s4.5.6] Diagram: a line graph of rate of enzyme activity against temperature. Labels: axes with units, plotted points, smooth curve rising to a peak (optimum) then falling, one circled anomalous point off the curve.
[bio2-s4.6.b] A graph is only useful once you can say what it shows. Describing a trend, naming its key features and classifying the relationship between the variables are the first steps in analysing data.
[bio2-s4.6.0] Describe the trend in words and quote data, e.g. “the rate increased from 2 to 8 cm³ per minute as temperature rose from 10 °C to 40 °C, then fell to zero by 60 °C”. Using figures makes the description specific.
[bio2-s4.6.1] Name key features of a graph: a peak or optimum (the value giving the highest result), a plateau (levelling off), and the point where a line crosses zero.
[bio2-s4.6.2] A positive correlation means that as one variable increases, the other increases. A negative correlation means that as one increases, the other decreases. No correlation means there is no pattern.
[bio2-s4.6.3] A straight line through the origin shows that the variables are directly proportional, so doubling one doubles the other.
[bio2-s4.6.4] Diagram: three small scatter graphs side by side. Labels: positive correlation (points rise), negative correlation (points fall), no correlation (points scattered), with both axes marked.
[bio2-s4.7.b] A graph also lets you find values that were not measured and work out how fast something is changing. Each use has its own limits.
[bio2-s4.7.0] Interpolation is reading a value from the graph between plotted points. It is fairly reliable, because the line is supported by data on both sides.
[bio2-s4.7.1] Extrapolation is extending the line beyond the data range to predict a value. It is less reliable, because the pattern may not continue, e.g. an enzyme’s rate does not keep rising with temperature.
[bio2-s4.7.2] The gradient (steepness) of a line graph of amount against time is the rate. A steeper line means a faster rate.
[bio2-s4.7.3] A line levelling off means the reaction is slowing, e.g. as substrate (the substance the enzyme acts on) is used up.
[bio2-s4.8.b] Finding a pattern between two variables is not the same as finding out why. A correlation is a starting point, and several different explanations can lie behind it.
[bio2-s4.8.0] A correlation shows that two variables change together. It does not show that one causes the other.
[bio2-s4.8.1] Other explanations of a correlation are chance, especially with a small sample; a third variable (a confounding variable) that affects both; or a cause that runs the other way.
[bio2-s4.8.2] Illustrative example: people who carry cigarette lighters are more likely to develop lung cancer, but the lighter does not cause cancer. Smoking, the third variable, causes both, because smokers carry lighters and smoking causes lung cancer.
[bio2-s4.8.3] Evidence that one variable causes a change in another comes from a controlled experiment (a fair test with a control, where only the factor being tested changes), together with a biological mechanism that explains it.
[bio2-s4.8.4] Surveys and secondary data alone usually show correlation, because they cannot control other variables. There is more on the strategies in Questioning and predicting.
[bio2-s4.9.b] Real data rarely come out perfectly, and sometimes one result sits well away from the rest. You need to recognise these results and know exactly how to treat them.
[bio2-s4.9.0] An anomalous result (outlier) does not fit the pattern of the other results. It is far from the other repeats at the same value, or off the line of best fit on a graph.
[bio2-s4.9.1] Possible causes are a mistake in measuring or recording, an uncontrolled variable, faulty equipment, or an unusual biological sample.
[bio2-s4.9.2] Check the reading and the calculation, and repeat that measurement if possible. This shows whether it was a simple mistake.
[bio2-s4.9.3] Leave the anomalous result out of the mean and the line of best fit, but keep it in the results table and report it with a possible reason. Hiding it would be dishonest. There is more on this integrity in Designing and conducting investigations.
[bio2-s4.9.4] A result is not anomalous just because it disagrees with the hypothesis. If repeats confirm it, it may show the hypothesis is wrong or reveal something new.
[bio2-s4.10.b] The conclusion is where the data answer the question the investigation set out to test. A good conclusion states the finding, backs it with evidence, explains it and admits its limits.
[bio2-s4.10.0] A conclusion states the relationship found between the independent and dependent variables, e.g. “As sucrose concentration increased, the percentage change in mass of the potato cylinders decreased.”
[bio2-s4.10.1] A conclusion is justified with evidence: quote values or describe the trend in the graph, and say whether the data support or do not support the hypothesis. Never say that the data prove it.
[bio2-s4.10.2] A conclusion explains the result using biological knowledge, e.g. water left the cells by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane) into the more concentrated solutions.
[bio2-s4.10.3] A conclusion is limited to what was tested: the range of the independent variable, the organism or tissue used, and the conditions. It does not claim more than the data show.
[bio2-s4.10.4] Confidence in a conclusion depends on whether the test was fair and valid, the sample size, the number of repeats, the spread of the data, the number of anomalies, and whether the results are repeatable and reproducible. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s4.10.5] A conclusion is valid when it is supported by valid, reliable data.
