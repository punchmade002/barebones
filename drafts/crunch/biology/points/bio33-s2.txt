TARGET bio33-s2: Investigation: growth of microorganisms
[bio33-s2.0.b] Before testing what helps or harms microorganisms, you need to know what counts as growth and how to put a number on it. Most investigations in this subsection measure growth in one of the ways below; the antibacterial test measures the width of a clear zone instead.
[bio33-s2.0.0] Growth of a microbial population means an increase in the number of cells (bacteria and yeasts dividing) or in the size of the mycelium (the mass of thread-like filaments that makes up a mould), not the growth of one cell.
[bio33-s2.0.1] A colony is a visible mass of cells on a solid medium (a jelly-like surface the cells grow on), grown by repeated division from a single cell or spore. Counting colonies therefore estimates how many living cells were on the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.2] Bacterial colonies are usually small, round, shiny or matt spots; mould colonies are fuzzy and spread outwards. This difference in appearance helps you tell which organism has grown.
[bio33-s2.0.3] On plates, count the colonies on each plate if they are separate, or measure the diameter of a mould colony with a ruler under the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.0.4] Alternatively, estimate the percentage of the plate's surface covered, using a grid of squares drawn on the base. This works when colonies have merged and cannot be counted.
[bio33-s2.0.5] In liquid broth cultures, measure the cloudiness (turbidity) with a colorimeter, an instrument that measures how much light passes through. Cloudier broth has more cells and absorbs more light.
[bio33-s2.0.6] For yeast, count cells with a microscope and a counting chamber (a slide marked with a grid of known volume), or measure the CO₂ produced.
[bio33-s2.0.7] Microorganisms need a source of nutrients (for energy and raw materials), water, a suitable temperature and pH and, for most, oxygen. Each of these is one factor tested later in this subsection.
[bio33-s2.0.8] Harmful chemicals and high external solute concentrations (a lot of dissolved salt or sugar outside the cells) inhibit growth, so these are tested too.
[bio33-s2.1.b] Microorganisms are everywhere: in the air, on skin and on benches. Aseptic technique is how you grow only the organism you chose, without letting it or any contaminant spread.
[bio33-s2.1.0] Aseptic technique is the set of procedures that keep unwanted microorganisms out of cultures, so that only the intended organism grows and results are valid.
[bio33-s2.1.1] Aseptic technique also keeps the cultured microorganisms from escaping onto people and surfaces, for safety.
[bio33-s2.1.2] Any culture may contain or become contaminated with harmful microorganisms, so every culture is treated as a possible pathogen (a microorganism that causes disease).
[bio33-s2.1.3] Before starting: tie back long hair, cover cuts with a waterproof plaster, wash hands with soap and water, and wipe the bench with disinfectant. Each step reduces the microorganisms that could get into the culture or onto you.
[bio33-s2.1.4] Do not eat, drink or touch the face during the practical, because this could carry cultured microorganisms into your mouth or eyes.
[bio33-s2.1.5] Sterilise all equipment and media before use: nutrient agar, broths and glassware are heated in an autoclave (or pressure cooker) at 121 °C for 15 minutes, which kills all microorganisms, including bacterial endospores (tough resting cells that survive ordinary heat).
[bio33-s2.1.6] Plastic Petri dishes (the shallow lidded dishes that hold the agar) are bought pre-sterilised and sealed, because plastic would melt in an autoclave.
[bio33-s2.2.b] Once equipment is sterile, the risk is airborne microorganisms and anything that touches the culture. These bench habits deal with both.
[bio33-s2.2.0] Inoculating loop (a wire loop used to transfer microorganisms): heat it in the hottest part of a Bunsen flame until it glows red-hot, before and after every use, to kill any microorganisms on it.
[bio33-s2.2.1] Let the loop cool for a few seconds, without putting it down, before touching the culture, so that the heat does not kill the microorganisms being transferred.
[bio33-s2.2.2] Work close to a lit Bunsen burner: the rising hot air carries airborne microorganisms upwards and away from the open plates and bottles.
[bio33-s2.2.3] When opening a culture bottle or tube, hold the cap in the little finger and never put it down on the bench, because the bench could contaminate the inside of the cap.
[bio33-s2.2.4] Pass the neck of the bottle briefly through the flame after opening and before closing it, to kill microorganisms at the mouth and create an upward air current.
[bio33-s2.2.5] Open a Petri dish lid only slightly, tilted up like a clam shell, and for as short a time as possible, so that airborne microorganisms do not land on the agar.
[bio33-s2.3.b] After inoculating (putting the microorganism onto the plate), the plate must be closed, labelled, incubated (kept warm to let growth happen) and finally disposed of safely. Each rule here has a reason.
[bio33-s2.3.0] After inoculating, secure the lid to the base with two or four short strips of sticky tape, not sealed all the way round, so that oxygen can still enter. Sealing the lid fully would make conditions anaerobic (without oxygen), which would favour the growth of harmful anaerobic bacteria.
[bio33-s2.3.1] Label the base, not the lid (which could be swapped), around the edge with name, date and treatment.
[bio33-s2.3.2] Incubate plates upside down, lid at the bottom, so that condensation forming on the lid does not drip onto the agar and spread the colonies, and the agar does not dry out.
[bio33-s2.3.3] Incubate school cultures at about 25 °C, never at 37 °C, because body temperature favours the growth of microorganisms that are pathogenic to humans.
[bio33-s2.3.4] Never open plates after incubation: examine and count colonies through the closed lid. Never culture samples from the human body, toilets or animal waste, because these are likely to contain pathogens.
[bio33-s2.3.5] Disposal: put used plates and cultures into an autoclave bag and autoclave them (121 °C, 15 minutes) before putting them in the bin, or soak them in strong disinfectant. Disinfect the bench again and wash hands thoroughly at the end.
[bio33-s2.3.6] Spills: cover with paper towels soaked in disinfectant, leave for the time stated on the disinfectant, then clear up wearing gloves, and tell the teacher.
[bio33-s2.3.7] Ethanol, used to sterilise a glass spreader, is highly flammable: keep the beaker of ethanol away from the Bunsen flame. Pass the spreader through the flame only briefly, to burn off the ethanol, holding it pointing down.
[bio33-s2.4.b] A culture medium is the food and water the microorganisms grow on or in. Choosing the right one, and a safe organism, is the first step of every investigation.
[bio33-s2.4.0] A culture medium supplies microorganisms with nutrients and water. Nutrient agar contains agar, a jelly extracted from seaweed, which sets solid and which most microorganisms cannot digest, so the surface stays firm while they grow.
[bio33-s2.4.1] Nutrient agar also contains nutrients such as meat or yeast extract and peptone (partly digested protein), giving amino acids, sugars, vitamins and minerals.
[bio33-s2.4.2] Nutrient broth is the same as nutrient agar without the agar, so it is liquid.
[bio33-s2.4.3] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds grow better on malt agar or potato dextrose agar, which are sugar-rich and slightly acidic.
[bio33-s2.4.4] Suitable microorganisms are baker's yeast suspended in sterile water or sugar solution, moulds such as Rhizopus from bread, or non-pathogenic bacterial cultures supplied for schools.
[bio33-s2.4.5] The source of the microorganism must be known, so the risk is low.
[bio33-s2.5.b] A poured plate is a layer of sterile agar in a Petri dish. How you then put the microorganisms on it depends on what you want to see: separate colonies or an even covering.
[bio33-s2.5.0] Pouring a plate, step 1: melt the sterile agar in its bottle in a boiling water bath.
[bio33-s2.5.1] Step 2: cool the agar to about 50 °C, which is still liquid and cool enough not to kill an organism added to it (agar sets as it cools below about 40 °C).
[bio33-s2.5.2] Step 3: flame the neck of the bottle, to kill microorganisms at the mouth.
[bio33-s2.5.3] Step 4: lift the Petri dish lid slightly and pour in enough agar to cover the base (about 15–20 cm³).
[bio33-s2.5.4] Step 5: replace the lid, swirl gently and leave on a level surface to set.
[bio33-s2.5.5] Streaking: draw the flamed, cooled loop, dipped in the culture, across the agar in a zigzag. This thins the cells out along the line, so they form separate colonies.
[bio33-s2.5.6] Spreading: spread a set volume (e.g. 0.1 cm³) of a culture or diluted suspension evenly with a sterile glass spreader to make a lawn (growth covering the whole plate). A lawn is needed for the antibacterial test. There is more on this in the antibacterial and antifungal investigation below.
[bio33-s2.5.7] Diagram: a Petri dish after inoculation, shown upside down in the incubator. Labels: base labelled with name, date and treatment, lid held with two short strips of tape. Beside it, a flamed inoculating loop held in a Bunsen flame.
[bio33-s2.6.b] All six factor investigations below follow one plan: change one thing, keep everything else the same, and compare against controls. Learn this plan once and you can design any of them.
[bio33-s2.6.0] Aim: to investigate how a named factor affects the growth of a named microorganism. Each investigation changes one factor, the independent variable, and measures growth after a fixed time; the dependent variable is colony count, colony diameter, percentage cover or turbidity.
[bio33-s2.6.1] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair): the same microorganism, culture and volume of inoculum (the amount of culture added) on every plate; the same medium and volume of agar; and the same incubation time.
[bio33-s2.6.2] Also controlled, except when it is the factor tested: the same temperature, pH, nutrient level and water availability, and the same method of measuring.
[bio33-s2.6.3] Uninoculated control: an uninoculated plate (sterile medium only) is incubated with the others. No growth on it shows that the medium and technique were sterile, so any growth on the test plates came from the inoculum.
[bio33-s2.6.4] A reference plate with normal conditions (e.g. normal pH, no added salt, or a water disc in the antibacterial test) is compared with the tested conditions, so you can tell what the factor changed.
[bio33-s2.6.5] Replicates: set up at least three plates for each value of the factor and calculate the mean, so that one contaminated or unusual plate does not mislead. Worked example: colony counts of 42, 38 and 46 at 25 °C give a mean of 126 ÷ 3 = 42 colonies.
[bio33-s2.6.6] Record results in a table: value of the factor, growth on each replicate, and the mean. Plot a bar chart for categories or a line graph for a continuous factor, with mean growth on the y-axis.
[bio33-s2.6.7] Conclusion: describe the trend in the mean results and say whether they support the hypothesis. Explain the results using the biology given for each factor, and compare them with secondary data.
[bio33-s2.7.b] Temperature changes how fast the chemical reactions inside cells run. This investigation compares growth at several temperatures, and tests high temperatures by heating the culture before plating.
[bio33-s2.7.0] Hypothesis: “Growth increases with temperature up to an optimum, then decreases.” The optimum is the temperature at which growth is fastest.
[bio33-s2.7.1] Independent variable: temperature, i.e. about 4 °C, 15 °C, 20 °C and 25 °C, plus 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C for the heated cultures.
[bio33-s2.7.2] Step 1: inoculate identical plates with the same volume of the same culture, e.g. yeast.
[bio33-s2.7.3] Step 2: incubate sets of three at about 4 °C (refrigerator), 15 °C, 20 °C (room) and 25 °C (incubator) for the same time, e.g. 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.7.4] Step 3: count the colonies or measure colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.7.5] Step 4: to test high temperatures safely, heat tubes of the culture in water baths at 40 °C, 60 °C and 80 °C (or boil one) for 10 minutes before plating them. Heating the culture first avoids incubating dangerously warm plates.
[bio33-s2.7.6] Step 5: incubate all the plates at 25 °C, so that the only difference between them is the heat treatment.
[bio33-s2.8.b] The results fit a pattern: slow growth when cold, faster when warmer, and death when too hot. The explanation comes from enzymes, the proteins that control the reactions in cells.
[bio33-s2.8.0] Expected result: little or no growth at 4 °C. Growth increases with temperature over the range tested. Cultures heated to about 60 °C or more grow poorly or not at all.
[bio33-s2.8.1] Explanation: growth depends on enzyme-controlled reactions, and at low temperatures molecules move slowly, so reactions and growth are slow.
[bio33-s2.8.2] Cold does not kill the cells: growth resumes when they are warmed, which is why refrigerated food still goes off slowly and frozen food starts to go off when thawed.
[bio33-s2.8.3] Above the optimum, enzymes and other proteins are denatured (their shape is permanently changed so they stop working), so cells die. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of enzyme activity.
[bio33-s2.8.4] Bacterial endospores survive higher temperatures than active cells, so heating that kills the active cells may leave endospores alive.
[bio33-s2.9.b] pH measures how acidic or alkaline a solution is (7 is neutral). This investigation grows bacteria on agar made up at different pH values to find the range they prefer.
[bio33-s2.9.0] Hypothesis: “Bacteria grow best at about neutral pH and poorly in strongly acidic or alkaline conditions.”
[bio33-s2.9.1] Independent variable: the pH of the agar or broth, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11.
[bio33-s2.9.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar (or broth) at different pH values, e.g. 3, 5, 7, 9 and 11, by adding buffer solutions (which hold the pH steady) or dilute acid or alkali, checked with a pH meter, before pouring.
[bio33-s2.9.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of culture.
[bio33-s2.9.4] Step 3: incubate all at the same temperature for the same time.
[bio33-s2.9.5] Step 4: measure growth.
[bio33-s2.10.b] Bacteria and fungi prefer different pH ranges, and the reason lies in the proteins that run the cell. The same effect is used to preserve food.
[bio33-s2.10.0] Expected result: most bacteria grow best near neutral pH (about pH 6–8) and little in strongly acidic conditions.
[bio33-s2.10.1] Fungi such as yeasts and moulds tolerate, and often grow best in, slightly acidic conditions (about pH 5–6). Repeating the investigation with a fungus and a bacterium shows this difference.
[bio33-s2.10.2] Explanation: pH outside the optimum changes the charges on enzymes and other proteins, so their shape changes and they work slowly or are denatured.
[bio33-s2.10.3] Acid conditions are used to preserve food, e.g. pickling in vinegar, because few microorganisms grow well at low pH.
[bio33-s2.10.4] Yogurt keeps longer because the lactic acid made by its bacteria lowers the pH.
[bio33-s2.11.b] Nutrients are the food a microorganism uses for energy and to build new cells. This investigation compares media with and without nutrients, or with increasing amounts of sugar.
[bio33-s2.11.0] Hypothesis: “Microorganisms grow more when more nutrients are available, until another factor limits growth.”
[bio33-s2.11.1] Independent variable: the nutrient content of the medium, e.g. plain agar compared with nutrient agar, or glucose at 0%, 1%, 2% and 5% for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.2] Step 1: compare plain agar (agar and water only, no nutrients) with nutrient agar; or use broths (or agar) with increasing concentrations of glucose, e.g. 0%, 1%, 2%, 5%, for yeast.
[bio33-s2.11.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of yeast culture.
[bio33-s2.11.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together at the same temperature.
[bio33-s2.11.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or percentage cover.
[bio33-s2.12.b] More food means more growth, but only up to a point. The results also show a link forward to the solute investigation, because very high sugar is harmful.
[bio33-s2.12.0] Expected result: little or no growth on plain agar, because there is nothing for the cells to use.
[bio33-s2.12.1] Growth increases with nutrient concentration and then levels off, as another factor, such as space or the build-up of wastes, becomes limiting (the one that stops growth rising further).
[bio33-s2.12.2] At very high sugar concentrations, growth falls again. There is more on this in Investigation: effect of external solute concentration.
[bio33-s2.12.3] Explanation: nutrients supply the energy, released by respiration of sugars, and the raw materials for new cells, i.e. sugars, amino acids, minerals such as nitrate and phosphate, and vitamins.
[bio33-s2.12.4] Variation: starch agar shows that only microorganisms that secrete amylase (the enzyme that digests starch) can use starch.
[bio33-s2.12.5] Flooding the plate with iodine after incubation leaves a clear zone around such colonies, where the starch has been digested. The rest of the plate turns dark because iodine stains starch.
[bio33-s2.13.b] This investigation uses bread and mould instead of agar plates. It tests whether mould needs moisture by comparing dried bread with moistened bread.
[bio33-s2.13.0] Hypothesis: “Moulds grow only on food that is moist.”
[bio33-s2.13.1] Independent variable: the water content of the bread, dried compared with moistened.
[bio33-s2.13.2] Step 1: take slices of the same bread (without preservatives, which would inhibit growth); dry some fully in an oven at a low temperature and moisten others with the same volume of sterile water.
[bio33-s2.13.3] Step 2: expose each slice to the air of the same room for the same time, e.g. 30 minutes, so that mould spores land on it.
[bio33-s2.13.4] Step 3: seal each in a clear plastic bag or sealed dish and label it.
[bio33-s2.13.5] Step 4: keep all in the same warm, dark place, about 25 °C, for 5–7 days.
[bio33-s2.13.6] Step 5: estimate the percentage cover of mould on each, using a transparent grid of squares held over the bag, e.g. 37 of 100 squares covered = 37%.
[bio33-s2.13.7] Do not open the bags, because the spores released could be breathed in.
[bio33-s2.14.b] Water is essential for life, and microorganisms are no exception. The result explains why drying is an old way of keeping food.
[bio33-s2.14.0] Expected result: mould grows on the moist bread and not, or very little, on the dried bread.
[bio33-s2.14.1] Explanation: microorganisms need water because their enzymes work only in solution, nutrients are absorbed in solution after extracellular digestion (digestion outside the cell), and water is needed for cells to grow and divide.
[bio33-s2.14.2] Dried foods, e.g. dried fruit, pasta, powdered milk and cereals, keep for a long time for this reason.
[bio33-s2.14.3] Safety: mould spores can trigger allergies and asthma, so keep the bags sealed throughout and dispose of them unopened.
[bio33-s2.15.b] A solute is something dissolved in water, such as salt or sugar. This investigation adds increasing amounts of solute to the agar to see what happens to growth.
[bio33-s2.15.0] Hypothesis: “Growth decreases as the concentration of salt (or sugar) outside the cells increases.”
[bio33-s2.15.1] Independent variable: the concentration of sodium chloride (salt), e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10% or 20%, or of sucrose (sugar).
[bio33-s2.15.2] Step 1: make nutrient agar containing increasing concentrations of sodium chloride (e.g. 0%, 2%, 5%, 10%, 20%) or of sucrose.
[bio33-s2.15.3] Step 2: inoculate each with the same volume of bacterial culture.
[bio33-s2.15.4] Step 3: incubate all the plates together for the same time.
[bio33-s2.15.5] Step 4: measure growth by colony count or colony diameter.
[bio33-s2.16.b] Salt and sugar slow growth because of what they do to water movement across the cell surface. This is the basis of salting and sugaring food.
[bio33-s2.16.0] Expected result: growth decreases as the solute concentration rises, with little or no growth at the highest concentrations.
[bio33-s2.16.1] Explanation: when the solution outside a cell has a higher solute concentration, and so lower water concentration, than the cytoplasm, water leaves the cell by osmosis (the movement of water across a membrane towards the more concentrated solution).
[bio33-s2.16.2] The cell then loses water, its enzymes cannot work, and it stops growing or dies.
[bio33-s2.16.3] In a very dilute solution water enters the cell, but the bacterial or fungal cell wall stops the cell bursting.
[bio33-s2.16.4] This is the basis of preserving food with salt (salted fish and bacon) and sugar (jam). There is more on this in Osmosis in food preservation and plant health.
[bio33-s2.17.b] Some chemicals stop microorganisms growing. Knowing which type acts on which group, and what the investigation sets out to show, comes before the method.
[bio33-s2.17.0] Antibacterial chemicals kill bacteria or stop them growing: antibiotics (e.g. penicillin, streptomycin), antiseptics (used on living tissue, e.g. on skin) and disinfectants (used on surfaces and objects).
[bio33-s2.17.1] Antifungal chemicals, e.g. the antifungal creams used for athlete's foot, or fungicides, act on fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.2] Antibiotics do not affect fungi, and antifungals generally do not affect bacteria, because the two groups have different structures. There is more on this in Bacteria and fungi.
[bio33-s2.17.3] Hypothesis: “Different antiseptics (or antibiotics) inhibit the growth of a bacterium to different extents.”
[bio33-s2.17.4] Independent variable: the antibacterial or antifungal chemical on each disc; dependent variable: the diameter of the clear zone around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.b] In the disc diffusion method, paper discs soaked in chemicals are placed on a lawn of bacteria. Wherever the chemical stops growth, a clear zone appears around the disc.
[bio33-s2.18.0] Step 1: spread a set volume of the culture evenly over a nutrient agar plate with a sterile spreader, to make a lawn.
[bio33-s2.18.1] Step 2: using sterile forceps (flamed and cooled), dip paper discs of the same size (e.g. 6 mm, cut with a hole punch and sterilised) in each chemical for the same time, drain them, and place them on the lawn, well spaced.
[bio33-s2.18.2] Step 3: include a control disc dipped in sterile distilled water. Ready-made antibiotic discs, or a multi-disc with several antibiotics on one ring, can be used instead.
[bio33-s2.18.3] Step 4: tape and label the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.4] Step 5: incubate upside down at about 25 °C for 2–3 days.
[bio33-s2.18.5] Step 6: measure the diameter of the clear zone around each disc with a ruler on the base of the plate.
[bio33-s2.18.6] For an antifungal chemical, make a lawn of yeast on malt agar and use discs of the antifungal, with a water control and, to show the difference in antibacterial and antifungal action, an antibiotic disc.
[bio33-s2.18.7] Diagram: a lawn plate with four discs. Labels: each disc's chemical, clear zones of inhibition of different sizes, and no zone around the water control.
[bio33-s2.19.b] The clear zones are the result. Their size tells you how well each chemical works, and the way they form explains why.
[bio33-s2.19.0] Expected result: a clear zone, the zone of inhibition, forms around discs whose chemical stops the bacterium growing.
[bio33-s2.19.1] There is no clear zone around the water control, which shows that the disc and the water alone do not stop growth.
[bio33-s2.19.2] The larger the zone, the more effective the chemical is against that organism at that concentration. In other words, the bacterium is more sensitive to it.
[bio33-s2.19.3] On the yeast lawn, the antifungal gives a clear zone and the antibiotic gives none.
[bio33-s2.19.4] The chemical diffuses outwards from the disc into the agar, so its concentration falls with distance; bacteria cannot grow where the concentration is high enough to kill or inhibit them.
[bio33-s2.19.5] A zone's size also depends on how fast the chemical diffuses, so it is a guide to strength, not an exact measure.
[bio33-s2.19.6] A bacterium that grows right up to an antibiotic disc is resistant to that antibiotic.
[bio33-s2.19.7] In hospitals the same test is used to find which antibiotic will work against the bacterium from a patient. There is more on this in Emergence and spread of infectious disease.
[bio33-s2.20.b] Comparing zone areas, not just diameters, is fairer. Here is the calculation for a clear zone of diameter 18 mm around a 6 mm disc.
[bio33-s2.20.0] Radius = 9 mm; area of the zone = π r² = 3.14 × 9 × 9 ≈ 254 mm².
[bio33-s2.20.1] Subtracting the disc (radius 3 mm, area ≈ 28 mm²) gives a clear area of about 226 mm², which is the area where bacteria were actually stopped from growing.
[bio33-s2.20.2] Areas let zones be compared fairly, because doubling the diameter quadruples the area.
[bio33-s2.21.b] Your own results are primary data. A good conclusion also checks them against data collected by other people.
[bio33-s2.21.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, e.g. other class groups, textbooks, published research or food-safety guidance.
[bio33-s2.21.1] Conclusions must be supported by both primary and secondary data: compare your own results with the class's pooled results and with published findings.
[bio33-s2.21.2] Published findings include that most human pathogens grow best near body temperature and neutral pH, and that most bacteria grow little below refrigerator temperature.
[bio33-s2.22.b] Each factor you investigated is used to keep food safe. Preserving food means stopping microorganisms growing, either by killing them or by making conditions unsuitable.
[bio33-s2.22.0] Temperature: refrigeration and freezing slow or stop growth but do not kill microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.1] Heat: pasteurisation, heating milk briefly to a temperature below boiling and then cooling it quickly, kills most microorganisms.
[bio33-s2.22.2] Sterilisation: canning and ultra-heat treatment (UHT) heat food enough to sterilise it, and sealing keeps microorganisms out.
[bio33-s2.22.3] Water: drying removes water, which microorganisms need for their enzymes to work.
[bio33-s2.22.4] Solute: salting and sugaring use high solute concentration, which draws water out of microbial cells.
[bio33-s2.22.5] pH: pickling uses low pH.
[bio33-s2.23.b] The remaining factors give further preservation methods and wider uses in hygiene and in industry.
[bio33-s2.23.0] Chemicals: chemical preservatives inhibit growth.
[bio33-s2.23.1] Oxygen: vacuum packing removes oxygen, slowing aerobic microorganisms (those that need oxygen to grow).
[bio33-s2.23.2] Health and hygiene: antiseptics on wounds and skin, disinfectants on surfaces, and aseptic technique in surgery. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s2.23.3] Industry: bioreactors (vessels in which microorganisms are grown on a large scale) are kept at the optimum conditions found by such investigations to maximise growth.
[bio33-s2.24.b] Every fault below can make results unreliable. Each has a simple fix, so learn each error together with its fix.
[bio33-s2.24.0] Contamination by airborne or skin microorganisms gives colonies of a different appearance; check with the uninoculated control, and repeat with better aseptic technique.
[bio33-s2.24.1] Unequal inoculum, i.e. different volumes or numbers of cells, on different plates; use a measured volume from a well-mixed culture.
[bio33-s2.24.2] Colonies that grow together cannot be counted; dilute the culture so that colonies are separate, or measure percentage cover instead.
[bio33-s2.24.3] Agar or broth that is not at the intended pH or concentration; check with a pH meter and measure accurately.
[bio33-s2.24.4] Temperature not constant, e.g. the “room temperature” plates are near a radiator.
[bio33-s2.24.5] Discs not equally soaked, or chemicals not of known concentration; soak all discs for the same time.
[bio33-s2.24.6] Too few replicates; use at least three and calculate means.

CANDIDATES bio33-s1: Bacteria and fungi
[bio33-s1.0.b] Microorganisms are living things so small that you need a microscope to see them. Bacteria and fungi matter most for nutrient cycling, because they return the nutrients locked in dead matter to the soil.
[bio33-s1.0.0] Microorganisms are organisms too small to be seen without a microscope, e.g. bacteria, many fungi (such as yeasts and moulds), protists and viruses.
[bio33-s1.0.1] Most microorganisms are harmless or useful; only some are pathogens (organisms that cause disease). There is more on this in Pathogens.
[bio33-s1.0.2] Bacteria and fungi are the main decomposers: they feed on dead organisms, dead leaves, dung and other wastes, breaking down complex organic compounds (carbon-containing molecules made by living things).
[bio33-s1.0.3] Decomposers release carbon dioxide (by respiration) and minerals such as ammonium and phosphate into the soil, where plants reuse them.
[bio33-s1.0.4] Without decomposers, nutrients would stay locked in dead matter and plants would run out of the minerals they need to grow.
[bio33-s1.0.5] Other bacteria carry out particular steps of the nitrogen cycle. Nitrogen-fixing bacteria convert nitrogen gas from the air into a usable form; they live free in soil or in the root nodules (small swellings) of legumes.
[bio33-s1.0.6] Nitrifying bacteria convert ammonium to nitrate, a form of nitrogen that plants can take up through their roots.
[bio33-s1.0.7] Denitrifying bacteria convert nitrate back to nitrogen gas, returning nitrogen to the air.
[bio33-s1.1.b] Bacteria are the simplest cells you need to know. Their outer layers shape and protect the cell, and each layer has a different job.
[bio33-s1.1.0] Bacteria are single-celled prokaryotes, which means they have no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles (compartments inside the cell, each wrapped in its own membrane).
[bio33-s1.1.1] Most bacteria are about 1–10 µm long, so they appear as tiny dots or rods under the light microscope, and seeing their parts needs the electron microscope.
[bio33-s1.1.2] Cell wall: a rigid layer outside the membrane, made of peptidoglycan, a polymer of sugars and amino acids.
[bio33-s1.1.3] The cell wall gives the cell its shape and stops it bursting when water enters by osmosis (water moving into the cell because it is more concentrated inside).
[bio33-s1.1.4] Penicillin works by stopping bacteria building their cell wall, so the weakened cells burst. There is more on this in Acquired immunity, prevention and treatment.
[bio33-s1.1.5] Capsule (slime layer): a sticky layer of polysaccharide (a large sugar molecule) outside the wall, present in some bacteria.
[bio33-s1.1.6] The capsule protects the cell from drying out and from being engulfed (taken in and destroyed) by white blood cells, and helps it stick to surfaces such as teeth. Bacteria with capsules are often more harmful.
[bio33-s1.2.b] Inside the wall, a bacterium has the same basic machinery as any cell, but it is not packaged in organelles. Everything floats in the cytoplasm, or sits on the membrane.
[bio33-s1.2.0] Cell membrane: controls what enters and leaves the cell. The enzymes of respiration are on it, because bacteria have no mitochondria.
[bio33-s1.2.1] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents where most reactions take place. It contains food stores such as granules of glycogen or lipid.
[bio33-s1.2.2] Nucleoid: one circular chromosome of DNA lies free in the cytoplasm in a region called the nucleoid. Bacteria have DNA but no nucleus.
[bio33-s1.2.3] The nucleoid is not enclosed by a membrane and the DNA is not wound around histones (proteins that DNA coils around in eukaryotes). The chromosome carries the genes for all the cell's essential functions.
[bio33-s1.2.4] Plasmids: small extra rings of DNA, separate from the chromosome, which copy themselves independently.
[bio33-s1.2.5] Plasmids carry a few genes that are useful in some conditions, e.g. antibiotic resistance, and can be passed from one bacterium to another, even of a different species. Plasmids are used as vectors (carriers of new genes) in genetic engineering.
[bio33-s1.2.6] Ribosomes: small (70S) ribosomes, free in the cytoplasm, where proteins are made.
[bio33-s1.2.7] Bacterial 70S ribosomes are smaller than the 80S ribosomes in eukaryotic cytoplasm, which is why some antibiotics can block them without harming human cells.
[bio33-s1.3.b] Some bacteria also have threads on their surface. They let the cell swim, stick to things, or swap DNA with another bacterium.
[bio33-s1.3.0] Flagellum (plural flagella): a long, whip-like thread, present in some bacteria, that rotates like a propeller to move the cell, e.g. towards food.
[bio33-s1.3.1] Pili (fimbriae): short, hair-like threads on the surface of some bacteria that attach them to surfaces and host cells (the living organisms a bacterium infects).
[bio33-s1.3.2] A longer sex pilus links two bacteria so that a copy of a plasmid can pass between them.
[bio33-s1.3.3] Diagram: draw a large rod-shaped bacterium. Label capsule, cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, nucleoid with circular chromosome, plasmids, ribosomes, flagellum, pili and a food-storage granule, with a function written beside each label.
[bio33-s1.4.b] Bacteria are classified partly by shape, which you can recognise under the microscope. There are four shapes to know, each with a named example.
[bio33-s1.4.0] Cocci (singular coccus) are spherical: Streptococcus forms chains and Staphylococcus forms clusters.
[bio33-s1.4.1] Bacilli (singular bacillus) are rod-shaped, e.g. Escherichia coli.
[bio33-s1.4.2] Spirilla are spiral-shaped.
[bio33-s1.4.3] Vibrios are comma-shaped, e.g. Vibrio cholerae, which causes cholera.
[bio33-s1.4.4] Diagram: the shapes, each labelled: a chain of cocci, a cluster of cocci, a rod (bacillus), a spiral (spirillum) and a comma (vibrio).
[bio33-s1.5.b] Bacteria multiply by simple splitting, which is quick and makes identical copies. When conditions turn hostile, some of them form a tough resting structure that lets them survive.
[bio33-s1.5.0] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission: the circular chromosome is copied, the cell grows and the two copies move apart.
[bio33-s1.5.1] The cell then divides in two as a new wall forms across the middle, giving two genetically identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.2] In ideal conditions binary fission can happen as often as every 20 minutes, so populations grow very fast. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.
[bio33-s1.5.3] Variation in bacteria comes only from mutations (changes in DNA) and from genes passed on plasmids, because binary fission produces identical cells.
[bio33-s1.5.4] Some bacteria, e.g. Clostridium and Bacillus, form an endospore when conditions become unfavourable, such as a lack of nutrients or water.
[bio33-s1.5.5] In an endospore the chromosome and a little cytoplasm are enclosed in a tough, thick wall inside the cell, and the rest of the cell breaks down.
[bio33-s1.5.6] Endospores survive boiling, drying, freezing and many disinfectants for years; when conditions improve, each grows into one new active bacterium.
[bio33-s1.5.7] Endospores are why equipment is sterilised in an autoclave at 121 °C rather than by boiling, and why tetanus can be caught from soil.
[bio33-s1.6.b] Bacteria feed in many ways. Autotrophs, the first group, make their own food from simple inorganic substances, using either light or chemical energy.
[bio33-s1.6.0] Autotrophic bacteria make their own food from simple inorganic substances such as carbon dioxide and water.
[bio33-s1.6.1] Photosynthetic bacteria use light energy, e.g. cyanobacteria, which contain chlorophyll and release oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.2] Some other photosynthetic bacteria, such as purple and green sulfur bacteria, use hydrogen sulfide in place of water and release no oxygen.
[bio33-s1.6.3] Chemosynthetic bacteria get energy by oxidising inorganic chemicals (adding oxygen to them, which releases energy) instead of from light, e.g. nitrifying bacteria in soil.
[bio33-s1.6.4] Nitrifying bacteria oxidise ammonium to nitrite and nitrite to nitrate, and use the energy released to make food from carbon dioxide.
[bio33-s1.7.b] Most bacteria are heterotrophs: they cannot make food, so they take in food made by other organisms. They also differ in whether they can live with oxygen.
[bio33-s1.7.0] Heterotrophic bacteria take in food made by other organisms, and most bacteria are heterotrophic.
[bio33-s1.7.1] Saprophytic bacteria feed on dead organic matter. A saprophyte (saprotroph) secretes digestive enzymes out of its cell onto the food, which is extracellular digestion.
[bio33-s1.7.2] A saprophyte then absorbs the small soluble products, e.g. sugars and amino acids, through its wall and membrane. Saprophytic bacteria are decomposers, and they also cause food to rot.
[bio33-s1.7.3] Parasitic bacteria feed on a living host and harm it, causing disease, e.g. Mycobacterium tuberculosis, which causes tuberculosis.
[bio33-s1.7.4] Mutualistic bacteria live with a host to the benefit of both, e.g. nitrogen-fixing bacteria in clover root nodules and bacteria in the gut.
[bio33-s1.7.5] Obligate aerobes need oxygen to respire.
[bio33-s1.7.6] Obligate anaerobes are killed by oxygen, e.g. Clostridium, which is why tetanus grows in deep wounds where oxygen cannot reach.
[bio33-s1.7.7] Facultative anaerobes respire aerobically when oxygen is present and anaerobically when it is not, e.g. E. coli.
[bio33-s1.8.b] Fungi are eukaryotes that look plant-like but are not plants. Most are built from thread-like hyphae, and they spread by spores.
[bio33-s1.8.0] Fungi are eukaryotes: each cell, or each section of hypha, has nuclei with a nuclear membrane, mitochondria and 80S ribosomes.
[bio33-s1.8.1] Fungal cell walls are made of chitin, not cellulose.
[bio33-s1.8.2] Fungi have no chlorophyll, so they cannot photosynthesise; they store carbohydrate as glycogen and lipid, not starch.
[bio33-s1.8.3] Most fungi, such as moulds and mushrooms, are made of hyphae (singular hypha): long, branching, thread-like tubes with a wall around cytoplasm containing many nuclei. A mass of hyphae is a mycelium.
[bio33-s1.8.4] Some fungi have cross-walls (septa) dividing the hyphae into cells; Rhizopus has none.
[bio33-s1.8.5] Mushrooms are the reproductive structures of a mycelium that grows mostly hidden in soil or wood.
[bio33-s1.8.6] Fungi reproduce by spores, tiny reproductive cells released in great numbers and carried by air, water or animals. Each spore can grow into a new mycelium on a suitable food; fungi also reproduce sexually.
[bio33-s1.9.b] Yeasts are the exception among fungi, because they are not made of hyphae. Each yeast is a single cell.
[bio33-s1.9.0] Yeasts are single-celled fungi, e.g. Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's and brewer's yeast), which are oval cells.
[bio33-s1.9.1] Yeasts reproduce asexually by budding: a small outgrowth grows from the parent cell, the nucleus divides by mitosis (copying itself to give two identical nuclei), and the bud separates as a new cell.
[bio33-s1.9.2] Yeast can respire aerobically or ferment sugar to ethanol and CO₂, which is why it is used in baking and brewing.
[bio33-s1.10.b] Rhizopus is a mould you can see growing on stale bread. Its body is made of different kinds of hypha, each with a job.
[bio33-s1.10.0] Rhizopus stolonifer (black bread mould) is a common mould that grows on bread, fruit and other moist, starchy or sugary food.
[bio33-s1.10.1] Its spores are everywhere in the air, which is why bread left moist and warm goes mouldy within days.
[bio33-s1.10.2] Hyphae: the mycelium is made of branched hyphae with no cross-walls, so each hypha is a continuous tube of cytoplasm with many nuclei. The walls of the hyphae contain chitin.
[bio33-s1.10.3] Stolons are hyphae that grow horizontally over the surface of the food, spreading the mould to new areas; the name stolonifer means “bearing stolons”.
[bio33-s1.10.4] Rhizoids are short, branched, root-like hyphae that grow down into the food where a stolon touches it.
[bio33-s1.10.5] Rhizoids are hyphae, not roots: they anchor the mould, secrete digestive enzymes into the food and absorb the products, not water from soil.
[bio33-s1.10.6] Sporangiophores are upright hyphae that grow up from the stolons, above the rhizoids, each carrying a sporangium at its tip.
[bio33-s1.11.b] The upright hyphae carry the spore cases. Spores are how Rhizopus spreads, and the food it grows on is also its only source of nutrients.
[bio33-s1.11.0] A sporangium (plural sporangia) is a round spore case at the tip of a sporangiophore, white at first and black when ripe, which gives the mould its black, fuzzy look.
[bio33-s1.11.1] Inside the sporangium, many spores form around a dome-shaped swelling at the top of the sporangiophore, the columella, which supports the sporangium.
[bio33-s1.11.2] When the sporangium is ripe, its wall breaks and the spores are released and carried by air currents.
[bio33-s1.11.3] Each spore that lands on moist food grows into a new hypha and mycelium; this is asexual reproduction, so the offspring are genetically identical.
[bio33-s1.11.4] Rhizopus is a saprophyte, feeding on dead organic matter. Being heterotrophic, it cannot make food, so it must grow on its food.
[bio33-s1.11.5] Diagram: draw Rhizopus on bread. Label mycelium, hyphae, stolon, rhizoids penetrating the bread, sporangiophores, a sporangium cut open (spores, columella) and spores being released.
[bio33-s1.12.b] Fungi cannot make their own food, so every fungus feeds on organic matter made by other organisms. They differ in whether that food is dead, living or shared.
[bio33-s1.12.0] All fungi are heterotrophic; none is autotrophic, because fungi have no chlorophyll.
[bio33-s1.12.1] Most fungi are saprophytes, feeding on dead organic matter by extracellular digestion (digesting food outside the cell).
[bio33-s1.12.2] Rhizopus secretes enzymes from its rhizoids and hyphae into bread: amylase digests starch to maltose and glucose, and proteases digest proteins to amino acids.
[bio33-s1.12.3] The soluble products diffuse back and are absorbed, then used for respiration and growth.
[bio33-s1.12.4] Mushrooms and other wood- and leaf-rotting fungi are the main decomposers of the cellulose and woody material of dead plants, which most bacteria cannot break down.
[bio33-s1.12.5] Parasitic fungi feed on living hosts and harm them, e.g. athlete's foot in humans and ash dieback in ash trees.
[bio33-s1.12.6] Mutualistic fungi live in partnership with other organisms to the benefit of both: mycorrhizal fungi with plant roots, and the fungus in a lichen.
[bio33-s1.13.b] Bacteria and fungi both decompose dead matter, but their cells are built very differently. These contrasts are the first set to learn, as a pair for each feature.
[bio33-s1.13.0] Cellular nature: bacteria are prokaryotic, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; fungi are eukaryotic, with nuclei, mitochondria and other membrane-bound organelles.
[bio33-s1.13.1] DNA: bacteria have one circular chromosome, not wound around histones, plus plasmids; fungi have several linear chromosomes of DNA wound around histones, inside a nucleus.
[bio33-s1.13.2] Ribosomes: bacteria have 70S ribosomes; fungi have 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
[bio33-s1.13.3] Cell wall: bacteria have peptidoglycan walls; fungi have chitin walls.
[bio33-s1.13.4] Organisation and size: bacteria are single cells about 1–10 µm long, which grow in colonies but each cell lives independently.
[bio33-s1.13.5] Most fungi are multicellular, with hyphae that form a mycelium visible to the naked eye, although yeasts are single cells, larger than most bacteria.
[bio33-s1.13.6] Food stores: bacteria store glycogen or lipid granules; fungi store glycogen and lipid, and neither stores starch.
[bio33-s1.14.b] The other differences are in how they feed, reproduce and respond to drugs. The groups also share some features, so a full comparison includes both.
[bio33-s1.14.0] Nutrition: bacteria can be autotrophic (photosynthetic or chemosynthetic) or heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic); fungi are always heterotrophic (saprophytic, parasitic or mutualistic).
[bio33-s1.14.1] Reproduction: bacteria reproduce by binary fission, and some form endospores for survival; fungi reproduce by spores (moulds) or budding (yeasts), and also sexually.
[bio33-s1.14.2] Treatment of infections: antibiotics act on bacteria, targeting the peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosomes, and not on fungi; fungal infections need antifungal drugs.
[bio33-s1.14.3] Similarities: both can be decomposers that digest food outside their cells and absorb it; both have cell walls, a cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
[bio33-s1.14.4] Both groups include pathogens and many useful species. There is more on this in Microorganisms in industry.

CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.

CANDIDATES bio25-s1: Diffusion, osmosis and active transport
[bio25-s1.0.b] Every cell has to take in what it needs and get rid of what it doesn't, and the cell membrane is the gatekeeper that controls this. Understanding what the membrane lets through, and how, explains the three transport processes in this subsection.
[bio25-s1.0.0] Every cell is surrounded by a cell membrane: a phospholipid bilayer (a double layer of fat-based molecules) with proteins scattered through it, known as the fluid mosaic model. The membranes around organelles (the small working parts inside a cell) are built the same way.
[bio25-s1.0.1] Materials constantly cross the membrane: cells take in oxygen (O₂), glucose, water and ions (charged particles), and release carbon dioxide (CO₂) and wastes such as urea. This is the transport and transfer of materials across membranes.
[bio25-s1.0.2] The membrane is selectively permeable: it lets some substances through and not others. Small, non-polar molecules (ones with no electrical charge difference across them) such as oxygen and carbon dioxide pass straight through the bilayer.
[bio25-s1.0.3] Water passes through slowly between the phospholipids and quickly through protein water channels (aquaporins). Ions and large polar molecules such as glucose cross only through specific channel or carrier proteins, and large molecules such as proteins and starch cannot cross at all.
[bio25-s1.0.4] Three terms for barriers are easy to mix up. Fully permeable lets water and all solutes through, e.g. the plant cell wall. Semi-permeable (partially permeable) lets water and small molecules through but not larger ones, by the size of its pores, e.g. Visking (dialysis) tubing.
[bio25-s1.0.5] Selectively permeable describes a living membrane, which controls what crosses using its proteins and, unlike the other two, can move substances actively (using energy).
[bio25-s1.0.6] A solution is a solute (e.g. sugar or salt) dissolved in a solvent (water). A concentrated solution has much solute and so a lower concentration of water; a dilute solution has little solute and so a higher concentration of water.
[bio25-s1.1.b] Diffusion is the simplest way for substances to move in and out of cells, and it needs no energy from the cell. It is the baseline against which osmosis and active transport are compared.
[bio25-s1.1.0] Diffusion is the net movement of molecules or ions from a region of their higher concentration to a region of their lower concentration, until they are evenly spread. The difference in concentration between two regions is the concentration gradient, and diffusion is movement down the gradient.
[bio25-s1.1.1] Diffusion is passive: it needs no energy from the cell (no ATP, the cell's energy-carrying molecule), because it results from the random movement of particles, which have kinetic (movement) energy.
[bio25-s1.1.2] Individual particles move in all directions, but more move from the crowded region than into it, which gives the net movement. It happens in gases and liquids, in living and non-living systems, with or without a membrane.
[bio25-s1.1.3] Examples in living things: oxygen diffuses from the air in the alveoli (the air sacs of the lungs) into the blood while carbon dioxide diffuses the other way. There is more on this in Gas exchange and the breathing mechanism.
[bio25-s1.1.4] Carbon dioxide diffuses into a leaf through the stomata (pores) while oxygen diffuses out, and digested food molecules diffuse from the small intestine into the blood. A non-living example is the scent of perfume spreading through a room.
[bio25-s1.1.5] Facilitated diffusion is passive diffusion of ions and polar molecules, such as glucose, down their concentration gradient through specific channel or carrier proteins. They need the proteins because they cannot cross the bilayer directly; it still needs no ATP, e.g. glucose enters red blood cells this way.
[bio25-s1.1.6] Factors that increase the rate of diffusion are a steeper concentration gradient, higher temperature (particles move faster), a larger surface area, a shorter distance (a thinner barrier) and smaller particles.
[bio25-s1.1.7] Exchange surfaces such as alveoli and villi (finger-like folds of the small intestine wall) have a large surface area, a thin wall and a steep gradient kept up by blood flow, so diffusion is fast. Osmosis depends on the same factors.
[bio25-s1.2.b] Water crosses membranes by its own process, because it is a small molecule that moves in and out of cells all the time. Osmosis is diffusion of water, and it explains why cells swell and shrink.
[bio25-s1.2.0] Osmosis is the net movement of water molecules from a region of higher water concentration (a dilute solution) to a region of lower water concentration (a more concentrated solution) across a semi-permeable (selectively permeable) membrane.
[bio25-s1.2.1] Osmosis is a special case of diffusion: it is the diffusion of water only, and it happens only across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. It is passive and needs no ATP.
[bio25-s1.2.2] Why water moves: solute molecules attract water molecules around them, so fewer water molecules are free to move in a concentrated solution. More free water molecules cross the membrane from the dilute side than from the concentrated side.
[bio25-s1.2.3] This gives a net flow of water into the concentrated solution, until the concentrations are equal or a pressure, such as that of a cell wall, stops it. Water still crosses in both directions; the flow is only greater one way.
[bio25-s1.2.4] Diagram: a U-tube (or beaker) divided by a semi-permeable membrane, dilute solution one side, concentrated sucrose solution the other; larger arrow of water towards the concentrated side, liquid level rising there. Label: semi-permeable membrane, water molecule, sucrose molecule (too large to cross), net movement of water.
[bio25-s1.3.b] Cells sit in watery surroundings, so osmosis decides whether they gain or lose water. Animal cells and plant cells react differently because plant cells have a cell wall.
[bio25-s1.3.0] Three terms describe the solution outside a cell compared with the cell's contents. Hypotonic means more dilute than the cell, so water enters the cell; hypertonic means more concentrated than the cell, so water leaves it; isotonic means the same concentration, so there is no net movement.
[bio25-s1.3.1] Animal cells in a hypotonic solution (e.g. pure water) gain water, swell and may burst, because they have no cell wall to hold them in. Red blood cells bursting is called haemolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.2] In a hypertonic solution animal cells lose water and shrink, and red blood cells become shrivelled and spiky (crenated). This is why body fluids must be kept isotonic with cells (osmoregulation). There is more on this in Water.
[bio25-s1.3.3] Plant cells in a hypotonic solution take water into the vacuole (the large water-filled sac in the cell). The cytoplasm is pushed against the cell wall, which resists further swelling, so the cell becomes turgid (firm) and doesn't burst. The pressure of the contents against the wall is turgor pressure.
[bio25-s1.3.4] Plant cells in a hypertonic solution lose water from the vacuole. The cell becomes flaccid (soft), then the cytoplasm and cell membrane shrink away from the cell wall, which is called plasmolysis.
[bio25-s1.3.5] The gap between wall and membrane fills with the outside solution, because the cell wall is fully permeable. Plasmolysis can be reversed (deplasmolysis) if the cell is put back into water before it is damaged.
[bio25-s1.3.6] Diagram: a plant cell in water (turgid, large vacuole, cytoplasm pressed against wall), in isotonic solution (flaccid) and in concentrated solution (plasmolysed, membrane and cytoplasm pulled from wall). Label: cell wall, cell membrane, vacuole, cytoplasm. Also red blood cells in water (swollen, bursting), isotonic (normal disc) and concentrated solution (crenated).
[bio25-s1.4.b] Diffusion and osmosis only move things down a gradient, but cells often need to collect substances that are already scarcer outside than inside. Active transport does this by spending energy.
[bio25-s1.4.0] Active transport is the movement of molecules or ions across a membrane from a region of lower concentration to a region of higher concentration, against the concentration gradient, using energy from ATP.
[bio25-s1.4.1] Because it needs ATP, active transport happens only in living cells and depends on respiration (the process that makes ATP). It stops or slows if respiration is stopped, e.g. by lack of oxygen, low temperature or a respiratory poison such as cyanide.
[bio25-s1.4.2] Cells that carry out much active transport have many mitochondria (the organelles that make ATP), e.g. root hair cells, cells lining the kidney tubules and cells of the small intestine.
[bio25-s1.4.3] Root hair cells take up mineral ions, e.g. nitrate, from soil water in which they are more dilute than in the cell. There is more on this in Transport in Plants.
[bio25-s1.4.4] Glucose and amino acids are absorbed from the small intestine into the blood, and reabsorbed from the kidney tubules (the tiny tubes that filter blood) into the blood, by active transport.
[bio25-s1.5.b] Active transport is carried out by carrier proteins in the membrane, which act as pumps. Each pump works in the same four steps.
[bio25-s1.5.0] 1. The substance binds to a specific carrier protein (a pump) in the membrane, so each pump moves only the substance it fits.
[bio25-s1.5.1] 2. ATP made in respiration releases energy, which changes the shape of the carrier protein.
[bio25-s1.5.2] 3. The change in shape moves the substance across the membrane and releases it on the other side.
[bio25-s1.5.3] 4. The carrier returns to its original shape, ready to pick up another molecule.
[bio25-s1.5.4] Diagram: a carrier protein in a phospholipid bilayer, a molecule binding on the low-concentration side, ATP → ADP + Pi releasing energy, carrier changing shape, molecule released on the high-concentration side. Label: carrier protein, phospholipid bilayer, ATP, direction of movement against the gradient.
[bio25-s1.6.b] This carrier pump moves two ions in opposite directions at once. It is found in animal cells and is essential for nerves.
[bio25-s1.6.0] The sodium–potassium pump is a carrier protein in the membranes of animal cells, including nerve cells. It uses one ATP to pump three sodium ions (Na⁺) out of the cell and two potassium ions (K⁺) into it, both against their concentration gradients.
[bio25-s1.6.1] It keeps Na⁺ high outside the cell and K⁺ high inside, which nerve cells need in order to carry impulses, and it helps control cell volume.
[bio25-s1.7.b] The three processes are easy to confuse, so exams ask you to compare them. Compare them on the same five points each time: energy, direction, what moves, membrane and proteins, and where they happen.
[bio25-s1.7.0] Energy: diffusion and osmosis are passive, so they need no ATP; active transport needs ATP from respiration.
[bio25-s1.7.1] Direction: diffusion and osmosis go down a concentration gradient (high to low concentration of the substance moving; for osmosis, the water concentration), whereas active transport goes against it, from low to high.
[bio25-s1.7.2] What moves: diffusion moves any particle, gases and solutes alike; osmosis moves only water; active transport moves particular solutes, mainly ions and small molecules such as glucose, but not water.
[bio25-s1.7.3] Membrane and proteins: diffusion can happen with or without a membrane and needs no proteins for small non-polar molecules; osmosis needs a semi-permeable membrane; active transport needs specific carrier proteins in a living membrane.
[bio25-s1.7.4] Where: diffusion and osmosis happen in living and non-living systems, e.g. Visking tubing, but active transport happens only in living cells, because it depends on respiration.
