TARGET bio12-s2: The theory of evolution by natural selection
[bio12-s2.0.b] Evolution is the big idea of this chapter, and natural selection is the process that drives it. Adaptation is the link between the two: it is the kind of feature that selection favours.
[bio12-s2.0.0] Evolution is the change in the inherited characteristics of a population (a group of the same species living in one place) over many generations. Over long periods it can produce new species.
[bio12-s2.0.1] In genetic terms, evolution is a change in how common particular alleles (versions of a gene) are in a population.
[bio12-s2.0.2] An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment. Because it is inherited, it can be passed to offspring, which is what lets selection act on it.
[bio12-s2.0.3] Structural adaptations are body features, for example a cactus's leaves reduced to spines, which reduces water loss.
[bio12-s2.0.4] Behavioural adaptations are ways of acting, for example birds migrating to warmer regions in winter.
[bio12-s2.0.5] Physiological adaptations concern the chemistry of the body, for example lactase (the enzyme that digests milk sugar) made by adults. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.0.6] Natural selection is the process by which individuals with inherited characteristics better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more than others, so those characteristics become more common in the next generation. It is the main mechanism of evolution.
[bio12-s2.1.b] A rationale is the reasoning behind an idea. Darwin did not start with a theory: he started with observations on a long sea voyage that the idea of fixed species could not explain.
[bio12-s2.1.0] Charles Darwin (1809–1882), an English naturalist, sailed around the world on HMS Beagle from 1831 to 1836.
[bio12-s2.1.1] He observed that species varied from place to place: the finches and giant tortoises of the Galápagos Islands differed from island to island and resembled species on the nearby South American mainland.
[bio12-s2.1.2] On the same voyage he found fossils (preserved remains) of extinct animals in South America that resembled living species there, which suggested the living species were related to the extinct ones.
[bio12-s2.1.3] The observations the theory had to explain were: variation within species; the fit between organisms and their environments; species on islands resembling those on the nearest mainland; and fossils of extinct species resembling living ones.
[bio12-s2.1.4] Darwin and Wallace needed an explanation for these observations because the older idea of fixed, separately created species did not explain them.
[bio12-s2.2.b] Observations alone were not enough. Ideas from geology, economics and farming gave Darwin the pieces of his explanation, and a second naturalist reached it independently.
[bio12-s2.2.0] Charles Lyell, a geologist, argued that the Earth is very old and is shaped by slow processes over vast time, which gave time for gradual change in living things.
[bio12-s2.2.1] Thomas Malthus argued that populations tend to grow faster than their food supply, so there is a struggle for existence (not everyone can survive).
[bio12-s2.2.2] Breeders of pigeons, dogs and farm animals showed that selection over generations could change a species greatly. This is artificial selection (humans choosing which animals breed).
[bio12-s2.2.3] Alfred Russel Wallace (1823–1913), working in the Malay Archipelago, reached the same idea independently and wrote to Darwin.
[bio12-s2.2.4] Papers by both were read together at the Linnean Society of London on 1 July 1858. Darwin then published On the Origin of Species in November 1859, giving the evidence in detail.
[bio12-s2.3.b] The theory rests on six numbered steps, from observation to inference, and on one point about what selection acts on. Each step follows from the one before it.
[bio12-s2.3.0] 1. Overproduction: organisms produce more offspring than can survive (for example a single cod releases millions of eggs; an oak tree produces thousands of acorns), yet population sizes stay fairly stable over time.
[bio12-s2.3.1] 2. Struggle for existence: resources such as food, water, space and mates are limited, so individuals compete, and many die from predation, disease or harsh conditions before reproducing.
[bio12-s2.3.2] 3. Variation: individuals of a species differ, and many of the differences are inherited (they come from sexual reproduction and mutation, so offspring are not identical to their parents).
[bio12-s2.3.3] 4. Survival of the fittest: individuals whose inherited variations suit them better to the environment are more likely to survive and reproduce. Fittest means best adapted to that environment, with the greatest reproductive success, not the strongest. The phrase was coined by Herbert Spencer and later used by Darwin.
[bio12-s2.3.4] 5. Inheritance: the survivors pass the favourable alleles to their offspring, so these alleles and characteristics become more common in the next generation, and less favourable ones become less common.
[bio12-s2.3.5] 6. Over many generations the population becomes better adapted to its environment. Different populations in different environments change in different ways and, given time and isolation, may become separate species (this is called speciation).
[bio12-s2.3.6] Selection acts on individuals, since they survive or not, but it is populations that evolve; an individual's genes do not change during its life.
[bio12-s2.3.7] Diagram: a flow chart of the six numbered steps, or a three-panel sequence for the peppered moth: a mixed population on a soot-darkened tree, birds eating the visible pale moths, and the next generation mostly dark.
[bio12-s2.4.b] The peppered moth is the classic worked example of natural selection. It shows every step of the theory, and shows that selection follows the environment.
[bio12-s2.4.0] The peppered moth (Biston betularia) rests on tree trunks by day and is eaten by birds. It has a pale, speckled form and a dark (melanic) form; the difference is controlled by one gene, and the dark allele arose by mutation.
[bio12-s2.4.1] Before the Industrial Revolution, tree trunks in England were pale and thick with lichens, and almost all moths were pale, so they were camouflaged; the rare dark moths were easily seen and eaten.
[bio12-s2.4.2] In the 1800s, air pollution from coal killed the lichens and blackened tree trunks with soot near industrial cities such as Manchester. Now the dark moths were camouflaged and the pale ones were eaten more.
[bio12-s2.4.3] So dark moths survived and reproduced more, passing on the dark allele, and the dark form became the most common in industrial areas.
[bio12-s2.4.4] After clean-air laws in the 1950s reduced pollution, the trunks became paler again and the pale form again became the most common. Selection changed direction when the environment changed; evolution is not a fixed march in one direction.
[bio12-s2.5.b] A good theory explains what we already see. Natural selection explains why all living things resemble each other in some ways, and why some organisms are so narrowly specialised.
[bio12-s2.5.0] Common origins: the theory explains why all living things share features such as cells, DNA, the same genetic code and ATP (the energy-carrying molecule), because they are all descended from common ancestors.
[bio12-s2.5.1] More closely related species share a more recent common ancestor. This is the basis of classification by phylogeny (grouping organisms by their evolutionary relationships). There is more on this in Classification.
[bio12-s2.5.2] Highly specialised organisms are the result of natural selection over many generations in a particular, stable environment, producing close adaptation to a narrow way of life.
[bio12-s2.5.3] Examples: the giant panda's diet is almost entirely bamboo, and the koala's is almost entirely eucalyptus leaves.
[bio12-s2.6.b] A good theory also makes predictions. Because natural selection depends on the environment, the theory lets us predict which organisms will cope when conditions change, and which will change fastest.
[bio12-s2.6.0] If the environment changes, the theory predicts that organisms will adapt (if suitable variation exists and there is time), move, or become extinct. They do not change simply because they need to.
[bio12-s2.6.1] Highly specialised organisms, which depend on one food or habitat and often reproduce slowly, are most at risk of extinction when that resource or habitat is lost, because they have no alternative food or home and cannot evolve quickly enough.
[bio12-s2.6.2] Generalists, which can use many foods and habitats, are more likely to survive change, because if one resource disappears they can switch to another.
[bio12-s2.6.3] Organisms with short generation times (little time between birth and reproducing) and large populations are predicted to evolve fastest, because new variation arises and spreads quickly.
[bio12-s2.6.4] Examples: bacteria evolve resistance to antibiotics, insects evolve resistance to pesticides, and the influenza virus changes so much each year that the flu vaccine must be updated annually. These predictions are borne out. There is more on antibiotic resistance in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.7.b] Artificial selection (selective breeding) is the process by which humans choose which individuals breed, so that characteristics useful or attractive to humans become more common over generations.
[bio12-s2.7.0] Dogs: all dog breeds, from the Chihuahua to the Great Dane, were bred from wolves.
[bio12-s2.7.1] Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi were all bred from the wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea), each by selecting a different part or feature of the plant.
[bio12-s2.7.2] Dairy cows have been bred for high milk yield, and modern wheat for large grains that stay on the plant until harvested.
[bio12-s2.7.3] Darwin used artificial selection as evidence that selection over generations can produce large changes, and as an analogy for natural selection: humans select in the farm, the environment selects in the wild.
[bio12-s2.8.b] Both kinds of selection work in the same way, with some individuals reproducing more than others. They differ in who does the choosing and in what is chosen.
[bio12-s2.8.0] Who or what selects: the environment (predators, climate, food supply, disease) in natural selection; humans in artificial selection.
[bio12-s2.8.1] Characteristics favoured: those that increase survival and reproduction in the wild, versus those wanted by humans, which may harm the organism. For example, flat-faced dog breeds such as pugs and bulldogs have breathing problems.
[bio12-s2.8.2] Speed: natural selection is usually slow, over many generations; artificial selection is relatively fast, because humans choose strongly.
[bio12-s2.8.3] Outcome: natural selection leads to adaptation to the environment and, eventually, new species; artificial selection leads to new breeds and varieties of the same species (all dogs are one species).
[bio12-s2.8.4] Genetic variation: usually maintained in large wild populations, but often reduced in artificial selection, because a few chosen individuals are bred and inbred, making the population more vulnerable to disease. The Irish potato example is in Variation.
[bio12-s2.8.5] Similarity: both depend on inherited variation and on some individuals reproducing more than others.
[bio12-s2.9.b] When Darwin proposed the theory it had real gaps, and the main one was inheritance. Later discoveries in genetics filled the gaps and strengthened the theory.
[bio12-s2.9.0] Darwin did not know how characteristics were inherited or where new variation came from.
[bio12-s2.9.1] Many people then believed in blending inheritance (offspring being a mixture of both parents), which would dilute any favourable variation within a few generations.
[bio12-s2.9.2] Mendel published his work on inheritance in 1866, but it was widely recognised only from 1900. It showed that inheritance is by discrete units (genes) that are not blended, so favourable alleles are not diluted away.
[bio12-s2.9.3] Later, the discovery of DNA and mutation explained the source of new variation. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio12-s2.9.4] Combined with genetics in the early 20th century, the theory became the modern synthesis: evolution is a change in allele frequencies in populations, caused mainly by natural selection acting on variation that comes from mutation and sexual reproduction.
[bio12-s2.10.b] Critics in Darwin's day also challenged the evidence for the theory, and it caused controversy beyond science. Later evidence answered the scientific criticisms.
[bio12-s2.10.0] Critics in Darwin's time said there were too few transitional fossils (fossils showing a stage between one type of organism and another), and that the Earth was too young for such slow change.
[bio12-s2.10.1] Since then many transitional fossils have been found, and radiometric dating (dating rocks by the decay of radioactive atoms in them) shows the Earth is about 4.6 billion years old, which gives plenty of time for slow change. There is more on this in Evidence for evolution.
[bio12-s2.10.2] The theory caused controversy because it conflicted with the religious belief, then widespread, that species were created separately in their present form.
[bio12-s2.10.3] Why evolution is accepted as the best explanation is in The importance of the theory.

CANDIDATES bio12-s1: Variation
[bio12-s1.0.b] No two members of a species are exactly alike. These differences are called variation, and they have two very different origins: the genes an organism inherits and the conditions it lives in.
[bio12-s1.0.0] Variation is the differences between individuals of the same species, such as in height, eye colour, blood group or resistance to a disease.
[bio12-s1.0.1] Genetic (inherited) variation is caused by differences in alleles (the different versions of a gene) and genes, so it can be passed to offspring in the DNA.
[bio12-s1.0.2] Environmental (acquired) variation is caused by the conditions an organism lives in, e.g. a scar, a suntan, muscles built by exercise, or a plant's size in poor soil. It is not passed on through the DNA, so a parent's exercised muscles do not appear in its children.
[bio12-s1.0.3] Most characteristics depend on both causes: phenotype (the observable trait) = genotype (the alleles an organism carries) + environment. For example, height is set by genes but also depends on diet.
[bio12-s1.0.4] Only genetic variation matters for evolution, because only it can be inherited and acted on by natural selection (the survival and reproduction of the best-suited individuals).
[bio12-s1.1.b] Traits vary in two patterns. Some sort individuals into a few clear groups, while others spread them along a smooth range. The pattern tells you how many genes are involved and how much the environment matters.
[bio12-s1.1.0] Discontinuous variation: individuals fall into a few distinct groups with no in-between forms, e.g. the human ABO blood group, round or wrinkled pea seeds, or albinism (lack of pigment).
[bio12-s1.1.1] Discontinuous traits are usually controlled by one or a few genes, with little effect from the environment, so a person cannot change their blood group by diet or lifestyle. The data are shown in a bar chart, because each group is a separate category.
[bio12-s1.1.2] Continuous variation: individuals show a smooth range between two extremes, e.g. human height, mass, skin colour or leaf length.
[bio12-s1.1.3] Continuous traits are usually controlled by many genes together with the environment, so there are many small steps between the extremes. Most individuals are near the middle, giving a bell-shaped (normal) distribution shown in a histogram.
[bio12-s1.1.4] Diagram: a bar chart of the four ABO blood groups (discontinuous) beside a bell-shaped histogram of height in a class (continuous). Labels: blood group or height class on the x-axis; number of people on the y-axis.
[bio12-s1.2.b] Sexual reproduction does not make new genes. It reshuffles the alleles already in the population, and it does so so thoroughly that each offspring is a new combination. Asexual reproduction, with no shuffling, shows what is lost without it.
[bio12-s1.2.0] Sexual reproduction does not create new alleles. It produces new combinations of existing alleles in every generation, so every offspring (except identical twins) is genetically unique.
[bio12-s1.2.1] Independent assortment: which chromosome of each homologous pair (the two matching chromosomes, one from each parent) enters a gamete (sex cell) is random and independent of the other pairs. One person can therefore make 2²³, about 8.4 million, different combinations of chromosomes in their gametes.
[bio12-s1.2.2] Random fertilisation: any one of the father's millions of sperm types can fertilise any one of the mother's egg types. Two parents can therefore produce about 8.4 million × 8.4 million, about 70 trillion (7 × 10¹³), different combinations of chromosomes, before other sources of variation are counted.
[bio12-s1.2.3] Two parents: each offspring combines alleles from two different individuals, so alleles that arose in different family lines come together. The choice of mate also varies, which adds further variety.
[bio12-s1.2.4] Asexual reproduction has one parent and no gametes. Cells divide by mitosis (bacteria divide by binary fission), so offspring are genetically identical clones, e.g. bacteria, potato tubers and strawberry runners. Their only source of genetic variation is mutation.
[bio12-s1.2.5] A population of clones may all be destroyed by the same disease or change in the environment, because none has a different allele that could resist it.
[bio12-s1.2.6] Example of the risk: the potatoes grown in Ireland before the Great Famine were mostly a single variety, the Lumper, propagated from tubers, so the plants were almost genetically identical. When potato blight, caused by Phytophthora infestans, arrived in 1845, none was resistant and the crop failed across the country.
[bio12-s1.2.7] Diagram: two homologous pairs (one long, one short, coloured to show the chromosome from the mother and from the father) in a cell, the two possible ways they can line up before separating, and the four resulting kinds of gamete. Labels: homologous pair, gamete, maternal chromosome, paternal chromosome.
[bio12-s1.3.b] Sexual reproduction can only shuffle alleles that already exist. Mutation is the only source of new alleles, so it is the original source of all genetic variation.
[bio12-s1.3.0] A mutation is a change in the DNA: in the base sequence of a gene (the order of its chemical letters), or in the structure or number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.3.1] Mutations happen spontaneously, mainly as rare copying errors when DNA is replicated (copied before a cell divides).
[bio12-s1.3.2] Mutagens, such as ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays), ultraviolet light and certain chemicals, e.g. some in tobacco smoke, increase the rate of mutation.
[bio12-s1.3.3] Mutations are random: mutagens change the rate but not the direction. The environment does not cause a mutation that helps the organism adapt, and a need for a change does not make it happen.
[bio12-s1.3.4] Each gene mutates rarely, but there are many genes and many individuals, so new mutations arise in every generation.
[bio12-s1.3.5] Organisms that reproduce very fast, such as bacteria, accumulate mutations quickly because they pass through so many generations. Antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic is quickly passed on to its many descendants.
[bio12-s1.4.b] Mutations are grouped by the size of the change. A gene mutation alters the letters of one gene, while a chromosome mutation changes a whole stretch of a chromosome, or the number of chromosomes.
[bio12-s1.4.0] Gene (point) mutation: a change in the base sequence of one gene, e.g. one base replaced by another, or a base added or lost. It creates a new allele of that gene.
[bio12-s1.4.1] Example of a gene mutation: sickle-cell anaemia is caused by a change of a single base in a gene for haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells).
[bio12-s1.4.2] Chromosome mutation: a change in the structure of a chromosome, such as a section lost (deletion), copied (duplication), turned around (inversion) or moved to another chromosome (translocation). It affects many genes at once, because a chromosome carries many genes.
[bio12-s1.4.3] Changes in chromosome number also cause variation. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21 (47 chromosomes in total instead of 46). It is a change in number, not one of the four structural changes above.
[bio12-s1.4.4] In plants, whole extra sets of chromosomes (polyploidy) are common; bread wheat has six sets.
[bio12-s1.5.b] A mutation is a random change, so it has no built-in tendency to help or harm. Its effect depends on what the change does and on the environment the organism lives in.
[bio12-s1.5.0] Most mutations are neutral (no effect, e.g. in non-coding DNA, or a change that gives the same amino acid) or harmful; a few are beneficial in a particular environment. So it is false to say a mutation always has a negative effect.
[bio12-s1.5.1] Chromosome mutations are usually harmful in animals, but polyploidy is often tolerated in plants, and many crop plants are polyploid.
[bio12-s1.5.2] Beneficial example, lactase persistence: most mammals stop making lactase, the enzyme that digests milk sugar, after weaning (when they stop drinking milk). A mutation in the DNA controlling the lactase gene keeps it switched on in adults.
[bio12-s1.5.3] The lactase mutation became common in populations with a long history of dairy farming, such as northern Europe including Ireland, because adults who could digest milk had an advantage.
[bio12-s1.6.b] A mutation only matters for the next generation if it reaches the gametes. The cell in which it happens decides whether it is inherited.
[bio12-s1.6.0] Only mutations in cells that make gametes (or in a gamete) are inherited, because only gametes join to form the next generation.
[bio12-s1.6.1] A mutation in a body cell (a somatic mutation), e.g. a skin cell damaged by ultraviolet light, affects only the cells descended from it and may cause cancer, but is not passed to offspring.
[bio12-s1.7.b] Both processes produce genetic variation, but in different ways. Set side by side, they show why natural selection needs both.
[bio12-s1.7.0] What it does: sexual reproduction shuffles existing alleles into new combinations; mutation creates new alleles or new chromosome arrangements.
[bio12-s1.7.1] How often: sexual reproduction happens in every generation, in every offspring; mutation happens rarely and at random.
[bio12-s1.7.2] Effect on the individual: sexual reproduction makes every offspring different, but each allele in the new combination was already present and working in a parent. Most mutations are neutral or harmful, and a few are beneficial.
[bio12-s1.7.3] Organisms: sexual reproduction occurs only in sexually reproducing organisms; mutation occurs in all organisms, including those that reproduce asexually.
[bio12-s1.7.4] Together, mutation supplies new alleles and sexual reproduction spreads them into new combinations, giving the variation on which natural selection acts.

CANDIDATES bio11-s2: Key concepts of information transfer
[bio11-s2.0.b] Inheritance starts with a few basic ideas: what counts as one kind of organism, what is passed on, and what carries it. These terms are used throughout genetics.
[bio11-s2.0.0] A species is a group of organisms with similar features that can interbreed in natural conditions to produce fertile offspring (offspring that can themselves reproduce). A horse and a donkey can mate, but their offspring, the mule, is sterile, so horses and donkeys are different species.
[bio11-s2.0.1] The species definition has limits: it cannot be applied to organisms that reproduce only asexually, because they never interbreed, or to fossils, because dead organisms cannot be tested for interbreeding. There is more on how new species form in Evidence for evolution.
[bio11-s2.0.2] Heredity is the passing of characteristics (traits), by means of genes, from parents to offspring, that is, from one generation to the next. A gene is a section of DNA that carries the instructions for a trait.
[bio11-s2.0.3] Genetics is the study of heredity (how traits are passed on) and of variation (the differences between individuals).
[bio11-s2.0.4] A chromosome is the structure that carries genes from one generation to the next. Body cells carry chromosomes in homologous pairs (two matching chromosomes, one from each parent), and each gamete carries one chromosome from each pair.
[bio11-s2.1.b] Parents pass genes on through special sex cells. Following the chromosome number from parent to gametes to zygote shows how each generation gets its genes and why the number stays constant.
[bio11-s2.1.0] A gamete is a sex cell: a sperm or egg in animals, or in flowering plants the male gamete nucleus in the pollen grain and the egg cell in the ovule.
[bio11-s2.1.1] Gametes are haploid, with one set of chromosomes (one from each pair), so a human gamete has 23 chromosomes. They are made by meiosis. Gametes must be haploid because fertilisation adds the two sets together, so otherwise the chromosome number would double every generation.
[bio11-s2.1.2] Sexual reproduction involves the fusion of two gametes, usually from two parents. The offspring receive half their chromosomes from each parent, so they are genetically different from both parents and from each other. There is more on this in Variation.
[bio11-s2.1.3] Asexual reproduction involves one parent and no gametes, so it produces genetically identical offspring, called clones.
[bio11-s2.1.4] Fertilisation is the fusion of the nucleus of a male gamete with the nucleus of a female gamete to form a diploid zygote (a cell with two sets of chromosomes), the first cell of the new organism. It restores the diploid number: in humans 23 + 23 = 46 chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.5] The zygote divides by mitosis, so every body cell carries the same set of chromosomes.
[bio11-s2.1.6] Diagram: life-cycle flow: parent (2n = 46) → meiosis → gametes (n = 23, sperm and egg) → fertilisation → zygote (2n = 46) → mitosis → offspring; label haploid, diploid, meiosis, fertilisation and mitosis.
[bio11-s2.2.b] A gene is a section of DNA for one trait, such as seed shape. Genes come in slightly different versions, and these versions, called alleles, are what make individuals differ.
[bio11-s2.2.0] An allele is one of the alternative forms of a gene, found at the same locus (position) on homologous chromosomes. Alleles differ slightly in base sequence, as they arise by mutation, so they may produce different versions of the trait.
[bio11-s2.2.1] In peas, the gene for seed shape has an allele for round seeds (R) and one for wrinkled seeds (r).
[bio11-s2.2.2] A diploid organism has two alleles for each autosomal gene (a gene on a non-sex chromosome), one on each homologous chromosome, one inherited from each parent.
[bio11-s2.2.3] A population may have more than two alleles of a gene, but each individual carries only two, because it has only two homologous chromosomes. The human ABO blood-group gene has three alleles (A, B and O) in the population.
[bio11-s2.2.4] Homozygous means having two identical alleles of a gene (RR or rr). A homozygous organism is also called pure-breeding, because it passes on the same allele in every gamete.
[bio11-s2.2.5] Heterozygous means having two different alleles of a gene (Rr). A heterozygous organism is also called a hybrid for that gene.
[bio11-s2.2.6] Diagram: a pair of homologous chromosomes with one gene locus marked, one chromosome carrying R and the other r (heterozygous); a second pair carrying r and r (homozygous); label homologous chromosomes, locus and allele.
[bio11-s2.3.b] Two terms separate what an organism's genes are from what it looks like or does. They are not always the same, because the environment and dominance both play a part.
[bio11-s2.3.0] Genotype is the genetic make-up of an organism: the alleles it has for a particular trait, written with letters, such as RR, Rr or rr.
[bio11-s2.3.1] Phenotype is the observable (physical, chemical or behavioural) characteristics of an organism that result from its genotype, such as round or wrinkled seeds, or blood group A.
[bio11-s2.3.2] Phenotype = genotype + environment. The genotype sets the possible range, and the environment affects how it is expressed.
[bio11-s2.3.3] Identical twins have the same genotype but may differ in mass because of diet and exercise, so the same genes can give different phenotypes.
[bio11-s2.3.4] Hydrangea plants of the same variety have blue flowers in acid soil and pink flowers in alkaline soil, so soil pH changes the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.3.5] A plant with the genes for chlorophyll is pale yellow if grown in the dark, because the environment (no light) stops the pigment forming.
[bio11-s2.3.6] Different genotypes can give the same phenotype: RR and Rr are both round, because one allele is dominant. So the phenotype does not always reveal the genotype. There is more on how to find the genotype (the test cross) in Monohybrid crosses.
[bio11-s2.4.b] When an organism is heterozygous, the two alleles do not always show equally. Dominant and recessive describe which allele is seen in the phenotype and which is hidden.
[bio11-s2.4.0] A dominant allele is expressed in the phenotype of a heterozygote, masking the effect of the other allele. It is written with a capital letter (R), and a dominant trait shows in both RR and Rr.
[bio11-s2.4.1] A recessive allele is expressed in the phenotype only when no dominant allele is present, that is, only in the homozygous condition (rr). It is written with the lower-case letter (r).
[bio11-s2.4.2] In a heterozygote, a recessive allele is present but hidden, and it can still be passed on to offspring. It does not disappear and is not weaker.
[bio11-s2.4.3] Often the dominant allele codes for a working protein and the recessive allele for a non-working one. One working copy makes enough protein, so Rr looks the same as RR.
[bio11-s2.4.4] In peas, R codes for a working starch-branching enzyme; rr seeds make less starch and wrinkle. There is more on this chain in Genes, proteins and traits.
[bio11-s2.4.5] Dominant does not mean common or better. The allele causing Huntington's disease is dominant but rare, and harmful.
[bio11-s2.4.6] Cystic fibrosis and albinism are caused by recessive alleles, so two carriers (heterozygotes) can have an affected child.
[bio11-s2.4.7] A carrier is a heterozygous individual who carries a recessive allele for a condition but does not show it.
[bio11-s2.5.b] Having a gene is not the same as using it. Gene expression is how a cell turns the information in a gene into something that affects the organism.
[bio11-s2.5.0] Gene expression is the process by which the information in a gene is used to make its product (a protein or a functional RNA), so that the gene affects the phenotype.
[bio11-s2.5.1] A gene that is being used is switched on (expressed); one that is not is switched off.
[bio11-s2.5.2] Every body cell has the same genes, but each cell type expresses only some of them, which is why cells become specialised.
[bio11-s2.5.3] Expression is controlled by regulatory DNA (stretches of DNA that switch genes on or off), by signals such as hormones, and by the environment: some genes are switched on only in response to a stimulus.
[bio11-s2.5.4] There is more on how the environment and behaviour change expression in Genetic and epigenetic mechanisms.
[bio11-s2.6.b] Four more terms extend the basic vocabulary. Each has a short definition and, where there is one, a named example.
[bio11-s2.6.0] Epigenetic expression is a change in gene expression that is not caused by a change in the DNA base sequence, but by chemical tags on the DNA or on the histones (the proteins DNA is wound around) that switch genes on or off.
[bio11-s2.6.1] Epigenetic tags can be caused by the environment and behaviour, can be passed on when cells divide, and some are reversible.
[bio11-s2.6.2] Incomplete dominance means neither allele is dominant over the other, so the heterozygote has a phenotype intermediate between the two homozygotes. For example, red-flowered and white-flowered snapdragons give pink-flowered heterozygotes. This contrasts with complete dominance, where the heterozygote looks like the dominant homozygote.
[bio11-s2.6.3] Linkage: genes located on the same chromosome are linked and tend to be inherited together, because they do not separate into different gametes. Linked genes do not assort independently.
[bio11-s2.6.4] Sex-linkage: a gene located on a sex chromosome (in humans almost always the X chromosome) is sex-linked, so the trait's inheritance pattern differs between males and females. Examples are red–green colour blindness and haemophilia, which are much more common in males.
