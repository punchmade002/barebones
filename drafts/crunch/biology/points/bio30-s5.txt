TARGET bio30-s5: Investigation: rate of transpiration
[bio30-s5.0.b] Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from a plant, mostly through its leaves. The rate of transpiration is the volume, or mass, of water lost by a plant per unit time. It depends on how steep the water vapour concentration gradient is between the air spaces of the leaf, which are saturated with water vapour, and the outside air; how fast water evaporates; and how wide the stomata (the pores in the leaf surface) are open.
[bio30-s5.0.0] Air movement: wind blows away the layer of humid air that builds up around the leaf, keeping the outside air dry and the gradient steep, so the rate increases. In still air the humid layer builds up, the gradient flattens and the rate falls.
[bio30-s5.0.1] Temperature: warmer water molecules have more kinetic energy (movement energy), so they evaporate from the mesophyll cell walls (the moist walls of the cells inside the leaf) and diffuse out faster. Warm air can also hold more water vapour, so the gradient is steeper. The rate increases with temperature.
[bio30-s5.0.2] At very high temperatures the plant may begin to wilt and close its stomata, which stops water vapour escaping, and then the rate falls.
[bio30-s5.0.3] Surface area: more leaves, or larger leaves, mean more stomata and more evaporating surface, so the rate increases roughly in proportion to the leaf area.
[bio30-s5.0.4] Cuticle (the waxy covering of the leaf) is waterproof, so only a little water is lost through it; most is lost through the stomata. A thick cuticle means less loss than a thin one. A surface coated with an artificial waterproof layer, such as petroleum jelly, loses almost none.
[bio30-s5.0.5] Light intensity: stomata open in the light and close in darkness, so the rate increases with light intensity, until the stomata are fully open, and is very low in darkness. A lamp also warms the leaf, which raises the rate further unless the temperature is controlled.
[bio30-s5.0.6] CO₂ concentration: a high concentration of CO₂ around the leaf partly closes the stomata, so the rate falls; a low concentration opens them wider, so the rate rises. The Higher Level explanation is in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s5.0.7] Humidity, not named by the specification but controlled when the other factors are tested: humid air reduces the gradient, so the rate falls. This matters whenever a shoot is enclosed in a plastic bag, as in the CO₂ method, because the air in the bag becomes humid.
[bio30-s5.1.b] A good investigation changes one factor at a time, so that any change in the result can be blamed on that one factor. Planning starts with an aim and a hypothesis (a testable prediction).
[bio30-s5.1.0] Aim: to investigate the effect of one factor (air movement, temperature, surface area, cuticle, light intensity or CO₂ concentration) on the rate of transpiration of a leafy shoot. Change one factor per investigation.
[bio30-s5.1.1] Example hypotheses, each resting on the theory above: “Increasing air movement increases the rate of transpiration”; “The rate of transpiration increases as temperature increases”; “The rate increases as leaf area increases”.
[bio30-s5.1.2] More example hypotheses: “Covering the lower surface of a leaf reduces water loss more than covering the upper surface”; “The rate increases as light intensity increases”; “Increasing the CO₂ concentration around a shoot reduces its rate of transpiration”.
[bio30-s5.1.3] The potometer (the apparatus used to measure uptake) measures water uptake, not water loss. The two are almost equal, because nearly all the water a shoot takes up is lost by transpiration.
[bio30-s5.1.4] Only a tiny amount of the water taken up is used in photosynthesis or to keep the cells turgid (firm and full of water). The mass-loss method measures water loss directly.
[bio30-s5.2.b] Once the aim is set, name what you change, what you measure and what you keep the same, and say what each comparison is checked against.
[bio30-s5.2.0] Independent variable (the one factor you change), using light intensity as the example: the lamp distance, or the light meter reading in lux. For the other factors, the independent variable is swapped and everything else is kept constant.
[bio30-s5.2.1] Dependent variable (the one you measure), using light intensity as the example: the rate of water uptake, which is the distance moved by the air bubble per minute, converted to a volume per minute.
[bio30-s5.2.2] Controlled variables (kept the same so the test is fair), using light intensity as the example: the same shoot (same species, same number and size of leaves), temperature (a thermometer by the shoot and a heat shield), air movement (no fans, windows closed).
[bio30-s5.2.3] More controlled variables: humidity (the same room), CO₂ (the same room air), the time allowed to adjust, and the length of each timing.
[bio30-s5.2.4] Controls: a baseline reading for the same shoot in normal room conditions, still air, room temperature and normal light, to compare each change against. A leafless stem in the potometer, with all leaves removed and the cut surfaces sealed, shows that almost all the water is lost through the leaves.
[bio30-s5.2.5] Two more controls: for the cuticle investigation the control is an untreated leaf, and for the CO₂ investigation it is an identical bag with no CO₂ added or removed, so that any effect of the bag itself is allowed for.
[bio30-s5.3.b] The same basic kit serves every factor, with a few extra items added for particular methods. Know what each item is for, because exams ask you to name the apparatus and describe the set-up.
[bio30-s5.3.0] Bubble potometer: a capillary tube (a very narrow tube) with a scale, a reservoir or syringe with a tap for resetting the bubble, and rubber tubing or a bung to hold the shoot.
[bio30-s5.3.1] Other apparatus: clamp stand; a leafy shoot of a woody shrub with a stiff stem, e.g. laurel or privet; sharp knife or scalpel; a basin or sink of water; petroleum jelly (Vaseline); paper towels.
[bio30-s5.3.2] Measuring and fair-test apparatus: stopwatch; ruler; thermometer; bench lamp (LED preferred) and metre stick; light meter (optional); electric fan; heat shield, such as a flat-sided tank or beaker of water.
[bio30-s5.3.3] For the CO₂ method: a clear plastic bag with string or tape; soda lime, to absorb CO₂; and a CO₂ source, either a flask of marble chips with dilute hydrochloric acid connected by tubing, or a CO₂ cylinder.
[bio30-s5.3.4] For the cuticle and surface-area methods: graph paper, for measuring leaf area; and, for the alternative methods, an electronic balance, cobalt chloride paper, forceps and clear tape.
[bio30-s5.4.b] The first stage is to build a potometer that is full of water, airtight and free of air bubbles. If the water column is broken or the joints leak, the readings are meaningless.
[bio30-s5.4.0] 1. Cut a leafy shoot from the plant, then immediately recut the stem at a slant under water, a few centimetres further up, so that no air enters the xylem (the vessels that carry water up the stem).
[bio30-s5.4.1] An air bubble in the xylem would break the water columns, so the shoot could not draw water up. There is more on this in Transport of water and minerals.
[bio30-s5.4.2] 2. Submerge the potometer in the basin of water so that it fills completely, with no air bubbles.
[bio30-s5.4.3] 3. Fit the shoot into the rubber tubing or bung under water, so that the joint is airtight.
[bio30-s5.4.4] 4. Take the assembled potometer out of the water and clamp it upright, with the capillary tube horizontal.
[bio30-s5.4.5] 5. Seal all joints with petroleum jelly to prevent leaks.
[bio30-s5.4.6] 6. Gently dry the leaves with a paper towel, since water on their surface would evaporate and add to the loss.
[bio30-s5.5.b] Once the apparatus is ready, you introduce a single air bubble and use its movement along the scale to measure how fast the shoot takes up water.
[bio30-s5.5.0] 7. Introduce one air bubble into the capillary tube by lifting its open end out of the water for a moment, then return it to a beaker of water.
[bio30-s5.5.1] 8. Leave the shoot for about 5 minutes to adjust to the conditions, so that the readings reflect the present conditions and not the previous ones.
[bio30-s5.5.2] 9. Record the starting position of the bubble on the scale, start the stopwatch, and record the distance the bubble moves towards the shoot in a set time, e.g. 5 minutes. The bubble moves towards the shoot because the shoot is drawing water out of the tube.
[bio30-s5.5.3] 10. Return the bubble to the start of the scale by opening the reservoir tap, or by pushing water in from the syringe.
[bio30-s5.5.4] 11. Repeat at least three times under the same conditions, and calculate the mean, so that one odd reading does not distort the result.
[bio30-s5.5.5] Diagram: a bubble potometer clamped on a stand. Label the leafy shoot sealed in with petroleum jelly, water-filled tube, reservoir with tap, horizontal capillary tube with scale, air bubble, beaker of water at the open end; draw an arrow for the bubble's direction.
[bio30-s5.6.b] Bubble movement is only a distance, so it has to be turned into a rate, and sometimes into a volume. These worked examples show each calculation step by step.
[bio30-s5.6.0] Rate of water uptake = distance moved by the bubble ÷ time, e.g. in mm per minute. Use this when all the readings use the same potometer, because then the capillary tube is the same and distances can be compared directly.
[bio30-s5.6.1] To give a volume, use the volume of a cylinder (the water in the capillary tube forms a cylinder): volume = πr² × distance moved, where r is the radius of the capillary tube.
[bio30-s5.6.2] Worked example: radius 0.5 mm, bubble moves 30 mm in 5 minutes. Volume = 3.14 × 0.5² × 30 = 3.14 × 0.25 × 30 = 23.6 mm³ in 5 minutes, so the rate is 23.6 ÷ 5 = 4.7 mm³ per minute.
[bio30-s5.6.3] To compare shoots of different sizes, divide by the leaf area, because a bigger shoot loses more water simply by having more leaf. Draw round each leaf on 1 cm² graph paper, count the squares that are at least half covered, and add up the total for all the leaves.
[bio30-s5.6.4] Worked example: 4.7 mm³ per minute ÷ 50 cm² = 0.094 mm³ per minute per cm².
[bio30-s5.6.5] Mass-loss method: rate = mass lost ÷ time, e.g. in g per hour. Percentage mass loss = (mass lost ÷ starting mass) × 100, which allows leaves of different sizes to be compared.
[bio30-s5.6.6] Worked example: a leaf of 2.0 g that weighs 1.5 g after 24 hours has lost 0.5 g, which is 25%.
[bio30-s5.7.b] Here the independent variable is how much air moves past the shoot, set with an electric fan. The idea is to test whether moving air keeps the concentration gradient steep.
[bio30-s5.7.0] 1. Take the baseline reading in still air, as in the potometer setup.
[bio30-s5.7.1] 2. Place an electric fan at a fixed distance, e.g. 1 m, from the shoot on its lowest setting. Wait 5 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.7.2] 3. Repeat on the medium and highest settings, or with the fan at decreasing distances.
[bio30-s5.7.3] 4. Check that the fan does not change the temperature by reading the thermometer near the shoot, because a change in temperature would also change the rate.
[bio30-s5.7.4] Expected: the rate increases as air movement increases, because moving air carries away the humid layer around the leaf.
[bio30-s5.8.b] Temperature is changed by carrying out the readings in places that are at different, steady temperatures. The main difficulty is warming the shoot without also moving the air or changing the light.
[bio30-s5.8.0] 1. Take readings with the potometer in places at different, steady temperatures, e.g. a cool room, the laboratory and a warm room, measuring the air temperature beside the shoot with a thermometer each time.
[bio30-s5.8.1] 2. Allow the shoot about 10 minutes to reach each temperature before taking three readings.
[bio30-s5.8.2] 3. Do not use a fan heater blowing on the shoot, because it changes air movement as well as temperature. If a heater is used, place it at a distance, behind a heat shield.
[bio30-s5.8.3] 4. Keep the light intensity the same in each place, with the same lamp and no direct sunlight, because light also affects the rate.
[bio30-s5.8.4] Expected: the rate increases with temperature.
[bio30-s5.9.b] Here the independent variable is the total leaf area of the shoot, which is reduced stage by stage by removing leaves. Because each leaf carries stomata, less leaf means less surface to lose water from.
[bio30-s5.9.0] 1. Take readings with all the leaves on the shoot.
[bio30-s5.9.1] 2. Remove one or two leaves by cutting them off at the base of the leaf stalk, seal the cut surfaces with petroleum jelly so that no water escapes there, and take new readings.
[bio30-s5.9.2] 3. Repeat until no leaves remain.
[bio30-s5.9.3] 4. Measure the area of each removed leaf on graph paper, as in the calculation method, so that the total leaf area left at each stage is known. Alternatively, compare shoots with different total leaf areas.
[bio30-s5.9.4] Expected: the rate falls as the leaf area falls, roughly in proportion, and the leafless stem gives almost no movement of the bubble. Plot the rate against the leaf area.
[bio30-s5.10.b] This investigation compares leaves with and without a waterproof coating. Petroleum jelly acts as an artificial, thicker cuticle, so coating different surfaces shows where water is really lost.
[bio30-s5.10.0] 1. Pick four similar leaves of the same size from the same plant.
[bio30-s5.10.1] 2. Leave leaf A untreated, as the control. Coat the upper surface of leaf B with a thin layer of petroleum jelly, coat the lower surface of leaf C, and coat both surfaces of leaf D. The petroleum jelly acts as an artificial, thicker waterproof cuticle.
[bio30-s5.10.2] 3. Seal the cut end of each leaf stalk with petroleum jelly.
[bio30-s5.10.3] 4. Weigh each leaf on an electronic balance.
[bio30-s5.10.4] 5. Hang the leaves on a string across the room, in the same conditions.
[bio30-s5.10.5] 6. Reweigh each leaf every day for several days, and calculate the percentage mass loss. The loss in mass is the water lost.
[bio30-s5.10.6] Expected results: A loses the most mass. B loses nearly as much as A, because its upper surface has a thick cuticle and few stomata. C loses much less, because the stomata on its lower surface are blocked. D loses almost none.
[bio30-s5.10.7] Conclusion: the cuticle greatly reduces water loss, and most water is lost through the stomata on the lower surface.
[bio30-s5.11.b] The cuticle comparison can also be run on a potometer shoot instead of on separate hanging leaves, which gives a rate of water uptake rather than a mass loss.
[bio30-s5.11.0] Variation using the potometer: coat the upper surfaces, then the lower surfaces, of all the leaves on the potometer shoot, and take readings after each, compared with the untreated reading.
[bio30-s5.11.1] Alternative: compare shoots of plants with a thick, shiny waxy cuticle, such as laurel, and plants with thin, soft leaves of similar area.
[bio30-s5.12.b] Cobalt chloride paper is a quick way to show that water vapour leaves a leaf, and from which surface. It supports the main methods rather than replacing them.
[bio30-s5.12.0] Dry cobalt chloride paper is blue, and it turns pink when it absorbs water.
[bio30-s5.12.1] Using forceps, place a small square on the upper surface and one on the lower surface of a leaf still on the plant, and cover each with clear tape so that the paper only picks up water vapour from the leaf.
[bio30-s5.12.2] Time how long each square takes to turn pink.
[bio30-s5.12.3] Expected: the paper on the lower surface turns pink first, showing that more water vapour leaves through the lower surface, where most of the stomata are.
[bio30-s5.13.b] Light intensity is varied by moving a lamp to different distances from the shoot, with a heat shield so that the lamp changes only the light. Light matters because it controls whether the stomata are open.
[bio30-s5.13.0] 1. Work in a darkened room, so that room light does not add to the lamp.
[bio30-s5.13.1] 2. Place a lamp at a set distance from the shoot, e.g. 10 cm, with a heat shield between them. Wait 5 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.13.2] 3. Repeat at 20, 30, 40 and 50 cm, measuring the distance from bulb to shoot with a metre stick. Alternatively, use a light meter, since intensity does not fall in simple proportion to distance. There is more on this in Investigation: rate of photosynthesis.
[bio30-s5.13.3] 4. Take a final set of readings in darkness, with the lamp off and the shoot covered with a black bag or box.
[bio30-s5.13.4] 5. Check the thermometer beside the shoot at each distance, because the lamp can warm the shoot.
[bio30-s5.13.5] Expected: the rate is highest with the lamp closest, and very low in darkness, when the stomata close.
[bio30-s5.14.b] CO₂ is varied by enclosing the shoot in a clear plastic bag and either adding CO₂ or absorbing it. The bag causes its own problem, because the air inside becomes humid, so the method needs a control bag.
[bio30-s5.14.0] 1. Take readings with the shoot in a clear plastic bag sealed loosely around the stem, with nothing added. This is the control for the effect of the bag and its humidity.
[bio30-s5.14.1] 2. Raised CO₂: pass CO₂ into the bag through a tube from a flask of marble chips and dilute hydrochloric acid, or from a CO₂ cylinder. Tie the bag, wait 10 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.14.2] 3. Lowered CO₂: place a small open dish of soda lime, which absorbs CO₂, inside the bag, not touching the leaves. Tie the bag, wait 10 minutes and take three readings.
[bio30-s5.14.3] 4. Keep the shoot in bright light throughout, at a constant temperature, and keep each run short, because humidity builds up inside the bag.
[bio30-s5.14.4] Expected: raised CO₂ lowers the rate, and lowered CO₂ raises it slightly. The effect is smaller and harder to detect than the effects of light or air movement, so careful repeats and comparison with secondary data are needed.
[bio30-s5.15.b] This investigation combines sharp blades, glass, electricity near water and some hazardous chemicals. Each hazard has a simple precaution.
[bio30-s5.15.0] Cut away from the body on a tile, and take care with the knife or scalpel. Glass capillary tubes break easily, so handle them gently and report breakages.
[bio30-s5.15.1] Keep the lamp, the fan and their cables away from the basin and the potometer, and dry your hands before touching switches, because water and electricity are a dangerous mixture. Keep fingers clear of the fan blades.
[bio30-s5.15.2] Lamp bulbs, except LEDs, get very hot, so let them cool before moving them.
[bio30-s5.15.3] Cobalt chloride paper is toxic and can cause cancer. Handle it only with forceps, wear gloves, and wash your hands afterwards.
[bio30-s5.15.4] Soda lime is corrosive, because it contains sodium hydroxide. Wear safety glasses and gloves, and do not touch it. Dilute hydrochloric acid is an irritant, so wear safety glasses.
[bio30-s5.15.5] Wash hands after handling plants, since some, such as laurel, are poisonous if eaten, and after handling petroleum jelly.
[bio30-s5.16.b] Primary data are the results you collect yourself. They only become evidence when they are tabulated, graphed and explained using the theory of how each factor works.
[bio30-s5.16.0] Record the primary data in a table: the independent variable (e.g. lamp distance in cm, fan setting, temperature in °C or leaf area in cm²), the three readings of distance moved in the set time, the mean, and the rate in mm per minute or mm³ per minute.
[bio30-s5.16.1] For the cuticle investigation, record the starting mass, each day's mass and the percentage mass loss for each leaf.
[bio30-s5.16.2] Plot the mean rate on the y-axis against the independent variable on the x-axis, with units. Use a bar chart for categories, such as fan settings, treatments A to D, or still and moving air, and a line graph for continuous variables, such as temperature, leaf area or light intensity.
[bio30-s5.16.3] Expected patterns: the rate rises with air movement, temperature, leaf area and light intensity. It falls with a thick or artificial cuticle, especially on the lower surface, and with raised CO₂.
[bio30-s5.16.4] At high light intensity the rate levels off once the stomata are fully open, because they cannot open any wider.
[bio30-s5.16.5] Conclusion: state the relationship shown by the primary data, explain it using the theory of how each factor works (gradient, evaporation and stomatal opening), and say whether it supports the hypothesis. Use the mean of the repeats, and compare with other groups' results.
[bio30-s5.16.6] Diagram: four leaves hanging on a string, labelled A untreated, B upper surface coated, C lower surface coated and D both surfaces coated, with a bar chart of the expected percentage mass loss, with A highest, B slightly lower, C much lower and D lowest.
[bio30-s5.17.b] Secondary data are results collected by someone else. They let you test your conclusions against a larger body of evidence, and they are especially useful where a school laboratory cannot show an effect clearly.
[bio30-s5.17.0] Secondary data are results collected by others, such as other groups' class results, published graphs and tables, weather data (temperature, humidity and wind speed from a weather station or Met Éireann, Ireland's national weather service), or results from electronic sensors and data loggers measuring humidity or mass loss.
[bio30-s5.17.1] Pooling class results increases the sample size and checks reproducibility (whether other people get the same results).
[bio30-s5.17.2] Interpreting a graph of transpiration over 24 hours: the rate is very low at night, when the stomata are closed, and the air is often cooler and still. It rises after dawn as the stomata open, light increases and the air warms.
[bio30-s5.17.3] It peaks around the early afternoon, when light and temperature are highest and the air is driest, and it falls in the evening.
[bio30-s5.17.4] On a hot, dry afternoon the graph may dip if the plant begins to wilt and closes its stomata.
[bio30-s5.17.5] Because the effect of CO₂ is small in a school laboratory, published results from controlled-atmosphere experiments can be used for the CO₂ factor. These are plants grown at different CO₂ concentrations, with all other conditions fixed, and they support the conclusion that higher CO₂ reduces stomatal opening and transpiration.
[bio30-s5.17.6] Compare primary and secondary data by checking whether your results show the same trend as the published data. If they differ, look for an uncontrolled variable or a source of error, and evaluate the sources for reliability. There is more on this in Recognising bias and evaluating sources.
[bio30-s5.18.b] Every method has weaknesses that can make results unreliable. For each one, know both the problem and the fix.
[bio30-s5.18.0] The potometer measures water uptake, not water loss, and a small amount of the water taken up is used by the plant, so uptake is slightly higher than the true loss.
[bio30-s5.18.1] Air leaks at the joints, or air entering the cut stem, stop or slow the bubble and give a falsely low rate. Cut the stem under water and seal the joints with petroleum jelly.
[bio30-s5.18.2] Changing one factor often changes another: a lamp heats the shoot, a fan or heater moves air, and a plastic bag raises humidity. Use a heat shield, keep heaters away, use a control bag and check the thermometer.
[bio30-s5.18.3] Room conditions, such as temperature, humidity, sunlight through windows and people moving about, change during a lesson. Take readings close together in time, and repeat them.
[bio30-s5.18.4] Too little time to adjust after each change gives a rate that still reflects the previous condition. Wait about 5 to 10 minutes.
[bio30-s5.18.5] A cut shoot behaves differently from a whole plant and may wilt during a long investigation. Use a healthy shoot and a short investigation, or a fresh shoot for each run, and measure its leaf area.
[bio30-s5.18.6] Reading the bubble position by eye on a scale is imprecise. Read at eye level and repeat at least three times, calculating a mean.

CANDIDATES bio30-s2: Transport of water and minerals
[bio30-s2.0.b] Water moves in one continuous flow, from the soil through the plant and out into the air. This flow is the transpiration stream, and its first part carries water from the soil into the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.0] Overview: water moves from the soil into the root hairs (tiny outgrowths of root cells that give a large surface for absorption), across the root cortex (the layer of cells between the surface and the centre), into the xylem (the water-carrying tubes), up the stem and into the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.1] It then leaves the leaves as water vapour through the stomata (pores in the leaf surface), completing the continuous flow called the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.0.2] Stage 1, into the root hair by osmosis: soil water is a dilute solution with a higher water concentration, while the cell sap in the root hair's vacuole is more concentrated, with a lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.3] Water therefore passes in through the selectively permeable cell membrane, moving from higher to lower water concentration.
[bio30-s2.0.4] Stage 2, across the cortex: water moves from cell to cell by osmosis, each cell inwards being slightly more concentrated than the one before, so the water keeps flowing inwards. It also seeps along the cellulose cell walls and through the spaces between cells.
[bio30-s2.0.5] Water then passes through the endodermis (the ring of cells around the centre of the root) into the xylem at the centre of the root.
[bio30-s2.0.6] Stage 3, up the xylem: water travels up the root and stem xylem as continuous columns, which carry it into the veins of the leaves. The force that moves it is the transpiration pull, which comes from evaporation in the leaves.
[bio30-s2.0.7] At Higher Level, root pressure and the cohesion–tension model give a fuller account of how water rises, in their own blocks below.
[bio30-s2.1.b] Once water reaches the leaf it has to get out again, and the way it leaves is what pulls the whole column up from the roots. This part of the route also shows that the plant itself does no work to lift the water.
[bio30-s2.1.0] Stage 4, into the leaf cells: water leaves the xylem in the veins and passes by osmosis into the mesophyll cells (the cells in the middle layer of the leaf), keeping them turgid (firm and full of water) and supplying a small amount for photosynthesis.
[bio30-s2.1.1] Stage 5, out of the leaf: water evaporates from the moist walls of the mesophyll cells into the air spaces, which become saturated with water vapour. The vapour then diffuses out through the open stomata into the drier air outside, from a higher to a lower concentration of water vapour.
[bio30-s2.1.2] Transpiration pull: as water evaporates from the leaves, more water is drawn out of the xylem to replace it, so the whole column of water in the xylem is pulled up from the roots, like drinking through a straw.
[bio30-s2.1.3] The energy for the transpiration pull comes from the sun's heat, which causes evaporation; the plant uses none of its own ATP (its cell energy currency) to move water up, so the pull is a passive process.
[bio30-s2.1.4] Diagram: an outline of a whole plant with arrows showing the route soil water, root hair, cortex, root xylem, stem xylem, leaf vein, mesophyll cell, air space, stoma, air; label osmosis (root hair, cortex), evaporation (mesophyll cell walls) and diffusion (stoma).
[bio30-s2.2.b] Transpiration is the process that drives the water flow described above. It brings real benefits, but it is also a cost the plant cannot avoid, so you need to be able to give both sides.
[bio30-s2.2.0] Transpiration is the loss of water vapour from the parts of a plant above the ground, mainly through the stomata of the leaves. Small amounts are lost through the cuticle (the waxy surface layer) and the lenticels (small pores in woody stems).
[bio30-s2.2.1] Most of the water a plant absorbs is lost by transpiration; only a small fraction is used in photosynthesis or kept in the cells. There is more on this in Photosynthesis as an anabolic process.
[bio30-s2.2.2] Transpiration benefits the plant by pulling water and dissolved minerals up from the roots to the leaves, the transpiration stream.
[bio30-s2.2.3] It keeps the leaf cells supplied with water, so they stay turgid and the leaves stay firm and spread out to the light. Evaporation also cools the leaves in strong sunlight, because evaporating water carries heat away.
[bio30-s2.2.4] Transpiration is unavoidable. The stomata must be open to let CO₂ in for photosynthesis, and water vapour escapes through the same pores.
[bio30-s2.2.5] If water is lost faster than the roots can take it in, the cells lose turgor (they go limp) and the plant wilts. Prolonged wilting stops photosynthesis and can kill the plant, so the plant balances gas exchange against water loss.
[bio30-s2.2.6] Conditions that increase transpiration are bright light, high temperature, wind, low humidity, a large leaf area and a thin cuticle. Each is explained and investigated in Investigation: rate of transpiration.
[bio30-s2.3.b] A stoma is a pore that lets CO₂ in and water vapour out, and the plant can open or close it. The guard cells on either side of the pore do this by changing how much water they hold.
[bio30-s2.3.0] Each stoma is a pore between two guard cells. The wall of a guard cell next to the pore, the inner wall, is thicker and less stretchy than the outer wall, and this difference is what makes the cell bend when it swells.
[bio30-s2.3.1] Opening: in the light, guard cells actively take in potassium ions (K⁺) from neighbouring epidermal cells (the ordinary cells of the leaf surface), using ATP. This raises the solute concentration in the guard cells, so water enters them by osmosis from the surrounding cells.
[bio30-s2.3.2] The guard cells swell and become turgid. Because the thick inner wall stretches less than the thin outer wall, the cells bend outwards and curve apart, and the pore opens.
[bio30-s2.3.3] Closing: potassium ions leave the guard cells, water follows by osmosis, the guard cells become flaccid (limp) and straighten, and the pore closes.
[bio30-s2.3.4] Diagram: a pair of guard cells drawn twice, once open (turgid, curved apart, pore open) and once closed (flaccid, straight, pore closed); label guard cell, thick inner wall, thin outer wall, chloroplast, nucleus and stoma (pore); add arrows for K⁺ and water entering when the stoma opens.
[bio30-s2.4.b] The plant does not open its stomata at random. It opens them when CO₂ is needed and closes them when water must be saved, and it places them where least water is lost.
[bio30-s2.4.0] Stomata are usually open in daylight, when CO₂ is needed for photosynthesis, and when the concentration of CO₂ inside the leaf is low. There is more on this in CO₂ as a controlling factor in plant stomata.
[bio30-s2.4.1] Stomata close at night, when there is no photosynthesis and so no need for CO₂, and in very hot, dry conditions. They also close when CO₂ concentration inside the leaf is high.
[bio30-s2.4.2] When water is short and the plant begins to wilt, a chemical signal from the roots closes the stomata, saving water even though photosynthesis slows.
[bio30-s2.4.3] Stomata are mostly on the lower surface of a broad leaf, where it is shaded and cooler, so less water evaporates from them. Leaves floating on water, such as the water lily, have them on the upper surface instead, because the lower surface touches the water.
[bio30-s2.5.b] Because transpiration cannot be stopped, plants survive by limiting it. Their leaves and habits are adapted to lose less water, most of all in dry, windy or cold places.
[bio30-s2.5.0] Leaf features that reduce water loss include a waxy, waterproof cuticle, thicker on the leaves of plants in dry or windy places; most stomata on the lower, shaded surface; and stomata that close at night and in drought.
[bio30-s2.5.1] Deciduous trees (trees that drop their leaves each year) shed their leaves in autumn, so they do not lose water in winter, when cold soil water is hard to absorb.
[bio30-s2.5.2] Evergreen conifers have needle-shaped leaves with a small surface area and a thick cuticle, which means little water evaporates even though the leaves stay on all year.
[bio30-s2.5.3] Plants of dry habitats (xerophytes) have extra adaptations. Marram grass of sand dunes rolls its leaves into a tube, with the stomata on the inside surface, trapping humid air. Cacti have leaves reduced to spines and store water in a swollen green stem.
[bio30-s2.6.b] Water is not the only thing roots take in. They also absorb dissolved minerals from the soil, but minerals enter by a different process from water, and that process costs the plant energy.
[bio30-s2.6.0] Plants need minerals such as nitrate, phosphate, potassium, magnesium and calcium. They are taken up from the soil water as dissolved ions, for example nitrate (NO₃⁻), potassium (K⁺) and magnesium (Mg²⁺).
[bio30-s2.6.1] Mineral ions are usually more concentrated inside the root hair cells than in the soil water, so they must be pumped in against their concentration gradient by carrier proteins in the cell membrane, using ATP from respiration. This is active transport.
[bio30-s2.6.2] Where the soil concentration is higher than in the cell, some ions also enter by diffusion.
[bio30-s2.6.3] Because active transport needs ATP, roots need oxygen for aerobic respiration. In waterlogged soil the air spaces fill with water, the roots get too little oxygen, mineral uptake falls and the plant grows poorly.
[bio30-s2.6.4] This is one reason soil must be well drained, and why roots have air spaces in the cortex.
[bio30-s2.6.5] The ions move across the cortex to the xylem and are carried up, dissolved in the water of the transpiration stream, to the leaves, growing tips and other parts, where they are taken into cells. The faster the transpiration, the faster minerals reach the leaves.
[bio30-s2.6.6] Pumping mineral ions into the root also makes the root cells more concentrated than the soil water, which keeps water entering by osmosis.
[bio30-s2.7.b] Transpiration pulls water up from above, but roots can also push it up from below. This push is called root pressure, and it is an active process that needs living root cells.
[bio30-s2.7.0] Root pressure is a pressure in the root xylem that pushes water a short distance up the stem from below.
[bio30-s2.7.1] Living root cells actively pump mineral ions into the xylem, using ATP. This makes the xylem sap more concentrated than the surrounding cells, so water passes into the xylem by osmosis, building up a pressure that pushes the water upwards.
[bio30-s2.7.2] Evidence: when a stem is cut off near ground level, sap oozes from the cut stump for some time, pushed out by the roots. Grapevines pruned in spring bleed sap in this way.
[bio30-s2.7.3] Guttation: on cool, humid nights or early mornings, when transpiration is very slow, drops of water are forced out through special pores at the tips and edges of leaves, for example on grass and strawberry leaves.
[bio30-s2.7.4] Guttation drops are not dew, because they appear at the leaf edges, not all over the surface.
[bio30-s2.7.5] Root pressure needs living, respiring root cells. It stops if the roots are killed, cooled or deprived of oxygen.
[bio30-s2.7.6] That it stops in these conditions shows that root pressure is an active process.
[bio30-s2.7.7] Root pressure is most significant at night and in spring, before the leaves have opened and when transpiration is low, and it can refill xylem columns that contain air bubbles.
[bio30-s2.7.8] Root pressure is far too weak to raise water to the top of a tall tree, and some plants show little or none, so it is not the main mechanism.
[bio30-s2.8.b] Root pressure is too weak to lift water to the top of a tall tree, so something else must do it. The cohesion–tension model explains how a pulling force from the leaves, and water's tendency to stick together, raises an unbroken column of water.
[bio30-s2.8.0] The cohesion–tension model explains how water rises to the tops of the tallest trees. It was proposed in 1894 by two Irish scientists at Trinity College Dublin, the botanist Henry Dixon and the physicist John Joly.
[bio30-s2.8.1] 1. Transpiration: water evaporates from the walls of the mesophyll cells and diffuses out through the stomata.
[bio30-s2.8.2] 2. The mesophyll cells replace the lost water by osmosis from the cells deeper in the leaf, and these take water from the xylem in the veins.
[bio30-s2.8.3] 3. Tension: removing water from the top of the xylem puts the column of water in the xylem under tension, a pulling force or negative pressure, which is passed all the way down the stem to the roots. This is the transpiration pull.
[bio30-s2.8.4] 4. Cohesion: water molecules are attracted to each other by hydrogen bonds, so they stick together and the column does not break. Each molecule pulled up drags the one behind it, so the whole unbroken column moves up as one.
[bio30-s2.8.5] 5. Adhesion: water molecules are also attracted to the walls of the narrow xylem vessels. This helps hold the column up against gravity and stops it pulling away from the walls.
[bio30-s2.8.6] 6. At the bottom, water is drawn into the root xylem from the cortex, and into the root hairs from the soil, to replace it.
[bio30-s2.8.7] The process is passive, so the plant spends no ATP on it. The lignified (stiffened with lignin) walls of the xylem stop the vessels collapsing inwards under the tension.
[bio30-s2.9.b] A model is only convincing if observations support it. Two observations show that the water in the xylem really is under tension, and one other factor is too weak to matter.
[bio30-s2.9.0] The trunk of a tree becomes very slightly narrower during the day, when transpiration is fastest, as the tension pulls the xylem walls inwards.
[bio30-s2.9.1] When a xylem vessel under tension is cut, air is drawn in rather than water leaking out. If air enters the xylem the column breaks and water flow in that vessel stops.
[bio30-s2.9.2] This is why the stems of cut flowers should be cut again under water before being placed in a vase.
[bio30-s2.9.3] Capillarity, the rise of water up narrow tubes by adhesion, raises water only a short way and is a minor factor.
[bio30-s2.10.b] The two mechanisms work together to move water, but they differ in direction, energy source, strength and timing. Comparing them in pairs is the clearest way to keep them apart.
[bio30-s2.10.0] Root pressure pushes water from below; cohesion–tension pulls it from above.
[bio30-s2.10.1] Root pressure is active, using ATP in the root cells; cohesion–tension is passive, powered by the sun's energy, which evaporates the water.
[bio30-s2.10.2] Root pressure raises water only a short distance; cohesion–tension is the main mechanism and can lift water to the top of the tallest trees.
[bio30-s2.10.3] Root pressure is most important at night and in spring, when transpiration is low; cohesion–tension works when the stomata are open and transpiration is fast, mainly by day.

CANDIDATES bio2-s2: Designing and conducting investigations
[bio2-s2.0.b] Every investigation has factors that are deliberately changed, measured or kept the same. Sorting them into the right type is the first step in designing a fair investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.0] A variable is any factor that can change or be changed in an investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.1] The independent variable is the one variable the investigator deliberately changes, for example the temperature of the water bath in an enzyme investigation, or the concentration of sucrose solution in an osmosis investigation.
[bio2-s2.0.2] The dependent variable is the variable that is measured, because it is expected to change as a result; it depends on the independent variable. Examples: the volume of oxygen released per minute by catalase, or the percentage change in mass of potato cylinders.
[bio2-s2.0.3] Controlled variables are all other variables that could affect the dependent variable, so they are kept the same. For enzyme rate these are pH (kept constant with a buffer), substrate concentration and volume, the amount of enzyme, and the time of measurement.
[bio2-s2.0.4] For osmosis the controlled variables are the size and surface area of the cylinders, the potato they are cut from, the volume of solution, the time in solution and the temperature. Any of these could change the result if allowed to vary.
[bio2-s2.0.5] Name variables precisely, with their unit and how each is measured: “temperature” is an independent variable, but “the enzyme” is not, because it is not a factor you change by amount.
[bio2-s2.1.b] Saying a variable is controlled is not enough; the plan must say how it is held constant, and which values of the independent variable will be tested.
[bio2-s2.1.0] Variables are kept constant with equipment and technique: a thermostatically controlled water bath or incubator for temperature, because these hold a set temperature over time; a buffer solution for pH, because a buffer resists changes in pH.
[bio2-s2.1.1] Use a graduated pipette, syringe or graduated cylinder for volumes, so each set-up gets the same amount, and a stopwatch for timing.
[bio2-s2.1.2] For tissue, use the same cork borer and trim every piece to the same length, so that size and surface area are the same in every set-up.
[bio2-s2.1.3] Choose a range of at least five values of the independent variable, spread across the range in which a change is expected, for example 0 °C to 60 °C in 10 °C steps for an enzyme. This gives enough points to show a pattern.
[bio2-s2.1.4] Add closer intervals around a key point such as an apparent optimum (the value that gives the highest rate), so that the key point is located more exactly.
[bio2-s2.1.5] A preliminary (pilot) trial is a quick practice run. It helps choose the range and checks that the method works before the full investigation.
[bio2-s2.2.b] To show that the factor you changed caused the result, you need a fair test and a control. These two ideas are linked but are not the same thing.
[bio2-s2.2.0] A fair test is an investigation in which only the independent variable is changed and all other variables are controlled, so any change in the dependent variable can be put down to the independent variable.
[bio2-s2.2.1] A control (control experiment) is a set-up identical to the experiment in every way except that the factor being tested is absent or not applied. It is a standard for comparison, showing that the result is due to the factor being investigated and not something else.
[bio2-s2.2.2] Example of a control: boiled (denatured) enzyme used in place of active enzyme. Boiling denatures enzymes, so this shows that the enzyme, rather than the substrate breaking down on its own, causes the reaction.
[bio2-s2.2.3] A negative control is a set-up expected to give no response, for example boiled enzyme, or distilled water in place of the test solution.
[bio2-s2.2.4] A positive control is a set-up known to give a response, which shows that the method can detect one. For example, glucose is a reducing sugar and gives a positive Benedict’s test, so a known glucose solution shows the food test works.
[bio2-s2.2.5] Controlled variables and the control are different: controlled variables are the factors kept the same in every set-up, while the control is a separate set-up used for comparison.
[bio2-s2.3.b] A fair test is not much use unless it also measures the right thing. Validity is about whether the investigation really answers the question asked.
[bio2-s2.3.0] An investigation is valid if it actually measures what it sets out to measure and answers the question asked. This needs a fair test and a suitable dependent variable.
[bio2-s2.3.1] Example of an invalid investigation: if a lamp is moved closer to pondweed to increase light intensity and also warms the water, temperature has changed as well as light, so the investigation cannot show the effect of light alone.
[bio2-s2.3.2] A heat shield, such as a beaker of water between the lamp and the plant, restores validity because the water absorbs heat and so reduces the heating.
[bio2-s2.3.3] A conclusion is valid only if it is supported by data from a valid investigation. In other words, a flawed method cannot give a trustworthy conclusion, however neat the results look.
[bio2-s2.4.b] Students often use these words interchangeably, but each one describes something different about a measurement.
[bio2-s2.4.0] Error in measurement is the difference between a measured value and the true value. Every measurement has some error, and error is not the same as a mistake. There is more on this in Objectivity and data quality, which covers the types of error.
[bio2-s2.4.1] Accuracy is how close a measurement or a mean is to the true value. It is improved by calibrated equipment (checked against a known standard) and a method that measures the right thing.
[bio2-s2.4.2] Precision is how close repeated measurements are to each other, meaning there is little spread around the mean.
[bio2-s2.4.3] The precision of an instrument is its resolution: the smallest change it can show. A balance reading to 0.01 g is more precise than one reading to 1 g, and a graduated pipette is more precise than a beaker for measuring 5 cm³.
[bio2-s2.4.4] Precise results need not be accurate: a balance that reads 0.5 g too high gives readings close to each other (precise) but all wrong (inaccurate).
[bio2-s2.4.5] Diagram: four targets showing accurate and precise; precise but not accurate; accurate on average but not precise; neither.
[bio2-s2.5.b] One measurement can be a fluke. Reliability is about whether results can be trusted because they come out the same when the investigation is repeated.
[bio2-s2.5.0] Reliability is how far results can be trusted because they are consistent when the investigation is repeated. It is improved by replicates, repeats and a large sample size. There is more on judging reliability in Objectivity and data quality.
[bio2-s2.5.1] Replicates are several identical set-ups at each value of the independent variable, run at the same time, for example three potato cylinders in each sucrose concentration.
[bio2-s2.5.2] Repeats are carrying out the whole measurement again. Both replicates and repeats allow a mean to be calculated and an anomalous result (one that does not fit the pattern) to be spotted.
[bio2-s2.5.3] Sample size is the number of organisms, samples or measurements in each group. Living things vary naturally: two potato cylinders, two leaves or two people are never identical.
[bio2-s2.5.4] A larger sample, chosen at random, reduces the effect of individual variation and gives a more representative and reliable result.
[bio2-s2.5.5] Sample size is limited by time, cost, materials and the ethics of using organisms, so a plan has to balance these against the need for reliable results.
[bio2-s2.6.b] The equipment you choose decides how good your data can be. A plan should pick each item for a reason, not just name it.
[bio2-s2.6.0] Choose equipment whose resolution suits the size of the change: a syringe, graduated pipette or burette for small volumes rather than a beaker; a balance reading to at least 0.01 g for small masses; a stopwatch for time; a thermometer or temperature probe for temperature.
[bio2-s2.6.1] Choose equipment that measures objectively where possible, meaning the reading does not depend on opinion. A colorimeter measures colour or cloudiness as a number instead of judging by eye.
[bio2-s2.6.2] A gas syringe or an inverted graduated cylinder over water measures the volume of gas, instead of counting bubbles, which vary in size.
[bio2-s2.6.3] Data loggers with sensors (temperature, pH, oxygen, light) record readings automatically at set intervals, so no readings are missed and there is no human timing error.
[bio2-s2.6.4] Justify each choice in the plan: say what it measures, why its resolution or range is suitable, and how it reduces error.
[bio2-s2.7.b] Practical work can cause harm, so risks are thought through before starting. A risk assessment is the written record of that thinking.
[bio2-s2.7.0] A risk assessment is carried out before practical work. It identifies each hazard (anything that could cause harm), judges the risk (how likely and how serious the harm is) and states a control measure that reduces it.
[bio2-s2.7.1] General control measures: wear safety glasses and a lab coat; tie back long hair; heat with a water bath rather than a direct flame where possible; clean up spills at once; wash hands after practical work; never eat or drink in the laboratory.
[bio2-s2.7.2] Hot water baths and boiling tubes can scald or burn, so use tongs or a test-tube holder.
[bio2-s2.7.3] Corrosive or irritant reagents such as hydrogen peroxide or Benedict’s reagent can harm skin and eyes, so wear glasses and gloves and avoid skin contact.
[bio2-s2.7.4] Sharp scalpels and cork borers can cut, so cut downwards onto a tile, away from the body.
[bio2-s2.7.5] Microorganisms can cause infection, so use aseptic technique (methods that keep unwanted microbes out), sealed plates and disinfected benches, and dispose of cultures by sterilising them. There is more on this in Microorganisms.
[bio2-s2.7.6] Electrical equipment must be kept away from water, because water conducts electricity and risks shock.
[bio2-s2.7.7] Safety also covers the organisms used: living material should be treated with care and returned to its habitat where possible.
[bio2-s2.8.b] Science only works if people can trust what is reported. Scientific integrity means being honest in how investigations are carried out and reported.
[bio2-s2.8.0] Record all results as they were measured, including unexpected and anomalous ones, because a result you did not expect may still be real.
[bio2-s2.8.1] Never invent results (fabrication) or change them to fit the hypothesis (falsification).
[bio2-s2.8.2] Never present others’ work or data as your own (plagiarism), so acknowledge every source.
[bio2-s2.8.3] Integrity also means reporting limitations and errors openly, and not leaving out data because they do not support the hypothesis.
[bio2-s2.9.b] Investigations involve living things, so the investigator must weigh the harm they might cause. Ethics sets the rules for how animals, people and habitats are treated.
[bio2-s2.9.0] Ethics with animals: use animals only where there is no alternative, and cause the minimum harm or stress.
[bio2-s2.9.1] The 3Rs: Replace (use non-animal alternatives such as cell cultures or computer models), Reduce (use the fewest animals that give reliable results), and Refine (improve methods to minimise suffering).
[bio2-s2.9.2] Return organisms collected from the wild to where they were found.
[bio2-s2.9.3] Ethics with human participants covers informed consent (participants know what is involved and agree freely), the right to withdraw at any time, confidentiality of personal data, and no physical or psychological harm.
[bio2-s2.9.4] Investigations on people, for example measuring reaction time after a caffeinated drink, must take account of health conditions and exclude anyone at risk, because caffeine could harm them.
[bio2-s2.9.5] Ethics also covers the environment: avoid damaging habitats when collecting. There is more on the wider ethical, social and economic consequences of research in Science, scientists and society.
[bio2-s2.10.b] A plan must be detailed enough that someone else could repeat the investigation exactly. A worked outline for the effect of temperature on catalase (the enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide into water and oxygen) shows the structure. There is more on the full method in Enzymes.
[bio2-s2.10.0] A plan states, in order: the question and hypothesis; the independent, dependent and controlled variables; the control; and the equipment and reagents with reasons for choice.
[bio2-s2.10.1] It then gives a numbered method with amounts, concentrations, times and temperatures; the number of replicates or repeats; how data will be recorded; a risk assessment; and ethical considerations.
[bio2-s2.10.2] Worked outline, catalase: the independent variable is temperature, with five or more values set with water baths.
[bio2-s2.10.3] Worked outline, catalase: the dependent variable is the volume of oxygen collected in a fixed time, and the controlled variables are pH (kept with a buffer), the volume and concentration of hydrogen peroxide, and the mass or volume of the enzyme source.
[bio2-s2.10.4] Worked outline, catalase: the control is boiled enzyme; there are three replicates at each temperature; and safety glasses are worn for hydrogen peroxide.
