TARGET hom-family-caring-s2: 2. Child–parent relationships
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.b] Young children depend entirely on adults to meet their basic needs for growth and survival. If a physical need is missed, the child's health and development suffer, so each one matters.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.0] Food: a balanced diet in suitable amounts supports growth, and new foods are introduced as the child grows so that the body gets all the nutrients it needs.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.1] Shelter: a warm, dry, safe home protects children from weather and hazards, because young children cannot regulate their body temperature or avoid danger for themselves.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.2] Clothing: suitable clothing protects against cold and injury and allows free movement, so the child can play and explore.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.3] Sleep and rest: regular sleep supports growth, concentration and the repair of the body.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.4] Health care: regular check-ups, immunisation (vaccination against disease) and prompt treatment of illness prevent disease and support healthy development.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.5] Hygiene: washing, care of teeth and toilet training prevent infection, because germs spread easily where hygiene is poor.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.6] Safety and protection: supervision and safe surroundings prevent accidents and abuse, since young children cannot recognise danger.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.b] Children need more than food and shelter. Their emotional and mental needs shape how confident they feel and how well they relate to others, now and as adults.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.0] Love and affection: warm physical and verbal affection helps children feel valued, so they grow up believing they matter.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.1] Security and trust: a dependable, consistent caregiver teaches children that the world is predictable and safe.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.2] Attachment: a strong bond with a consistent caregiver forms the basis for later relationships, because the child learns what a trusting relationship feels like.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.3] Praise and encouragement: recognising effort builds self-esteem (belief in your own worth) and motivation.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.4] Stimulation and learning: play, conversation, reading and exploration develop language and thinking.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.5] Routine and boundaries: consistent limits help children learn self-control and understand expectations.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.6] Social contact: interaction within the family teaches children to form friendships and relate to others.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.b] How well a child's needs are met in the early years affects the adult they become.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.0] When physical and psychological needs are met, children grow into well-adjusted, confident adults who are able to contribute to society.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.1] When needs go unmet, children are more likely to grow up insecure, with poor self-esteem and difficulty forming relationships.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.b] Adolescents are changing quickly in body and mind. They still need care, but they need it in a different form from young children, with more freedom and respect.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.0] Growth: rapid growth (the growth spurt) increases the body's need for energy, protein, calcium and iron, and adequate physical activity supports healthy development.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.1] Sleep: adolescents need extended sleep because they are growing, and changes in their body clock can make early starts difficult.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.2] Independence: adolescents need increasing freedom to make choices, with parents gradually reducing direct control while keeping limits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.3] Identity and self-esteem: adolescents build a personal identity through relationships, interests and feedback from others.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.4] Peer acceptance: friendships become central, so a safe place at home to meet friends supports them.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.5] Privacy: adolescents need personal space and private time, which parents should respect, because it helps them develop their own identity.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.6] Emotional support: adolescents need to be listened to without judgement when problems arise, so they feel able to turn to parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.7] Guidance on risks: adolescents benefit from advice on the risks they meet, so they can make safe choices as they gain independence.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.b] The family is where children's needs are met day to day, mostly through ordinary routines and the way adults behave.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.0] Daily routines: regular meal, sleep and homework times give structure and security, so the child knows what to expect.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.1] Modelling: children learn behaviour, language and values by watching how adults act.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.2] Teaching: parents teach self-care, manners, safety and social skills during everyday activities.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.3] Health monitoring: parents arrange medical care, watch for illness and promote healthy eating and exercise.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.4] Responsiveness: a prompt response to a child's distress builds attachment and trust.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.5] The home is where language, values and early skills are first learned, before formal schooling begins. This first learning within the family is called primary socialisation.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.b] Families do not meet every need alone. The State also helps with the cost and care of rearing children.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.0] Education through schools, for example, gives every child the chance to learn and develop.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.1] Child Benefit is a regular payment to parents for each child, which helps with the cost of rearing children.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.2] Medical cards give access to medical care for families who qualify, so health needs are not left untreated because of cost.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.3] Supports for disadvantaged pupils in school help children from poorer backgrounds to take part and do well.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.4] Child-protection services through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, protect children from harm.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.b] Children are not just dependants. They have rights, which are things every child is entitled to, and these are linked to the needs children have.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.0] Right to life, and to a name and nationality, so the child has an identity and belongs to a country.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.1] Right to be cared for by parents and to live with them, unless this would be contrary to the child's interests.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.2] Right to protection from abuse and neglect, because children cannot defend themselves.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.3] Right to education and to develop physically, mentally, socially, morally and spiritually.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.4] Right to rest, play, leisure, culture and recreation, because play is how children learn and relax.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.5] Right to protection from discrimination, so every child is treated fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.6] Right to maintenance, meaning financial support from parents. The duty of parents to maintain their children is in the Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.7] Right to express an opinion and be heard in matters affecting the child, in line with age and maturity. Where parents live apart, children also have the right to see both parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.b] Children's rights come from international and Irish law, and they are balanced with the duties of parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.0] The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child was adopted in 1989 and ratified by Ireland in 1992.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.1] The Irish Constitution, since the Children Amendment, states the State's duty to protect children's rights.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.2] Rights come with parents' duties, and the State steps in only where a child's welfare requires it.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.3] The Child Care Act 1991 sets out the State's duty to protect children whose health, development or welfare is at risk.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.4] The Child Care Act 1991 gives the State, through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, powers to protect children at risk. These include care orders and emergency care orders, which can place the child outside the home, for example with foster carers.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.b] Conflict is common in adolescence because young people are moving towards adulthood while parents are still responsible for them. Most causes come back to the wish for independence.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.0] Adolescents seek independence and may feel their parents are out of touch with them.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.1] Peer and media influence can bring adolescents' values into conflict with the values of their parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.2] Adolescents accept adult responsibilities but are not yet allowed adult freedoms, which causes frustration.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.3] Relationships with peers and partners can take priority over schoolwork and family commitments, leading to disagreement.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.4] Parents may struggle to let go gradually and trust young people, such as by allowing more freedom, while still needing to set limits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.5] Common flashpoints include curfews (a time to be home by), technology and money.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.b] Conflict is easier to resolve when both sides stay calm and work towards a solution together instead of trying to win.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.0] Firm but fair discipline: rules are enforced consistently and, where possible, explained and agreed.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.1] Listening and defining the issue: each side states its view and the problem is named clearly rather than made personal.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.2] Negotiation and compromise: possible solutions are weighed together and reviewed after a trial period.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.3] Home atmosphere: adolescents are more likely to resolve conflict when they feel free to talk to parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.4] Parents can draw on their greater life experience while still hearing the young person's view.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.5] Parents need to give trust gradually, so the young person has the chance to show responsibility.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.b] Role-play lets you practise handling a disagreement, such as a curfew, in a safe way.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.0] 1. Choose a conflict, for example an adolescent who wants a later curfew, and give one person the part of the adolescent and one the parent.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.1] 2. Each person states their view without interrupting, so each side feels heard.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.2] 3. Use "I" statements, such as "I feel left out when I have to leave early", so the other person is not blamed.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.3] 4. Name the problem clearly and suggest a compromise, for example a later curfew at weekends.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.4] 5. Agree a trial period and a time to review it, then discuss how it went.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.b] Communication is how family members share what they need and feel. Good communication keeps relationships strong and helps problems get solved.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.0] Communication lets members express needs, feelings and opinions, so that each person feels heard, valued and secure.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.1] Problems discussed openly are more likely to be solved before they become serious conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.2] Bottled-up feelings, such as anger kept hidden, can lead to resentment, stress and withdrawal.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.3] Discussion of spending, meals, childcare and the care of vulnerable members allows household work to be shared fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.4] Explaining the reasons for rules, rather than simply imposing them, makes them more likely to be accepted.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.5] Children learn language, empathy and negotiation by taking part in conversation with adults.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.6] Older members who encourage and praise achievement build confidence in younger members.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.7] Secrecy or aggressive criticism creates mistrust, loneliness and conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.b] Good communication is a skill, and it can be blocked by everyday habits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.0] Active listening means listening without interrupting, using calm language and checking understanding before replying.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.1] Taking turns to speak gives everyone a chance to be heard, so no one person dominates.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.2] Family meetings give a regular, planned time to discuss plans and problems, so issues are raised before they grow.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.3] Barriers to communication include lack of time, screens, differences in age and tone of voice.

CANDIDATES hom-family-caring-s1: 1. Roles and responsibilities in the family
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.b] A family is not the same from one decade to the next. It passes through stages, and each stage changes who does what and how heavy the workload is. A role is the pattern of behaviour expected of a person in a position such as parent or child, and a responsibility is a duty that goes with that role.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.0] 1. Couple (establishment) stage: partners set up a shared home and divide household tasks, often according to paid work, time and skills.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.1] 2. Childbearing (expanding) stage: parents take on the provider, nurturing, educating and socialising roles for the first time, and the workload is at its peak.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.2] 3. Child-rearing stage (children of school age and adolescents): parents keep these roles while children take on more household tasks and independent responsibilities, and parents move from direct control towards guidance.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.3] 4. Launching stage: grown children leave home, so parents' roles change from daily care to advice and support at a distance, and the workload eases.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.4] 5. Later life stage (a retired couple, or one partner alone): older members may move from giving care to needing it, and adult children often take on practical, financial and emotional support for them.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.5] Roles are not fixed. They are shaped by the stage the family is in, by each member's age, health, paid work and skills, and by social attitudes. Textbooks label the stages slightly differently, so describe each one as well as naming it.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.b] Parents carry a bundle of different roles at once. These roles can be shared between two parents or carried by one, and they matter because a parent who fills them well, with firm and fair explained rules, gives children the best start.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.0] Provider: supplying food, clothing, shelter and money for the family's needs.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.1] Nurturer: giving love, affection and emotional security, so that children feel safe and valued.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.2] Educator: teaching skills, language, values and good habits through daily routines and example.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.3] Socialiser: introducing children to the norms, beliefs and behaviour expected in family and community, so they learn how to get on with others.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.4] Protector: ensuring safety, supervising children and making decisions in their best interests.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.5] Manager: planning the household budget, meals, schedules and maintenance of the home.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.6] Disciplinarian: setting boundaries and consequences that are explained and applied consistently, because children cope better with rules they understand.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.b] Adolescents are not just receivers of care. As they grow, they take on real roles and responsibilities that prepare them for adult life, and these are best treated as growing responsibility rather than just chores.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.0] Contributing to the household: helping with cooking, cleaning, shopping and minding younger siblings, so the workload is shared and parents have more time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.1] Managing their own schoolwork, timekeeping and personal belongings, which builds the self-discipline they will need as adults.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.2] Developing independence: making decisions about friends, leisure and part-time work within agreed limits, so freedom grows alongside trust.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.3] Respecting family rules and the privacy of others in shared space, so that everyone in the home can live together comfortably.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.4] Sharing in family decisions, such as holidays or how to spend leisure time, which gives them a voice in family life.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.5] Some adolescents earn money through part-time work, or give informal care to a sibling or grandparent.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.b] Who does what in a family has long depended on whether a person is male or female. These expectations have changed a great deal, but they have not disappeared.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.0] Traditional segregated roles (separate, with each partner's tasks kept apart) assigned men as breadwinners and women as homemakers and primary carers of children. The father earned and the mother ran the home, in contrast to joint roles, where partners share tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.1] Where women work outside the home, they often still carry the larger share of domestic work and childcare, a pattern called the double shift, or second shift (a paid job followed by unpaid work at home).
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.2] Men's roles have expanded to include direct childcare, cooking and shared decision-making, and some fathers take the primary caring role.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.3] Gender stereotypes can limit children's roles and experience, for example expecting girls to help in the kitchen and boys to do outdoor jobs.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.4] Sharing household tasks equally reduces overload and resentment, and gives children a model of shared responsibility.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.5] Adult daughters often take on more of the care of ageing parents than sons do.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.b] Family roles have shifted a lot in recent times because society, money and technology have all changed. Each factor below matters because it changes who earns, who cares and who does the housework.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.0] Women's participation in paid employment has increased, so many households depend on two earners and domestic roles have had to be shared. Greater gender equality and changed attitudes support this.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.1] Lone-parent families have increased, so one adult often carries the provider, carer and household roles alone.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.2] Smaller families, by choice or because of later marriage and childbearing, mean each child receives more parental time and money.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.3] Greater acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce and blended families has created new parenting roles, such as step-parent.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.4] Weaker extended-family and community support means parents have fewer relatives and neighbours to share childcare and other tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.5] Longer life expectancy means some families care for parents or grandparents while still raising children, a pattern called the sandwich generation (adults caught between caring for children and ageing parents at once).
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.6] Rising housing, childcare and education costs make two incomes necessary for many households, shifting the provider role to both partners. Unemployment can also reverse traditional provider and carer roles.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.7] Longer working hours and commuting reduce the time available for family meals, shared activities and housework. Labour-saving appliances cut housework time so tasks can be divided differently, while mobile phones and the internet allow remote working and contact but also blur work and home time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.b] Role conflict arises when the demands of two or more roles held by one person cannot be met at the same time. It is common in modern families, where one adult may be a worker, parent, partner and carer all at once.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.0] Work-family conflict: a parent is needed at work during the hours a child is ill or has a school event.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.1] Role overload: one person carries too many responsibilities, such as employee, parent, carer and homemaker, for the time available.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.2] Conflict between partners over who does which tasks, or whose career takes priority.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.3] Intergenerational conflict: an adult may have to care for an older parent while still raising children at home.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.4] Consequences include fatigue, stress, less quality time with the family, reduced quality of parenting, tension between partners and guilt.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.5] Reducing conflict: explicit agreement on the division of tasks, flexible working arrangements, shared decision-making and help from extended family or services, such as childcare.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.b] One way to see how roles have changed is a short case study or interview with an older person, asking about family life fifty to one hundred years ago. A planned set of questions gives you something to compare with family life today.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.0] 1. Decide who to interview, such as a grandparent or an older neighbour, and agree a time, so they are ready to talk.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.1] 2. Ask who earned the money in the family and who cared for the children, to show the provider and carer roles at that time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.2] 3. Ask who did the housework and what tasks children were expected to do, to show how household work was divided.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.3] 4. Ask about family size, since the number of children affects how much care and work each person carried.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.4] 5. Record the answers accurately in your own notes, and write up only what the person told you, with no invented findings.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.5] 6. Compare the answers with family life today, looking for the kinds of change expected: more women in paid work, smaller families, shared tasks, labour-saving appliances and more mobile adolescents.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s3: 3. Primary and secondary social groups, kinship, socialisation
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.b] A social group is two or more people who interact regularly and share a sense of belonging, such as a purpose, an identity or a place. Groups differ in how close and how formal their relationships are, and a crowd of strangers is not a group.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.0] A primary group is small, intimate and face-to-face, with long-lasting relationships; examples are the family and close friends.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.1] Primary groups meet emotional needs and give a sense of belonging, so they shape a person's personality, values and sense of identity. They are also where primary socialisation mainly happens.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.2] A secondary group is larger and more formal, with relationships that are impersonal and often short-term or limited to a role; examples are a school class, a workplace, a trade union, a sports club and a political party.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.3] Secondary groups are organised around a specific goal, such as completing a course or producing goods. Members often join by choice and leave when the purpose is finished.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.4] To compare them: primary groups are small, close, lasting and personal, while secondary groups are larger, formal, often temporary and purpose-based. A person belongs to both types at once, and some groups, such as a small close-knit club, sit between the two.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.5] An in-group is a group with which a person identifies and feels loyalty; an out-group is a group that members see as different from or opposed to their own.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.6] A reference group is a group whose norms and values (the standards of behaviour it expects) a person uses to judge their own behaviour, even if they are not a member of it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.7] A peer group is made up of people of similar age and status. It is important in adolescence and can act as a primary or a secondary agent of socialisation, depending on how close its members are.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.b] Kinship is the network of family ties between people who are related by blood, marriage or adoption. It is a relationship, not a type of household, so it includes relatives who live apart.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.0] Kinship means family ties: relationships based on blood (common ancestry or lineage), marriage or adoption.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.1] Consanguineal kin are related by blood, for example parents, children, siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.2] Affinal kin are related through marriage, for example a spouse, a spouse's parents, who are in-laws, and step-relatives. The terms consanguineal and affinal are optional; blood and marriage are enough.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.3] A nuclear family is parents and their dependent children living together as one household.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.4] An extended family includes kin beyond the nuclear family, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who live together or keep in close contact.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.5] The family of orientation is the family a person is born into; the family of procreation is the family a person forms through their own marriage or partnership and children.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.6] Kinship is found in every society, which is part of why the family is called universal.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.7] Descent, the line through which kinship is traced, can run through the mother's side, the father's side or both; most modern Western societies trace both sides.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.b] Kin networks matter because relatives are often the first people to turn to when help is needed. They have also changed as society has changed.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.0] Kin networks provide practical and emotional support, including childcare, financial help, care for older members and a sense of identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.1] Kinship obligations include mutual support and, in many societies, inheritance rights between relatives.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.2] Kin networks have become looser as families have become smaller and more mobile, but relatives keep in contact through phones and social media.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.b] Socialisation is the lifelong process by which people learn the norms, values, behaviour, roles and skills of their society and so become members of it. Without it a child could not use language or form relationships.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.0] Socialisation is lifelong, so it continues in adolescence and adulthood as well as in childhood.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.1] Primary socialisation takes place in early childhood, mainly within the family, where children learn language, toilet training, manners, self-control, right from wrong and a first sense of identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.2] Secondary socialisation takes place later in wider settings such as school, the peer group, the workplace, religious institutions and the media. There people learn the roles and norms of that setting, such as punctuality, rules, teamwork and respect for authority.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.3] Through socialisation the culture of a society is passed from one generation to the next, which maintains continuity over time. It also produces conformity and social order and lets individuals take part in society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.4] People learn through observation, imitation, rewards and punishments (sanctions), role models and direct instruction. In the family, children learn through observation, imitation and consistent discipline.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.5] Anticipatory socialisation is learning the norms of a role or group that a person hopes to join, such as a student learning workplace norms before starting an apprenticeship.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.6] Resocialisation is learning new norms after a major change in life, such as entering the army or prison, where old habits are replaced.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.b] Agents of socialisation are the people and institutions that pass on norms and values. Each one teaches something different.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.0] The main agents are the family, school, peer group, mass media, religion and the workplace.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.1] School socialises formally through the curriculum and informally through the hidden curriculum, the unwritten lessons taught by its rules and routines.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.2] The peer group becomes an important agent in adolescence, when young people test independence and learn the norms of their age group.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.3] Mass media, including television, the internet and social media, transmit norms and values and shape consumer habits.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.b] Monogamy is one form of marriage; the other forms are classed as polygamy. It describes how many partners a person has at one time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.0] Monogamy is marriage, or a partnership, with one partner at a time, traditionally one husband and one wife.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.1] Monogamy is the norm in the Western world, and it is the only form of marriage recognised in Ireland. It is not the only form worldwide.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.2] Polygamy is marriage to more than one spouse at the same time; polygyny is one man with several wives, and polyandry is one woman with several husbands. Both are found in some other societies.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.3] Polygamy is illegal in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.4] Bigamy is entering a marriage while still legally married to another person, without the death or divorce of the first spouse. It is an offence in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.5] Serial monogamy is a succession of monogamous marriages, one after another, following divorce or the death of a spouse.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.6] Spouses in a monogamous marriage are expected to remain faithful and loyal to each other, live together, and support one another and their children financially.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.7] Civil and religious marriages are both legally recognised in Ireland, and both are monogamous. Same-sex marriage is also lawful in Ireland and is monogamous, being a marriage of two people.
