[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.0.b] When a family loses its income, the State is not the only source of help. Voluntary organisations are non-profit bodies set up to meet a social need, and they step in where people are struggling.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.0.0] A voluntary organisation is a non-profit group run largely by volunteers, often with some paid staff. Because it is independent of government, it can respond quickly and locally.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.0.1] Many receive some State funding, but they are still not State bodies. Statutory (State-run) bodies such as SOLAS, MABS and the Department of Social Protection are a different thing.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.0.2] They are funded by donations, fundraising, charity shops and State grants, so they can keep helping people without charging them.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.0.3] Unfunded services, such as volunteer-run listening services, fill gaps that statutory services do not cover, for example by giving someone a friendly person to talk to.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.1.b] The Society of St Vincent de Paul (SVP) is the standard example of a voluntary organisation that supports people at risk of poverty or unemployment. Its strength is that help comes from neighbours who know the local area.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.1.0] SVP works through local volunteer groups called conferences. They visit households in their own area, in confidence, and assess the help needed, so families are not shamed by asking publicly.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.1.1] It gives practical help: food or fuel vouchers, help with essential household bills, school books and uniforms, furniture and advice, and also emergency accommodation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.1.2] It also runs hostels and housing, homework clubs and education supports, so it helps with long-term needs as well as emergencies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.1.3] SVP raises its funds through charity shops and public and church collections, such as church-gate collections, rather than relying only on the State.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.2.b] Several other voluntary organisations work with particular groups. Each has a clear role you can state in one line.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.2.0] Simon Community provides support and emergency accommodation to people who are homeless or at risk of homelessness, with housing and outreach (going out to meet people on the street).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.2.1] Focus Ireland works to prevent and end homelessness. It provides accommodation, training in personal and life skills, and information on job opportunities, as well as housing, support and advice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.2.2] Barnardos supports children and families, and Age Action supports older people, so each targets a group that is at particular risk of poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.2.3] Rotary and Lions clubs give local fundraising and practical support, and Men's Sheds give men a place to meet and work together, which reduces isolation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.b] When the main earner is unemployed, a family needs more than money. Voluntary organisations meet several different needs, and they also work on behalf of everyone affected.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.0] Financial or material help: vouchers, help with bills, clothing and food, so the family can cover essentials while income is low.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.1] Advice, information and advocacy (speaking up for someone): they direct clients to State entitlements, MABS and Citizens Information, linking people to services they are entitled to.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.2] Coping and skills classes, and a friendly visit and someone to talk to, which ease the stress and isolation that unemployment brings.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.3] Voluntary organisations act as pressure groups: they gather evidence on local need and campaign and lobby the Government for changes in public policy.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.4] Voluntary work benefits the individual through purpose, skills, confidence and friendships, and it may lead to a job.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.3.5] It benefits the community through quick, low-cost local services that complement State services and build community spirit.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.4.b] Community initiatives are locally run responses to unemployment and early school leaving, often funded by the State. They work because they are close to the people they help.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.4.0] Community-based projects build confidence, work experience, skills and social networks, which help people move back into paid work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.4.1] Community education centres offer adult literacy and numeracy classes and personal development courses, often for people who left school early.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.4.2] Education and Training Boards (ETBs) provide adult and further education, and many community courses are delivered through them. Youthreach is for early school leavers and the Vocational Training Opportunities Scheme (VTOS) is for unemployed adults.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.4.3] Community training centres and local training projects offer practical and vocational skills, often with a route to a recognised qualification. They are funded through SOLAS and the ETBs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.4.4] Local groups identify training needs in their area, such as skills gaps in the local economy, and design courses to meet them, so training leads to real jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.5.b] Some schemes give people paid work in their own community, so they keep working habits and earn while they look for a job. These are State-funded employment schemes run locally.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.5.0] Community Employment (CE) provides part-time temporary paid work on local community projects for long-term unemployed people, in areas such as the environment, heritage, sport, tourism and community services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.5.1] Tús gives temporary work placements in local communities through local development companies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.5.2] The Rural Social Scheme gives income support to low-income farmers and fishers for community work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.5.3] Local development companies deliver the Social Inclusion and Community Activation Programme (SICAP 2024-2028). It is funded by the Department of Rural and Community Development and the Gaeltacht, with EU funding through the ESF+.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.5.4] SICAP aims to reduce poverty and social exclusion by supporting communities and individuals, with courses, job-readiness and help starting a business.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.b] A co-operative is a business owned and democratically controlled by its members, who pool resources, skills and knowledge to meet their needs and share its surplus.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.0] Each member has one vote regardless of how much they have invested, and members elect the board. This is why no wealthy investor can take control.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.1] The surplus (profit after costs and reserves) is shared among members in proportion to their use of the co-operative or reinvested in the business, rather than paid to outside shareholders.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.2] Types include agricultural co-operatives (farming, dairy, fishing), housing co-operatives (owned by their members, who build or manage homes), and worker co-operatives (owned by their employees) and consumer co-operatives (owned by their customers).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.3] Credit unions are member-owned co-operatives where members save and borrow at fair cost. They offer savings accounts and affordable loans, a key service for low-income households.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.4] A community co-operative can run a shop, a childcare service or local transport where no private business will, creating local jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.5] Co-operatives respond to poverty and unemployment because profits stay with members and the local area rather than leaving the community with outside owners.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.6.6] Optional international principles: voluntary and open membership, democratic member control, member economic participation, autonomy and independence, education and training, co-operation among co-operatives, and concern for community.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.b] A cottage industry is small-scale production by an individual or family, usually at home or in a small workshop, using traditional or craft skills. It is a separate idea from a co-operative, which is a member-owned business.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.0] Irish examples are hand-knitted Aran jumpers, hand-weaving including Donegal tweed, Carrickmacross lace, pottery and other handmade crafts, often sold to local and tourist markets.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.1] Artisan food producers and farmers' markets are also accepted as community initiatives.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.2] They give families an extra or sole source of income, especially in rural areas where other employment is limited, and they keep traditional skills alive.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.3] They need little capital, because the equipment is simple and production takes place at home.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.4] Limitations include seasonal or irregular income, small output, difficulty in marketing and competing with mass production, limited marketing reach, no employment benefits and reliance on tourism.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.5] Local Enterprise Offices can help producers with training and grants to develop craft businesses.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.7.6] In the clothing and textile industry, cottage firms supply handmade clothing and textiles.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.8.b] The Combat Poverty Agency is a past tense topic: it was a State body, not a voluntary one, and it has been closed. Answer about it in the past tense.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.8.0] The Combat Poverty Agency was a State body that researched poverty, advised government and funded local anti-poverty projects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.8.1] It was dissolved on 1 July 2009 and its work merged into the Social Inclusion Division of the Department of Social Protection (then the Department of Social and Family Affairs).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s7.8.2] It no longer exists as an independent agency, so current answers should name the bodies and organisations that carry out this work today, such as the Department's Social Inclusion Division and the Roadmap for Social Inclusion, SICAP, and voluntary organisations such as SVP.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s4: 4. Concepts of poverty
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.b] Poverty is more than having a low income. It is a lack of the income and resources needed for basic needs and for normal participation in society, and it is examined through two concepts.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.0] Poverty means being without enough income and resources (material, cultural and social) for an acceptable standard of living.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.1] It means inadequate food, clothing, shelter and warmth, because there is not enough income or resources to pay for them.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.2] It also means being unable to take part in socially acceptable activities that most people take for granted, which is why poverty brings social exclusion (being shut out from ordinary community life).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.3] Poverty includes the lack of opportunities, comforts and self-respect that other people enjoy, so it cannot be measured by income alone.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.4] Poverty is examined through two concepts: absolute poverty and relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.b] Absolute poverty is the most severe form: a person cannot afford the things needed to stay alive and healthy.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.0] Absolute poverty is income too low to meet basic physical needs for food, clothing, warmth and shelter, so health and survival are threatened.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.1] The standard is a fixed minimum, set regardless of how other people in society live. It does not change with the living standards of the surrounding society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.2] Absolute poverty is rare in a developed country like Ireland, but homeless people, and people living without proper housing, clothing or medical care, may experience it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.3] In Ireland the main concern is relative poverty rather than absolute poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.b] Relative poverty is judged against the living standards of the society in which a person lives, so it exists even in a wealthy country.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.0] A person in relative poverty lives below the standard of living that society regards as basic, so cannot take part in activities that are normal for others.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.1] The poverty line is the level of income below which a household is counted as poor: the estimated minimum income for basic food, clothing and shelter and an acceptable standard of living. A household below it is at risk of poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.2] In Irish official statistics the Central Statistics Office (CSO) sets the line at 60% of median equivalised household income. The median is the middle income, and equivalised means adjusted for household size and composition.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.3] Because the poverty line moves as incomes change, relative poverty can persist even when incomes rise.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.4] Examples of relative poverty: being unable to afford a warm waterproof coat, two pairs of strong shoes, adequate heating for the home, or a meal with meat, chicken or fish (or a vegetarian equivalent) every second day.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.5] Photographs of the same type of home or setting from different times and places show that the accepted standard of living changes, which is why poverty is relative.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.6] A child's clothing or a family's home in a photograph from another time or place may look very different from today's norm, yet be an ordinary standard for its own society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.b] Governments need numbers to track poverty. Two official measures are used, and the second is stricter than the first.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.0] The at-risk-of-poverty rate is the share of people whose income is below the poverty line (60% of median income). It is another way of describing relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.1] Enforced deprivation means being unable to afford basic items that most people regard as normal, such as those in the examples of relative poverty. It is not by choice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.2] The CSO counts a household as deprived if it lacks two or more of 11 listed items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.3] Consistent poverty means being both below the poverty line and in enforced deprivation, lacking at least two of the 11 items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.4] Consistent poverty is narrower and more severe than the at-risk-of-poverty rate, because it counts only people who both have a low income and cannot afford the basics.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.5] The CSO publishes these measures every year from the Survey on Income and Living Conditions (SILC), an EU-wide household survey. Social Justice Ireland also publishes a count of people in poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.b] The level of poverty in Ireland has followed the economy and unemployment. It is also not spread evenly across the population.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.0] Poverty fell from the 1960s and 1970s onwards as living standards rose, but rose again during the recession of the 1980s, when unemployment was high.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.1] Poverty fell during the Celtic Tiger period of economic growth in the 1990s and early 2000s, because employment grew.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.2] Poverty rose again after the 2008 downturn as unemployment soared and living costs rose, and rose further with high housing and living costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.3] The COVID-19 pandemic increased the risk of poverty for people who lost work or income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.4] Today poverty is a real problem for a sizeable minority of people, with children and some adult groups at higher risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.5] Poverty is not spread evenly. Risk differs by household type, by labour-market status, by region (urban black spots and some rural areas) and by age.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.b] Some groups face a higher risk of poverty because of low income, limited skills, or caring responsibilities. Each group below has its own reason for being at risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.0] Lone-parent households, particularly those without paid work, because they have one income and childcare costs make it hard to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.1] Unemployed people and long-term unemployed people, who depend on welfare or low-paid work; welfare payments are lower than wages, so they may be too low for their needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.2] Low-paid workers (the working poor), including those on the minimum wage who may not qualify for state supports, and people in insecure or part-time work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.3] People with disabilities or long-term illness, who may be unable to work or face extra costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.4] Older people, especially those living alone on a State pension in poorly insulated or substandard homes that are costly to heat, which can lead to illness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.5] Large families, because there are more children to feed and clothe on one income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.6] Travellers and other ethnic minorities, who face discrimination, lower education and employment, and poorer accommodation, including in the labour market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.7] Homeless people, who face isolation and poor health; asylum seekers, who have a restricted right to work while awaiting a decision; and refugees, who may have low income and language barriers and lack skills and qualifications.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.b] Education and opportunity also shape who is at risk. Two further groups are linked to low qualifications and low pay.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.0] Early school leavers and people with few qualifications are at risk because they face low pay and poor job prospects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.1] People in households in a cycle of deprivation lack education and opportunity, so low income passes from one generation to the next.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s6: 6. Statutory responses
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.b] The State pays income support to unemployed people through two routes, and the route decides what a claimant has to prove. All social welfare payments come from the Department of Social Protection.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.0] Social insurance payments (also called benefits) depend on a record of PRSI contributions paid while in work, and they are not means-tested. They are funded from those PRSI contributions.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.1] Social assistance payments are means-tested, which means the household's income and savings are assessed, and no contribution record is required, because they are paid on the basis of need. They are funded from general taxation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.2] Jobseeker's Benefit is the main social insurance payment for an unemployed person who has enough paid PRSI contributions, so the claimant's own contributions earn the right to it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.3] Jobseeker's Allowance is the main social assistance payment for an unemployed person who does not qualify for Jobseeker's Benefit or whose benefit has ended, so it acts as a safety net.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.4] A Jobseeker's Allowance claimant must be unemployed, capable of work, available for full-time work and genuinely seeking employment. This shows the payment supports people who want work, not people who have chosen not to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.5] Supplementary Welfare Allowance is a means-tested payment for people with no or very low income whose basic needs are not met. It also pays exceptional needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.b] Families with children face extra costs, so several payments are aimed at them. Some reach every family, and others are limited to low-income households.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.0] Child Benefit is paid for each qualifying child regardless of the parents' income. It is universal, not means-tested, so it reaches families on every income level.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.1] Payments for lone parents are means-tested, because they are aimed at those in need, and recognise that one adult is carrying the full cost of the household.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.2] The Working Family Payment (formerly Family Income Supplement) is a tax-free, means-tested weekly payment for low-paid workers who have a dependent child. It is an in-work payment, so it makes work pay and eases the poverty trap.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.3] Means-tested payments are reduced as income rises, so the household gains less from extra earnings than the wage itself.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.4] The Fuel Allowance is paid weekly to qualifying low-income households during the winter season to help with heating costs, which rise in cold weather.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.b] Besides cash payments, the State lowers the cost of essentials such as health, school, housing and childcare for families on low incomes. Each scheme either pays for something or removes a cost.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.0] Medical card: gives free GP visits and access to many health services, run by the HSE. Eligibility depends on income and, in some cases, health needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.1] Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance: a once-yearly payment, because the cost falls at the start of the school year, that helps qualifying families meet the cost of school uniforms and shoes.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.2] Household Benefits Package: help with electricity or gas and a free television licence for qualifying older people and people with certain disabilities or long-term illnesses.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.3] Housing supports: local authority (social) housing is let at rents related to income. The Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) is paid through the local authority to help households pay rent in the private rented sector, and the tenant's rent contribution is based on income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.4] Education supports: the school meals programme and free school book schemes ease the cost of school, and the free pre-school place (ECCE), an early-learning place before primary school, reduces childcare costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.5] Money Advice and Budgeting Service (MABS): a free, confidential, State-funded service, working under the Citizens Information Board, that helps people in debt, mainly low-income households, to budget and deal with creditors.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.b] These initiatives work through training, business supports and job creation rather than income support alone. A statutory body is one set up by law or run by the State.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.0] SOLAS is the State further education and training authority. It funds, plans and coordinates further education and training through the Education and Training Boards (ETBs).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.1] SOLAS supports apprenticeships, vocational courses and literacy, numeracy and digital training, and it provides skills training and upskilling, matching the skills of unemployed people to vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.2] Enterprise Ireland is the State agency that helps Irish-owned companies increase sales, exports and employment through grants, market advice and training. More sales and exports create jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.3] Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs) in each county are the first stop shop for people starting or growing small businesses, giving training, mentoring, advice and grants and encouraging job creation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.4] Intreo is the Department of Social Protection's public employment service. It gives job-seekers advice, job-matching and referral to training, so unemployed people can be linked to vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.5] Work-based learning and CV and job-search preparation give unemployed people the experience that employers ask for, which makes them more employable.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.6] Community Employment (CE) offers temporary part-time work placements on local community projects for long-term unemployed and other eligible people. It is a Department of Social Protection scheme, not a SOLAS one.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.7] Other schemes that support a return to education and training are the Vocational Training Opportunities Scheme (VTOS), the Back to Education Allowance and Youthreach, an ETB programme for early school leavers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by an overseas company that sets up or expands operations in Ireland. The State encourages it because it creates jobs, but it also brings risks.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.0] IDA Ireland is the State agency responsible for attracting overseas companies and for helping foreign companies already here to expand, through grants, site selection and advice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.1] A low corporation tax rate is a key incentive for multinational companies to locate in Ireland: 12.5%, and 15% for the largest multinational groups.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.2] Other attractions are a young, skilled, flexible, educated and English-speaking workforce, grant assistance, good telecommunications and access to the European Union's single market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.3] Foreign investment, for example a new plant, creates direct jobs in the foreign firms and indirect jobs in suppliers and services, so it reduces unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.4] It also brings tax revenue and new skills and technology into local labour markets, so workers can move on to other employers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.5] Dependence on a few large multinationals is a weakness: if a parent company closes or moves a plant, a local area can lose many jobs at once.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.6] Multinational employment tends to be concentrated in larger towns and cities, so rural areas benefit less from it. IDA Ireland aims to spread investment to regions outside the main cities.

CANDIDATES hom-work-leisure-s3: 3. Unpaid and voluntary work
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.b] Unpaid work is the work done in and around the home without any wage. It keeps households running and spares families the cost of paying for services.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.0] Unpaid work covers child care, housework, cooking, laundry, caring for an elderly parent or a person with a disability, gardening, household management, and DIY and home maintenance.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.1] It is done mainly within the household, without financial reward and often out of family duty, so it is not a free choice in the way voluntary work is.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.2] It keeps households and the community functioning and supports the paid workforce, because children and workers are cared for and fed. In this way it makes family life possible.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.3] Because it is unpaid, it saves families the cost of paying for these services themselves, such as child care or home help.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.4] It is not counted in measures of national paid output and has no wage, pension entitlement or sick pay of its own, so its contribution is often overlooked.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.5] Unpaid work can be monotonous, and it often falls unequally on women within the household.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.0.6] Carers can become isolated or stressed, because looking after a relative can take up most of the day.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.1.b] Many people look after a relative at home. They are unpaid carers, and the care they give matters to both families and the State.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.1.0] Unpaid carers look after relatives at home, such as an older person or a person with a disability. They are often helped by State supports such as carer's payments.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.1.1] They save the State and families large costs, because the care would otherwise have to be paid for through nursing homes, home help or hospital services.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.1.2] They give care in a familiar home setting, which is often more comfortable for the person being cared for.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.b] Voluntary work is unpaid work done freely, by choice, for people or a cause outside the household, usually through an organisation.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.0] The volunteer gets no wage, sick pay or pension, but expenses for travel or training may be repaid.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.1] Voluntary work is carried out through charities, community groups, sports clubs, schools, parish and church groups, youth organisations and local development groups. St Vincent de Paul is one example: a national voluntary agency using church-gate collections, charity shops and local visiting.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.2] Typical activities include running charity shops, fundraising, providing meals on wheels and transport for older people, visiting the housebound, and mentoring and coaching young people as club coaches or youth leaders.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.3] Other activities include running local events, community clean-ups, first aid and emergency or rescue services, and community care and support groups.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.4] Environmental volunteering includes litter clean-ups, tree planting, conservation work and local recycling projects.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.2.5] Volunteers need training, and Garda vetting through the organisation for work with children or vulnerable adults, so that those they help are protected.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.3.b] Both kinds of work are unpaid and neither is employment, but they differ in why, for whom and how they are done.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.3.0] Unpaid home work is largely a duty within the family, whereas voluntary work is chosen freely.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.3.1] Unpaid home work benefits the household, whereas voluntary work benefits people and causes in the wider community.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.3.2] Voluntary work is usually organised through an organisation, whereas home work is usually carried out individually.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.b] Volunteers give their time without pay, but they gain a great deal themselves. These benefits are personal, practical and social.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.0] Skills and experience such as teamwork, communication, organisation and leadership, plus training, can be listed on a CV and used for a reference.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.1] Confidence and self-esteem grow as the volunteer contributes to something valued, giving a sense of purpose and achievement.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.2] Social contact and friendships bring a feeling of belonging and reduce isolation, particularly for people who are retired, unemployed or new to an area.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.3] Volunteering gives people a sense of purpose and fulfilment, and a chance to develop their potential.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.4] Volunteering can lead to paid employment, or to expertise in a particular area, as it builds experience and references in that field.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.5] Empathy and insight into the lives of others develop through helping people in need, such as older or vulnerable people.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.4.6] Volunteering keeps people active and healthy, and is a good use of free time for people who are retired or between jobs.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.b] The community gains services, support and a stronger sense of belonging from the people who give their time. These benefits are separate from what the volunteers themselves gain.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.0] Services that would otherwise be unavailable or unaffordable can be provided, such as meals for older or housebound people, transport, youth clubs, sports coaching and support groups.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.1] Support for vulnerable groups, including older people, people with disabilities, lone parents, the homeless, people at risk of poverty and new arrivals, reaches those who might otherwise be missed.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.2] Volunteers build community spirit and social cohesion by bringing people from different backgrounds together around a shared purpose.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.3] Where young people are involved in clubs there is less crime and vandalism, because they have something positive to do.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.4] Local facilities such as halls, pitches and parks are built, maintained and improved through volunteer effort and fundraising.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.5] Volunteering saves the State and local bodies money and complements paid services, though it does not replace them.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.5.6] Volunteers raise awareness of local issues and encourage participation (civic participation) and active citizenship in decisions that affect the area.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.b] Volunteers do more than provide services. Their work changes how a community lives and how it treats its surroundings.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.0] Social change: volunteers campaign for services and for the rights of groups, and help people to claim the supports they are entitled to.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.1] Social change: volunteer-run projects build inclusion by supporting people with disabilities, minorities, Travellers and migrants to take part in community life, and they reduce isolation.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.2] Environmental change: litter picks, clean-ups of rivers and beaches, tree planting and conservation work reduce litter and protect habitats such as woodland, river banks and coastline.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.3] Environmental change: community gardens and growing schemes, recycling projects and local heritage projects change local habits and reduce waste.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.4] The Tidy Towns competition is a well-known example of a community improvement effort driven by local volunteers.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.6.5] Long-term change comes from volunteers passing on their knowledge and skills to others, so the work continues beyond any one project.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.7.b] Voluntary work depends on people's goodwill and spare time, which creates some limits.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.7.0] Volunteers can experience burnout when a few people carry a large share of the work.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.7.1] Groups depend on continued volunteer commitment, which can be hard to sustain as people take on paid work or family responsibilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.7.2] Volunteers can face out-of-pocket costs for travel, equipment or training that are not reimbursed.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.7.3] Volunteers are not employees, so they have no pay, sick pay or pension.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.7.4] Volunteers need training, and Garda vetting for work with children or vulnerable adults.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.8.b] Volunteering is helped along by national organisations and by ways of encouraging people to take part.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.8.0] Volunteer Ireland is the national volunteer development organisation, supporting Volunteer Centres and running National Volunteering Week. The first national strategy, the National Volunteering Strategy, was published by the Government in December 2020. Its period ended in 2025, so a successor may exist.
[hom-work-leisure-s3.8.1] Volunteering can be encouraged through training, recognition and repaying expenses, which helps to remove the out-of-pocket costs that put people off.
