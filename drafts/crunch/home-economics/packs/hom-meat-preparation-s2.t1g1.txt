TARGET hom-meat-preparation-s2: 2. Selection and storage
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.0.b] Meat spoils quickly, so the first job when buying is to judge how fresh it is. Your eyes, nose and fingers tell you most of what you need to know.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.0.0] Fresh red meat is a bright, even colour, firm and moist with a fresh smell and little drip in the pack. Grey, brown or green patches, a sticky or slimy surface or a sour smell mean the meat is spoiling.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.0.1] Fresh poultry has creamy, moist, unbroken skin and no strong smell, because broken skin and odours show bacteria are already at work.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.0.2] Fat should look white to creamy and firm, not yellow and soft.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.0.3] Lean cuts show little visible fat, so they suit people limiting fat. Marbling (fine streaks of fat within the muscle) in beef gives flavour and juiciness, which is why it adds to a cut's appeal.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.0.4] The shape and size of a cut should suit the way you plan to cook it.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.1.b] Where you buy meat and how you get it home both affect how safe it is, because meat that warms up gives bacteria the chance to multiply.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.1.0] A butcher cuts meat to order and gives advice. A supermarket sells prepacked meat with a wide choice, all labelled. Farm shops and markets are other sources.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.1.1] Check that the premises look clean, that meat is kept chilled and that raw and cooked meats are kept separate, so raw juices cannot contaminate cooked meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.1.2] Buy meat last on a shopping trip and carry it home in an insulated bag, so it stays chilled. Refrigerate or freeze it promptly when you get home.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.1.3] Buy frozen meat in solid, undamaged packs. Ice crystals inside, frost, thaw juice or freezer burn usually mean the meat has been stored badly or partly thawed, and you should not buy above the load line of the freezer cabinet.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.2.b] Loose meat is cut to order, but prepacked meat carries a label that tells you what you are buying and how to keep it safe. Reading it correctly is an easy source of marks.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.2.0] A prepacked meat label shows the name of the food, ingredients in descending order of weight (largest first) with allergens emphasised, the net quantity, a date mark, storage instructions, the name and address of the business, origin where required, and a nutrition declaration.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.2.1] A use-by date goes on highly perishable foods such as raw meat. It is a safety date, so the meat must not be eaten or used after it, because it may carry harmful bacteria even if it looks and smells fine.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.2.2] A best-before date, found on foods such as frozen or canned meat, is a quality date. After it the meat may be past its best but is not necessarily unsafe.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.2.3] Frozen and cooked meat products should also be kept to the storage instructions on the pack.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.3.b] Origin and traceability labelling lets a pack of meat be linked back to the animal and farm it came from, which matters when something goes wrong and meat has to be recalled.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.3.0] Beef labels show an animal reference code and the origin (the country of birth, rearing and slaughter, or one country) and the slaughterhouse. Origin labelling is also required for fresh, chilled and frozen pigmeat, sheep, goat and poultry under EU origin-labelling rules.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.3.1] The code and origin let meat be traced back to the farm of origin, so a problem can be found and the affected meat removed.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.4.b] At Higher Level you must name the law that sets out what a prepacked meat label has to contain.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.4.0] Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 sets the EU rules on food information, including ingredients listed in descending order of weight and allergens highlighted. It is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.5.b] If meat is faulty or unfit, you have legal rights, and State bodies enforce the rules that stop misleading claims and unsafe food.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.5.0] Faulty or unfit meat can be returned. The Consumer Rights Act 2022 gives rights for goods: they must be of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.5.1] The Consumer Protection Act 2007 prohibits misleading claims, including misleading food labelling, and is enforced by the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC).
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.5.2] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998 and food law prohibit unsafe food.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.b] Two shoppers can stand at the same meat counter and buy very different things. A list of influences, each with a meat example, answers this exam question well.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.0] Price and income: cheaper cuts such as shin, neck, mince, offal and chicken thighs or pieces cost less per portion than prime cuts such as fillet. Special offers, cost per portion, and bulk-buying with freezing also help a budget.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.1] Convenience, time and skills: ready-prepared, marinated and processed meats save time and cooking skill. The cooking equipment at home matters too, such as a cooker, microwave and freezer space, so a household without an oven may buy minced or ready-to-cook meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.2] Health: lean cuts, skinless poultry and low-fat, low-salt products suit people controlling fat, salt or weight. Red meat gives iron for those at risk of anaemia, and some people limit processed meat on advice.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.3] Family size, age and likes: portion sizes, children's preferences and tender meat for older people all matter. Religious and cultural rules affect choice, and vegetarians in the household change what is bought.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.4] Quality, appearance and freshness: colour, texture and smell are used to judge quality, and marbling in beef affects its appeal. Traceability and labelling also give confidence.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.5] Origin: many shoppers prefer Irish meat, and the Bord Bia Quality Mark signals assured Irish produce.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.6] Ethics and environment: animal welfare, organic, free-range and assured production, sustainability, food miles and packaging appeal to shoppers concerned about welfare and the environment.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.7] Packaging and portion size: small packs suit single people and reduce waste, while large family packs usually cost less per kilogram.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.6.8] Advertising, recipes, seasonality and in-store promotions influence choice and encourage shoppers to try new products.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.b] Shoppers cannot check on a farm for themselves, so Bord Bia does it for them. Its quality assurance schemes give buyers confidence that Irish meat meets agreed standards.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.0] Bord Bia is the Irish Food Board, the State agency that promotes Irish food and drink and runs quality assurance schemes. Its Quality Mark shows a product comes from an assured source.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.1] The farm-level scheme for beef and lamb is audited independently, and separate schemes exist for pigmeat and poultry. Meat processors are assured separately under a meat processor scheme, so meat is certified from farm through to processing.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.2] The standards cover animal welfare and care, regulated animal feed, competent herd management, records of veterinary medicines, livestock identification and traceability (each product can be traced back to its farm of origin), pest control, hygiene and the environment.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.3] Farms and processing plants are inspected to check compliance, and non-compliances must be corrected.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.4] Assured and non-assured product is kept apart in processing, so the assurance claim can be traced.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.7.5] Consumers gain confidence in quality, safety and welfare. Producers gain market access and sustainability credentials, and Bord Bia advises industry and government and promotes Irish meat to export markets.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.b] Meat is kept safe by controls at every stage, from the farm and abattoir to the shop. Many different bodies share the work.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.0] The Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine controls animal health, disease testing, veterinary controls on farms, residue (drug and banned-substance) testing, EU import rules, and approval and supervision of abattoirs.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.1] Veterinary inspectors examine animals before slaughter (ante-mortem inspection) and carcasses and organs after slaughter (post-mortem inspection) in approved abattoirs. Unfit meat is condemned.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.2] Residue testing checks meat for banned substances and for drug residues left by veterinary medicines. Hormonal growth promoters are banned in EU farm animals, and medicines have withdrawal periods before slaughter.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.3] Farmers record the medicines and feed they use. Animals are identified with ear tags and cattle passports, so meat is traceable from farm to pack.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.4] Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 requires food businesses to run HACCP-based food safety procedures (hygiene controls at the steps where hazards could arise). It is given effect in Ireland by the European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.8.5] The Bord Bia scheme adds independent assurance on top of these controls.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.9.b] At Higher Level you must know the national food safety body and the laws behind meat safety, and who actually carries out inspections.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.9.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) is the independent national food safety body, established under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998 to see that food is safe. It coordinates enforcement, advises and publishes guidance.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.9.1] The FSAI is not the inspector. Official veterinary staff, the HSE Environmental Health Service and local authorities carry out inspections of food premises under FSAI service contracts, alongside the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.9.2] Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, the general food law, prohibits placing unsafe food on the market and requires traceability one step back and one step forward in the supply chain.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.9.3] Regulation (EU) 2017/625, given effect by the European Union (Official Controls in Relation to Food Legislation) Regulations 2020, governs how food premises are inspected.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.b] Raw meat carries bacteria, and these multiply fast in warm conditions. Every storage rule exists to keep bacteria from growing or spreading.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.0] Keep raw meat in the refrigerator at 5 °C or below (the FSAI range is 0-5 °C), because bacteria multiply rapidly between 5 °C and 63 °C, the danger zone.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.1] Store raw meat wrapped or in a covered container on the lowest shelf, away from cooked and ready-to-eat food, so that juices cannot drip onto it.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.2] Use separate chopping boards and knives for raw meat, and wash hands and surfaces, to prevent cross-contamination (spreading bacteria from raw to ready-to-eat food).
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.3] Use raw meat before the use-by date on the pack, stored at 5 °C or below. If it has no date, cook or freeze it on the day of purchase.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.4] Minced meat, sausages and offal spoil faster than joints, because bacteria can spread through them over a larger surface area. Poultry often carries Salmonella and Campylobacter on its surface, which are hazards to handle with care.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.10.5] Vacuum-packed meat stays in its pack until needed and keeps longer unopened in the refrigerator, but once opened it must be treated as fresh.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.b] Freezing lets you keep meat longer, but only if it goes in fresh and is thawed safely. Cooked meat needs its own care because it is often eaten without further cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.0] Freeze fresh meat before its use-by date, in portions wrapped in moisture-proof packaging. Label and date it, and rotate stock so the oldest is used first.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.1] A freezer temperature of -18 °C or colder keeps meat safe, although quality declines the longer meat is kept frozen.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.2] Thaw frozen meat slowly in the refrigerator, on a tray or in a container on the bottom shelf, so juices cannot contaminate other food. Once thawed, cook it fully within the time on the pack.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.3] Do not refreeze raw thawed meat, because bacteria can multiply while it thaws. Cooked dishes made from it can be frozen.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.4] Cool cooked meat quickly, within about 1-2 hours in shallow containers, and refrigerate it promptly, because hot meat left to cool slowly allows bacteria to multiply.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.11.5] Reheat cooked meat until steaming hot, to a core temperature of 75 °C, and reheat it once only.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.b] Cold storage slows spoilage but does not stop it. Knowing what each method does and does not do explains why dates and handling rules still matter.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.0] Refrigeration slows the growth of micro-organisms and enzyme action but does not kill them, so meat still spoils eventually and use-by dates apply.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.1] Freezing inhibits enzyme activity and microbial growth but does not kill all micro-organisms, so they can regrow once the meat thaws.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.2] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals that damage and rupture cell walls, giving drip loss on thawing and a softer texture. Fast (blast) freezing at about -30 °C forms small crystals and causes less damage.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.3] Drip loss carries away extractives (flavour compounds) and soluble nutrients, mainly B vitamins, so the thawing liquid should be used in cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.4] Freezer burn leaves dry, grey-white or discoloured patches where air reaches the surface of the meat. Fat can also go rancid in poorly stored meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s2.12.5] Spoilage shows as slime, a sour smell or a colour change. Myoglobin (the red pigment in meat) oxidises to brown metmyoglobin on exposure to air, which is harmless early on but is a quality sign.

CANDIDATES hom-meat-preparation-s1: 1. Nutritional significance and structure of meat
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.b] Meat is not one food but three groups, and each differs in colour, fat and the nutrients it is valued for.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.0] Red meat is beef, lamb, pork and veal, the flesh of mammals, and it is the group most people mean by "meat".
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.1] Poultry is chicken, turkey and duck. The breast is paler than the leg or red meat because it contains less myoglobin (the red pigment that stores oxygen in muscle).
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.2] Poultry fat is lower than in most red meat, and most of it sits in and under the skin, so poultry is not fat free.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.3] Offal is the edible organs of the animal, such as liver, kidney and heart.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.4] Liver is the richest source of vitamin A, iron and several B vitamins among the offal, which is why offal is valued as cheap, nutrient-dense food.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.b] Nutritive value is about the nutrients meat contains. Meat is valued mainly for its protein, haem iron and B group vitamins, while its fat is mostly saturated and it supplies essentially no carbohydrate.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.0] Protein: meat is a high biological value (HBV) protein, meaning it supplies all the essential amino acids (those the body cannot make) in the proportions the body needs.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.1] Meat is about 20 to 30 per cent protein by weight.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.2] Protein supplies amino acids for growth and repair of body cells and for making enzymes, hormones and antibodies.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.3] Fat: meat fat is mainly saturated, and meat is about 10 to 30 per cent fat, varying with the animal, the cut and whether visible fat is trimmed. Chicken is lower in fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.4] Fat gives concentrated energy and insulation, and offal carries fat-soluble vitamins.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.5] Carbohydrate: meat contains essentially none, so it is served with a starchy food such as potatoes, pasta, rice or bread to supply energy and balance the meal.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.b] Besides its macronutrients, meat is an important source of iron and B vitamins, but it is deficient in vitamin C. Water and flavour compounds make up the rest of its character.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.0] Haem iron (the form of iron found in animal foods) is present in red meat, offal and the dark meat of poultry. The body absorbs it more readily than non-haem iron from plant foods.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.1] Iron is used to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood, and myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.2] Zinc supports enzyme activity, potassium supports muscle function, and phosphorus supports bones, teeth and energy release. Sulphur is present in the sulphur-containing amino acids.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.3] Sodium is high only in processed meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.4] B group vitamins help the body release energy from food. Cobalamin (B12) occurs naturally only in animal foods.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.5] Vitamins A and D are found mainly in liver. Meat is deficient in vitamin C (it is not a source), so serve it with vegetables or fruit.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.6] Water makes up about 50 to 60 per cent of meat. Raw lean meat is about 70 to 75 per cent water, so the figure varies with fat content and cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.7] Extractives are flavour compounds that dissolve out during cooking. They give meat its savoury taste and stimulate digestive juices.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.b] Dietetic value is about how suitable meat is for different people. Some groups gain a great deal from it, while others need to limit it.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.0] Growing children and teenagers need meat protein for growth, and adults need it for repair and maintenance of tissue.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.1] Haem iron is especially important for pregnant women, menstruating women (including teenage girls), children and teenagers, to prevent iron-deficiency anaemia.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.2] Older people and anyone with a poor appetite do well on tender, lean or minced cuts cooked gently, because tough or overcooked meat is harder to chew and digest.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.3] People with raised cholesterol or coronary heart disease, and anyone controlling weight, should limit saturated fat and cholesterol. They choose lean cuts, trim visible fat, remove poultry skin, grill rather than fry and limit processed meats.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.4] Low-income households find cheaper cuts and mince good value protein and iron.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.5] Vegetarians and vegans do not eat meat, so they need other sources of protein of similar quality, iron and B12.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.6] Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and the Irish Food Pyramid place meat in one shelf with poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts. They recommend lean and unprocessed choices and advise limiting red and processed meat because of their salt and saturated fat content.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.b] Meat is muscle, and its structure explains how it feels to eat and why some cuts are tougher than others. Muscle is built from fibres, connective tissue and fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.0] Muscle is made of long, thin fibres, each a single muscle cell, lying parallel and running in the direction of the muscle's pull.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.1] Groups of fibres form bundles, and the bundles are held together by connective tissue.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.2] Each fibre contains myofibrils made of the proteins actin and myosin, the contractile proteins, which slide past each other to shorten the muscle. The fibre is surrounded by muscle fluid containing water, extractives, soluble protein, vitamins and minerals.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.3] Connective tissue wraps fibres and bundles and attaches muscle to bone through tendons. It contains collagen (white and tough) and elastin (yellow and stretchy).
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.4] Fat is found as marbling between and within the muscle fibres, as a layer under the skin or as a covering layer, and around the organs. Marbling adds juiciness and flavour, and fat melts on cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.5] A tougher cut has longer, thicker fibres and more connective tissue.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.6] Diagram: draw a cross-section of muscle showing a bundle of fibres inside a sheath of connective tissue, with one fibre enlarged to show myofibrils (actin and myosin) and a label for fat (marbling). Label connective tissue, fibre, bundle and fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.b] Meat contains several different proteins. Some do the work of the muscle, some carry colour and some form connective tissue, and cooking affects each differently.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of the myofibrils and make up most of the fibre protein. They coagulate (set solid) and firm up on heating.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.1] Globulin is a soluble protein in the muscle fluid. It coagulates on heating and is partly lost in drip and cooking juices.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.2] Myoglobin is the red oxygen-storing pigment of muscle and gives raw meat its colour. It denatures (changes shape) on heating, so the meat changes from red to brown or grey.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.3] Collagen is an insoluble connective tissue protein that is tough when raw. It converts to soluble gelatine in slow moist cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.4] Elastin is a springy connective tissue protein in tendons and ligaments. It is very tough and cooking does not soften it, so it is removed or the meat is minced.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s6: 6. Safe food preparation
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.b] Almost every rule for handling food comes from two facts about food poisoning bacteria: they multiply fastest in warm food, and they travel easily from one food to another. Knowing these makes the individual rules easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.0] Food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest in the danger zone, 5°C to 63°C. This is why safe preparation keeps food cold, hot, or moving quickly through that range.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.1] Cross-contamination is the transfer of bacteria from raw food to cooked or ready-to-eat food, directly or by hands, chopping boards, cloths, utensils or meat juices. This is why raw and cooked foods are kept apart.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.2] Freezing does not kill bacteria, it only stops them multiplying, and thawing lets them start multiplying again. So frozen food must be handled with care once it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.b] Storage aims to keep food cold or frozen so bacteria cannot multiply, and to stop bacteria passing from one food to another. Short questions usually ask for three to six storage guidelines.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.0] Cover all food and store it in sealed containers, which prevents drying out, odour transfer and cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.1] Keep perishable food (food that spoils quickly) in the fridge at 5°C or below, which slows the growth of bacteria. Check the fridge and freezer temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.2] Store raw meat, poultry and fish on the lowest shelf, on a plate and loosely covered with the shop wrapping removed, so that juices cannot drip onto cooked or ready-to-eat food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.3] Keep raw and cooked foods apart, covered or in sealed containers, so bacteria cannot pass between them.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.4] Cool cooked food quickly before refrigerating or freezing it, and do not overpack the fridge, so that cold air can circulate around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.5] Check the use-by or best-before date of each food and follow the storage instructions on its label. Use-by is a safety date for highly perishable food, while best-before is a quality date.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.6] Remove fruit and vegetables from plastic wrapping, which encourages mould, and check them regularly for mould.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.7] Store dry foods in a cool, dry, well-ventilated cupboard, in vermin-proof containers, and keep storage areas clean.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.8] Keep perishable food out of the fridge for the shortest possible time while it is being prepared, because food left in warm air soon enters the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.b] Freezing keeps food safe for a long time, but only if it is frozen and thawed properly. Bacteria are not killed by freezing, so the care taken when thawing matters.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.0] Freeze food quickly at −25°C, then store it at −18°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.1] Defrost frozen food completely before cooking, preferably in the fridge on the bottom shelf, so that it thaws slowly and any drips cannot reach other food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.2] Never refreeze food that has thawed, because bacteria multiply while it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.b] Cooking and reheating must heat food right through to kill bacteria, and hot food must then stay hot or be eaten promptly. Otherwise bacteria multiply again in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.0] Cook food thoroughly, using the right time and temperature, and turn or stir it so that heat reaches the centre. Cook meat, poultry and minced meat right through, to a core temperature of at least 72°C for poultry.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.1] Reheat food quickly until it is above 100°C throughout, and reheat it only once.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.2] High-risk foods such as stews and gravies should be brought to boiling when reheated, because their moist, protein-rich nature suits bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.3] Cover food when reheating. In a microwave, stir or turn the food and allow standing time, so that heat reaches all parts evenly, and follow the appliance's instructions.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.4] Serve cooked food immediately. If holding it hot, keep it above 63°C, because bacteria multiply rapidly between 5°C and 63°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.5] Eat food that has been held hot within about 90 minutes (1.5 hours).
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.6] Do not keep food in the danger zone. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate them and use them soon.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.b] People carry food poisoning bacteria on their hands, hair, skin and clothes, so the person preparing food is a major source of contamination. Examiners usually ask for four to six personal hygiene points.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.0] Wash hands in hot water with liquid soap, and a nailbrush for the nails, before handling food, because hands carry food poisoning bacteria. Dry them on a clean towel or paper towel.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.1] Wash hands after the toilet, sneezing or coughing, changing nappies, handling pets, handling rubbish and handling raw meat.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.2] Keep nails short and clean and remove jewellery before preparing food, because dirt and bacteria collect under nails and around jewellery.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.3] Tie hair back or cover it. Wear a clean apron or clothing, and change it if it becomes soiled.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.4] Cover cuts, boils and sores with a brightly coloured waterproof plaster, so that infection cannot pass to food and the plaster can be seen if it falls into the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.5] Do not cough or sneeze over food, and do not taste food with your fingers or lick your fingers.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.6] Do not prepare or serve food for others while suffering from diarrhoea or vomiting, because the illness can pass to the people eating it.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.b] A clean kitchen with sensible habits stops bacteria building up on surfaces and equipment and passing to food. Four kitchen hygiene practices is a common exam question.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.0] Clean and disinfect work surfaces often, and wash equipment in hot water and detergent. Clean as you go.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.1] Use colour-coded chopping boards and separate utensils for raw and cooked foods, to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.2] Never prepare raw and cooked foods on the same surface.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.3] Handle food as little as possible during preparation, and keep it covered, because every touch can pass on bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.4] Floors, walls and work surfaces should be easy to clean, and the kitchen should have good lighting.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.5] Effective ventilation reduces condensation, which encourages the growth of micro-organisms.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.6] The kitchen should have a clean water supply and no access for pets or pests.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.7] Keep a covered waste bin and empty it regularly, and change clean cloths and dishtowels often.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s3: 3. Micro-organisms and the control of microbial spoilage
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.b] Spoilage is any change that makes food unfit or unpleasant to eat, such as an off smell, taste, colour or texture. Several causes are at work, but micro-organisms are the main one.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.0] Micro-organisms (bacteria, moulds and yeasts) are the main cause of spoilage, and they are the cause this subsection deals with.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.1] Enzyme action (the food's own chemicals continuing to work) causes changes such as browning and over-ripening.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.2] Physical damage and bruising break the surface of food, which lets other spoilage in.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.3] Moisture loss makes food shrink, wrinkle or harden, for example a dried-out piece of fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.4] Rancidity is the going-off of fats, which gives them an unpleasant smell and taste.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.5] Chemical contamination, for example by pesticides, antibiotics or metals, spoils food and can make it unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.6] Spoilage micro-organisms mainly make food unpleasant, so spoiled food is not always unsafe. Pathogens (disease-causing micro-organisms) can make food unsafe even when it looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.b] Bacteria are the micro-organisms that spoil most protein and moist foods, and they change food in several ways.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.0] Putrefaction: bacteria break down protein in meat, fish and eggs, producing foul odours and a slimy surface.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.1] Souring: lactic acid bacteria ferment the lactose (milk sugar) in milk, producing lactic acid that curdles the milk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.2] Bacteria that survive in food can produce toxins (poisons), which can make food unsafe even when the food looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.3] The foods at greatest risk are high-moisture, high-protein foods such as meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk and cooked rice, because bacteria find both water and nutrients in them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.b] Moulds and yeasts spoil foods that bacteria find harder to use, especially acidic and sugary ones.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.0] Moulds grow visibly on food as fluffy, powdery or coloured patches, for example on bread, cheese, fruit and jam, and some produce mycotoxins (poisons made by moulds) that are harmful if eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.1] Mould spores (tiny reproductive cells) spread through the air, which is how they land on food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.2] Yeasts ferment the sugars in fruit juices, jams, syrups and fruit, producing carbon dioxide, alcohol and off-flavours, and sometimes bubbling or swollen containers.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.3] Mould and yeast growth is mostly on the surface of food, where oxygen is available, but moulds also grow through open-textured foods such as bread.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.4] Acidic, sugary foods favour moulds and yeasts because they tolerate acid better than most bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.b] Micro-organisms spoil food only when the conditions they need are present. Remove or change one of them and growth slows or stops.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.0] Food: micro-organisms need nutrients, particularly protein and carbohydrate, as a source of energy and for building new cells.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.1] Moisture: their chemical reactions and nutrient absorption take place in solution, so dry foods support little growth and high-moisture foods are high risk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.2] Warmth: most spoilage and poisoning micro-organisms multiply fastest in the danger zone (5 to 63 °C), while cold slows growth, freezing stops it without killing them, and heat kills them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.3] Oxygen: aerobic micro-organisms such as moulds need oxygen, while anaerobic bacteria grow in sealed packs from which oxygen is absent, and facultative ones can adapt to either.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.4] pH (how acid or alkaline food is): most bacteria prefer a near-neutral pH, whereas moulds and yeasts tolerate acid. Lactic acid bacteria are an exception because they tolerate acid, but strongly acidic foods are generally safer.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.5] Time: micro-organisms need time to multiply, and some bacteria can double in number in about 20 minutes under ideal conditions (binary fission, where one cell divides into two).
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.b] Control of spoilage works either by destroying micro-organisms, keeping them away from food, or restricting one of the conditions they need. The first two principles deal with the micro-organisms themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.0] Heat treatments kill micro-organisms: cooking, pasteurisation, which uses a lower temperature for a set time, and sterilisation, which destroys all micro-organisms and their spores. Canning and bottling also use heat.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.1] Irradiation destroys micro-organisms and parasites in food by exposing it to controlled radiation, without making the food radioactive.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.2] Irradiation can extend shelf life and slow ripening and sprouting in fruit and vegetables, with little effect on colour, flavour or texture.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.3] Preventing micro-organisms reaching the food in the first place uses hygiene, clean equipment, covering food, sealed packaging, sterile containers and separating raw and cooked food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.b] The other principles leave micro-organisms alive but take away something they need. Most foods use more than one, and applying each to a named food is the usual exam task.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.0] Low temperature: refrigeration slows the growth of micro-organisms, and freezing inactivates them without destroying them, so growth resumes on thawing.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.1] Removing moisture: drying, salting, sugaring and concentrating reduce the water available to micro-organisms. Dissolved salt or sugar draws water out of microbial cells by osmosis, which dehydrates them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.2] Acidity: pickling in vinegar, or adding lemon juice, lowers the pH to a level that most bacteria cannot tolerate. Chutney and fermentation acids work the same way.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.3] Removing oxygen: vacuum packing and canning exclude air, preventing growth of aerobic micro-organisms, and modified-atmosphere packs replace the air around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.4] Preservatives: chemicals such as sulphur dioxide, benzoates and nitrites inhibit the growth of micro-organisms. Natural preservatives are salt, sugar, vinegar, alcohol and smoke.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.5] Combining principles: jam, for example, uses heat, sugar, acid and sealing together, so several principles protect the same food.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s8: 8. National agencies in food safety
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.b] Consumers cannot see how hygienically their food was produced, so the state sets up bodies to check it on their behalf. In Ireland one central body coordinates the system and other bodies do the inspecting.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.0] National agencies protect consumers by setting and enforcing food safety standards, inspecting food businesses, testing food and acting when food is unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.1] Food businesses must be monitored independently, because a business cannot be relied on to judge its own hygiene and customers have no way of checking it themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.2] An outbreak or contamination can cross counties and countries, so a national system is needed to trace it and respond quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.3] The Irish system has one central body, the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), which coordinates, and a group of official agencies that carry out the inspecting.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.b] The FSAI is the state body at the centre of food safety in Ireland. It sets the direction and coordinates the work, while others carry out most of the inspections.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) is the state body responsible for food safety in Ireland, established under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.1] The FSAI operates under the Department of Health, the government department responsible for health policy. The FSAI advises the Minister on food safety, but the Department itself does not inspect food premises.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.2] It co-ordinates the enforcement of food safety legislation in Ireland, working through the official agencies, and it audits their work rather than inspecting most premises itself.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.3] It protects consumers by ensuring that food is safe to eat and that food information is accurate.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.4] It works with food businesses to make sure food is produced safely, for example through guidance and training on hygiene and HACCP systems (the system of identifying and controlling hazards in food production).
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.5] It takes action, through the agencies, when a food premises is in breach of food regulations, because it is the agencies that can inspect and enforce on the ground.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.b] Beyond coordinating enforcement, the FSAI informs the public, handles emergencies and processes applications for new food products. It has one clear limit: it is about safety, not promotion.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.0] It gives consumers and food businesses authoritative advice and publishes food safety information, so that both groups can rely on one trusted source.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.1] It carries out scientific work, which gives its advice and decisions an evidence base.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.2] It processes applications for novel foods and for new methods of food processing, so that new products are assessed before they reach consumers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.3] It also handles food alerts, product withdrawals and recalls, works with the agencies on traceability and leads on food incidents.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.4] It does not promote Irish food at home or abroad; that is the role of Bord Bia (the Irish Food Board), which is why an exam statement that the FSAI promotes Irish food is false.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.b] The FSAI does not carry out most food controls itself. Official agencies do this work under service contracts with the FSAI, which means the FSAI can coordinate one national system without employing every inspector.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.0] The official agencies are the Health Service Executive (HSE), the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority and the local authorities.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.1] Official agencies carry out inspections, sampling and analysis of food in the places they cover, and they enforce the rules on the FSAI's behalf.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.2] The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority carries out the official food safety controls on fish and shellfish, from production and landing through processing to distribution, but not retail.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.3] Local authorities carry out food controls under contract to the FSAI in their own areas, mainly veterinary inspections of food premises that handle meat and other animal products.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.b] The HSE is the national health service, and its Environmental Health Service does most of the day-to-day inspection of food premises. Its staff are environmental health officers, who work for the HSE and not for the FSAI.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.0] Environmental health officers (EHOs), working in the HSE's Environmental Health Service, carry out the inspections of food premises, including restaurants, cafés, shops, hospital kitchens, manufacturers and wholesalers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.1] The service registers new food premises, so that every business handling food is known to the authorities before it starts trading.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.2] It inspects food premises and farms.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.3] It handles hygiene complaints and issues closure orders and enforcement notices where food premises are unsafe, which stops a risky business from continuing to sell food.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.4] It ensures food safety at public gatherings and events.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.5] It monitors compliance with food, labelling, additives and contaminants legislation, and takes samples of food for testing.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.6] It responds to rapid alerts and recalls, and it investigates food poisoning outbreaks.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.7] It educates and advises food businesses on compliance with the law.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.b] Inspectors can see a kitchen, but only a laboratory can tell what is in a food. The Public Analyst Laboratories provide that testing, and they analyse rather than inspect.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.0] The Public Analyst Laboratories are run by the HSE.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.1] They analyse food and water, including samples taken by environmental health officers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.2] They test food for compliance with food law and labelling law, for example its composition, additives, contaminants and microbiological quality (the bacteria in it).
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.3] They test samples submitted by members of the public, for a fee.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.4] Their results help ensure that food sold to consumers is safe and wholesome, and they give the evidence needed for legal action.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.b] This Department looks after food at the farm end of the food chain, where animals and animal products come from. Its controls follow food from the farm to the shop.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.0] It monitors food safety in meat and meat products, milk and milk products, and eggs and egg products.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.1] It monitors the use of pesticides in food production, so that residues (traces left on or in food) do not reach consumers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.2] It enforces EU controls on the import of animal-based foods from countries outside the EU.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.3] It is responsible for the registration and traceability of cattle, pigs and sheep, so that an animal can be traced if a problem is found.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.4] It ensures correct hygiene procedures are followed from the dairy farm, to the dairy, through to the retailer and the consumer.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.b] Because the inspecting is shared between several bodies, they need common ways of tracking and removing unsafe food. The FSAI coordinates all of these.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.0] The FSAI and the official agencies work together on inspections, sampling, alerts, product withdrawals and recalls.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.1] Traceability allows an unsafe product to be traced back to its supplier (one step back) and forward to the businesses that received it (one step forward).
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.2] A product withdrawal removes a product from the supply chain, whereas a product recall also collects it from consumers by asking them to return it.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.3] Rapid alerts pass warnings about unsafe food quickly to the agencies that must act on them, so that action can be taken before more people are harmed.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s9: 9. Food legislation
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.b] Food law protects consumers by making sure food is safe and honestly described. It comes from two sources, the EU and Ireland, and the two work together.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.0] An EU regulation applies directly in every member state, so it is not an Irish law. Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 and Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 are EU regulations, not Irish Acts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.1] Ireland also makes national regulations (called statutory instruments) to give an EU regulation effect, which means they set out how it is enforced and which bodies enforce it.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.2] The Oireachtas (the Irish parliament) passes Acts, such as the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.3] The purpose of all of this law is consumer protection: food that is safe to eat and described honestly.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.b] Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs is an EU regulation, which applies directly in every member state. It lays down hygiene rules for food businesses at all stages after primary production (the growing or rearing of the raw food). Ireland gives effect to it through national regulations.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.0] The European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006, amended in 2009 and 2010, give effect to Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 in Ireland.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.1] The Regulations set hygiene standards for food premises, equipment, food handling, water supply, waste and the personal hygiene of food handlers, so that every step where food could be contaminated is covered.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.2] They require food businesses to put in place and operate a food safety system based on HACCP (a system that finds the points where food could become unsafe and controls them).
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.3] They require staff to be trained and supervised in food hygiene and to follow hygiene procedures, so that the people handling food know how to prevent contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.4] They require food premises to be registered with the relevant competent authority (the official body in charge of checking them).
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.5] They permit authorised officers, for example environmental health officers, to inspect premises, and to close them or have food destroyed where public health is immediately threatened.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.6] How this protects the consumer: food is prepared in clean premises by trained staff under a system that prevents contamination, and unfit food can be taken off sale.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.b] Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on the provision of food information to consumers is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014. It applies from 13 December 2014, and the nutrition declaration from 13 December 2016. It lists the information that must appear on pre-packed food, so that shoppers can choose safely.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.0] A label must give the name of the food and a list of ingredients in descending order of weight at the time of manufacture, so the main ingredient comes first.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.1] Fourteen named allergens must be emphasised wherever they appear in the list of ingredients, so that people with an allergy can spot them at once.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.2] A label must give the net quantity of the food, and any special storage conditions or instructions for use where needed.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.3] A label must give the name and address of the food business operator, so the food can be traced to a named business.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.4] Country of origin or place of provenance must be given where leaving it out would mislead the consumer, and for certain meats and fresh produce.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.5] Drinks over 1.2% alcohol must show their alcoholic strength.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.6] A nutrition declaration gives the energy value, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt of the food.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.7] Labelling must not mislead the consumer about the nature, identity, properties, origin or method of production of the food. Nutrition and health claims are regulated separately.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.b] Two parts of the label matter most for safety: the allergens and the date mark. Both help the consumer avoid harm, but they work in different ways.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.0] The 14 allergens that must be emphasised are cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above a set level), lupin and molluscs.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.1] A use-by date must appear on highly perishable food, because such food can become unsafe to eat. After this date the food must not be eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.2] A best-before date (the date of minimum durability) shows the date until which the food keeps its quality, so food past this date is a matter of quality, not safety.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.b] Alongside the rules on hygiene and labelling, there is a general framework that sets the principles for all food law. In Ireland this is the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998. Across the EU it is Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, known as general food law.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998 provides the Irish legal framework for food safety. It set up the FSAI and gave it the functions of coordinating food safety law and its enforcement.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.1] The 1998 Act also names the official agencies that carry out controls under service contracts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.2] Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 sets the general principles and requirements of food law across the EU.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.3] Under general food law, food that is unsafe must not be placed on the market, and responsibility for safety rests with the food business.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.4] Food businesses must be able to trace food one step back to their supplier and one step forward to their customers, so that unsafe food can be found and withdrawn quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.5] General food law set up the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.6] Risk analysis under general food law has three parts: risk assessment (scientific, carried out by EFSA), risk management (decisions by the authorities) and risk communication. The precautionary principle may apply where the science is uncertain.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.7] The result is a common high level of protection of health and consumer interests from farm to fork, with fast withdrawal of unsafe food.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.b] Rules only protect consumers if someone checks that businesses follow them. Regulation (EU) 2017/625 sets out the rules for official controls, which are checks by public authorities that food businesses comply with food law at every stage of the food chain.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.0] Regulation (EU) 2017/625 is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Official Controls in Relation to Food Legislation) Regulations 2020.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.1] Official controls include inspection, sampling and analysis, and audit (a formal check of a business's records and systems).
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.2] Official controls are regular and risk-based, so that higher-risk businesses are checked more often. They are carried out without prior warning in most cases.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.3] Inspectors can take samples of food for testing as part of an official control.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.4] In Ireland the FSAI is the competent authority with overall responsibility. The official agencies (HSE, DAFM, SFPA and local authorities) carry out controls on its behalf under service contracts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.5] How this protects the consumer: independent checks on all food businesses, with enforcement action such as improvement notices, closure and withdrawal where the law is broken.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s1: 1. Consumer choices
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.b] Every purchase starts with how much a household can spend. Income sets the limit, and it also decides whether a household buys what it needs or what it merely wants.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.0] Household income sets the limit on spending, so a household on a tight budget compares unit prices (the price per kilo or per item), buys own-brand and bulk items, and delays non-essential purchases.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.1] Disposable income is the money left after tax and essential bills. It decides how much is free for discretionary spending (spending that is a choice, not a necessity) such as eating out or branded clothing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.2] When income rises, households often trade up to branded or higher-quality goods; when it falls, they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.3] Essential needs such as food, heating and shelter are bought first, and luxury wants are the first to be cut when income falls.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.4] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income. A wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.5] Credit and easy-pay arrangements can allow spending above current income, and the debt they create reduces future choice because part of later income is already committed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.b] Beyond money, choices reflect who the buyer is, who influences them and what the product offers. For a large purchase such as a washing machine, buyers weigh several of these together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.0] Personal preference, and values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethical concerns, and cultural or religious practice), all influence what is bought.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.1] Household composition narrows choice: family size, life-cycle stage and special diets, such as a coeliac or diabetic member or an allergy, determine which goods are suitable.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.2] Time pressure pushes busy households towards convenience foods, takeaway meals and online shopping, because these save time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.3] Other people influence choice through word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.4] For a large purchase, buyers weigh the energy rating, reliability of the brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.5] The environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor that some consumers weigh when choosing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.6] Impulse buying is an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout. It results from several of the factors above, such as advertising, values and income, working together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.b] Shoppers often choose by name rather than by comparing every product. Brands and own-brand goods are the two main kinds of product on the shelf, and they differ in price and in how they are promoted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.0] A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product and distinguishes it from competing goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.1] Branded goods are made by a manufacturer and are usually advertised nationally, so they tend to cost more than equivalent goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.2] Own-brand goods are sold under the retailer's own name. They are often cheaper because the retailer avoids the cost of national advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.3] Brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand, which saves the shopper the effort of comparing alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.b] Advertising is communication that informs consumers about goods and services and tries to create a want for them. It happens mostly outside the shop, in the media a consumer meets every day.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.0] Advertising makes people aware of new products and builds brand loyalty, so that consumers ask for the product by name.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.1] It also informs consumers about features, price and offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.2] Media used include television, radio, print, outdoor billboards, websites, social media, and sponsorship of sport and events.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.3] Celebrity endorsement transfers the image or popularity of a well-known person to the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.4] Product differentiation: advertising highlights a feature, such as a special ingredient, that sets one brand apart from similar goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.5] Advertising can make people feel they need products they cannot afford, and it can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.6] Advertising aimed at children and teenagers is especially influential. It can prompt impulse purchases and family purchases, because young consumers have less experience of judging value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.7] The advantage to the consumer is awareness of choice and information. The disadvantages are that the cost of advertising is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, and there is pressure to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.b] Merchandising is the arrangement and presentation of goods in a shop to encourage consumers to buy more, and to buy more often. It is what the retailer does inside the shop, in contrast to advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.0] Store layout: goods are arranged in clearly identifiable departments with space to browse, and essentials such as bread and milk are placed at the back so that shoppers pass other products on the way to them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.1] Route design: aisles are arranged to keep shoppers moving through the store, so that they see more products and spend longer browsing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.2] Product placement: complementary goods are shelved together, such as biscuits beside tea, so that buying one reminds the shopper of the other. Sweets and magazines are placed at the checkout as impulse items.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.3] Shelf position: premium or luxury goods are placed at eye level, where they are seen first, while cheaper products are often placed on lower or higher shelves.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.4] Special offers displayed at the end of aisles draw attention to promotional stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.5] In-store stimuli: background music that slows the pace of shopping, aromas such as freshly baked bread, good lighting and warmth, and colourful displays.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.6] Sampling and demonstrations let shoppers taste or try a product before buying it, which makes a purchase more likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.b] Pricing techniques make a price look lower, or make a purchase look more valuable, than it is. Discount offers and discount stores do lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.0] Charm pricing: a price set just below a round figure, so that the item appears cheaper than it is.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.1] Multi-buy offers, such as three items for a set price, encourage consumers to buy more than they would at a single-item price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.2] Clearance and reduction labels suggest extra value and can prompt impulse buying of goods the shopper did not plan to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.3] Loss leaders: a well-known product is sold at or below cost to draw shoppers into the store, who then buy other items at normal prices.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.4] Coupons and money-off vouchers reward a specific purchase and are often distributed through loyalty schemes or advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.5] Discount offers, and discount stores, lower the cost of goods, but a lower price is not always better value, so the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.b] Loyalty schemes reward repeat custom with points or discounts, and give retailers information about what customers buy. They are one of the shopping systems named in the syllabus.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.0] Customers earn points or tokens for each amount spent. The points are exchanged for money off goods, vouchers or goods after a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.1] Examples in Ireland include the Tesco Clubcard and the Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.2] Schemes encourage saving for special occasions, such as Christmas, by building up points over time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.3] Shoppers may spend more to reach a reward target, and points can take priority over price when they compare shops, so they return to the same shop.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.4] Card use gives retailers data on buying patterns, which helps them target offers and plan stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.5] Disadvantages: the scheme discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned purchases, and ties the shopper to one shop. It also requires the card to be carried.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.b] Easy-pay arrangements spread the cost of a purchase over time, so goods can be bought without paying the full price at once. This is a form of credit, which has both benefits and costs.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.0] Easy-pay schemes: a retailer or utility company lets the consumer pay for goods or a bill in instalments over a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.1] Hire purchase: the consumer uses the goods while paying instalments under an agreement, and ownership passes only when the final instalment is paid.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.2] Hire purchase is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of the credit), and gives the consumer a cooling-off period of ten days.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.3] Advantages of credit: goods can be used immediately, the cost is spread over time, larger items become affordable, and essentials can be bought in an emergency when cash is not available.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.4] Disadvantages: the total cost is higher than the cash price because of interest and charges, easy-pay can encourage buying of non-essential goods and overspending, and the debt reduces future choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.b] Both allow shopping from home, but the consumer cannot inspect the goods before buying them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.0] Television buying: goods are demonstrated on screen and ordered by phone or online, often with a limited-time offer that creates urgency.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.1] Mail order: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or website and delivered by post or courier.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.2] Advantages of mail order: it is convenient for people without time to shop, a wide range is available, there is no travel, and orders can be placed by post or phone.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.3] Disadvantages of mail order: goods cannot be seen, touched or tried on, delivery can take weeks, and postage adds to the cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.4] Distance purchases (those made without meeting the seller) carry a statutory right to cancel within a cooling-off period under the Consumer Rights Act 2022, because the buyer could not see the goods before ordering.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.b] Online shopping offers convenience and easy price comparison, but adds risks around payment, delivery and personal data.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.0] Advantages: access at any hour from home, easy comparison of prices across retailers, a wider range, customer reviews, and delivery to the door.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.1] Disadvantages: goods cannot be seen or handled before purchase, and delivery can be delayed or carry extra charges.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.2] Payment risk: card details can be taken through phishing emails (fake messages that trick people into giving details) or fake websites, so pay only on secure sites that show a padlock symbol and an address beginning https.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.3] Keep records of the order, so that there is proof of what was bought and paid for if something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.4] Overspending risk: stored card details and one-click ordering make impulse purchases quick and easy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.5] Retailers collect data on browsing and buying, which can be used to target advertising at the individual consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.b] Packaging does more than wrap a product. It protects the goods, carries information, and helps to sell the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.0] Packaging protects and preserves the product, so food stays safe and goods are not damaged on the way to the shop or home.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.1] It informs the shopper, because the pack carries the name, instructions and other details.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.2] It attracts attention on the shelf and conveys quality through colour, shape and design.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.3] It gives convenience, such as portion size and easy-open features, and it suggests quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.4] Care labels and safety or hazard symbols on non-food goods, for example on household cleaning agents, explain how to use the product and prevent accidents.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.b] Labels on pre-packed foods are a legal requirement, so shoppers can choose safely and compare products. The detail is kept short here and is consumer-focused.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.0] Mandatory food labels show the name of the food, ingredients listed in descending order of weight (the heaviest first), net quantity, a use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and instructions for use, and the name and address of the food business.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.1] Use-by dates apply to highly perishable foods and the food is unsafe after the date; best-before dates indicate quality, and the food may still be safe after them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.2] The 14 major allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold or capitals, so that people with allergies can spot them quickly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.3] A nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. The per 100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory, while the per-portion figure is optional.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.4] The country of origin must be shown where the law requires it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.5] Claims such as low fat, light or high fibre may be made only where the product meets defined criteria.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.6] Quality marks and symbols help consumers choose. The Bord Bia Quality Mark for Irish food shows that a product meets a defined standard, and the gluten-free symbol and allergen emphasis help people with special diets.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.7] EU food information rules are set out in Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
