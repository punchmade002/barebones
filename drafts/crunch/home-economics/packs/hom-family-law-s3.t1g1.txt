TARGET hom-family-law-s3: 3. Barring orders under the Domestic Violence Act 2018
[hom-family-law-s3.0.b] The Domestic Violence Act 2018 gives people legal protection from domestic violence and abuse. It lets a court make orders that keep an abuser away from the person they are hurting, and in some cases out of the home.
[hom-family-law-s3.0.0] The Act came into operation on 1 January 2019 and replaced the earlier domestic violence law with a single, modern code.
[hom-family-law-s3.0.1] Domestic violence under the Act includes physical, sexual and emotional or psychological abuse and threats, so the protection is not limited to physical attacks.
[hom-family-law-s3.0.2] The person who applies for an order is the applicant, and the person the order is made against is the respondent.
[hom-family-law-s3.0.3] Applications are normally made to the District Court, the local court that deals with most family matters.
[hom-family-law-s3.0.4] The court can act urgently, including without the respondent being present, where there is an immediate risk, because waiting for a full hearing could leave the applicant in danger.
[hom-family-law-s3.0.5] Each order is made in response to a specific application, and the court can attach conditions to the order.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.b] The barring order is the order that removes the abuser from the home. Because it affects who may live in a property, it is open to fewer people than the other orders.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.0] A barring order requires the respondent to leave the home they share with the applicant (the home the applicant lives in) and to stay away from it.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.1] It lasts for the period the court sets, which is up to three years, and it can be renewed. The court may set a shorter period.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.2] A spouse or civil partner can apply for a barring order, and they do not need to meet the property-interest condition that applies to cohabitants and parents.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.3] A cohabitant (a partner living with the respondent in an intimate relationship) can apply, as can a parent against a respondent of full age who is not a dependent person, such as a non-dependent adult child living in the home.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.4] For cohabitants and parents, the applicant's interest in the home (such as owning or renting it) must not be less than the respondent's. The court cannot make the order if the respondent has an interest and the applicant has none or a lesser one.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.5] This property-interest condition is what keeps barring orders narrower than the other orders.
[hom-family-law-s3.1.6] A barring order protects the applicant, and any dependent children, from further abuse in their own home. It removes the respondent temporarily and does not transfer ownership of the home or end a marriage.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.b] Not everyone who needs protection can get a barring order, and not every case needs the respondent removed from the home. Safety and protection orders protect the applicant while the respondent can stay at home.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.0] A safety order is a longer-term order that prohibits the respondent from using or threatening violence, from molesting or putting the applicant in fear, and from watching or following them.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.1] A safety order does not remove the respondent from the home, so it suits cases where the applicant only needs protection from violence, threats and following.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.2] Safety orders are open to the widest group, including people who have lived together, parents who have a child together but never lived together, parents against an adult child, and adults living together in a non-contractual relationship such as relatives.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.3] A protection order is a temporary order with the same effect as a safety order, made while an application for a safety order or barring order is waiting to be heard.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.4] A protection order protects the applicant in the meantime, so it can be granted quickly, even before the respondent has been heard.
[hom-family-law-s3.2.5] A protection order is available to anyone who could apply for a safety order or barring order.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.b] Sometimes the danger is too urgent to wait for a full barring order hearing. Two short-term versions of the barring order deal with these cases.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.0] An interim barring order is a short-term barring order made where there is an immediate risk of significant harm to the applicant or a dependent person, and a protection order would not be enough to protect them.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.1] An interim barring order lasts until the application for the full barring order is decided, so it covers the gap before the full hearing.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.2] An emergency barring order is for a person who lived with the respondent but could not get a barring order because they have a lesser interest in the home, or none.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.3] Examples are an unmarried partner without ownership of the home, or a parent of an adult respondent.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.4] The test for harm is the same as for the interim barring order: an immediate risk of significant harm to the applicant or a dependent person.
[hom-family-law-s3.3.5] An emergency barring order lasts a very short period, up to eight working days.
[hom-family-law-s3.4.b] Five orders can be made under the Act, and the quickest way to keep them apart is to ask two questions: does the respondent have to leave, and is the order urgent or long-term?
[hom-family-law-s3.4.0] Safety order: prohibits violence, threats and following; the respondent stays at home, so it is the least disruptive order; longer term.
[hom-family-law-s3.4.1] Protection order: the same protection, but temporary, while a safety order or barring order application waits to be heard.
[hom-family-law-s3.4.2] Barring order: the respondent must leave the home and stay away, for up to three years.
[hom-family-law-s3.4.3] Interim barring order: an urgent, short-term barring order that lasts until the full barring order application is decided.
[hom-family-law-s3.4.4] Emergency barring order: an urgent barring order for a person with a lesser or no interest in the home, lasting up to eight working days.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.b] The Act reaches beyond the couple. It protects dependent children, backs its orders with the criminal law and makes controlling behaviour an offence in its own right.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.0] The orders can protect the applicant and also a dependent child or other dependent person, so the court can protect a child who lives with the applicant.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.1] Tusla (the Child and Family Agency) can apply on behalf of a person, including a child, in some cases, for a safety or barring order.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.2] Breaking an order is a criminal offence, so the orders are enforced through the criminal law as well as the civil courts.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.3] The Gardaí can arrest a person who breaches an order, and the respondent can be prosecuted.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.4] Coercive control is a criminal offence under the Act. It means knowingly and persistently behaving in a controlling or coercive way towards a partner or former partner, with a serious effect on them.
[hom-family-law-s3.5.5] Examples of coercive control are isolating a person from family and friends, monitoring them, or controlling their money. It applies to people in an intimate or formerly intimate relationship.
[hom-family-law-s3.6.b] The steps are the same whichever order is needed: apply, get urgent help if there is immediate danger, have the case decided, and enforce the order through the Gardaí if it is broken.
[hom-family-law-s3.6.0] 1. The applicant applies to the District Court for the relevant order.
[hom-family-law-s3.6.1] 2. If there is an immediate risk, the court can make an urgent order, such as a protection order, an interim barring order or an emergency barring order, before the respondent is heard.
[hom-family-law-s3.6.2] 3. At the full hearing, the court decides whether to make a safety order or a barring order.
[hom-family-law-s3.6.3] 4. If the respondent breaches the order, it is reported to the Gardaí and the breach can be prosecuted.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s4: 4. When a marriage breaks down
[hom-marriage-s4.0.b] Counselling is the first option for a couple in difficulty. It helps partners talk through their problems while there is still a chance to put things right, so that separation or divorce does not become necessary.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.0] Counselling aims to help a couple resolve their problems before they become grounds for separation or divorce, which is why it is the first step rather than the last.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.1] The service is confidential, so partners can speak openly. They may attend together, separately or, where relevant, with their children.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.2] Several visits may be needed before the couple reach a resolution, because problems built up over time are rarely settled in one session.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.3] The counsellor facilitates the discussion and may offer advice, but does not decide for the couple. The couple work out their own way forward.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.4] Counsellors may refer a couple to a specialist counsellor for a particular problem, such as addiction or difficulties in sexual relations.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.5] Marriage counselling is provided by Accord and by other independent counselling services in Ireland.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.b] Mediation is an alternative to court for a couple who have already decided to separate or divorce. Instead of a judge deciding the outcome, the couple work out the terms themselves with help.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.0] The Family Mediation Service of the Legal Aid Board offers a confidential service to couples who have decided to separate or divorce. It was established in 1986, is State-funded and is free, whereas private mediators charge.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.1] An impartial, trained mediator meets the couple together and helps them negotiate their own agreement. The mediator does not take sides or decide for them.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.2] The issues negotiated include parenting arrangements, custody and access to children, finances and maintenance, and the family home and other property.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.3] Mediation keeps children out of conflict, encourages communication between the partners, and helps them accept the separation.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.4] Mediation is less costly, less adversarial and quicker than court proceedings, which pit the spouses against each other.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.5] The outcome is a written agreement that each partner can bring to their solicitor to be made legally binding.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.b] Legal separation is a legally binding agreement under which spouses live apart but remain married. It lets a couple settle practical arrangements without going to court.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.0] The spouses agree to live apart and set out the terms in a deed of separation, a legally binding written agreement.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.1] Typical terms are living apart, care and access arrangements for the children, maintenance, and what happens to the family home.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.2] A solicitor draws up the deed, and each spouse should have their own solicitor. A mediator may help them reach the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.3] Legal separation can be done without going to court if both spouses agree to the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.4] The spouses are still married, so neither can remarry. Only a divorce or a nullity decree allows that.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.5] Where the spouses cannot agree, a court can grant a judicial separation. This is a court order, so it is different from a deed of separation, which the couple agree themselves.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.b] Nullity and annulment both declare that a marriage was never valid, but only a legal nullity decree has effect in the eyes of the State. Do not confuse either with divorce, which ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.0] Legal nullity is a declaration by a court (the Circuit Court or High Court) that the marriage was never valid in law. The parties are then free to marry other people.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.1] Couples married in a civil or State-recognised religious ceremony need a legal nullity decree before either spouse can remarry.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.2] A marriage may be void, for example where one party is already married or in a civil partnership, the parties are within the prohibited degrees of relationship, or the formalities were not followed.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.3] Nullity may also be granted for a lack of capacity or free consent, or where a marriage is voidable, for example because one spouse cannot sustain a normal marital relationship, including inability to consummate.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.4] Church annulment is granted by a Church marriage tribunal, which studies the marriage and declares it invalid in the eyes of the Church.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.5] A church annulment has no legal effect in the State, so the couple remain legally married. A civil nullity decree or a divorce is still needed before either can remarry civilly.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.6] Nullity says the marriage never validly existed. Divorce ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.b] Divorce is a court decree that legally ends a valid marriage. It was introduced in Ireland by the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996, after the 1995 referendum.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.0] The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 was amended by the Family Law Act 2019, which shortened the living-apart period from four of the previous five years to two of the previous three, and came into force on 1 December 2019.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.1] First condition: the spouses must have lived apart for at least two of the previous three years. The couple can live apart under the same roof, and they do not have to be legally separated first.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.2] Second condition: there must be no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, meaning no realistic chance of the spouses getting back together.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.3] Third condition: proper provision has been or will be made for each spouse and for any dependent children, so that nobody is left without support.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.4] The couple must also have a link to Ireland: either living here permanently, or one of them having lived here for at least one year before applying.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.5] When granting a divorce, the court can make orders on custody and access, maintenance, the family home and other property, and succession rights, which extinguish a spouse's right to inherit from the other.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.6] A decree of divorce is final, and each spouse is then free to remarry.
