TARGET hom-clothing-selection-s2: 2. Influences on clothing
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.b] Clothing requirements are the needs an outfit must meet, and they vary between people and situations. Working out which needs matter most is the first step in choosing what to buy.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.0] Function: clothing must protect the body from weather, provide the movement an activity needs and be safe for the wearer's work or play. For example, a waterproof jacket needs a close-weave, water-repellent fabric so rain cannot soak through.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.1] Climate and season decide the weight of fabric, the amount of covering and the need for waterproof or insulating layers, because the clothing has to keep the body comfortable in the weather it will meet.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.2] Cost versus money available: a buyer weighs the price of a garment against their budget, and the purchase cost is balanced against durability and the number of times it will be worn, which is why a dearer, hardwearing garment can be better value.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.3] Availability: clothing can only be bought if it is stocked in the shops or online in the right size, colour and style, so what a person wears is limited by what they can actually find.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.4] Personal taste and body shape guide the choice of style, colour and fit, because people want clothes that suit them and feel comfortable.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.5] Occasion and activity define the level of formality, such as a school uniform, work wear, sportswear or a wedding outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.b] People do not choose clothes in isolation. The culture, beliefs and groups around them shape what feels right to wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.0] Culture shapes modesty and dress codes, and it inspires designs, because each culture has its own standards of what is suitable to wear. Traditional dress such as the sari and pashmina shawl, and traditional embroidery motifs, are examples.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.1] Religious and national traditions influence the clothing worn for ceremonies, festivals and rites of passage (the key events in life, such as a wedding or graduation).
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.2] Peer groups influence teenagers, who often adopt the styles and brands their friends wear to gain acceptance.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.3] Status: clothing signals a person's rank, wealth or profession, so what someone wears tells others about their position.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.4] Identification: uniforms show that a person belongs to a group or holds a role, such as the uniform of a nurse. The principle is that a uniform signals role.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.b] Most people see what is fashionable long before they see it in a shop. The media, admired public figures and designers decide which styles spread.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.0] Magazines, newspapers, television, film and advertising popularise fashion by showing what is worn, so people see new styles and want them.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.1] Young people copy the clothes worn by actors, presenters, musicians and sports stars they admire.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.2] Trendsetters are individuals or groups whose choices are copied by others, and they set the direction for the mass market. Examples are James Dean (jeans, white T-shirt, black leather jacket), Jackie Kennedy (A-line suit and pillbox hat) and Princess Diana.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.3] Fashion houses and designers launch collections at seasonal shows, which are then copied by high street retailers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.4] Fashion is the currently accepted style, and it changes over time, so clothing that was fashionable in one decade can look dated in the next.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.5] Trendsetters are people, fashion is the accepted style and a trend is a short-lived direction, so keep the three terms apart.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.3.b] Social conditions change what people want to wear, so designers and makers change the garments they produce.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.3.0] Social conformity, the wish to fit in with a group, drives demand for common styles, because people buy what others around them are wearing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.3.1] Changes in women's status and in male roles have altered the silhouettes and tailoring that designers offer, so garments such as women's trouser suits became common.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.b] Money decides both what consumers can spend and what it costs to make a garment, so the economy shapes design and construction.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.0] Prosperity encourages consumers to spend on fashion, including power dressing and luxury items, because they have more money to spare.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.1] In a downturn, consumers buy simpler, more sombre and lower-cost clothing, and manufacturers use cheaper materials to keep prices down.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.2] Production costs, including raw materials and labour, set the price a garment must be sold at.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.b] Technology changes how garments are designed and made, and how quickly new styles reach buyers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.0] Computer-aided design (CAD) allows designers to develop patterns and styles on screen before production, so ideas can be tested without making a sample.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.1] Advanced sewing machines, computerised cutting and mass production make garments faster and cheaper to make.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.2] New and high-tech fibres and blends, such as stretch fibres and microfibres, create fabrics with new properties.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.3] Media and communication allow new styles to reach markets around the world very quickly, for example through fashion shows and images shared by phone.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s1: 1. Society, culture, norms and mores, values
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.b] Sociology is the study of how people live together in groups and societies. Its starting point is the society itself, because human life is organised, not random.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.0] A society is a group of people who live in a defined territory and share a way of life, a set of institutions (the established organisations and arrangements of social life) and a sense of belonging.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.1] Social life in a society is organised: people depend on one another and follow common rules, which is why daily life is predictable rather than chaotic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.2] The term applies at different scales, such as Irish society, a small rural community or Western society, so always be clear which group you mean.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.b] Culture is what makes the way of life of one society different from another. It is much wider than the arts, because it covers almost everything a group of people share and hand on.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.0] Culture is the whole way of life of a society, passed from one generation to the next. It includes language, customs, beliefs, values, norms, mores, music, art, dress, food, religion, technology and objects. Irish culture is a good example.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.1] Culture is learned, not inherited biologically, so a child raised in any society picks up that society's way of life through socialisation (the process of learning how to live in a society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.2] Material culture is the physical objects a society makes and uses, such as clothing, tools, buildings and housing. Non-material culture is the intangible side: language, beliefs, values, norms, customs and rituals.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.3] Cultures differ between societies, and a culture changes over time. Differences show up in language, dress, food, religious practice, wedding customs and attitudes to age and gender roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.4] Members of a society share common symbols and meanings, though not every member values every symbol.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.5] Safe Irish examples of culture include Irish traditional music, the GAA, the Irish language as a cultural symbol, St Patrick's Day, Christmas customs and Irish wedding customs. Symbols like these carry shared meanings for those who value them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.6] A subculture is a smaller group within a society with some distinct values, norms or styles of its own, such as a youth music scene, a sporting club or a religious minority. It still shares the main culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.7] Ethnocentrism is judging another culture by the standards of your own culture. Cultural relativism is understanding a culture on its own terms, by its own standards, rather than by those of another culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.b] Every society has rules about how to behave, most of them unwritten. These rules come in different strengths, from everyday habits to rules with serious moral weight.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.0] A norm is an accepted, expected way of behaving in a given situation. It is a rule, mostly unwritten. The standard example is forming a queue; others are greeting people, keeping quiet in a library, turning up on time and dressing suitably for an interview or a funeral.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.1] Norms vary between cultures, between social settings and over time. For example, what is acceptable at a match is not acceptable in church, and what was normal fifty years ago may not be now. Norms about punctuality, personal space and greeting also differ from one culture to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.2] Mores (pronounced MOR-ays) are norms with strong moral weight, whose breach is seen as seriously wrong, such as the ban on theft, violence or disrespect to the dead. Breaking a more brings serious disapproval or punishment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.3] Customs, also called folkways, are everyday conventions and traditional ways of doing things, such as queueing, shaking hands, a birthday custom or a wedding custom. Breaking them is rude, not wrong, and brings only mild disapproval. This helps separate a norm from a more.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.4] Laws are norms that are written down and enforced by the State through officially appointed bodies such as the courts. Some mores become laws, such as the bans on theft and assault, but many norms are not laws, such as queueing.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.b] A norm only works if there is some pressure to follow it. Societies apply that pressure through sanctions, and the result is either conformity or deviance.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.0] A sanction is how a society enforces norms: a reward for conforming to a norm or a penalty for breaking one.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.1] Positive sanctions reward conformity, for example praise, a prize, an award or promotion.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.2] Negative sanctions punish breaking norms, for example a frown, ridicule, ostracism (being left out) or, at the formal end, fines and imprisonment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.3] Informal sanctions come from family, friends and neighbours. Formal sanctions come from official bodies such as the courts, schools and employers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.4] Conformity means following norms. Deviance is behaviour that breaks a norm.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.5] What counts as deviance depends on the society and the time, because norms vary between cultures and change over time, so the same behaviour may be deviant in one place and normal in another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.b] Values sit behind norms. A norm is the specific rule; a value is the broad belief that the rule expresses.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.0] Values are the beliefs and attitudes a society holds about what is right and wrong, good and bad, important and worth striving for.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.1] Values are general, abstract and long-lasting; norms are the specific rules that carry values into everyday behaviour. A value of respect for life lies behind the norm against violence, and a value of honesty lies behind the norm of not cheating in exams.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.2] Examples of values given in Irish and Western societies include family, education, honesty, fairness and equality, respect for others, religious belief, freedom, community and care for the vulnerable. These are examples of values, not a claim about every person.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.3] Core values are held strongly and change slowly; peripheral values are held less firmly and change more readily.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.4] Values are learned from the family first, then from school, peers, religion and the media. They differ between families, groups and generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.5] Values change over time, for example attitudes to marriage, gender equality and diversity. The changes feed through into new norms and laws.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.6] Values can conflict, such as personal freedom against duty to family, or career against family time. Individuals must balance them, and societies resolve such conflicts through norms and laws.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.b] The terms in this subsection are not separate lists. They fit together as one system, with culture as the umbrella.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.0] Culture is the umbrella. Values, norms, mores, customs, language, beliefs and symbols are its parts.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.1] Values are the broad beliefs. Norms and mores are the rules that put them into practice.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.2] Sanctions enforce norms and mores, and laws are the formal, written version of them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.3] A chain to remember: a value of honesty leads to a norm of not cheating, and cheating brings a sanction such as a teacher's disapproval or a penalty.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.b] Exams often ask you to explain four or five terms together, such as culture, norms, values and role. Each term is marked separately, so a clear layout earns the marks.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.0] Give each term its own line or short paragraph so that each one can be credited separately.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.1] Open with a one-sentence definition that uses the correct sociological meaning. Use the wording the schemes credit: way of life of a society for culture, accepted way of behaving for norms, and beliefs about what is right and wrong for values.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.2] Add the point that separates the term from the one most often confused with it, such as folkways from mores or norms from values.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.3] Give a specific example from Irish or everyday life for each term, such as queueing for a norm or Irish culture for culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.4] Where a term links to another in the set, say how: for example, values underpin norms, and norms are enforced by sanctions. A three-mark term answer is one definition point, one example or extension point and, where terms are related, a link.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s1: 1. Consumer choices
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.b] Every purchase starts with how much a household can spend. Income sets the limit, and it also decides whether a household buys what it needs or what it merely wants.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.0] Household income sets the limit on spending, so a household on a tight budget compares unit prices (the price per kilo or per item), buys own-brand and bulk items, and delays non-essential purchases.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.1] Disposable income is the money left after tax and essential bills. It decides how much is free for discretionary spending (spending that is a choice, not a necessity) such as eating out or branded clothing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.2] When income rises, households often trade up to branded or higher-quality goods; when it falls, they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.3] Essential needs such as food, heating and shelter are bought first, and luxury wants are the first to be cut when income falls.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.4] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income. A wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.5] Credit and easy-pay arrangements can allow spending above current income, and the debt they create reduces future choice because part of later income is already committed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.b] Beyond money, choices reflect who the buyer is, who influences them and what the product offers. For a large purchase such as a washing machine, buyers weigh several of these together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.0] Personal preference, and values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethical concerns, and cultural or religious practice), all influence what is bought.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.1] Household composition narrows choice: family size, life-cycle stage and special diets, such as a coeliac or diabetic member or an allergy, determine which goods are suitable.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.2] Time pressure pushes busy households towards convenience foods, takeaway meals and online shopping, because these save time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.3] Other people influence choice through word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.4] For a large purchase, buyers weigh the energy rating, reliability of the brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.5] The environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor that some consumers weigh when choosing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.6] Impulse buying is an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout. It results from several of the factors above, such as advertising, values and income, working together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.b] Shoppers often choose by name rather than by comparing every product. Brands and own-brand goods are the two main kinds of product on the shelf, and they differ in price and in how they are promoted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.0] A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product and distinguishes it from competing goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.1] Branded goods are made by a manufacturer and are usually advertised nationally, so they tend to cost more than equivalent goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.2] Own-brand goods are sold under the retailer's own name. They are often cheaper because the retailer avoids the cost of national advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.3] Brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand, which saves the shopper the effort of comparing alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.b] Advertising is communication that informs consumers about goods and services and tries to create a want for them. It happens mostly outside the shop, in the media a consumer meets every day.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.0] Advertising makes people aware of new products and builds brand loyalty, so that consumers ask for the product by name.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.1] It also informs consumers about features, price and offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.2] Media used include television, radio, print, outdoor billboards, websites, social media, and sponsorship of sport and events.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.3] Celebrity endorsement transfers the image or popularity of a well-known person to the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.4] Product differentiation: advertising highlights a feature, such as a special ingredient, that sets one brand apart from similar goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.5] Advertising can make people feel they need products they cannot afford, and it can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.6] Advertising aimed at children and teenagers is especially influential. It can prompt impulse purchases and family purchases, because young consumers have less experience of judging value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.7] The advantage to the consumer is awareness of choice and information. The disadvantages are that the cost of advertising is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, and there is pressure to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.b] Merchandising is the arrangement and presentation of goods in a shop to encourage consumers to buy more, and to buy more often. It is what the retailer does inside the shop, in contrast to advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.0] Store layout: goods are arranged in clearly identifiable departments with space to browse, and essentials such as bread and milk are placed at the back so that shoppers pass other products on the way to them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.1] Route design: aisles are arranged to keep shoppers moving through the store, so that they see more products and spend longer browsing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.2] Product placement: complementary goods are shelved together, such as biscuits beside tea, so that buying one reminds the shopper of the other. Sweets and magazines are placed at the checkout as impulse items.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.3] Shelf position: premium or luxury goods are placed at eye level, where they are seen first, while cheaper products are often placed on lower or higher shelves.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.4] Special offers displayed at the end of aisles draw attention to promotional stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.5] In-store stimuli: background music that slows the pace of shopping, aromas such as freshly baked bread, good lighting and warmth, and colourful displays.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.6] Sampling and demonstrations let shoppers taste or try a product before buying it, which makes a purchase more likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.b] Pricing techniques make a price look lower, or make a purchase look more valuable, than it is. Discount offers and discount stores do lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.0] Charm pricing: a price set just below a round figure, so that the item appears cheaper than it is.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.1] Multi-buy offers, such as three items for a set price, encourage consumers to buy more than they would at a single-item price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.2] Clearance and reduction labels suggest extra value and can prompt impulse buying of goods the shopper did not plan to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.3] Loss leaders: a well-known product is sold at or below cost to draw shoppers into the store, who then buy other items at normal prices.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.4] Coupons and money-off vouchers reward a specific purchase and are often distributed through loyalty schemes or advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.5] Discount offers, and discount stores, lower the cost of goods, but a lower price is not always better value, so the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.b] Loyalty schemes reward repeat custom with points or discounts, and give retailers information about what customers buy. They are one of the shopping systems named in the syllabus.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.0] Customers earn points or tokens for each amount spent. The points are exchanged for money off goods, vouchers or goods after a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.1] Examples in Ireland include the Tesco Clubcard and the Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.2] Schemes encourage saving for special occasions, such as Christmas, by building up points over time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.3] Shoppers may spend more to reach a reward target, and points can take priority over price when they compare shops, so they return to the same shop.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.4] Card use gives retailers data on buying patterns, which helps them target offers and plan stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.5] Disadvantages: the scheme discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned purchases, and ties the shopper to one shop. It also requires the card to be carried.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.b] Easy-pay arrangements spread the cost of a purchase over time, so goods can be bought without paying the full price at once. This is a form of credit, which has both benefits and costs.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.0] Easy-pay schemes: a retailer or utility company lets the consumer pay for goods or a bill in instalments over a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.1] Hire purchase: the consumer uses the goods while paying instalments under an agreement, and ownership passes only when the final instalment is paid.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.2] Hire purchase is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of the credit), and gives the consumer a cooling-off period of ten days.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.3] Advantages of credit: goods can be used immediately, the cost is spread over time, larger items become affordable, and essentials can be bought in an emergency when cash is not available.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.4] Disadvantages: the total cost is higher than the cash price because of interest and charges, easy-pay can encourage buying of non-essential goods and overspending, and the debt reduces future choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.b] Both allow shopping from home, but the consumer cannot inspect the goods before buying them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.0] Television buying: goods are demonstrated on screen and ordered by phone or online, often with a limited-time offer that creates urgency.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.1] Mail order: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or website and delivered by post or courier.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.2] Advantages of mail order: it is convenient for people without time to shop, a wide range is available, there is no travel, and orders can be placed by post or phone.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.3] Disadvantages of mail order: goods cannot be seen, touched or tried on, delivery can take weeks, and postage adds to the cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.4] Distance purchases (those made without meeting the seller) carry a statutory right to cancel within a cooling-off period under the Consumer Rights Act 2022, because the buyer could not see the goods before ordering.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.b] Online shopping offers convenience and easy price comparison, but adds risks around payment, delivery and personal data.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.0] Advantages: access at any hour from home, easy comparison of prices across retailers, a wider range, customer reviews, and delivery to the door.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.1] Disadvantages: goods cannot be seen or handled before purchase, and delivery can be delayed or carry extra charges.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.2] Payment risk: card details can be taken through phishing emails (fake messages that trick people into giving details) or fake websites, so pay only on secure sites that show a padlock symbol and an address beginning https.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.3] Keep records of the order, so that there is proof of what was bought and paid for if something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.4] Overspending risk: stored card details and one-click ordering make impulse purchases quick and easy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.5] Retailers collect data on browsing and buying, which can be used to target advertising at the individual consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.b] Packaging does more than wrap a product. It protects the goods, carries information, and helps to sell the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.0] Packaging protects and preserves the product, so food stays safe and goods are not damaged on the way to the shop or home.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.1] It informs the shopper, because the pack carries the name, instructions and other details.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.2] It attracts attention on the shelf and conveys quality through colour, shape and design.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.3] It gives convenience, such as portion size and easy-open features, and it suggests quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.4] Care labels and safety or hazard symbols on non-food goods, for example on household cleaning agents, explain how to use the product and prevent accidents.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.b] Labels on pre-packed foods are a legal requirement, so shoppers can choose safely and compare products. The detail is kept short here and is consumer-focused.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.0] Mandatory food labels show the name of the food, ingredients listed in descending order of weight (the heaviest first), net quantity, a use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and instructions for use, and the name and address of the food business.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.1] Use-by dates apply to highly perishable foods and the food is unsafe after the date; best-before dates indicate quality, and the food may still be safe after them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.2] The 14 major allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold or capitals, so that people with allergies can spot them quickly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.3] A nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. The per 100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory, while the per-portion figure is optional.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.4] The country of origin must be shown where the law requires it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.5] Claims such as low fat, light or high fibre may be made only where the product meets defined criteria.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.6] Quality marks and symbols help consumers choose. The Bord Bia Quality Mark for Irish food shows that a product meets a defined standard, and the gluten-free symbol and allergen emphasis help people with special diets.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.7] EU food information rules are set out in Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
