TARGET hom-meat-preparation-s3: 3. Preparation and cooking
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.b] Meat is muscle, and muscle is made of fibres held together by connective tissue (the tough, stringy tissue that binds the fibres). How tough a piece of meat is depends on how much of this tissue it has and on what happens to the animal and the meat before and during cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.0] Age of the animal: older animals have longer, thicker muscle fibres and more connective tissue, and the collagen (the main protein of connective tissue) in it is more cross-linked, so the meat is tougher. Young animals such as veal, lamb and chicken are tender.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.1] Activity of the muscle: muscles that work hard, such as the legs, neck, shoulder, shin and flank, have larger fibres and more connective tissue than the little-used loin, sirloin or fillet. This is why the cut of meat decides its toughness.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.2] Treatment before slaughter: an animal that is rested and not stressed gives better meat, while poor rest or stress before slaughter lowers the quality of the meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.3] Treatment after slaughter: if meat is not hung long enough, the muscle stays stiff from rigor mortis (the stiffening of muscle after death). Chilling too quickly after slaughter makes the muscle contract (cold shortening), so the meat is tough.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.4] Cooking: high heat, fast cooking or overcooking coagulates (sets) the protein and squeezes out juices, so the meat is dry and tough.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.5] Connective tissue and cooking: collagen is not broken down by quick dry cooking, so tough cuts need slow moist cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.0.6] Fat content: marbled meat (meat with fine streaks of fat through the muscle) feels juicier and more tender.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.b] Tenderising means making tough meat softer to eat. Each method works by one of four principles: mechanical (physically breaking fibres), chemical (acid or salt), enzymic (enzymes splitting protein) or heat (slow moist cooking).
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.0] Hanging (maturing): after slaughter rigor mortis stiffens the muscle. During hanging in a cold room the stiffness passes, glycogen (the muscle's store of sugar) turns to lactic acid, and the meat's own proteolytic (protein-splitting) enzymes break down fibres and connective tissue. Beef is hung for about 1 to 3 weeks.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.1] Mincing: a mincer breaks fibres and connective tissue into small pieces (a mechanical principle), so tough cuts become tender in products such as burgers and bolognese.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.2] Pounding with a steak hammer or mallet, or cutting across the grain, breaks up the long muscle fibres and connective tissue mechanically. Piercing with needles, skewers or forks does the same and lets marinade or brine get into the meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.3] Proteolytic enzymes: papain from papaya, bromelain from pineapple, ficin from figs and actinidin from kiwi split the protein bonds in muscle and collagen. They are sprinkled on or injected into the meat before cooking, and act mostly on the surface and as cooking warms the meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.4] Acid marinades: lemon juice, vinegar, wine or yoghurt soften muscle protein by acid action and add flavour. A marinade works only on the surface, so it does not tenderise a whole joint or make meat safe.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.5] Brining and curing: a salt solution dissolves some muscle proteins so the meat holds more water and stays juicy and tender during cooking, as in bacon and ham.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.6] Slow moist cooking (stewing, braising, casseroling, pot roasting) converts collagen to gelatine, which softens the connective tissue and separates the fibres. Elastin (another connective protein) is not softened, so trim it or mince it.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.1.7] Pressure cooking: steam at high pressure and temperature converts collagen to gelatine quickly, so tough cuts tenderise in much less time.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.b] Heat changes the protein, fat, colour, flavour and nutrients of meat all at once. Some changes make meat safe and tasty, and others, if the cooking goes on too long, spoil it.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.0] Coagulation: heating makes the protein denature and set (coagulate), so the meat firms and its fibres harden and shrink. Overcooking makes the protein tough and dry and lowers its digestibility.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.1] Shrinkage: water, juices and melted fat are lost as meat cooks, so it loses weight and size. Lower, slower cooking loses less.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.2] Fat melts during cooking, and in roasting it bastes the meat as it drips. The fat that runs out can be poured away.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.3] Collagen converts to gelatine in moist cooking, which tenderises the meat. Elastin does not change.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.4] Myoglobin (the red pigment in muscle) denatures at cooking temperatures, so red meat changes to brown or grey and the juices turn from pink to clear in well-cooked meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.5] Micro-organisms are destroyed, so thorough cooking makes meat safe to eat. Cook to 75 °C at the centre for poultry, pork, mince, sausages and burgers.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.6] Flavour develops: extractives (flavour compounds in the meat) are released, and the Maillard reaction between amino acids and sugars at a high surface temperature browns the surface and gives a roasted flavour. Surface protein coagulates and browns.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.2.7] Nutrients: correctly cooked protein stays available and is easier to digest. Water-soluble vitamins, especially thiamine (B1), and minerals dissolve into the cooking juices or drip, so use the juices in gravy or sauce. Some B vitamins are destroyed by heat, while fat-soluble vitamins are mostly retained.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.3.b] Every cooking method works by moving heat into the meat in one of a few ways. Naming the way heat travels is the 'underlying principle' examiners want when you describe a method.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.3.0] Conduction: heat passes through a solid such as a pan, so frying uses conduction through hot fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.3.1] Convection: heat moves through a liquid or hot air, so stewing in liquid and roasting in a hot oven rely on convection.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.3.2] Radiation: infrared heat travels directly from the source, so grilling and barbecuing rely on radiant heat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.3.3] Microwaves (electromagnetic waves) make the water molecules in the food vibrate, producing heat inside the food. They penetrate only about 2 to 4 cm.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.3.4] Stewing: meat is simmered in a small amount of liquid at about 80 to 90 °C, which tenderises it and keeps it moist. Heat reaches the meat by conduction through the pot and convection in the liquid.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.b] The best cooking method depends on how much connective tissue a cut has. Tender cuts have little, so they need only quick cooking, but tough cuts have a lot, so they need long, gentle, moist cooking to turn it to gelatine.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.0] Tender cuts such as fillet, sirloin, rump steak, loin chops, lamb cutlets, pork loin and chicken breast suit dry methods: grilling, frying, roasting, stir-frying or barbecuing, which cook them quickly.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.1] Tough cuts such as shin, chuck, brisket, neck, flank, oxtail, stewing steak and chicken legs need slow moist cooking (stewing, braising, casseroling, pot roasting, pressure cooking) or mincing.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.2] Lamb shanks and beef shin become tender when braised slowly in liquid, because the long moist heat turns their collagen to gelatine.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.3] Joints for roasting include leg of lamb, loin of pork, topside and whole poultry. Gammon and ham are boiled or baked, and sausages and burgers are grilled or baked.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.4] Mince is fried and then simmered in dishes such as bolognese and chilli, or shaped into burgers and grilled. It must be cooked through.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.5] Poultry is cooked until no pink meat remains, the juices run clear and the centre is steaming hot (75 °C at the core). It can be roasted whole or in portions, stewed or casseroled.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.4.6] Liver and kidney are cooked quickly by frying or grilling, or gently braised, because long cooking toughens them, especially liver.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.b] An evaluation question asks you to judge one method of cooking meat, not list several. One well-evaluated method is better than a list.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.0] An evaluation names the method, states its underlying principle (how heat reaches the food), describes its effect on the meat (texture, colour, flavour, nutrient loss, fat), and weighs advantages against disadvantages.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.1] Stewing advantages: it tenderises tough cuts because collagen turns to gelatine, keeps the meat moist, makes use of cheap cuts, and the liquid becomes a sauce so nutrients that leached out are kept. It can be left unattended and uses little fuel, especially in a slow cooker.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.2] Stewing disadvantages: it takes a long time, and nutrients leach into the liquid and are lost if it is discarded, giving a lower vitamin content.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.3] Frying: it is fast and browns the surface, but it adds fat and dries or toughens the meat if overcooked.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.4] Grilling: radiant heat browns tender cuts quickly with little added fat, because the fat drains away, but it can dry out lean meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.5] Roasting: dry heat in a hot oven suits tender joints, and fat drips away from a rack.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.6] Microwaving: it is fast and efficient, but it does not brown meat unless combined with a grill, and it can cook unevenly.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.5.7] Other methods that can be evaluated include boiling, poaching, steaming, braising, pressure cooking and barbecuing. Each needs a name, a principle and an effect on the meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.b] A family on a low income can still eat meat if it chooses cheaper cuts, stretches them with other foods and cooks them well. Exam menus ask for a suitable meal with reasons.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.0] Choose inexpensive cuts such as mince, chicken thighs or portions, sausages, liver, shin or stewing steak, and cook them by slow moist methods such as stew, casserole, curry or shepherd's pie.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.1] Stretch mince with vegetables or lentils; a bolognese with carrots, onions and lentils uses less meat without losing flavour.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.2] Starchy foods such as potatoes, pasta, rice or bread complete the meal, make it filling and supply energy.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.3] Cabbage, other vegetables, fruit and potatoes add vitamin C, which meat does not contain.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.4] Meat supplies protein for growing children and haem iron (the well-absorbed form of iron) for teenage girls and pregnant women, so it is worth including in a low-cost family meal.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.5] Stock made from bones, and the cooking juices, make soups and gravies that keep extractives and water-soluble vitamins in the meal.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.6] Batch cooking a casserole and freezing the portions saves fuel and time. Slow cookers and one-pot meals also save fuel.
[hom-meat-preparation-s3.6.7] Give reasons for the menu: cost, nutritive value, ease of cooking, family tastes, skills and equipment, and fuel saving. Examples of two-course menus are spaghetti bolognese with an apple crumble, or vegetable soup followed by spaghetti bolognese.

CANDIDATES hom-meat-preparation-s1: 1. Nutritional significance and structure of meat
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.b] Meat is not one food but three groups, and each differs in colour, fat and the nutrients it is valued for.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.0] Red meat is beef, lamb, pork and veal, the flesh of mammals, and it is the group most people mean by "meat".
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.1] Poultry is chicken, turkey and duck. The breast is paler than the leg or red meat because it contains less myoglobin (the red pigment that stores oxygen in muscle).
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.2] Poultry fat is lower than in most red meat, and most of it sits in and under the skin, so poultry is not fat free.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.3] Offal is the edible organs of the animal, such as liver, kidney and heart.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.0.4] Liver is the richest source of vitamin A, iron and several B vitamins among the offal, which is why offal is valued as cheap, nutrient-dense food.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.b] Nutritive value is about the nutrients meat contains. Meat is valued mainly for its protein, haem iron and B group vitamins, while its fat is mostly saturated and it supplies essentially no carbohydrate.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.0] Protein: meat is a high biological value (HBV) protein, meaning it supplies all the essential amino acids (those the body cannot make) in the proportions the body needs.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.1] Meat is about 20 to 30 per cent protein by weight.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.2] Protein supplies amino acids for growth and repair of body cells and for making enzymes, hormones and antibodies.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.3] Fat: meat fat is mainly saturated, and meat is about 10 to 30 per cent fat, varying with the animal, the cut and whether visible fat is trimmed. Chicken is lower in fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.4] Fat gives concentrated energy and insulation, and offal carries fat-soluble vitamins.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.1.5] Carbohydrate: meat contains essentially none, so it is served with a starchy food such as potatoes, pasta, rice or bread to supply energy and balance the meal.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.b] Besides its macronutrients, meat is an important source of iron and B vitamins, but it is deficient in vitamin C. Water and flavour compounds make up the rest of its character.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.0] Haem iron (the form of iron found in animal foods) is present in red meat, offal and the dark meat of poultry. The body absorbs it more readily than non-haem iron from plant foods.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.1] Iron is used to make haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood, and myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.2] Zinc supports enzyme activity, potassium supports muscle function, and phosphorus supports bones, teeth and energy release. Sulphur is present in the sulphur-containing amino acids.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.3] Sodium is high only in processed meat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.4] B group vitamins help the body release energy from food. Cobalamin (B12) occurs naturally only in animal foods.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.5] Vitamins A and D are found mainly in liver. Meat is deficient in vitamin C (it is not a source), so serve it with vegetables or fruit.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.6] Water makes up about 50 to 60 per cent of meat. Raw lean meat is about 70 to 75 per cent water, so the figure varies with fat content and cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.2.7] Extractives are flavour compounds that dissolve out during cooking. They give meat its savoury taste and stimulate digestive juices.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.b] Dietetic value is about how suitable meat is for different people. Some groups gain a great deal from it, while others need to limit it.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.0] Growing children and teenagers need meat protein for growth, and adults need it for repair and maintenance of tissue.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.1] Haem iron is especially important for pregnant women, menstruating women (including teenage girls), children and teenagers, to prevent iron-deficiency anaemia.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.2] Older people and anyone with a poor appetite do well on tender, lean or minced cuts cooked gently, because tough or overcooked meat is harder to chew and digest.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.3] People with raised cholesterol or coronary heart disease, and anyone controlling weight, should limit saturated fat and cholesterol. They choose lean cuts, trim visible fat, remove poultry skin, grill rather than fry and limit processed meats.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.4] Low-income households find cheaper cuts and mince good value protein and iron.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.5] Vegetarians and vegans do not eat meat, so they need other sources of protein of similar quality, iron and B12.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.3.6] Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and the Irish Food Pyramid place meat in one shelf with poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts. They recommend lean and unprocessed choices and advise limiting red and processed meat because of their salt and saturated fat content.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.b] Meat is muscle, and its structure explains how it feels to eat and why some cuts are tougher than others. Muscle is built from fibres, connective tissue and fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.0] Muscle is made of long, thin fibres, each a single muscle cell, lying parallel and running in the direction of the muscle's pull.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.1] Groups of fibres form bundles, and the bundles are held together by connective tissue.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.2] Each fibre contains myofibrils made of the proteins actin and myosin, the contractile proteins, which slide past each other to shorten the muscle. The fibre is surrounded by muscle fluid containing water, extractives, soluble protein, vitamins and minerals.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.3] Connective tissue wraps fibres and bundles and attaches muscle to bone through tendons. It contains collagen (white and tough) and elastin (yellow and stretchy).
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.4] Fat is found as marbling between and within the muscle fibres, as a layer under the skin or as a covering layer, and around the organs. Marbling adds juiciness and flavour, and fat melts on cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.5] A tougher cut has longer, thicker fibres and more connective tissue.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.4.6] Diagram: draw a cross-section of muscle showing a bundle of fibres inside a sheath of connective tissue, with one fibre enlarged to show myofibrils (actin and myosin) and a label for fat (marbling). Label connective tissue, fibre, bundle and fat.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.b] Meat contains several different proteins. Some do the work of the muscle, some carry colour and some form connective tissue, and cooking affects each differently.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of the myofibrils and make up most of the fibre protein. They coagulate (set solid) and firm up on heating.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.1] Globulin is a soluble protein in the muscle fluid. It coagulates on heating and is partly lost in drip and cooking juices.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.2] Myoglobin is the red oxygen-storing pigment of muscle and gives raw meat its colour. It denatures (changes shape) on heating, so the meat changes from red to brown or grey.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.3] Collagen is an insoluble connective tissue protein that is tough when raw. It converts to soluble gelatine in slow moist cooking.
[hom-meat-preparation-s1.5.4] Elastin is a springy connective tissue protein in tendons and ligaments. It is very tough and cooking does not soften it, so it is removed or the meat is minced.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s5: 5. Properties
[hom-proteins-s5.0.b] Cooking and food preparation change protein in two linked steps. First the folded protein chain unfolds (denaturation), then the unfolded chains join up and set (coagulation). Both are permanent, and together they explain most of what happens to eggs, meat, fish and milk.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.0] Denaturation is the irreversible unfolding of the secondary and tertiary structure of a protein (the folded shape of its chain), without breaking its peptide bonds (the links between amino acids), so the chain keeps its amino acids but changes shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.1] Denatured chains lose the original shape and functional properties of the protein, because the function of a protein depends on its shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.2] Denatured chains can then link to one another, and this linking is coagulation.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.3] Coagulation is the setting of denatured protein into a solid or semi-solid mass, which is why a runny raw egg becomes firm when cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.4] Denaturation changes the texture and appearance of a food, and denatured protein is more digestible, so cooked protein foods are easier to digest than raw ones.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.5] Denaturation can be caused by heat, mechanical action (such as whisking), chemicals (such as acid and salt) and enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.6] Egg white coagulates from about 60 degrees C, turning from transparent to opaque and firm; the yolk sets at about 68 degrees C. These are approximate temperatures because eggs set over a range.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.7] Fish flesh changes from translucent to opaque as its protein coagulates during cooking, which is how you can tell fish is cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.8] Examples of coagulation in cookery: omelette, quiche, boiled egg, meringue, soufflé and lemon meringue pie.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.b] A foam is a mass of air bubbles held in a thin film. Egg white makes a good foam because its protein, albumen, can be stretched into a film around each bubble and then set by heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.0] Whisking or beating stretches and partly denatures the albumen chains, which unfold and arrange themselves around the air bubbles. This is mechanical action, not heat, that creates the foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.1] The protein film around each bubble holds the trapped air, forming a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.2] Gentle heat in cooking then coagulates the albumen around the bubbles, setting the foam permanently, which is why a baked meringue stays firm.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.3] Over-whisking denatures the protein too far and breaks the film, so the foam becomes grainy and collapses.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.4] Examples: meringue, pavlova, soufflé and whisked sponge cake.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.b] A gel is a liquid held in a soft solid. Some proteins, especially gelatine, can do this: they dissolve in hot liquid and set into a network as it cools.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.0] A gel forms when protein chains (gelatine) dissolved in a hot liquid cool and link into a network that traps the liquid, such as water.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.1] Gelatine is the main protein used to make gels in cookery.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.2] A firmer gel results from a higher concentration of gelatine, because more chains form a denser network.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.3] Examples: jelly, aspic, mousses and cheesecake.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.b] Dry heat cooks food without added liquid. It acts mainly on the surface protein, which is why roasted and grilled foods firm up and brown.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.0] Dry heat (roasting, baking, grilling and frying) denatures and coagulates the surface protein, causing shrinkage and firmness.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.1] Prolonged dry heat dries the muscle protein and makes meat tough and dry, so overcooked meat is chewy.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.2] Dry heat at high temperatures produces the Maillard reaction, which browns the surface of meat, bread and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.b] The golden-brown crust on bread and roast meat is not caused by sugar alone or by enzymes. It comes from protein and sugar reacting together under dry heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.0] The Maillard reaction is non-enzymic browning, which happens when the amino acids of protein react with a reducing sugar (a sugar such as glucose) on heating.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.1] It needs high heat and a dry surface with little moisture; it does not need enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.2] It produces brown colour and roasted, savoury flavour, as in a toasted bread crust, roast meat, grilled or toasted cheese and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.b] Moist heat cooks food in liquid or steam at lower temperatures. For meat it does something dry heat cannot: it changes the tough connective tissue into a soft jelly.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.0] Moist heat (boiling, stewing, poaching and braising) converts the collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) to gelatine, which tenderises meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.1] Collagen conversion needs long, slow cooking in moisture, so cheaper cuts of meat, which contain a lot of connective tissue, become tender.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.2] Prolonged high heat toughens muscle protein, so gentle moist heat is used for tender results.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.3] Heat breaks the weak bonds that hold the folded structure, which is why heat denatures protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.b] Whisking, beating and mincing physically stretch and break protein, so they change texture without any heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.0] Whisking, beating and mincing unfold protein chains; whisking egg white is the example that forms a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.1] Kneading bread dough develops the gluten (the protein of wheat flour) into an elastic network that traps the carbon dioxide from yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.2] Beating meat with a mallet breaks up the muscle fibres and tenderises it.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.3] Excessive agitation denatures protein so far that it becomes tough or curdled.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.b] Acids and salt change protein without any heating. That is why a splash of lemon juice curdles milk and a marinade softens meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.0] Acids such as lemon juice and vinegar denature protein; lemon juice added to milk curdles it, which is also how milk sours.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.1] A marinade of acid, such as vinegar, wine or lemon, tenderises meat by denaturing its surface protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.2] Alkalis also denature protein and change its texture.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.3] Cheese-making uses acid to help set the milk protein, and salt to increase the firmness of the curd (the solid part of the milk), which controls the texture of the cheese.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.4] The acid in the stomach denatures food protein before enzymes hydrolyse (break) it, so the chains are easier for digestive enzymes to reach.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.b] Enzymes are biological catalysts, and some of them work on protein. Cooks use them to set milk for cheese and to soften tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.0] Rennin, traditionally from the stomach of young calves, coagulates casein (the main protein of milk) for cheese-making.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.1] Papain, from papaya, is a proteolytic enzyme (one that breaks down protein) that tenderises tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.2] Proteolytic enzymes hydrolyse peptide bonds, so too much enzyme action makes protein soft and mushy.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.3] Enzymes in the digestive tract hydrolyse protein to amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.4] Heat inactivates enzymes, which is why cooking stops enzymic tenderising.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.b] This investigation shows denaturation in practice by comparing egg white that is left alone, heated and whisked.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.0] Aim: to show how heat and mechanical action denature egg white protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.1] Independent variable: the treatment (heating in a water bath, or whisking); dependent variable: the change in appearance and texture of the white.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.2] Controlled variables: the same volume of egg white, the same starting temperature and the same type of dish.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.3] Control: place an untreated sample of egg white in a labelled dish for comparison.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.4] Method: heat a second sample gently in a water bath until it sets, then whisk a third sample until stiff.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.5] Safety: use fresh eggs, wash hands after handling raw egg, and do not taste raw egg white, because raw egg can carry bacteria.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.6] Expected result: the heated white turns opaque and firm, and the whisked white forms a stable foam; the untreated white stays clear and runny.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.7] Conclusion: heat and whisking both denature the protein of egg white and change its appearance and texture.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s2: 2. Heat transfer and the principles of cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.b] Cooking is more than making food hot. Heat changes the food in several useful ways, and each change is a reason examiners expect you to give.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.0] Cooking makes food safe by destroying harmful micro-organisms, so food must be heated right through to the centre, because bacteria deep inside a thick piece can survive if the heat never reaches them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.1] Heat denatures protein (changes its structure), so that digestive enzymes can act on it more easily.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.2] Cooking gelatinises starch (the starch grains swell and burst in water and heat), which makes rice, potatoes and pasta easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.3] Cooking softens the cellulose in plant cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to chew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.4] Heat develops flavour and changes colour and texture, which makes food more appetising and gives variety in the diet.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.5] Cooking destroys enzymes and most spoilage micro-organisms, which extends the time food stays fit to eat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.b] Whatever the method, cooking is heat moving from a source into the food. The cook controls how much heat reaches the food, for how long, and whether moisture or fat is present.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.0] Too low a temperature or too short a time leaves food undercooked and unsafe, because the centre never gets hot enough; too high a temperature or too long a time dries, toughens and burns the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.1] Thickness governs cooking time: thin or small pieces cook quickly and evenly, while a large joint needs lower heat for longer so that the centre cooks before the outside burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.2] Moist methods prevent food drying out; dry methods need added fat, basting (spooning hot fat or juices over the food) or a covered dish to keep food juicy.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.3] Food type governs the method: cuts rich in connective tissue need long, slow cooking, while delicate foods need short cooking at gentle heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.4] Cooked food must be served promptly or cooled quickly, because the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C is where bacteria multiply fastest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.b] Conduction is heat transfer through a solid from one molecule to the next, without the material itself moving. It is the way heat passes from a hot pan into the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.0] As molecules at the hot end are heated they vibrate faster and pass energy to their neighbours, until the whole piece is hot.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.1] Metals such as aluminium, copper and steel conduct heat quickly, which is why saucepans have metal bases; glass, ceramic, wood, plastic and air conduct heat slowly (they are insulators), which is why handles and oven gloves are made from them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.2] Pan-based methods begin with conduction: the cooker heats the pan base, which heats the liquid or fat in the pan, and that then heats the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.3] Boiling, poaching, stewing, shallow frying and stir-frying all depend on conduction through the pan base.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.4] Searing a steak in a hot pan: heat conducts from the pan surface into the outer layers of the meat and then gradually towards the centre.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.5] Baking trays and cake tins conduct heat into the base of a cake, so the base sets before the top.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.6] A large solid piece of food takes longer to cook through, because the heat has to travel inwards from the surface molecule by molecule.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.7] Diagram: arrows from a gas flame through a pan base into the food, labelled as conduction, with the direction of heat flow marked.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.b] Convection is heat transfer through a liquid or gas by currents. The heated fluid expands and becomes less dense so it rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place and is heated in turn.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.0] Natural convection occurs in a pot of boiling water, in a stew and in the hot air of a conventional oven, because the heated fluid rises on its own and the cooler fluid sinks.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.1] Forced convection: the fan in a fan oven circulates the hot air, so food cooks faster and more evenly than in a conventional oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.2] Deep-fat frying: convection currents in the hot oil carry heat to every surface of the immersed food at once.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.3] Steaming: hot steam rises from boiling water and carries heat to the food, and it also condenses on cooler food, releasing its latent heat (the heat stored in steam) to cook it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.4] Examples: boiling, stewing, steaming, deep-fat frying, and baking or roasting in an oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.5] Diagram: a pot of water with arrows showing hot water rising from the base, cooling at the surface and sinking at the sides.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.b] Radiation is heat transfer by infrared rays that travel in straight lines from a hot source and heat the first solid surface they strike. No solid, liquid or gas is needed to carry it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.0] The surface struck by radiant heat absorbs it, so browning and crisping happen on that surface while the inside of the food cooks by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.1] Grilling uses an element or flame above or below the food to heat the surface, sealing and browning it; thin pieces cook quickly because the heat is intense.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.2] Barbecuing and toasting use radiant heat from glowing coals, gas flames or electric elements.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.3] Dark, dull (matt) surfaces absorb radiant heat more readily than shiny surfaces, which reflect it; this is why foil reflects heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.4] Radiant heat weakens with distance from the source, so the height of the grill rack is adjusted to control how much heat the food receives.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.5] Diagram: straight arrows from a grill element to the top surface of a piece of food, labelled as radiation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.b] In practice most cooking uses more than one type of heat transfer at once. Microwave cooking is a special case, because the heat is made inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.0] Roasting uses radiation from the oven walls and element, convection by the hot air, and conduction inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.1] Boiling uses conduction through the pan base, then convection in the water, which is at 100 °C when boiling.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.2] In microwave cooking, a magnetron produces microwaves (electromagnetic waves) that make the water molecules in the food vibrate, and this makes heat inside the outer layer of the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.3] Once the heat is made in the outer layer, it spreads inwards by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.b] Each type of food reacts to heat in its own way, so the principles above are applied differently to each one. The right choice gives food that is safe, tender and keeps its colour and nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.0] Meat: tough cuts, rich in collagen, need long, slow, moist cooking so that collagen converts to gelatine; tender cuts can be grilled or fried quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.1] Fish: its flesh has little connective tissue and sets quickly, so it needs short cooking at gentle heat; poaching, steaming, grilling, baking and frying all suit it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.2] Eggs: egg proteins set (coagulate) at moderate temperatures, so eggs and custards need gentle heat; high heat makes scrambled egg rubbery and custard curdle.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.3] Vegetables and fruit: short cooking in a little boiling water, or steaming, keeps colour, texture and water-soluble vitamins, which would otherwise dissolve into the cooking water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.4] Cereals, rice and pasta: starch needs water and heat to gelatinise, so they are cooked in plenty of boiling water or steam.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.5] Milk and cheese: milk scorches on direct heat and cheese turns stringy at high temperatures, so sauces containing them are cooked gently.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.6] Fats and oils: fat overheats, smokes and breaks down if heated too far, so frying oil must be watched, an oil with a high smoke point chosen for deep-fat frying, and it should not be reused too often.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.7] Pastry and cakes: the fat is kept cold when rubbed in, the dry heat of the oven sets the flaky structure, and the oven should not be opened early when baking a cake.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s3: 3. Choice of cooking method
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.b] Cooking methods fall into three groups according to what carries the heat to the food. The right choice depends on the food, the eater and the result you want.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.0] Moist methods use water, stock or steam as the medium. Dry methods use hot air or radiant heat (heat given off as rays from a hot element or flame) with little or no added liquid. Fat-based methods use fat or oil as the medium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.1] The food: tender cuts and delicate foods such as fish, eggs and vegetables suit quick methods, because they need little time to become tender. Tough, fibrous cuts need long, moist methods, which soften the fibres.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.2] Nutrition: steaming, stir-frying and grilling use little or no water and short times, so they conserve water-soluble vitamins. Boiling loses them into the cooking water unless that liquid is used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.3] Palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat): dry and fat-based methods brown and crisp the surface, adding colour and flavour, while moist methods give a soft, tender texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.4] Diet: steaming, poaching, boiling and grilling on a rack add little or no fat, which suits a diet that limits fat. Deep-fat frying adds fat and energy because the food absorbs fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.5] Consumer needs: an older person who has difficulty chewing or swallowing needs soft, moist, easily digested food, so a moist method is the better choice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.6] Time and equipment: a pressure cooker shortens cooking time, while stewing and braising are long, slow methods in a covered pan or oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.7] Cost: slow moist cooking makes an inexpensive cut of meat tender and palatable, so you do not have to buy an expensive cut.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.b] In moist methods the food is cooked in water, stock or steam. The temperature of the liquid is what separates them: boiling is hottest, simmering is just below it and poaching is lower again.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.0] Boiling: food is cooked in rapidly bubbling water at boiling point, 100 °C at sea level. It is quick and used for vegetables, pasta, rice, eggs and potatoes, but water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) into the water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.1] Simmering: liquid is kept just below boiling point, with small bubbles breaking the surface. It is gentler than boiling and is used for stocks, soups, stews and stewed fruit.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.2] Stewing: food is cooked slowly and gently in a small amount of liquid in a covered pan or casserole, on the hob or in the oven. It suits tough meat and vegetables, as in Irish stew, a traditional stew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.3] Stewing keeps nutrients because the liquid is served with the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.4] Steaming: food is cooked by the steam from boiling water without touching the water, in a steamer, on a plate over a pan or in a covered bowl in a pan. No fat is added, and nutrients and shape are kept. It suits fish, chicken, vegetables, rice and steamed puddings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.5] Poaching: food is cooked gently in liquid kept slightly below simmering point, about 85 °C, on the hob or in the oven. It suits delicate foods that would break up or toughen in fiercer heat, such as eggs, fish and fruit like pears.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.6] Braising: tough meat or vegetables are browned in fat, then cooked slowly with a little liquid in a covered dish, in the oven or on the hob. The long, slow, moist cooking tenderises tough cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.7] Pressure cooking: food is cooked in a sealed pan in which steam builds up pressure. This raises the temperature above 100 °C and shortens the cooking time. It is used for stocks, stews, tough meat and pulses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.b] Dry methods cook food with hot air or radiant heat rather than liquid. They brown the surface, which adds colour and flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.0] Roasting: meat, poultry, potatoes or vegetables are cooked uncovered in the oven in a roasting tin, usually with a little fat. The surface browns while the centre cooks by conduction and convection.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.1] Baking: food is cooked by the dry heat of an oven, mainly by convection (hot air circulating) and radiation. It is used for cakes, bread, pastry, fish, pasta and potato dishes, as with fish pie and lasagne.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.2] Grilling: food is cooked by radiant heat from an element or flame above or below it. The heat seals the surface, so it is a fast method. It suits tender cuts, steaks, chops, thin fish, sausages, rashers, tomatoes, mushrooms, peppers and kebabs.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.3] Barbecuing: this is grilling outdoors on a grid over burning charcoal or gas, using radiant heat, which gives a smoky flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.4] Grilling lowers the fat content of food, because fat drips away from it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.b] Grilling is fast and the heat is fierce, so food can burn outside while it is raw inside, or dry out. These rules prevent that.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.0] Preheat the grill to the required temperature so that the surface seals on contact.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.1] Oil the grid or the food to prevent sticking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.2] Use thin pieces, so that heat reaches the centre before the surface burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.3] Do not add salt before grilling, because salt draws moisture to the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.4] Turn the food with tongs rather than a fork, so that its juices stay inside instead of running out through holes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.5] Use the correct cooking time, since grilled food dries out and overcooks quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.b] Fat-based methods, usually called frying, cook food in hot fat or oil. They give a crisp, golden surface, but the amount of fat used varies a great deal from one method to another.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.0] Shallow frying: food is cooked in hot fat in a shallow pan, partly covered by the fat and turned once or twice, giving a crisp surface. It is used for eggs, fish, chops and pancakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.1] Deep-fat frying: food is fully immersed in hot oil in a deep pan or fryer, which seals the surface quickly. It is used for chips and fish in batter. The food absorbs some fat, so it is higher in fat and energy than grilled food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.2] Stir-frying: small, thin pieces are tossed and stirred constantly over high heat in a wok or large pan with a little oil. Because the cooking is so quick, it keeps colour, crispness and vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.3] Dry frying: food is cooked in a non-stick pan without added fat, using the fat already in foods such as bacon rashers and sausages.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.4] Pot roasting: meat is cooked in a little fat in a covered pan on the hob, so it cooks in its own juices and steam and stays moist. It is not done in the oven and not uncovered.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.b] A microwave oven is a fast method that suits reheating and cooking small amounts. It works differently from the other methods, so its advantages and limits are worth knowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.0] Microwave ovens cook and reheat quickly with little added fat and water, so vitamins are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.1] They do not brown food unless a grill or combination mode is used, so the surface stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.2] Containers must be microwave-safe, and metal must not be used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.b] Examiners often ask you to give dishes or foods for a method. These are the examples that are accepted.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.0] Baking: apple crumble, sponge, soda bread, fish pie, lasagne, pizza and muffins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.1] Grilling: kebabs, chicken satay, burgers, fish, rashers, sausages, tomatoes, mushrooms and bruschetta.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.2] Steaming: fish, chicken, rice, Christmas pudding (a traditional steamed pudding), fruit and vegetables. Roasting: meat, potatoes and vegetables.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.3] Frying has four types: dry, shallow, deep and stir-fry. The moist methods are boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.4] Classifying methods: moist covers boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking. Dry covers baking, grilling, roasting and barbecuing. Frying covers dry, shallow, deep and stir-frying.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.b] Steaming is a good choice for an older person because it is simple and gives soft food that is easy to eat. It also has weaknesses, so a full evaluation covers ease, nutrition and palatability.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.0] Ease: steaming needs little attention or equipment, a whole meal can cook in a tiered steamer, and it suits small portions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.1] Texture: the moist, soft food is easy to chew and swallow, which suits an older person with difficulty chewing or swallowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.2] Nutrition: steaming adds no fat, so it suits a diet that limits fat. Because the food does not sit in water, water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.3] Palatability: steamed food keeps its colour and moisture, but it can taste bland and does not brown. Overcooking spoils its texture and colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.4] Fish, chicken, rice and vegetables suit steaming; steamed puddings are a traditional example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.5] Herbs, lemon juice and a sauce add flavour without added salt, which suits a diet that limits salt.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s4: 4. Sources
[hom-proteins-s4.0.b] Protein comes from both animal and plant foods, so everyone can meet their needs whatever they eat. Exam answers earn marks for naming a variety of foods from each group.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.0] Animal protein sources are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Cheese and yoghurt belong in the list because they are made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.1] Plant protein sources are soya beans and soya products, pulses (lentils, peas and beans), cereals (wheat, rice, maize and oats), nuts and seeds.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.2] Cereals such as wheat, rice and maize supply protein as well as carbohydrate, so everyday foods like wheat, bread and pasta are plant protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.3] Potatoes and vegetables contain only small amounts of protein, so they are not counted as main protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.b] Albumin is the protein most people meet first, in the white of an egg. It is a good example of a protein that is tied to one particular food.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.0] Albumin is the main protein of egg white (ovalbumin), and it is also found in lean meat.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.1] Lactalbumin, a related albumin, is found in the whey of milk, the watery part left when the casein is removed.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.2] Albumin is a soluble globular protein. It dissolves in water and coagulates (sets) when heated, which is why raw egg white turns solid and white on cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.b] Casein is the main protein of milk, so it follows milk into every food made from milk. Cheese-making depends on changing it.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.0] Casein (the older term is caseinogen) is the main protein of milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.1] Casein is present in cheese and yoghurt, the products made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.2] Casein is coagulated by the enzyme rennin, which is the first step in making cheese because it forms the curd. Acid also coagulates it, which is why milk curdles when it sours.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.b] Gelatine is the protein behind jelly, but it is not found in raw meat. It is made when another protein, collagen, is heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.0] Gelatine is not present in raw meat; it forms when collagen from animal bones, skin and connective tissue is heated in moisture.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.1] Gelatine sets to a gel when it cools, so it is used in jelly, aspic, gelatine-set mousses and desserts, marshmallows and some sweets.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.2] Gelatine comes from animal sources, so foods set with it are not vegetarian.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.b] Gluten is the protein that gives wheat bread its structure. It does not exist in dry flour; it is formed when the flour is wet.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.0] Gluten is the protein of wheat and wheat flour.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.1] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin, both already present in the flour, are mixed with water. Flour itself contains gliadin and glutenin, not formed gluten.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.2] Gluten is elastic and stretchy, and kneading develops it. It gives bread dough its elastic, stretchy texture and traps the carbon dioxide made by yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.3] Foods that depend on wheat gluten include bread, pasta, cakes, pastry and other flour products.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.b] Each of these proteins is tied to a food or food group. Pairing every named protein with one food is the quickest way to answer a naming question.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.0] Albumin: found in egg white and meat, and it sets when heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.1] Casein: found in milk, cheese and yoghurt, and it is coagulated by rennin.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.2] Gelatine: found in set desserts such as jelly, and it is made from collagen.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.3] Gluten: found in wheat flour and bread, and it traps gas in dough.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.b] Meat and fish are made of muscle and connective tissue, and each contains its own proteins. Higher Level questions ask you to name them.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they contract to move the muscle. They are found in the flesh of meat and fish.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.1] Collagen is the main protein of connective tissue and is found in meat, fish and bones.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.2] Collagen in meat and fish is the source of gelatine when it is heated in moisture; this is why cheap tough cuts of meat tenderise on slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.3] Elastin in meat and globulin in egg are also acceptable answers when asked for proteins in meat or egg.
