MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-work-leisure-s8: 8. Evaluating leisure facilities
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.b] Leisure facilities in a community come from three kinds of provider: public bodies, commercial businesses and voluntary groups. Each is paid for and run differently, and that shapes the cost, range and condition a user can expect.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.0] Public facilities are often provided or part-funded by local authorities (councils). They include swimming pools, leisure centres, libraries, parks, playgrounds, sports pitches, walking and cycling trails, community halls and arts centres.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.1] Commercial facilities are private businesses run for profit, such as gyms and private health clubs, cinemas, bowling alleys, golf clubs, cafés and entertainment venues.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.2] Commercial facilities depend on fees from users for their income, so the fee charged must cover running costs and make a profit.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.3] Voluntary and community facilities are run by non-profit groups and volunteers. They include GAA clubs, soccer and tennis clubs, youth clubs, active retirement groups, drama and musical societies, and community centres.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.4] Voluntary facilities are funded by membership fees, fundraising and grants, so they are not free: clubs usually charge a membership fee.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.5] Voluntary facilities depend on volunteers and member subscriptions, so their range and condition vary from club to club.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.0.6] Some facilities suit particular groups. Retired people, for example, often use golf, bingo, gym or leisure centre classes, library and book clubs, the swimming pool, the cinema, a choir or musical society, a drama group and card games.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.b] An evaluation judges a facility against fixed criteria, so that two facilities can be compared on the same basis. A list of features is not an evaluation: you must weigh strengths against weaknesses for a particular user.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.0] Cost: the fees charged, judged against what the user can afford, including membership and joining fees, pay-as-you-go charges, class fees, equipment, clothing, travel and concessions.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.1] Value for money: the benefit the user gains, judged against the cost. It depends on how often the facility is used and whether it suits the user.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.2] Location and accessibility: the ease of reaching the facility and using it, including distance, transport, parking and access for people with disabilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.3] Range and quality: the number and variety of activities, such as pools, classes, courts or equipment, and the condition of the equipment. Classes should suit different ages and abilities, and there should be family provision.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.4] Convenience: whether the opening hours and booking arrangements suit the user's working and family pattern. Crowding, waiting time and childcare on site also matter.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.5] Safety, hygiene and staffing: clean changing rooms and equipment, qualified instructors and lifeguards, supervision during sessions, first aid, security and emergency procedures.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.6] Atmosphere and suitability: whether the facility suits the user's age, gender, interests and ability, and whether it suits individual or family use.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.1.7] Benefits: social (the chance to meet people and join groups, classes or teams), physical (fitness), emotional (relaxation) and intellectual (skill).
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.b] A facility is only useful if the person can reach it and use it at a time that suits them. Accessibility is about getting there and getting in, and convenience is about fitting the facility into daily life.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.0] Location: a facility close to home or work reduces travel time and cost, so it is more likely to be used regularly.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.1] Transport: the availability of buses or trains and the provision of parking affect how easily a user can get there.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.2] Access for people with disabilities: ramps, lifts, accessible changing rooms and pool hoists allow people with mobility difficulties to use the facility.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.3] Opening hours: early, late and weekend hours suit people who work or attend school during the day.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.4] Booking systems: online booking and advance booking of classes make it easier to plan use, while queues and walk-in only systems can discourage use.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.5] Family access: facilities that allow children and adults to use the same area together make family leisure easier to organise.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.2.6] On-site childcare, short waiting times and uncrowded sessions also make a facility more convenient to use.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.b] The price on the door is only part of what a facility costs. To judge it fairly you need to add up every kind of cost the user will meet over a period such as a year.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.0] Membership fee: a regular fee, usually paid monthly or yearly, that gives unlimited or extended use of the facility.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.1] Joining fee: a one-off payment made when a person first becomes a member of a facility.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.2] Two types of charge a facility makes: fixed charges, such as a membership or joining fee, do not change with use, while variable charges, such as pay-as-you-go, class and equipment fees, rise with each visit.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.3] Pay-as-you-go charge: a fee paid each time the facility is used, which suits people who visit only occasionally. Membership suits regular users.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.4] Class and course fees: extra charges for instructor-led sessions, which are often separate from the basic entry or membership fee.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.5] Concessions: reduced fees for students, children, older people, unemployed people and families, which lower the cost for these groups.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.6] Hidden costs: travel, equipment, clothing, locker or towel hire, parking and refreshments add to the total cost of using a facility.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.3.7] Total annual cost: the sum of membership or joining fees, pay-as-you-go charges, class fees, extras and travel for one year.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.b] Cost is not the same as value. A low price does not make a facility good, because value compares what the user gains with what the user pays.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.0] Value is the benefit a person gains from a facility, measured in fitness, enjoyment, social contact and skill.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.1] Value for money is the benefit gained for each euro spent, so a cheap facility that is rarely used may offer poor value.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.2] A facility offers more value when its range of activities matches the user's interests, and when the user actually uses those activities.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.3] Cost per visit is found by dividing the total annual cost by the number of visits made in the year.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.4] A low cost per visit shows that the facility is used often, which generally means better value for regular users.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.5] Value depends on the user: a person who uses only the pool may find a high-fee gym poor value, even though it offers more activities.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.4.6] A dearer facility used often may give better value than a cheap one used rarely, so value depends on use.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.b] The syllabus asks for a cost and value comparison of two facilities. Follow the same steps each time so that the comparison is fair and ends in a justified conclusion for a named user or family.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.0] Step 1: select two facilities that serve the same community and are used for the same purpose, such as fitness or swimming, for example a local authority leisure centre and a private gym, or a community club and a commercial club.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.1] Step 2: find each facility's fees: membership, joining, pay-as-you-go and class fees. To investigate, list the facilities, visit them or check their websites, and collect the fees.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.2] Step 3: calculate the total annual cost of each facility, including the travel cost of getting there.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.3] Step 4: estimate the number of visits per year for the user, and divide the total annual cost by that number to find the cost per visit.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.4] Step 5: list the benefits each facility gives the user, including fitness, social contact, relaxation and skill, and compare them with the cost. Also compare location, access, hours, range of activities, condition, safety, staffing and atmosphere.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.5.5] Step 6: state which facility gives better value for the user, and say what would change the conclusion, such as a change in visits or income, or a different household member.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.6.b] An evaluation ends in a judgement, not a list. You weigh each facility's strengths against its weaknesses for a particular user, then suggest how the facility could do better.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.6.0] An evaluation weighs the strengths of each facility against its weaknesses, then reaches a judgement for a named type of user.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.6.1] A facility with low fees but few activities may suit a student who wants cheap exercise, but not a family wanting varied activities.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.6.2] When you name a facility, give two developed points, each a strength or a limit. For example, a swimming pool gives low-impact exercise and social contact for retired people, but may involve travel and set sessions.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.6.3] Improvements a facility could make include extended opening hours, reduced fees or concessions for families and older people, better access for people with disabilities, and more classes for older people or families.
[hom-work-leisure-s8.6.4] Improvements to booking and information, such as online booking and online timetables, make a facility easier to use and so raise its value.

CANDIDATES hom-work-leisure-s7: 7. Leisure
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.b] Leisure is time, not an activity. An activity counts as leisure when the person chooses it freely in time left over after necessary tasks.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.0] Leisure is the residual time (the time left over) after essential activities have been completed.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.1] Essential activities include paid work, travel to work, school and study, sleep, meals, personal care, household tasks and caring for dependants. These come first, so they are not leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.2] The individual chooses how to spend leisure time, for ease, relaxation or enjoyment. Because it is freely chosen, leisure is not the same as free time spent on chores.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.3] Leisure pursuits are the activities a person chooses within leisure time, such as sport, music, reading, hobbies, crafts, gardening, socialising, drama, games, cinema and travel.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.4] The same activity can be work for one person and leisure for another, for example gardening or cooking, depending on whether it is chosen and enjoyed.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.5] Leisure can be active (sport, walking, dance) or passive (reading, TV, listening to music), and can be done alone, with friends or with family.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.0.6] Sport is only one form of leisure, so do not treat leisure and sport as the same thing.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.b] Leisure does more than fill spare time. It gives individuals, families and society a set of benefits, which matter more today because many jobs are sedentary and pressured.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.0] Relaxation: leisure relieves the stress and fatigue that come from paid work, study and daily demands, so people recover and cope better.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.1] Physical wellbeing: active leisure improves fitness, weight control, heart health and strong bones and muscles, and helps prevent illness. This matters more as many jobs involve sitting.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.2] Skill development: leisure allows people to learn and practise new skills, from swimming to a musical instrument.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.3] Challenge: competitive and creative pursuits give a sense of achievement and personal growth.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.4] Social interaction: leisure brings people together, forming friendships and wider social networks and community involvement, which reduces isolation.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.5] Family bonding: shared leisure builds relationships, communication and shared memories between family members.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.6] Modelling: parents who use free time constructively are role models, showing children how to do the same.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.1.7] Work-life balance: leisure offsets the demands of work and helps people sustain their working lives, staying productive and avoiding burnout. It also gives enjoyment and gratifies the senses.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.2.b] Leisure benefits the wider community as well as individuals, because what people do in their free time adds up across a whole society.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.2.0] A healthier population results when many people take part in active leisure, which reduces demands on health services.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.2.1] Tourism and sport industries grow from people's demand for leisure activities and travel, which provides jobs and activity in the economy.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.2.2] Community spirit is strengthened when people join clubs, teams and local events together.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.3.b] Leisure can be pursued by a person on their own or shared by a household. Each kind gives different benefits, and families need to balance both.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.3.0] Individual leisure: a person chooses and pursues an activity alone or with a group that is not their family, such as a solo hobby, running or a club.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.3.1] Family leisure: members of one household take part together, such as family outings, walks, cycling, picnics, meals out, board games, cooking, holidays, sport or games at home.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.3.2] Family leisure builds bonds, communication and a shared identity, and gives children common experiences and memories.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.3.3] Family leisure needs activities suitable for all ages and a cost the family can manage, so everyone can take part.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.3.4] Individual leisure allows a person to develop personal interests, independence and identity, which can benefit the whole family.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.b] Leisure helps people develop in different ways. Physical and social development are two of the areas examiners name, so give each its own heading in an answer.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.0] Physical development: sports and active pursuits build muscle tone, improve fitness and increase energy levels.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.1] Physical development: coordination is developed through activities such as ball games, dance and gymnastics, and skills such as swimming are learned.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.2] Physical development: weight-bearing activities, such as walking, running and dancing, help reduce the risk of osteoporosis (a condition where bones become weak and brittle) and help with weight control.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.3] Social development: team sports require co-operation, communication and sharing of responsibility.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.4] Social development: winning and losing teach people to cope with success and failure, which they meet in wider life.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.5] Social development: joining a club or team helps a person make new friends, expand their social circle and feel they belong to a community.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.4.6] Social development: leisure can develop leadership skills, for example through captaining a team or running a club.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.b] Leisure also shapes how people feel and think. Examiners sometimes add intellectual development to physical, social and emotional development.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.0] Emotional development: competitive activities can improve self-esteem and confidence, and give a sense of achievement.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.1] Emotional development: relaxing pursuits such as yoga, reading or gardening reduce stress and support mental wellbeing.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.2] Emotional development: being part of a team or community gives a sense of belonging, and coping with success and failure builds resilience.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.3] Emotional development: fun and enjoyment encourage positive emotions and a positive outlook.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.4] Intellectual development: card games, bridge, chess and puzzles develop concentration, analysis, problem solving and planning.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.5.5] Intellectual development: debating and drama develop listening skills, argument and self-expression, and creative pursuits such as art encourage creativity and learning.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.b] What people do in their free time is shaped by the society and culture around them, not only by personal taste.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.0] Social group: a person's socio-economic group affects which activities are available and affordable.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.1] Local factors: the clubs, facilities and transport in a person's area shape which activities they can join.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.2] Trends: popular activities, such as skateboarding, spread through peer groups and the media, and greater access to foreign travel has made it a common leisure choice.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.3] Technology, social media and education also shape leisure choices, because they change what people know about and can do.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.4] Culture: traditions tied to a country or region, such as hurling, Gaelic football, camogie, Irish music and Irish dancing in Ireland. The GAA (Gaelic Athletic Association) is the national amateur sports body.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.5] Religious and community traditions, and a family's ethnic and cultural background, also influence the activities people take up.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.6.6] Family traditions: activities handed down through families, such as music or sport, shape a person's leisure from childhood, so children often take up the leisure activities their parents enjoy.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.b] A person's job decides how much time and money they have for leisure, and even the kind of leisure they want.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.0] Disposable income: the money left after essential spending determines how much can be spent on leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.1] Cost of leisure: membership fees, joining fees, pay-as-you-go charges and special clothing or equipment, plus travel, all add to the cost.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.2] Low-cost choices: people with little disposable income often choose free or inexpensive activities, such as walking, the library or the park.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.3] Working hours: long or irregular hours reduce the time available for leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.4] Type of work: a sedentary (desk-based) worker may choose an energetic activity, and an active worker may prefer a quiet pursuit, so leisure balances the job.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.5] Leisure can be an extension of work, for example when a person's job involves sport or the arts, or when colleagues' clubs lead to a new pastime.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.6] Part-time work, job-sharing and career breaks create more time for leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.7.7] Recession: a fall in income reduces spending on costly activities such as foreign holidays, while people spend more time on free or home-based activities.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.b] Leisure changes as a family moves through the life cycle (the stages from young adults, to parents of young children, to retirement). The stage a person is at matters more than their age alone.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.0] Young people have fewer commitments and more time for leisure, and teenagers often prefer to spend leisure time with their peers.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.1] Couples with young children usually have the least time and disposable income for leisure, because childcare takes both.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.2] Families with school-going children often have leisure built around school sport, clubs and outings.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.3] Retired couples usually have more time and often more money for leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.4] Older people may have physical limits that restrict them to less strenuous or sedentary pursuits.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.8.5] Some activities are regarded as too dangerous for young children, so families choose age-appropriate pursuits that are suitable and safe for them.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.9.b] Gender can affect which leisure activities people choose and how much time they have for them.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.9.0] Some activities are male- or female-dominated, which can affect whether a person feels welcome to take part.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.9.1] Gender affects the time available for leisure, because household and caring duties still fall mainly on women in many families. Traditionally women had less time for this reason.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.9.2] Changing gender roles have reduced the strength of traditional divisions in leisure choices, and many sports and activities are now open to everyone, though some differences remain.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.10.b] Two further factors affect what a person can do in their leisure time.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.10.0] Health and ability limit or shape the activities a person can take part in.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.10.1] The facilities available locally decide which activities are within reach.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.b] This practical shows how much of a day is really leisure and what leisure costs. It also lets you compare families at different stages of the life cycle.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.0] Aim: to find out how a person spends their day and how much of that time is leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.1] Method step 1: keep a record of each hour of a typical day, noting each activity.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.2] Method step 2: sort each entry into essential activities (sleep, personal care, paid work, household tasks) and leisure.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.3] Method step 3: add the hours in each category and express the leisure time as a percentage of the day.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.4] Method step 4: list each leisure pursuit and cost it, including membership fees, equipment, travel and pay-as-you-go charges.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.5] Method step 5: list the physical, social, emotional and intellectual benefits of each pursuit beside its cost, so that value can be judged.
[hom-work-leisure-s7.11.6] Case study method: compare the time-use records of families at different life-cycle stages, such as a young couple, a couple with young children, teenagers and a retired couple.
