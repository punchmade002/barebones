TARGET hom-cereals-grains-s1: 1. Structure and nutritional significance
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.0.b] A cereal grain is the seed of a grass, and the wheat grain is the standard example. It has three parts, and its nutrients are spread unevenly across them, so what you eat depends on which parts are kept.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.0.0] Bran: the outer covering, made of several layers, about 13% of the wheat grain. It is made mainly of cellulose, an insoluble fibre, and it is removed when white flour is milled.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.0.1] Endosperm: the inner and largest part of the grain. It stores starch, and it also contains most of the grain's protein, which forms gluten when wheat flour is mixed with water. White flour is made from it, and it is the food store for the young plant.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.0.2] Germ: the small embryo at the base of the grain, about 2% of the grain, which would grow into a new plant. It is rich in vitamin E, unsaturated fat, protein and B group vitamins.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.0.3] Diagram: longitudinal section (cut lengthways) of the wheat grain, showing bran around the outside, a large endosperm, and the germ at the base. Label all three parts and the crease along one side.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.b] Each part of the grain supplies different nutrients, so you need to name the nutrient and say what it does in the body.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.0] Bran: its cellulose is dietary fibre (roughage), which adds bulk to faeces, absorbs water and stimulates peristalsis (the muscle waves that push food along the gut). This prevents constipation and aids digestion.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.1] Bran also supplies B group vitamins and minerals such as iron, magnesium and phosphorus.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.2] Endosperm: starch is the main carbohydrate in cereals and supplies energy for heat and activity. The protein it contains is of low biological value, meaning it is low in one or more essential amino acids.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.3] Germ: it supplies most of the grain's unsaturated fat, and vitamin E, which acts as an antioxidant (it protects body cells from damage).
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.4] Germ: it also supplies protein and B group vitamins such as thiamine (vitamin B1), which are concentrated in the germ.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.1.5] Refining removes most of the bran and germ, so white flour and refined cereals lose most of their fibre, B group vitamins, vitamin E and minerals unless these are added back.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.b] The nutritive value of a food is the nutrients it supplies. Cereals are mainly a starchy food, with smaller amounts of several other nutrients.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.0] Protein: provides protein for growth and repair of body cells, but cereal protein is of low biological value because it is low in the essential amino acid lysine (an amino acid the body cannot make).
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.1] Eating cereals with pulses, milk or eggs improves the protein's value, because the foods make up for each other's missing amino acids (complementary proteins).
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.2] Carbohydrate: starch provides energy for heat and activity, and wholegrain cereals also provide fibre.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.3] Fibre: wholegrain cereals provide insoluble fibre from the bran, and oats and barley provide soluble fibre.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.4] Fat: cereals are low in total fat, and the fat they contain is mainly unsaturated and concentrated in the germ.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.5] Vitamins: B group vitamins (thiamine, niacin and riboflavin) and vitamin E, which is found in the germ.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.6] Minerals: calcium, iron and phosphorus, much of the iron and phosphorus being found in the bran and germ.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.2.7] Water: cereals are dry foods with a low water content, so they keep well when stored correctly.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.b] The dietetic value of a food is how useful it is in the diet, for health and for everyday eating. Cereals score well for cost, fibre and convenience, with a few limits.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.0] Energy: starchy cereals are an inexpensive source of energy and form the staple food (the food a diet is built around) in many countries.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.1] Constipation: the fibre in wholegrain cereals increases stool bulk and stimulates peristalsis, so it helps prevent constipation and bowel disorders.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.2] Blood cholesterol and blood sugar: soluble fibre from oats and barley can help lower blood cholesterol and slows the absorption of glucose after a meal.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.3] Satiety (feeling full): fibre-rich foods give a feeling of fullness, which supports weight control.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.4] Fat: the fat in cereals is unsaturated, so it does not contribute to cholesterol build-up.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.5] Versatility: cereals can be used in sweet and savoury dishes, and they are quick and easy to prepare and inexpensive.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.6] Limit, coeliac disease: gluten, found in wheat, barley and rye, and in oats unless they are labelled gluten-free, cannot be tolerated by people with coeliac disease.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.3.7] Limit, phytic acid: phytic acid in the bran binds iron and calcium (so the body cannot absorb them) and reduces their absorption.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.b] A wholegrain cereal keeps the whole grain rather than just the starchy centre. This is why wholegrain products are promoted as part of a healthy diet.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.0] A wholegrain cereal product contains all three parts of the grain: bran, endosperm and germ. Examples are wholemeal flour and bread, brown rice, porridge oats, wholegrain breakfast cereals and wholewheat pasta.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.1] Wholegrain products provide more fibre, B group vitamins, vitamin E and minerals than refined products made from the same cereal. They also release energy more slowly and are filling, so their fibre raises fibre intake.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.2] In the Food Pyramid, wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice form the second shelf, so wholegrain choices fit into the balance of the whole diet.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.3] Phytic acid in the bran binds iron, calcium and zinc and reduces their absorption; very high fibre intakes can have the same effect.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.4] Beans on wholemeal toast is an example of combining cereal protein with pulses, which raises the biological value of the protein.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.5] A wholegrain label does not cancel excess sugar, fat or salt added to some products, so the nutrition panel must still be checked.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.4.6] A high-fibre day for a school child could use wholegrain breakfast cereal, brown bread, and brown rice or wholewheat pasta.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.b] Cereals are healthy for most people, but some people avoid or limit them for medical or dietary reasons.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.0] Coeliac disease or gluten intolerance: gluten damages the lining of the small intestine, so gluten-containing cereals must be avoided.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.1] Weight gain: large portions of starchy foods, especially with added fat or sugar, supply more energy than the body uses, so low-carbohydrate or weight-loss diets limit them.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.2] Blood sugar: refined cereals are digested quickly and raise blood glucose quickly, so people managing blood sugar, or following low glycaemic index diets, often choose wholegrain cereals.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.3] Bloating: some people limit cereals because they cause bloating.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.4] Hidden salt and sugar: many breakfast cereals, breads and sauces contain added salt or sugar.
[hom-cereals-grains-s1.5.5] Main sources of cereals in the Irish diet are bread, pasta, rice, breakfast cereals, porridge, biscuits and cakes.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s3: 3. Sources
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.0.b] Monosaccharides are the single-unit sugars, the simplest carbohydrates. Only some turn up in food as themselves, so it helps to know which foods supply each one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.0.0] Glucose occurs naturally in fruit, such as grapes, and in some vegetables, so everyday fruit and vegetables supply it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.0.1] Glucose syrup, a refined sugar used in sweets and processed foods, is another source of glucose in the diet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.0.2] Fructose, also called fruit sugar, occurs naturally in fruit and is the main sugar in honey. Honey contains both fructose and glucose, with fructose the larger part.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.0.3] Galactose is not usually eaten free in food. It is released when lactose, the sugar in milk, is digested, which is why the marking schemes call it the sugar from digested milk.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.0.4] Galactose comes from lactose, not from a plant food, so it is not a sugar you eat in quantity.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.b] Disaccharides are double sugars, made of two sugar units joined together. Each common one has a typical food source, and much of the sugar in a modern diet is sucrose added to processed food.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.0] Sucrose, the table sugar, is extracted from sugar beet and sugar cane and sold as white and brown sugar. It also occurs naturally in many fruit and vegetables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.1] Sucrose is added to biscuits, cakes, sweets, jam, fizzy drinks, breakfast cereals and other processed foods.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.2] Lactose, the milk sugar, is found in milk and milk products such as yoghurt. If cheese is mentioned, only say that hard cheese has almost no lactose, because most of it is lost in the whey or fermented in making it; fresh and soft cheeses keep some.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.3] Maltose, the malt sugar, is produced when starch is broken down during the germination of cereal grains, especially barley, which is the basis of malt. Malt is found in malted drinks, malt extract and malt loaf, and is used in brewing.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.4] Added (free) sugars in processed food include those in biscuits, cakes, sweets, chocolate, jam, fizzy drinks, ice cream, breakfast cereals and sauces. Check the ingredients list to find them.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.1.5] Natural sugars in whole fruit and vegetables and in milk come packaged with fibre and other nutrients. Added sugars supply energy with few other nutrients, which is why the two are not equal.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.2.b] Starch is the main carbohydrate in the Irish diet and the main energy-supplying carbohydrate in staple foods (the basic foods a diet is built on). It comes mostly from cereals and from root crops.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.2.0] Cereals and cereal products are rich in starch: bread, breakfast cereals, porridge oats, pasta, rice and flour. The grains themselves, such as wheat, oats, barley, maize and rice, are rich sources, and flour is made from ground grain.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.2.1] Potatoes, rice, pasta and bread are the main starch foods in the Irish diet, so they are the easiest examples to give.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.2.2] Potatoes and other root vegetables contain starch, and so do pulses such as peas, beans and lentils, which also contain fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.2.3] Porridge oats and many breakfast cereals are starch-based, so a breakfast of cereal or porridge is a starch source.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.2.4] Glycogen, a polysaccharide, is present in small amounts in meat. It is not a significant dietary source.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.b] Non-starch polysaccharides (NSP) are the plant polysaccharides, mainly cellulose and pectin, that human digestive enzymes cannot break down. Together they make up the dietary fibre of food, the part of plant food that passes through the gut undigested.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.0] Cellulose is found in the skins of fruit and vegetables, in leafy and other vegetables, in wholegrain cereals and in bran. Wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta, nuts and seeds also contain it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.1] Pectin is found in the cell walls of fruit, especially apples and citrus fruit (the peel and pith of citrus). It is also in soft fruits such as gooseberries and blackcurrants, and unripe fruit has more.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.2] Wholemeal and wholegrain products have more fibre than white versions because the bran, the outer layer of the grain, is kept. White flour and polished (white) rice have the bran removed in refining, so white bread has little fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.3] Seeds such as sunflower, sesame, pumpkin and linseed are high in fibre, as are nuts, and peas, beans and lentils.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.4] Other good fibre sources are the skins of fruit and vegetables, fruit and vegetables generally, wholemeal bread, brown rice and pasta, bran, porridge oats and high-fibre breakfast cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.3.5] Fibre comes only from plants, so meat, fish, eggs and dairy foods contain none.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.4.b] Fibre sources fall into two kinds, depending on whether the fibre dissolves in water. This sorts the foods above into two groups.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.4.0] Insoluble fibre is cellulose and wheat bran. Its sources are wholemeal bread, bran and vegetable skins.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.4.1] Soluble fibre is pectin, plus the soluble fibre in oats, beans and lentils. Its sources are fruit, oats and pulses.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.b] Exam questions often ask for sugar, starch and fibre sources side by side. A summary of foods by carbohydrate makes these easy to answer, and many foods contain more than one kind.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.0] Sugars: fruit, honey, table sugar, sweets, jam, cakes, biscuits and milk. Starch: rice, pasta, potatoes, bread, flour and cereals. Fibre: wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetable skins, nuts, seeds and brown rice.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.1] Bread has starch, some sugar and fibre, with more fibre in wholemeal bread. Wholemeal bread should not be called high in sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.2] Fruit has sugars, pectin and cellulose, so it supplies a sugar and fibre together.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.3] Potatoes in their skins have starch and fibre, while milk has lactose only.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.4] Higher Level table: glucose, fructose and galactose come from fruit, honey and digested milk. Sucrose, lactose and maltose come from table sugar, milk and barley. Starch, cellulose and pectin come from potatoes and cereals, vegetables and fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s3.5.5] Poster or collage: head a poster with the classification groups (sugars, starch, fibre) and stick pictures of foods under each group.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s4: 4. Sources
[hom-proteins-s4.0.b] Protein comes from both animal and plant foods, so everyone can meet their needs whatever they eat. Exam answers earn marks for naming a variety of foods from each group.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.0] Animal protein sources are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Cheese and yoghurt belong in the list because they are made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.1] Plant protein sources are soya beans and soya products, pulses (lentils, peas and beans), cereals (wheat, rice, maize and oats), nuts and seeds.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.2] Cereals such as wheat, rice and maize supply protein as well as carbohydrate, so everyday foods like wheat, bread and pasta are plant protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.3] Potatoes and vegetables contain only small amounts of protein, so they are not counted as main protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.b] Albumin is the protein most people meet first, in the white of an egg. It is a good example of a protein that is tied to one particular food.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.0] Albumin is the main protein of egg white (ovalbumin), and it is also found in lean meat.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.1] Lactalbumin, a related albumin, is found in the whey of milk, the watery part left when the casein is removed.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.2] Albumin is a soluble globular protein. It dissolves in water and coagulates (sets) when heated, which is why raw egg white turns solid and white on cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.b] Casein is the main protein of milk, so it follows milk into every food made from milk. Cheese-making depends on changing it.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.0] Casein (the older term is caseinogen) is the main protein of milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.1] Casein is present in cheese and yoghurt, the products made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.2] Casein is coagulated by the enzyme rennin, which is the first step in making cheese because it forms the curd. Acid also coagulates it, which is why milk curdles when it sours.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.b] Gelatine is the protein behind jelly, but it is not found in raw meat. It is made when another protein, collagen, is heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.0] Gelatine is not present in raw meat; it forms when collagen from animal bones, skin and connective tissue is heated in moisture.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.1] Gelatine sets to a gel when it cools, so it is used in jelly, aspic, gelatine-set mousses and desserts, marshmallows and some sweets.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.2] Gelatine comes from animal sources, so foods set with it are not vegetarian.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.b] Gluten is the protein that gives wheat bread its structure. It does not exist in dry flour; it is formed when the flour is wet.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.0] Gluten is the protein of wheat and wheat flour.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.1] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin, both already present in the flour, are mixed with water. Flour itself contains gliadin and glutenin, not formed gluten.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.2] Gluten is elastic and stretchy, and kneading develops it. It gives bread dough its elastic, stretchy texture and traps the carbon dioxide made by yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.3] Foods that depend on wheat gluten include bread, pasta, cakes, pastry and other flour products.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.b] Each of these proteins is tied to a food or food group. Pairing every named protein with one food is the quickest way to answer a naming question.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.0] Albumin: found in egg white and meat, and it sets when heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.1] Casein: found in milk, cheese and yoghurt, and it is coagulated by rennin.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.2] Gelatine: found in set desserts such as jelly, and it is made from collagen.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.3] Gluten: found in wheat flour and bread, and it traps gas in dough.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.b] Meat and fish are made of muscle and connective tissue, and each contains its own proteins. Higher Level questions ask you to name them.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they contract to move the muscle. They are found in the flesh of meat and fish.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.1] Collagen is the main protein of connective tissue and is found in meat, fish and bones.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.2] Collagen in meat and fish is the source of gelatine when it is heated in moisture; this is why cheap tough cuts of meat tenderise on slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.3] Elastin in meat and globulin in egg are also acceptable answers when asked for proteins in meat or egg.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s3: 3. The other B-group vitamins
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.b] The B-complex is a family of separate water-soluble vitamins that often occur together in the same foods. They are named by number or by name, and between them they keep energy release, nerves, skin and blood working.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.0] The family includes B1 (thiamine), B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin) and B6 (pyridoxine), plus folate and B12, which also belong to the B complex.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.1] The B-complex vitamins act mainly as coenzymes (helpers that enzymes need in order to work), helping the enzymes that release energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein. They also keep the nervous system, skin and red blood cells healthy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.2] The B vitamins do not supply energy themselves. The energy comes from the carbohydrate, fat and protein in food, and the vitamins only help release it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.3] The B-complex vitamins are water-soluble, so any excess is excreted in urine. Most are not stored in the body in useful amounts, so they must be supplied daily in the diet. B12 is the exception, as it is stored in the liver.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.4] Because they dissolve in water, B vitamins are also lost into cooking water and during storage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.5] Wholegrain cereals, meat, pulses and fortified breakfast cereals are the main dietary sources. Fish, eggs, milk and dairy, nuts, yeast extract and green vegetables also contribute.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.6] White flour and polished rice lose much of their B vitamins in milling (grinding and refining the grain), whereas wholegrain products keep them.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.0.7] The RDA (recommended daily allowance) is the amount that meets the needs of almost everyone. B vitamin needs rise with energy intake, especially thiamine and niacin, which are closely linked to energy release.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.b] Thiamine is the B vitamin most closely tied to carbohydrate, so the more carbohydrate a person eats, the more thiamine they need. Without it the nerves, muscles and heart suffer.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.0] Thiamine sources include wholegrain cereals and wholemeal bread, fortified breakfast cereals, pork and bacon, liver, pulses (peas, beans, lentils), nuts, yeast extract, eggs and potatoes.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.1] Thiamine acts as a coenzyme that helps release energy from carbohydrate, so the need for it rises with carbohydrate intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.2] Thiamine is needed for normal nerve function and keeps the muscles, including the heart, healthy. It also supports appetite.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.3] Beriberi (beri-beri) is the thiamine deficiency disease, with loss of appetite, fatigue, weakness, muscle wasting, depression, irritability and memory loss.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.4] Dry beriberi damages the nerves, causing tingling, numbness, pain, weakness and paralysis. Wet beriberi affects the heart, causing heart failure and swelling (oedema).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.1.5] Heavy drinkers are at risk of deficiency because heavy alcohol use reduces the absorption of thiamine and the diet is often poor. This can lead to confusion and memory problems.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.b] Riboflavin helps release energy from all three energy nutrients and protects the surfaces of the body, such as the skin, mouth and eyes. Its deficiency shows up mostly in these places.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.0] Riboflavin sources include milk and dairy products (the largest contribution in many diets), eggs, liver and kidney, lean meat, yeast extract, green leafy vegetables, mushrooms and fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.1] Riboflavin helps release energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.2] Riboflavin helps maintain healthy skin, eyes and mucous membranes (the moist linings of the mouth and other body passages), and it helps growth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.3] A deficiency causes sore, cracked lips and cracks and soreness at the corners of the mouth, known as angular cheilitis (also called cheilosis or angular stomatitis).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.4] A deficiency also causes a sore, red, smooth, inflamed tongue and skin disorders, such as greasy, scaly skin around the nose.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.2.5] Deficiency also causes sore eyes that are sensitive to light, and slow growth in children.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.b] Niacin, also called nicotinic acid, is unusual because the body can make some of its own. It helps release energy and keeps three body systems healthy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.0] Niacin sources include meat (especially liver and poultry), fish, peanuts and other nuts, wholegrain cereals, pulses, mushrooms, eggs, milk, fortified cereals and yeast extract.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.1] The body can make niacin from the amino acid tryptophan, found in protein foods such as milk and eggs. This is why milk and eggs prevent deficiency even though they contain little niacin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.2] Niacin helps release energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein in the cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.3] Niacin keeps the skin, nervous system and digestive system healthy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.4] Deficiency causes pellagra, remembered as the four Ds: dermatitis (a skin rash in areas exposed to sunlight), diarrhoea, dementia (confusion and memory loss) and, if untreated, death.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.3.5] Deficiency may also bring tiredness, weight loss and irritability. Pellagra was historically found where maize was the main food.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.4.b] Pyridoxine is the B vitamin of protein use. It lets the body handle amino acids, build haemoglobin and make the chemicals nerves use to communicate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.4.0] Pyridoxine sources include chicken, turkey, pork, fish such as tuna and salmon, liver, eggs, potatoes, bananas, pulses, wholegrain cereals, nuts and fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.4.1] Pyridoxine is needed for the breakdown and use (metabolism) of amino acids, so it is central to protein metabolism and growth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.4.2] Pyridoxine helps the body form haemoglobin in red blood cells, which is why deficiency can cause anaemia.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.4.3] Pyridoxine takes part in making neurotransmitters, the chemicals that carry messages between nerve cells, so it supports the nervous system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.4.4] Deficiency is rare. It causes skin rashes around the mouth and nose, a sore mouth, confusion, depression, irritability, anaemia, and tingling in the hands and feet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.5.b] Exams often give a list of conditions and ask which vitamin is lacking in each. These pairs are worth learning as one set.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.5.0] Night blindness is vitamin A, rickets is vitamin D and scurvy is vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.5.1] Beri-beri is vitamin B1 (thiamine) and pellagra is niacin (B3).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.5.2] Neural tube defects are linked to folic acid. Megaloblastic anaemia is B12 (or folate) deficiency, and pernicious anaemia is B12 deficiency caused by a lack of intrinsic factor.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.b] Being water-soluble, most B vitamins are lost into cooking water, and most are also sensitive to heat, light, alkalis or oxidation. Each vitamin has its own main weakness, and niacin and riboflavin are the more stable ones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.0] Thiamine is destroyed by alkalis (such as bicarbonate of soda) and by prolonged heat, and it is lost into cooking water. It is also destroyed by sulphur dioxide (sulphites) used as a preservative, but it is stable in acid conditions.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.1] Riboflavin is destroyed by light (ultraviolet) and by alkalis, which is why milk in clear bottles loses riboflavin and is better stored in cartons or opaque bottles. Some is also lost in cooking water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.2] Riboflavin is heat-stable and acid-stable in normal cooking, and it gives a yellow-green colour to solutions that contain it, such as the yellow of some foods and urine.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.3] Niacin is the most stable of the B vitamins. It is stable to heat, light, air, acid and alkali, so little is lost in normal cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.4] Niacin is soluble in water and can be lost into cooking water if the food is boiled and drained.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s3.6.5] Pyridoxine is sensitive to light and alkali, and it is lost into cooking water. It is stable in acid conditions and fairly stable to heat in ordinary cooking, but is lost in the processing and milling of cereals.
