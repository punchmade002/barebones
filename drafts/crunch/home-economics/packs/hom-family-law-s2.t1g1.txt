TARGET hom-family-law-s2: 2. The Family Home Protection Act 1976
[hom-family-law-s2.0.b] Without this law, a spouse who owned the family home could sell or mortgage it without the other knowing and leave the family homeless. The Act gives the family, and especially the children, security of a home.
[hom-family-law-s2.0.0] The family home is the dwelling in which the spouses ordinarily live together as their home. The Act protects the family from losing it because one spouse deals with it alone.
[hom-family-law-s2.0.1] The spouse who does not own the home, and any dependent children, are the people the Act mainly protects, because they are the ones who would lose their home if the owner sold it.
[hom-family-law-s2.0.2] The Act protects the family home whether it is owned outright or held in only one spouse's name, so whose name is on the title deeds (the documents that show ownership) makes no difference.
[hom-family-law-s2.0.3] A spouse with no legal ownership of the home is still protected. The Act does not make that spouse an owner; it gives a veto, meaning the power to block a sale, lease or mortgage of the home.
[hom-family-law-s2.0.4] The protection depends on marriage and applies while the spouses are married and living together in the home. Unmarried cohabiting couples are not covered by this Act.
[hom-family-law-s2.0.5] The Act is in force as amended by the Family Law Act 1995.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.b] The core of the Act is a consent rule. One spouse cannot deal with the family home alone, and the agreement of the other spouse must be written and given in advance.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.0] A spouse cannot sell, lease (let to someone else for a period) or mortgage (borrow money using the home as security) the family home without the written consent of the other spouse. All three actions are covered.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.1] The consent must be given before the deal (prior consent), so agreement given afterwards does not meet the rule.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.2] Consent must be in writing, so an oral agreement to a sale is not enough. A general written consent given in advance can count as consent for later deals, following the 1995 amendment.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.3] If the required consent is missing, the sale, lease or mortgage is void, which means it has no legal effect, and this is why the rule protects the family so strongly.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.4] Under s.3(8)(b), a dealing without consent is treated as not void unless a court declares it void or the parties state in writing within six years that it is void.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.5] There are limited exceptions to the voidness rule, such as a purchaser who paid full value and did not know it was the family home.
[hom-family-law-s2.1.6] A purchaser or lender should check that the required spousal consent has been given before completing a transaction involving the home, because without it the deal may be void.
[hom-family-law-s2.2.b] The Act works as a sequence of steps, from checking that the home is covered to what happens if the rules are ignored or consent is refused.
[hom-family-law-s2.2.0] 1. Establish that the property is the family home, meaning the dwelling where the spouses ordinarily live together.
[hom-family-law-s2.2.1] 2. Before any sale, lease or mortgage, the owner-spouse must obtain the other spouse's written consent.
[hom-family-law-s2.2.2] 3. If consent is withheld unreasonably, the owner-spouse can apply to the court to dispense with (do without) the consent. The court does so only where it is unreasonable to withhold it, taking all circumstances into account, including the needs of any dependent children.
[hom-family-law-s2.2.3] 4. If a transaction goes ahead without the required consent, it is void, because the Act itself says so, and it has no legal effect.
[hom-family-law-s2.2.4] The court can also dispense with or give the consent where the other spouse cannot be found after reasonable enquiries, lacks the mental capacity to consent, or has deserted, so a missing or incapable spouse does not block every deal.
[hom-family-law-s2.3.b] The Act has practical effects on lenders, and it is one of several laws that protect a family when a marriage breaks down.
[hom-family-law-s2.3.0] A lender taking a mortgage over a family home will want the other spouse's written consent before it lends, which is the practical effect of the Act on housing finance.
[hom-family-law-s2.3.1] The protection is in addition to any ownership rights, so it does not depend on whose name appears on the title deeds.
[hom-family-law-s2.3.2] The Family Home Protection Act protects the home during the marriage. On judicial separation or divorce, the court can make orders about who keeps the family home as part of its wider powers.
[hom-family-law-s2.3.3] Under the Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989 and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996, a court can make property orders that transfer or adjust ownership of the family home between the spouses.
[hom-family-law-s2.3.4] A spouse can register a notice of the marriage against the title of the home, which warns any future buyer or lender that the other spouse's written consent is needed.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s2: 2. Cultural variations and the marriage relationship
[hom-marriage-s2.0.b] The arrangements around marriage are not the same everywhere. They differ in who may marry, who chooses the partner and how many partners a person may have.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.0] The minimum age for marriage varies between countries and cultures; in Ireland both partners must be at least 18.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.1] In an arranged marriage, parents or families choose the spouse on the basis of background and suitability, usually with the couple's consent. This contrasts with a marriage freely chosen by the couple themselves.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.2] Arranged marriage is traditional in some communities, for example Sikh, Japanese and Travelling communities, but not for every member of those communities.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.3] Some cultures restrict the choice of spouse by religion, social class or kinship (family relationship), meaning a person may not marry someone from a particular group.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.4] Monogamy, meaning one husband and one wife at a time, is the norm in Western countries, including Ireland, so one marriage at a time is also what the law there recognises.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.5] Polygamy, meaning more than one spouse at a time, is the general term and is not permitted in Ireland. Marrying again without the death or divorce of the first spouse is bigamy, which is a criminal offence.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.6] Polygamy has two forms: polygyny, where one man has more than one wife, and polyandry, where one woman has more than one husband.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.7] Same-sex marriage is legal in some countries, including Ireland since the Marriage Act 2015, and not recognised in others.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.b] The same legal marriage can be celebrated in different ways, depending on the couple's beliefs. The choice of ceremony is one more way marriage varies between people and cultures.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.0] Marriage may be a civil ceremony before a registrar or a religious ceremony; both are legally valid when the marriage is registered.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.1] Some couples choose a secular or humanist ceremony, which has no religious content.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.2] Interfaith marriages join partners of different religions, so the couple must agree how the ceremony and their family's religious life will be arranged, for example which traditions the children will follow.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.b] Weddings are full of customs that carry meaning for the people who follow them. These customs differ by culture and religion, in dress, symbols, venue and the form of the ceremony.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.0] Dress and symbols such as a veil and a wedding ring, and throwing rice or confetti, are wedding customs that have been popular for centuries in many cultures.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.1] Jewish weddings are held under a canopy, which symbolises the couple's future home.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.2] At the end of a Jewish wedding, a glass is broken under foot to recall the destruction of the ancient Temple in Jerusalem.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.3] Quaker couples marry at a public gathering with no priest or official present, because Quakers believe God alone joins a couple as husband and wife.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.4] The ceremony can take place in a church, mosque, synagogue, registry office or other approved location, depending on the couple's religion and the law of the country.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.b] Marriage is more than a private promise. It is a public commitment that carries legal obligations, and couples keep it going through how they live day to day.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.0] Spouses make a public commitment to each other, usually through vows or declarations before witnesses during the ceremony, sealed by registration of the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.1] Because marriage carries legal obligations, spouses owe each other the rights and responsibilities below, which the law can enforce.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.2] Commitment is maintained, so that the marriage lasts, by working on the relationship, communicating positively and remaining faithful and loyal.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.3] Commitment is also shown through sharing responsibility for the home, money and children, for example through shared finances and a shared home.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.b] A right is something each spouse is entitled to receive from the other because they are married. These rights are the other side of the responsibilities each partner takes on.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.0] Each other's company: spouses are entitled to live together as a couple.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.1] Sexual relations: each spouse is entitled to sexual relations with the other, and consummation is the basis of the marriage. An unconsummated marriage can be annulled (declared never to have been valid) in some circumstances.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.2] Faithfulness: each spouse is entitled to the other's loyalty and faithfulness within a monogamous marriage, because the marriage is a promise to one partner only.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.3] Inheritance: under the Succession Act 1965, a spouse who dies without a will (intestate) leaves the whole estate to the surviving spouse if there are no children.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.4] If there are children and no will, the surviving spouse takes two-thirds of the estate and the children share one-third.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.5] Legal right share: a surviving spouse can claim a share of the estate even where a will leaves them less, so a will cannot cut them out. The share is one half if there are no children and one third if there are children.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.b] Marriage also places duties on each partner, towards each other and towards their children. Some of these are backed by law and some depend on commitment.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.0] Financial support: each spouse must support the other (a dependent spouse) and any dependent children. The Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976 allows a spouse to seek maintenance through the court.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.1] Joint guardianship: married parents are both guardians of their children and share responsibility for their care and upbringing, under the Guardianship of Infants Act 1964 as amended by the Children and Family Relationships Act 2015.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.2] Children's needs: parents must provide for their children's physical, emotional, social, moral, educational and spiritual needs.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.3] Commitment: spouses must work on the relationship, show loyalty and keep their commitment to the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.4] Positive communication: spouses must talk openly about decisions, finances and family matters to keep the marriage functioning.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.5] Caring for a sick spouse is also a responsibility, since marriage means looking after your partner when they are ill.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s2: 2. Housing finance
[hom-housing-s2.0.b] Few buyers can pay for a home from savings alone, so most borrow. The amount a buyer must borrow is the purchase price minus the deposit, plus any costs of buying that the buyer cannot pay from savings.
[hom-housing-s2.0.0] The factors that determine the amount of finance required are the purchase price (which depends on the size, type and location of the house, and whether it is new or second-hand) and the buyer's own savings and deposit.
[hom-housing-s2.0.1] Additional costs of buying add to the total needed, so a buyer must budget for more than the price: solicitor's (legal) fees for the transfer of ownership, and stamp duty, a tax paid on the purchase.
[hom-housing-s2.0.2] Further costs are the valuation and survey carried out for the lender (to check what the house is worth and what condition it is in), an application or lender fee, land registry fees (to record the new owner), home insurance, and any fitting-out, furnishing or repair costs.
[hom-housing-s2.0.3] A deposit is the buyer's own money paid towards the purchase price. Lenders require a minimum percentage deposit, set by Central Bank rules, and supply the balance as a mortgage, so saving a deposit is a main barrier for first-time buyers.
[hom-housing-s2.0.4] A mortgage is a long-term loan to buy property, repaid with interest in regular (usually monthly) instalments over a term of many years. Interest is the cost of borrowing the money.
[hom-housing-s2.0.5] A mortgage is secured on the house itself, which means that if repayments are not made the lender can take possession of the property and sell it to recover the loan. This is called repossession.
[hom-housing-s2.1.b] A buyer usually combines several sources of money. Some are the buyer's own, some are borrowed from a lender, and some come from the State or family.
[hom-housing-s2.1.0] The buyer's own savings are the first source, and they make up the deposit that lenders require before they will lend the balance.
[hom-housing-s2.1.1] A mortgage from a bank or other lending institution is the main source of finance, because it provides the large sum that savings cannot cover.
[hom-housing-s2.1.2] A local authority home loan is government-backed and is for people who qualify but cannot get a commercial loan.
[hom-housing-s2.1.3] A credit union loan is usually a smaller amount, for example to help with a deposit or home improvements.
[hom-housing-s2.1.4] Help from parents or family, as a gift or a loan, is often used towards the deposit.
[hom-housing-s2.1.5] State supports for buyers include the First Home shared equity scheme (the State and participating lenders take a share in a new home, which the buyer can buy back), the Help to Buy incentive for first-time buyers of new homes, and affordable purchase schemes run by local authorities.
[hom-housing-s2.2.b] A lending institution assesses whether the applicant can repay the loan and whether the property is a suitable security for it. Each condition below protects the lender by making sure the loan can be repaid, or the property sold if it is not.
[hom-housing-s2.2.0] Borrowing limit: the loan is capped at a set multiple of the applicant's gross income (income before tax), so the borrower is not lent more than can be repaid. The multiple is set by Central Bank rules and changes.
[hom-housing-s2.2.1] Loan-to-value limit: the loan cannot exceed a set share of the property's value, so the buyer must provide the balance as a deposit. The rules differ for first-time buyers and others.
[hom-housing-s2.2.2] Secure, continuous employment: the applicant must be in stable work so that repayments can be met over the term. The self-employed supply accounts instead.
[hom-housing-s2.2.3] Proof of income: payslips, a P60 (the yearly summary of pay and tax), and tax and bank statements show the lender what the applicant earns.
[hom-housing-s2.2.4] Good financial record: a clean credit history with no missed repayments or unpaid debts, a record of regular saving and reasonable spending habits. Existing debts and commitments reduce the amount lent.
[hom-housing-s2.2.5] Term of the loan: repayments are spread over a long period, but an older applicant is usually offered a shorter term so the loan ends before retirement.
[hom-housing-s2.2.6] Property: the lender has it valued and surveyed, and it must be in good condition and good value as security. The lender checks the title deeds through a solicitor, and the buyer must have buildings insurance for the term.
[hom-housing-s2.2.7] Mortgage protection insurance, plus proof of identity and address so the lender can confirm who the borrower is.
[hom-housing-s2.3.b] A mortgage is a large debt that may last decades, and the borrower could die before it is repaid. A mortgage protection policy is a life assurance policy that protects the lender's loan and the borrower's family if the borrower dies during the term.
[hom-housing-s2.3.0] Mortgage protection is a term life assurance policy for the length of the mortgage and the amount of the loan. Life assurance means premiums (regular payments) are made and a sum is paid out when the insured person dies.
[hom-housing-s2.3.1] If the borrower dies during the term, the insurer repays the outstanding mortgage balance to the lender, so the family keeps the home.
[hom-housing-s2.3.2] Without cover, the remaining debt would fall on the borrower's family, who could be forced to sell the home.
[hom-housing-s2.3.3] Life assurance in general can run for life, whereas mortgage protection runs for the term only, and the sum insured falls as the loan is repaid (a decreasing term policy).
[hom-housing-s2.3.4] Under the Consumer Credit Act 1995 a lender must make sure that the borrower has mortgage protection, with exceptions such as where cover cannot be obtained because of age or health.
[hom-housing-s2.3.5] The borrower may buy mortgage protection from the lender's group scheme, an insurer or a broker, and cannot be forced to buy it from the lender.
[hom-housing-s2.3.6] Mortgage protection does not pay the repayments if the borrower is ill, disabled or made redundant, because it pays only on death. Income protection is a separate policy.
[hom-housing-s2.3.7] Annuity and pension-linked mortgages require a mortgage protection policy to be taken out with the loan.
[hom-housing-s2.3.8] An endowment mortgage does not need a separate protection policy, because its life assurance policy already covers the loan.
[hom-housing-s2.4.b] Mortgages differ in how the capital (the sum borrowed) and the interest are repaid. Annuity, endowment, pension-linked and tracker mortgages are the main types to be able to describe.
[hom-housing-s2.4.0] Annuity (repayment) mortgage: each repayment pays part of the interest and part of the capital borrowed, so the amount owed falls over time. Early repayments are mostly interest. It is the most common type, the rate may be fixed, variable or a combination, and it needs mortgage protection.
[hom-housing-s2.4.1] Endowment mortgage: the borrower pays only interest on the loan, plus a premium on a life assurance (endowment) policy designed to repay the loan when it matures. The policy yield may not fully cover the loan, and no separate protection policy is needed. It is no longer widely available.
[hom-housing-s2.4.2] Pension-linked mortgage: interest is paid on the loan and contributions are paid into a pension scheme, and the loan is repaid from the pension lump sum on retirement. It needs mortgage protection, is rare today, and was popular with self-employed people because of tax relief on pension contributions.
[hom-housing-s2.4.3] Tracker mortgage: the interest rate follows a published benchmark rate, the European Central Bank (ECB) rate, plus a fixed margin, so repayments rise and fall with the ECB rate. Trackers are no longer offered to new borrowers, but you may be asked to describe one.
[hom-housing-s2.4.4] Interest-only mortgage: the borrower pays only the interest for a period, so the capital is still owed at the end of that period. It is rarely offered.
[hom-housing-s2.4.5] Current account (offset) mortgage: savings held in a linked current account reduce the balance on which interest is charged. It is rarely offered.
[hom-housing-s2.4.6] Local authority mortgages and loans are also accepted as types of home finance.
[hom-housing-s2.5.b] Interest is the cost of borrowing, so the rate on a mortgage decides how much the borrower pays each month and in total. A borrower can choose how the rate behaves and how long the loan lasts.
[hom-housing-s2.5.0] A fixed rate is set for an agreed period of a number of years, so repayments are the same during that period and the buyer is protected from rises. The buyer misses out if rates fall, and ending early may carry a charge.
[hom-housing-s2.5.1] A variable rate changes with the lender's rate, which tends to follow market and ECB rates, so repayments rise when rates rise and fall when rates fall. This means there is risk for the borrower.
[hom-housing-s2.5.2] A combination (split) mortgage divides the loan between a fixed portion and a variable portion.
[hom-housing-s2.5.3] A fixed rate is often higher than the variable rate at the start, because the lender takes on the risk of rate changes.
[hom-housing-s2.5.4] A longer loan term gives smaller repayments but more interest in total, because interest is charged for more years.
[hom-housing-s2.5.5] A shorter loan term means higher repayments but less interest paid in total.
