MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-consumer-rights-s3: 3. Consumer responsibility: information and rights
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.b] A purchase involves several parties, and each has a different relationship to the consumer. Knowing who is who tells you where to go when something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.0] The consumer is an individual who buys goods or services for personal use, not for business, so a shopper buying a kettle for the kitchen is a consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.1] The retailer or trader sells goods or services in the course of business and is the consumer's first point of contact for a complaint, because the consumer's contract is with the seller.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.2] Under consumer law the retailer is responsible for faults in goods, even when the manufacturer made the fault, and must deal with the complaint.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.3] The manufacturer makes the product and is the other party to any manufacturer's guarantee (a written promise to repair or replace a faulty product).
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.4] A guarantee is a contract between the manufacturer and the consumer. It is usually offered in addition to the consumer's statutory rights (rights set by law), not instead of them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.5] The supplier or distributor moves goods from the manufacturer to the retailer, so the consumer rarely deals with this party directly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.b] Informed decisions rest on reliable information. It comes from the product itself, from the seller and from independent sources, and each should be checked against the others.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.0] Labels, instructions and care labels give the contents or ingredients, how to use and care for the product, and warnings about hazards, so they should be read before buying and using.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.1] Unit pricing shows the price per kilogram or per litre, which allows comparison between different pack sizes.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.2] Advertising and sales material is useful but should be checked against the product label and independent information, because the seller wants to make the sale.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.3] Independent consumer publications and testing reports compare products on quality and value for money. The Consumers' Association of Ireland publishes comparative findings in Consumer Choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.4] Citizens Information gives free, confidential information on consumer rights, so a consumer can ask about a problem without cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.5] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission publishes guidance on consumer rights and on unfair trading.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.6] Terms and conditions, the returns policy and guarantee wording should be read before purchase, as they set out what the seller offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.7] Marks and symbols are information too: quality marks such as the Bord Bia Quality Mark, the CE mark (the manufacturer declares the product meets EU safety requirements), the energy rating label on appliances, and safety and hazard symbols.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.8] Reviews, word of mouth and online comparison sites are useful but may be biased, so check more than one source.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.b] A consumer's part in a transaction is to be informed, careful and honest. These are the consumer's duties, which are different from the rights the consumer is entitled to.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.0] Know your rights and the laws that protect consumers: the basic rights and remedies under consumer law apply whether or not the seller mentions them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.1] Be informed about goods and services before buying, and compare value, not only price, by weighing price against quality, durability and the cost of running or maintaining the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.2] Read and understand labels and terms before buying, including ingredients, dates, guarantee conditions and cancellation terms.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.3] Follow the manufacturer's instructions and care labels; a fault caused by misuse is unlikely to be the trader's responsibility.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.4] Keep receipts and proof of purchase, since they show when, where and what was bought, and are needed for a claim.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.b] The consumer's duties continue after the sale. A responsible consumer also acts quickly when something goes wrong and plans spending so that problems are less likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.0] Report faults promptly, stop using a faulty or unsafe product, and complain where appropriate, as delay can make a problem harder to prove.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.1] Give accurate, truthful information when complaining, including a clear description of the problem and the redress (the remedy) wanted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.2] Plan purchases and set a budget: check whether a product is needed before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.3] Resist pressure selling and impulse buying, and do not be rushed by sales talk, because a quick decision leaves no time to compare or check.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.4] Check the total cost, including interest and charges, before agreeing to credit or easy-pay arrangements, because the instalments can add up to far more than the cash price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.5] Use resources responsibly and consider the environment: reduce waste, and reuse and recycle packaging.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.b] Traditional buying principles still shape how a consumer should approach a purchase, even though the law now gives buyers more protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.0] Caveat emptor is a Latin principle meaning 'let the buyer beware': it places the duty of checking goods on the buyer, so the buyer had to inspect goods before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.1] Modern consumer law limits caveat emptor by giving statutory rights that apply automatically, but inspecting goods and asking questions before purchase remains the best protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.2] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income; a wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.3] Vague claims such as 'the best' or 'new' that lack supporting evidence should be questioned before they influence a purchase, because they sound impressive but promise nothing that can be checked.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.b] Consumer rights are what a buyer is entitled to expect. Papers list them in slightly different ways, so learn the rights below as the common set.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.0] Right to safety: goods and services must not endanger health or life.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.1] Right to honest and truthful information about goods and services, so that buyers can decide on accurate facts.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.2] Right to choice: a range of goods and services is available to choose from.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.3] Right to value for money: goods and services should be of a quality that matches the price paid, and goods should be of satisfactory quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.4] Right to redress: a consumer whose goods or service are faulty can seek repair, replacement or a refund.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.5] Right to services completed to a high standard, by a qualified person, using good materials.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.6] There is no right to a refund simply for a change of mind in a shop purchase; a retailer may offer an exchange or a credit note as goodwill.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.7] Rights and responsibilities are linked: each right is only as useful as the consumer's knowledge and action, so a right to information works only if labels are read.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s1: 1. Consumer choices
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.b] Every purchase starts with how much a household can spend. Income sets the limit, and it also decides whether a household buys what it needs or what it merely wants.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.0] Household income sets the limit on spending, so a household on a tight budget compares unit prices (the price per kilo or per item), buys own-brand and bulk items, and delays non-essential purchases.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.1] Disposable income is the money left after tax and essential bills. It decides how much is free for discretionary spending (spending that is a choice, not a necessity) such as eating out or branded clothing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.2] When income rises, households often trade up to branded or higher-quality goods; when it falls, they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.3] Essential needs such as food, heating and shelter are bought first, and luxury wants are the first to be cut when income falls.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.4] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income. A wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.5] Credit and easy-pay arrangements can allow spending above current income, and the debt they create reduces future choice because part of later income is already committed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.b] Beyond money, choices reflect who the buyer is, who influences them and what the product offers. For a large purchase such as a washing machine, buyers weigh several of these together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.0] Personal preference, and values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethical concerns, and cultural or religious practice), all influence what is bought.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.1] Household composition narrows choice: family size, life-cycle stage and special diets, such as a coeliac or diabetic member or an allergy, determine which goods are suitable.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.2] Time pressure pushes busy households towards convenience foods, takeaway meals and online shopping, because these save time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.3] Other people influence choice through word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.4] For a large purchase, buyers weigh the energy rating, reliability of the brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.5] The environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor that some consumers weigh when choosing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.6] Impulse buying is an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout. It results from several of the factors above, such as advertising, values and income, working together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.b] Shoppers often choose by name rather than by comparing every product. Brands and own-brand goods are the two main kinds of product on the shelf, and they differ in price and in how they are promoted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.0] A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product and distinguishes it from competing goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.1] Branded goods are made by a manufacturer and are usually advertised nationally, so they tend to cost more than equivalent goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.2] Own-brand goods are sold under the retailer's own name. They are often cheaper because the retailer avoids the cost of national advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.3] Brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand, which saves the shopper the effort of comparing alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.b] Advertising is communication that informs consumers about goods and services and tries to create a want for them. It happens mostly outside the shop, in the media a consumer meets every day.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.0] Advertising makes people aware of new products and builds brand loyalty, so that consumers ask for the product by name.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.1] It also informs consumers about features, price and offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.2] Media used include television, radio, print, outdoor billboards, websites, social media, and sponsorship of sport and events.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.3] Celebrity endorsement transfers the image or popularity of a well-known person to the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.4] Product differentiation: advertising highlights a feature, such as a special ingredient, that sets one brand apart from similar goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.5] Advertising can make people feel they need products they cannot afford, and it can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.6] Advertising aimed at children and teenagers is especially influential. It can prompt impulse purchases and family purchases, because young consumers have less experience of judging value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.7] The advantage to the consumer is awareness of choice and information. The disadvantages are that the cost of advertising is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, and there is pressure to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.b] Merchandising is the arrangement and presentation of goods in a shop to encourage consumers to buy more, and to buy more often. It is what the retailer does inside the shop, in contrast to advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.0] Store layout: goods are arranged in clearly identifiable departments with space to browse, and essentials such as bread and milk are placed at the back so that shoppers pass other products on the way to them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.1] Route design: aisles are arranged to keep shoppers moving through the store, so that they see more products and spend longer browsing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.2] Product placement: complementary goods are shelved together, such as biscuits beside tea, so that buying one reminds the shopper of the other. Sweets and magazines are placed at the checkout as impulse items.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.3] Shelf position: premium or luxury goods are placed at eye level, where they are seen first, while cheaper products are often placed on lower or higher shelves.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.4] Special offers displayed at the end of aisles draw attention to promotional stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.5] In-store stimuli: background music that slows the pace of shopping, aromas such as freshly baked bread, good lighting and warmth, and colourful displays.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.6] Sampling and demonstrations let shoppers taste or try a product before buying it, which makes a purchase more likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.b] Pricing techniques make a price look lower, or make a purchase look more valuable, than it is. Discount offers and discount stores do lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.0] Charm pricing: a price set just below a round figure, so that the item appears cheaper than it is.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.1] Multi-buy offers, such as three items for a set price, encourage consumers to buy more than they would at a single-item price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.2] Clearance and reduction labels suggest extra value and can prompt impulse buying of goods the shopper did not plan to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.3] Loss leaders: a well-known product is sold at or below cost to draw shoppers into the store, who then buy other items at normal prices.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.4] Coupons and money-off vouchers reward a specific purchase and are often distributed through loyalty schemes or advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.5] Discount offers, and discount stores, lower the cost of goods, but a lower price is not always better value, so the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.b] Loyalty schemes reward repeat custom with points or discounts, and give retailers information about what customers buy. They are one of the shopping systems named in the syllabus.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.0] Customers earn points or tokens for each amount spent. The points are exchanged for money off goods, vouchers or goods after a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.1] Examples in Ireland include the Tesco Clubcard and the Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.2] Schemes encourage saving for special occasions, such as Christmas, by building up points over time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.3] Shoppers may spend more to reach a reward target, and points can take priority over price when they compare shops, so they return to the same shop.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.4] Card use gives retailers data on buying patterns, which helps them target offers and plan stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.5] Disadvantages: the scheme discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned purchases, and ties the shopper to one shop. It also requires the card to be carried.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.b] Easy-pay arrangements spread the cost of a purchase over time, so goods can be bought without paying the full price at once. This is a form of credit, which has both benefits and costs.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.0] Easy-pay schemes: a retailer or utility company lets the consumer pay for goods or a bill in instalments over a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.1] Hire purchase: the consumer uses the goods while paying instalments under an agreement, and ownership passes only when the final instalment is paid.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.2] Hire purchase is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of the credit), and gives the consumer a cooling-off period of ten days.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.3] Advantages of credit: goods can be used immediately, the cost is spread over time, larger items become affordable, and essentials can be bought in an emergency when cash is not available.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.4] Disadvantages: the total cost is higher than the cash price because of interest and charges, easy-pay can encourage buying of non-essential goods and overspending, and the debt reduces future choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.b] Both allow shopping from home, but the consumer cannot inspect the goods before buying them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.0] Television buying: goods are demonstrated on screen and ordered by phone or online, often with a limited-time offer that creates urgency.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.1] Mail order: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or website and delivered by post or courier.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.2] Advantages of mail order: it is convenient for people without time to shop, a wide range is available, there is no travel, and orders can be placed by post or phone.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.3] Disadvantages of mail order: goods cannot be seen, touched or tried on, delivery can take weeks, and postage adds to the cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.4] Distance purchases (those made without meeting the seller) carry a statutory right to cancel within a cooling-off period under the Consumer Rights Act 2022, because the buyer could not see the goods before ordering.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.b] Online shopping offers convenience and easy price comparison, but adds risks around payment, delivery and personal data.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.0] Advantages: access at any hour from home, easy comparison of prices across retailers, a wider range, customer reviews, and delivery to the door.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.1] Disadvantages: goods cannot be seen or handled before purchase, and delivery can be delayed or carry extra charges.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.2] Payment risk: card details can be taken through phishing emails (fake messages that trick people into giving details) or fake websites, so pay only on secure sites that show a padlock symbol and an address beginning https.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.3] Keep records of the order, so that there is proof of what was bought and paid for if something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.4] Overspending risk: stored card details and one-click ordering make impulse purchases quick and easy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.5] Retailers collect data on browsing and buying, which can be used to target advertising at the individual consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.b] Packaging does more than wrap a product. It protects the goods, carries information, and helps to sell the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.0] Packaging protects and preserves the product, so food stays safe and goods are not damaged on the way to the shop or home.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.1] It informs the shopper, because the pack carries the name, instructions and other details.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.2] It attracts attention on the shelf and conveys quality through colour, shape and design.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.3] It gives convenience, such as portion size and easy-open features, and it suggests quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.4] Care labels and safety or hazard symbols on non-food goods, for example on household cleaning agents, explain how to use the product and prevent accidents.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.b] Labels on pre-packed foods are a legal requirement, so shoppers can choose safely and compare products. The detail is kept short here and is consumer-focused.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.0] Mandatory food labels show the name of the food, ingredients listed in descending order of weight (the heaviest first), net quantity, a use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and instructions for use, and the name and address of the food business.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.1] Use-by dates apply to highly perishable foods and the food is unsafe after the date; best-before dates indicate quality, and the food may still be safe after them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.2] The 14 major allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold or capitals, so that people with allergies can spot them quickly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.3] A nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. The per 100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory, while the per-portion figure is optional.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.4] The country of origin must be shown where the law requires it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.5] Claims such as low fat, light or high fibre may be made only where the product meets defined criteria.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.6] Quality marks and symbols help consumers choose. The Bord Bia Quality Mark for Irish food shows that a product meets a defined standard, and the gluten-free symbol and allergen emphasis help people with special diets.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.7] EU food information rules are set out in Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
