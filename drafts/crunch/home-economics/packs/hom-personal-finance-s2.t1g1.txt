MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-personal-finance-s2: 2. Household expenditure and budgeting
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.b] Expenditure is the money a household spends, and income sets the limit on it. Spending that goes beyond income leads to debt, so spending must be matched to income, and many factors shape how a household divides its money.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.0] Low-income households spend most of their income on essentials such as housing, food and heat, and a larger proportion on food than richer households do, so they have little or no discretionary spending (spending on non-essentials) or saving.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.1] Higher-income households spend more in total and a bigger share on discretionary items such as leisure, holidays and eating out, so they have more discretionary income (money left after essentials) and can also save and pay for credit.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.2] Family size and composition matter, for example the number of children and whether it is a one-parent or two-parent household, because each extra person adds to food, clothing and housing needs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.3] The stage of the family life cycle changes priorities: young children add costs for food, clothing, childcare and schooling, while teenagers cost more for food, clothing and education.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.4] Once children are independent and at retirement, spending falls and shifts towards health, leisure and heating; older children can also help decide and manage spending.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.5] Employment affects money, travel time and cost, and meal and sleep patterns. Working outside the home adds travel, convenience food and childcare costs, and shift work changes meal and sleep patterns and transport costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.6] Urban and rural location change patterns, for example through transport costs and housing costs: rural households depend more on cars, while urban rents and mortgages are higher. Heating costs also differ.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.7] Other influences include socio-economic class and the gender of the earner, personal values and priorities, health or special needs, advertising and peer pressure (the influence of friends and others the same age).
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.b] Not all spending is equally urgent. Households divide it into what they must pay to manage family life and what they can choose to spend once the necessities are covered.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.0] Essential expenditure is spending on necessities needed to manage family life, such as housing (rent or mortgage), utilities (electricity, heating), food, clothing, transport, insurance, medical bills, loan repayments and household bills.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.1] Discretionary expenditure is spending on non-essentials, made only after essential expenditure and savings have been provided for.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.2] Examples of discretionary expenditure: holidays, entertainment, alcohol, cosmetics, eating out, leisure and gym membership, and streaming services.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.3] Fixed essential costs stay the same amount at regular times each week or month, such as rent or a mortgage repayment, insurance and telephone bills, so they are easy to plan for.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.4] Irregular essential costs vary in amount or timing, such as clothing, doctor's bills, school expenses and car repairs, so a budget must set money aside for them. Some are also seasonal, such as heating, Christmas and back to school.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.5] Whether an item is essential depends on the household. The same item can be essential for one household and discretionary for another, for example a car for a rural family compared with a city family.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.6] Credit repayments are fixed commitments in the budget, and credit used for essentials adds to future spending because it must be paid back.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.b] A budget is a plan that sets expected net income against planned spending for a period such as a week, month or year. Following a series of steps means essentials are paid first and money is left for savings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.0] 1. List all income, using net (take-home) income, never gross, because net income is the money actually available to spend. Include wages, welfare, maintenance and any other income.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.1] 2. List planned fixed, irregular and discretionary expenditure for each week or month, including credit repayments, and choose the time period the budget will cover.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.2] 3. Plan essential needs first, such as rent or mortgage and household bills, before any discretionary spending, so the necessities are always covered.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.3] 4. Allocate money for short-term and long-term savings, and for emergencies, so unexpected costs do not have to be borrowed.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.4] 5. Allow for personal spending and special occasions such as Christmas and holidays, and for changes in circumstances, so these annual costs do not cause a shortfall.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.5] 6. Keep bills and receipts, and record one month of actual spending to find where overspending occurs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.6] 7. Compare income with expenditure and adjust. A surplus (income greater than spending) can be saved; a deficit (spending greater than income) means cutting discretionary spending, raising income or seeking advice, rather than borrowing.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.7] 8. Review the budget regularly, and change it when family circumstances or income change, so it stays realistic.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.b] When a budget shows overspending or a deficit, a household can cut costs without going without essentials. The savings come from shopping smarter and using less.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.0] Use a shopping list and stick to it, because this avoids impulse buying of items you did not plan to purchase.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.1] Choose own-brand goods and compare prices between shops, since similar goods can cost much less.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.2] Buy second-hand or in charity shops, use sales, and buy food in season, when it is cheaper and plentiful.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.3] Cook from scratch and choose cheaper cuts of meat, because convenience foods and prime cuts cost more.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.4] Cut energy use and switch supplier to reduce utility bills.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.5] Cancel unused subscriptions, such as gym membership or streaming services, so you are not paying for things you do not use.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.b] A budget does more than record numbers. It shows a household where its money goes and helps it avoid problems before they happen.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.0] Budgeting shows where money goes, and identifies overspending and impulse buying before they lead to unplanned debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.1] Budgeting reduces reliance on credit and supports a good credit record, because bills and repayments are planned for and paid on time.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.2] A budget gives financial security and reduces the worry and stress caused by unpaid bills, because it ensures bills are paid.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.3] A budget that allows some saving can provide for emergencies and cover unexpected events, such as job loss or an urgent repair.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.4] Budgeting helps a household reach its goals, because money is set aside for them.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.5] Budgeting builds money-management skills that transfer to independent living.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.b] A sample budget shows how a net income can be divided between headings so that every euro is planned. The first sample is a family of two adults and two children with a combined net income of 900 euro a week.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.0] Housing, the largest fixed essential cost, is allocated about a quarter of income (25 per cent): 225 euro.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.1] Food is allocated about a quarter of income (25 per cent): 225 euro, because food needs rise with growing children.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.2] Household bills are allocated about 15 per cent of income: 135 euro. Education is allocated about 10 per cent: 90 euro, to cover school costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.3] Clothing, leisure, savings, health and discretionary spending are each allocated about 5 per cent: 45 euro each, with savings kept for emergencies.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.4] A second sample, 800 euro a week for the same type of family, gives 200 euro for housing, 200 for food, 120 for household bills, 80 for education and 40 for each of the five 5 per cent headings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.5] The totals must add up to the net income, so the plan allocates the full net income, leaves no money unplanned, and is reviewed each month.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.6] The percentages are a planning guide for this sample, not a rule, and should be adjusted to the family's actual fixed costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.7] A lower income cannot cover the same percentages, so essentials come first while discretionary spending and savings shrink; a higher income allows more savings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.b] Households that are in debt, or at risk of it, can get free help from MABS. It advises and negotiates for them but does not lend money or pay their debts.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.0] MABS is a free, confidential and independent service for people in debt or at risk of debt, funded by the State through the Citizens Information Board.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.1] MABS helps a household work out its income and spending, and draws up a realistic budget and repayment plan (schedule) with the family.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.2] MABS negotiates with lenders, including moneylenders and mortgage lenders, on behalf of households in debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.3] MABS offers a mortgage arrears service, and advises on personal insolvency (when a person cannot pay their debts) and income maximisation, which means checking and claiming entitlements to State supports.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.4] MABS advises on financial products and interest rates, and points to cheaper sources of credit such as credit unions.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.5] MABS also teaches money-management skills, so households can manage their budgets themselves in future.

CANDIDATES hom-personal-finance-s1: 1. Household income
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.b] A household is a group who share a home and make shared decisions about income, spending and saving. It is the basic financial unit of the economy, because everything the economy produces is ultimately bought, taxed or worked for by households.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.0] Households spend on goods and services, which supports the businesses that supply them and the jobs those businesses provide.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.1] Households pay taxes, which fund public services and State welfare payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.2] Households buy property, and property purchases generate stamp duty, a tax that is income for the State.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.3] Households supply the workforce: children who grow up in families become the future workers, so families that teach children money management also prepare the next generation.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.4] Families usually run their finances independently, but they may need State aid, such as welfare payments or help from MABS, when income falls short of need.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.b] Households receive money in different forms depending on whether, and how, their members work. The main sources are wages, salaries, pensions and State payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.0] Wages: payment for hours worked or output, usually paid weekly or fortnightly, often for manual or hourly work. Because pay follows hours, a week with fewer hours means a smaller wage.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.1] Salaries: a fixed annual amount paid in regular instalments, usually for non-manual or professional work, so the pay is the same each period.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.2] Pensions: income after retirement from an employer's scheme, a personal pension fund or the State. Money paid into a pension while working is a voluntary deduction.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.3] Social welfare allowances and benefits: payments made by the State to people who are unemployed, ill, disabled, caring, retired, widowed or raising children.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.4] Other sources, in one line: profit from self-employment, rent from property, interest on savings, gifts and maintenance payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.b] Two households with similar jobs can still have very different incomes. Age, gender, social class, culture and employment pattern all shape how much a household earns and what it must spend.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.0] Age: income tends to rise with experience and promotion, then falls at retirement unless a pension has been arranged.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.1] Age: teenagers can earn part-time income, which eases the financial strain on the family.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.2] Gender: equal-pay law requires equal pay for the same work, but women are still under-represented in senior management, which men hold more of in many sectors. Women are also more likely to work part-time or take career breaks for caring.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.3] Social class: low qualifications or early school leaving can lead to low pay or unemployment, while higher education widens access to better-paid work.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.4] Culture: cultural attitudes shape whether women take paid work, which occupations are seen as suitable, and which spending priorities a household sets.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.5] Employment pattern: the number of family members in paid work, and whether hours are full-time, part-time, job-sharing, flexible or seasonal, all change household income.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.6] Disability, illness or addiction can reduce earning capacity and raise household costs, so income falls while spending needs rise.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.b] The pay a person earns is not the pay they take home, because deductions are taken off first. Knowing which figure is which matters for both exams and household planning.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.0] Gross income is income earned before any deductions are made.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.1] Net income is take-home pay: gross income after deductions have been made.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.2] Compulsory deductions from gross pay are PAYE, PRSI and USC. The law requires them, so the employee cannot choose to avoid them.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.3] Voluntary deductions are chosen by the employee and include union subscriptions, pension contributions, additional voluntary contributions (AVCs, extra payments into a pension), health insurance, savings, social club fees and holiday fund payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.4] Budgets are planned from net income, because net income is the money a household actually has to spend and save.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.b] The three compulsory deductions each work differently and each pays for something different. Between them they take money from gross pay for the State.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.0] PAYE (Pay As You Earn) is income tax deducted by the employer from each pay, based on the employee's earnings, and paid to the Revenue Commissioners (the State). It rises with income through a standard rate and a higher rate.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.1] Revenue works out the employee's credits and rate band, and the employer uses these details to calculate the PAYE to deduct.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.2] PAYE and USC go to the State to pay for public services such as health, education, roads and welfare.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.3] PRSI (Pay Related Social Insurance) is a compulsory percentage of pay, so it is related to income. It is paid by the employee, through deduction, and separately by the employer, and it funds social insurance benefits.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.4] Different PRSI classes exist, and self-employed people pay PRSI too. Employees earning below a set threshold are exempt from paying employee PRSI.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.5] USC (Universal Social Charge) is a compulsory charge on gross income, calculated at rates that rise in bands as income rises. It is separate from income tax.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.6] Employees with gross income below a set threshold are exempt from paying USC.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.b] A tax credit lowers the tax bill itself, so it is worth the same as an equal amount of tax saved. It is not a reduction in the pay the tax is worked out on.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.0] A tax credit reduces the amount of income tax a person has to pay. Examples are the personal credit and the employee (PAYE) credit.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.1] Income tax is worked out on gross income first, and the person's tax credits are then subtracted from the tax calculated. A credit does not reduce income before tax is worked out.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.2] Revenue works out the employee's credits and standard rate cut-off point (the income up to which the lower rate applies), and the employer uses them when deducting PAYE.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.3] Tax credits do not reduce the USC, which is charged separately on gross income.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.b] The State pays welfare in two ways. One is earned through PRSI contributions; the other is paid from taxes to people who do not qualify for the first, and it checks their means.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.0] Social insurance payments are paid to people who have made the minimum PRSI contributions, so the person has earned them, and they are not means tested.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.1] Examples of social insurance payments: Jobseeker's Benefit, Illness Benefit, Maternity Benefit, Carer's Benefit and the contributory State pension.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.2] Social assistance payments are paid from taxes to people whose PRSI contributions are insufficient to qualify for social insurance payments, and they are means tested.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.3] Examples of social assistance payments: Jobseeker's Allowance, Disability Allowance, Carer's Allowance and the non-contributory State pension. Carer's Benefit is the PRSI-based alternative to Carer's Allowance.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.4] A means test assesses the claimant's income and capital (usually including a partner's) against a set limit. The payment is reduced or refused as means rise above it.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.b] Some payments follow neither pattern exactly. A universal payment is paid to everyone who meets one condition, without a means test or a contribution record.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.0] Child Benefit: a monthly universal payment for each qualifying child. It is not means tested and does not depend on PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.1] Working Family Payment: a weekly payment to employees on low pay who have children, and means tested against a family-size income limit. It replaced the name Family Income Supplement.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.2] Supplementary Welfare Allowance: means tested, and paid to people whose means cannot meet their basic needs or those of their dependants.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.3] Supplementary Welfare Allowance can also be a temporary payment for people awaiting another benefit while their claim is being processed.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.4] Maternity Benefit: a social insurance payment for employees on maternity leave.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.5] Other payments a student may name: Carer's Allowance, Disability Allowance, Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance and Fuel Allowance.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.b] There are two State pensions for older people, and the difference between them is the same as the difference between social insurance and social assistance.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.0] The contributory State pension (formerly called the old age pension) is based on the claimant's record of PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.1] The contributory pension is not means tested, so other income does not normally remove entitlement to it.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.2] The non-contributory State pension is means-tested social assistance for an older person who lacks enough PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.3] Eligibility for the non-contributory pension depends on means and on residence conditions, including habitual residence in the State (a settled, normal home there).

CANDIDATES hom-resource-management-s2: 2. Components of management and decision-making
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.b] Inputs are what a family brings into the management process at the start: the demands made on it and the resources it has to meet them. Getting the inputs clear first matters because everything later depends on them.
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.0] Needs are necessities that must be met, such as food, shelter, warmth and clothing, so they come before everything else when resources are short.
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.1] Wants are things desired beyond necessities, such as a particular brand, a holiday or a new appliance. A want can be postponed or dropped when resources are short, which is the key difference from a need.
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.2] Goals are the outcomes the family sets out to achieve, such as a balanced weekly budget or a family meal on the table by six o'clock.
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.3] Values are what the family believes is important, and they shape which goals the family chooses.
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.4] Human resources are the time, energy, skills, knowledge and people in the family, who make and carry out decisions.
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.5] Material resources are equipment, ingredients, food, clothing, furniture and other household goods used in the task. Marking schemes also list economic resources (money: income and savings) and environmental resources (space, surroundings and community facilities).
[hom-resource-management-s2.0.6] Inputs are the starting point, so clear goals are the first step: a poorly defined or vague need or goal at this stage leads to a result that does not meet the family's needs.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.b] Throughputs are the actions a family takes to change its inputs into outputs. There are three: planning, organising and implementing.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.0] Planning means gathering information, considering the alternatives, weighing them up and deciding on a course of action.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.1] Organising means allocating tasks and responsibilities (who does what and when), arranging the resources each task needs and communicating with those involved.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.2] Implementing means putting the plan into action, such as booking, buying, cooking or cleaning.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.3] Changes are made during implementing as needs arise, such as an unexpected guest or a shortage of an ingredient, because no plan covers everything.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.4] A plan lists the steps in order and the resources each step needs, so that work can be checked against it.
[hom-resource-management-s2.1.5] Decision-making and communication run through all three throughputs, because choices have to be made and shared at every stage.
[hom-resource-management-s2.2.b] Outputs are the results produced by the throughputs, and they are judged against the inputs, especially the goals set at the start. Outputs also feed back into the next cycle, so the framework is a loop and not a straight line.
[hom-resource-management-s2.2.0] Goals achieved: whether the goals were met and whether needs were fulfilled and wants satisfied.
[hom-resource-management-s2.2.1] Changes in values, goals and standards: the experience may change what the family thinks is important or reasonable, or show that a goal or standard was unrealistic and needs to be revised.
[hom-resource-management-s2.2.2] Satisfaction: whether family members are happy with the result and whether their needs and wants were met.
[hom-resource-management-s2.2.3] Evaluation: an assessment of whether resources were used effectively, whether the task was organised efficiently, and whether time, money and energy were used well and the result met the family's needs.
[hom-resource-management-s2.2.4] Effective use of feedback: what is learned is fed back into the system as an input to the next cycle, so that the next round of planning is improved.
[hom-resource-management-s2.3.b] The framework can be used to plan any event or task, such as a graduation, a holiday, a party, a meal or a house move. It suits meal planning, money and home management, problem solving and decision-making. A family birthday party is used here as the example.
[hom-resource-management-s2.3.0] Inputs: the need is a celebration; the goal is a happy party for a child; the want is a particular cake; the human resources are time and help from relatives; the material resources are food, decorations and invitations; the money available sets the budget.
[hom-resource-management-s2.3.1] Throughputs: planning the guest list and the budget; organising who buys food, who prepares the room and who sends invitations; implementing the plan and adjusting for extra guests.
[hom-resource-management-s2.3.2] Outputs: whether the goal of a happy party was achieved, whether the money was kept within budget, whether members were satisfied, and what would improve the next event.
[hom-resource-management-s2.3.3] Feedback from the outputs is recorded so that the next party is planned more efficiently, which is why the framework is a cycle.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.b] A decision is a choice of one course of action from a set of alternatives. Families make decisions all the time, so a clear method and the involvement of everyone affected make the management process work better.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.0] The sequence of decision-making is: identify the problem or need, gather information, list the options, weigh each option against the goals and resources, choose one and act on it, then review the result.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.1] Review after acting: check whether the decision achieved the goal, and record what should be done differently next time, so that evaluation gives lessons for the future.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.2] Sharing information and ideas before a decision allows the family to allocate tasks fairly and to share values, views and concerns.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.3] Decisions made with the views of all members help to resolve conflict and issues and build commitment to the outcome.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.4] Decision-making helps the family achieve its goals and improves the efficiency of its work.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.5] Discussion gives members a broader view of the issues, because each person sees the problem differently.
[hom-resource-management-s2.4.6] Decisions are easier when the family's values and goals have been agreed beforehand, because options can then be judged against them.
[hom-resource-management-s2.5.b] Communication is the sharing of information, ideas, values and concerns between family members. Without it, decisions made by one person never reach the people who have to carry them out.
[hom-resource-management-s2.5.0] Communication is needed at every stage: agreeing goals in the inputs, allocating tasks in organising, reporting progress in implementing and giving feedback.
[hom-resource-management-s2.5.1] Open discussion of what is and is not working provides the feedback that is used to evaluate the outputs.
[hom-resource-management-s2.5.2] Practical methods include a family discussion or meeting, notes, a shared written list, a shared calendar and messaging.
[hom-resource-management-s2.5.3] Poor communication leads to duplicated or missed tasks, misunderstandings and conflict, because members do not know what has been decided or who is responsible. A decision that is not communicated to every member can lead to duplicated work, missed tasks and conflict.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.b] Families today live in many different ways, so a routine has to be planned around how the household actually works. Sharing the work fairly is what makes a routine last.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.0] Modern patterns of living include two working adults, shift workers, lone-parent households, a parent working from home, and children in childcare or after-school activities, and routines must be fitted around these.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.1] Work sharing: tasks such as cooking, shopping, laundry and cleaning are divided between members, so no one person carries the whole load.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.2] Responsibility for family tasks: each member is given a defined task for which they are accountable, matched to age and ability.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.3] Delegation: an adult passes a task to another member and gives them the instructions, time and resources needed to complete it, and trusts them to do it.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.4] Gender equity: household tasks are shared on the basis of ability, availability and fairness rather than assigned by sex.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.5] Checklists for routines: a daily or weekly checklist lists each task, who completes it and when it is due, so that nothing is forgotten.
[hom-resource-management-s2.6.6] Work schedules: a written timetable, such as a weekly cleaning rota or a meal planner, shows the sequence of tasks and who is responsible for each one.
[hom-resource-management-s2.7.b] Money is only one resource. Several others help a family run a good management system, and each makes its own contribution.
[hom-resource-management-s2.7.0] Time: allows planning and avoids waste; tasks are completed more efficiently when time is scheduled.
[hom-resource-management-s2.7.1] People: family members make and carry out decisions and tasks.
[hom-resource-management-s2.7.2] Skills and knowledge: support problem solving, healthy meals and budgeting.
[hom-resource-management-s2.7.3] Equipment: saves time and energy; a washing machine, for example, replaces hours of hand washing.
[hom-resource-management-s2.7.4] Human energy: allows tasks to be completed in an acceptable and harmonious way.
