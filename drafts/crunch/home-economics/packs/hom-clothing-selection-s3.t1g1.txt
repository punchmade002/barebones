TARGET hom-clothing-selection-s3: 3. Current fashion trends
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.b] A trend is recognised by the features it repeats across many garments and outfits. The elements of design (colour, shape, line, proportion, pattern and texture) give you the words to describe those features, and the examples below are only examples, because the trend shown in an exam may be any current one.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.0] Colour: bright, fluorescent or tonal colour schemes, colour panelling and monochrome outfits make a trend easy to spot from a distance.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.1] Shape and silhouette: oversized or boxy shapes, high-waisted trousers, and tailored coats or capes. Cropped tops are another example of a current shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.2] Line: exaggerated vertical lines add height, horizontal lines widen and shorten the look, and diagonal lines give contrast and movement. This is why a trend can be described through its lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.3] Proportion: for example a short jacket worn over a long dress, or a deep cropped top with wide trousers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.4] Pattern: geometric prints and bold retro statements. Retro patterns are old designs revived by designers, so name the pattern and say it is a revival.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.5] Texture: shiny finishes reflect light, while matt fabrics absorb it. Embroidery, sequins, beads and distressed or frayed surfaces add surface interest.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.6] Accessories: belts, large bags, statement jewellery and hats, which can carry a trend on their own.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.7] Seasonal examples are examples of their year, not fixed lists: summer 2024 gave light leggings, loose T-shirts, crochet knits, denim jackets, cut-outs, cargo trousers and oversized jackets. Teenage trends gave ripped denim, bomber jackets and chunky jewellery. Casual wear showed neon and panelled colour and geometric prints.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.b] Trends do not appear by accident. Several forces decide which colours, garments and styles become popular, and exams ask you to outline or evaluate these influences.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.0] Social media spreads a fashion image internationally within hours, and repeated exposure can turn a colour, garment or vintage look into a trend. This is why trends also increase sales.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.1] Influencers and bloggers display outfits and styling videos, give samples and feedback, and their endorsements can encourage or discourage a purchase.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.2] Celebrities, musicians and athletes are photographed in specific designers' clothes, and fans copy their style. Music and dance styles, sports and world events also shape what people wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.3] Designers and fashion houses define the season's direction through seasonal collections and fashion weeks. Ready-to-wear (mass-produced) clothing then copies the runway looks.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.4] Environmental concern has created demand for sustainable or circular fashion (clothing kept in use for as long as possible). This favours durable natural or recycled materials, repair and upcycling, and fast fashion is criticised for waste.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.5] Trends are also shaped by travel and by culture, including global and ethnic styles from other countries that become popular at home.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.6] Past decades return: retro and vintage styles and bold retro patterns are revived by designers and high street brands.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.b] Athleisure (also called leisure wear) is sportswear styling worn for everyday life, not sportswear for playing sport. It has become a major trend because it puts comfort first.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.0] Typical items include leggings or joggers, hoodies, zipped jackets and trainers, worn for work, school and leisure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.1] Stretch, breathable and easy-care fabrics give comfort and freedom of movement, and they wash easily, which suits busy everyday use.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.2] Its strengths are comfort, versatility, durability, affordability and adaptability to many occasions. It is casual and trendy and is promoted by influencers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.3] Athleisure can support activity and posture and a healthy active lifestyle, since clothing that is comfortable to wear makes it easier to exercise and move.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.4] It works as a wardrobe staple (a basic item worn again and again) when the pieces are neutral in colour and can be combined with smarter items.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.5] Its weaknesses are that it can look too informal for some workplaces and occasions, and that tight leggings and tops do not suit every body shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.6] Another weakness is that it may encourage a casual dress code to become the default in everyday life.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.b] Buying older or second-hand clothes has moved from a bargain habit to a fashion statement. The two terms are different, and both are examined.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.0] Vintage clothing is older clothing from a past era, often a previous decade. Pre-loved clothing is any second-hand clothing, so all vintage is pre-loved but not all pre-loved is vintage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.1] Both are bought in charity shops, vintage or second-hand shops, online marketplaces and swaps.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.2] Pre-loved clothing extends the life of garments already made, which reduces the demand for new production. This is why it is linked to sustainability and the reaction to fast fashion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.3] Reasons for its popularity: it is cheaper than new, it gives a unique, individual look because fewer people wear the same piece, and influencers and celebrities promote it. The economic climate also encourages it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.4] Distressed looks, such as frayed edges, grated patches and bleached areas, take inspiration from the vintage punk look, along with studs, sequins and embroidery.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.5] Retro statements, such as bold patterns and bright colours from earlier decades, are revived by designers and high street brands. They are eye-catching because they stand out from plain everyday wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.6] Upcycling changes an old garment into a new one, for example by adding patches, embroidery or appliqué (fabric shapes sewn on), dyeing, shortening, or customising denim with studs.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.7] To restyle a garment to follow a trend, add belts or embellishment, alter the hem or trouser length, add layers or shoulder pads, crop or deconstruct a jacket, distress or recolour jeans, or change the buttons, patches or linings.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.b] Accessories are the items added to an outfit rather than the main garments. They are often the quickest way to show a trend.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.0] Examples include belts, bags, scarves, hats, gloves, jewellery, sunglasses, footwear, ties, cufflinks and pocket squares.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.1] Accessories complete an outfit and create a focal point (the place the eye goes first), drawing attention to a feature of the look. A belt, for example, shows off the waist.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.2] They add colour and contrast, and they tie an outfit together by repeating a colour from another garment. A colourful scarf brightens a plain outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.3] Accessories can change the formality of a basic outfit, for example a scarf or hat that makes a plain outfit look smart or casual. They also help balance colour and proportion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.4] A vintage look can be created with items such as retro sunglasses, large brooches and patterned scarves.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.5] Footwear contributes to the overall look, complements the outfit and can create a focal point.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.6] Accessories are often the cheapest way to follow a trend, as one item can update or modernise an existing outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.b] A critical evaluation weighs the advantages and disadvantages of a trend rather than simply describing it. Describe the trend first, then judge it under the headings the question gives.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.0] Advantages: a trend can express identity, create employment in the fashion sector and bring new ideas to design.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.1] Disadvantages: fast fashion encourages frequent buying, leading to waste and to garments of low quality that wear out quickly.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.2] Cost: following trends can be expensive, and garments may date quickly once a trend passes.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.3] Comfort and function: a trend should be judged on whether the garment is comfortable and suits the wearer's activity.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.4] Suitability: a trend may not flatter every body shape, so a critical evaluation considers who it suits and who it does not, and the occasion it is worn for.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.5] Other headings the exam may give include colour, pattern, line, shape, proportion, texture, accessories, aesthetic appeal and influences.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.b] Elective questions ask you to sketch and describe an outfit built around one current trend, so you need to show the trend and explain your choices.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.0] Diagram: draw a front view of the outfit (add a back view if needed) and label the design features, such as line, shape, proportion and harmony. Give the colour and fabric, and say which trend it follows and why.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.1] Fabric reasoning: choose for a reason, such as absorbent, breathable, stretch, easy-care or shape-retaining. Cotton-elastane jersey suits fitted tops because it stretches, and polyester/cotton suits durable jackets and joggers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.2] Aesthetic appeal: explain how colour, line, balance and emphasis work together, and how the outfit suits the wearer and the occasion.

CANDIDATES hom-clothing-selection-s4: 4. Elements and principles of design in clothing
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.b] The elements are the visual building blocks of a garment, and each can be changed to alter the look of the outfit. There are five: line, shape, colour, texture and pattern.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.0] Line: a visible edge or direction in a garment, created by seams, necklines, hemlines, trims, pleats, fastenings and pattern. Where the eye is led along a line, the garment seems to go in that direction.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.1] Shape: the outline of a garment or of an area within it, such as a neckline, sleeve or pocket. The outline of the whole garment is its silhouette.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.2] Colour: hue (the actual colour), tone (how light or dark it is) and intensity (how bright or dull it is). Warm colours such as red and orange appear to advance, while cool colours such as blue and green appear to recede.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.3] Colour schemes can be monochrome or tonal (one colour in different shades), contrasting or complementary. Colour should also suit the wearer's skin, hair and eye tones.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.4] Texture: the surface quality of a fabric, which can be smooth, rough, shiny or matt, and which can be seen or felt.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.5] Pattern: the repetition of line, shape or colour over a surface, such as stripes, checks, herringbone, zig-zag, polka dots, florals or geometric prints.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.b] Because the eye follows lines, the direction of the lines in a garment changes how tall, wide or soft the wearer appears. Designers use this to flatter the figure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.0] Vertical lines give the impression of length, so they make the figure look slimmer and taller.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.1] Horizontal lines draw the eye across the body, which makes the figure look wider and shorter.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.2] Diagonal lines add contrast and movement, and they lead the eye across the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.3] Curved lines soften an outfit and add a feminine feel, such as the curve of a rounded neckline.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.4] A princess seam runs from the shoulder or armhole to the hem. It shapes the garment to the body without a waist seam, and the long unbroken line also helps the wearer look taller.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.b] Colour and texture change how much light a garment reflects or absorbs, and that changes how big the wearer appears. Remember that these are effects on appearance only.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.0] Dark colours appear to reduce the size of the figure because they absorb light, while light and bright colours appear to enlarge it. Dark navy and black also define and slim the silhouette.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.1] One colour worn from head to toe, or tonal shades of one colour, creates a longer, unbroken line, so the figure looks taller. Tonal shades also add depth.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.2] Contrasting or complementary colours at the top and bottom divide the outfit and can balance the shape of the figure, for example when the shoulders and hips are different widths.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.3] Shiny fabrics reflect light and increase the apparent size of the figure, while matt fabrics absorb light and reduce it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.4] Rough, thick or heavily textured fabrics look bulkier, while smooth fabrics give a neater, more streamlined finish.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.b] Pattern and shape work in the same way as line and colour: they decide where the eye goes and how wide or narrow the figure looks.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.0] Small patterns suit small figures, and a larger figure can carry a large pattern well.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.1] Vertical stripes add height, and horizontal stripes add width and draw attention to the middle of the figure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.2] A patterned area draws the eye to the place where it is worn, for example near the face or neckline for the upper body or at the hem.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.3] Narrow rectangular shapes slim the figure, while triangular or A-line shapes widen the hem or shoulders and so can balance the hips or shoulders.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.4] A fitted bodice with a full skirt gives an hourglass shape, and a fitted top with straight trousers gives a streamlined shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.b] The principles describe how the elements are arranged to create a unified garment. There are five: proportion, balance, emphasis, harmony and rhythm.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.0] Proportion: the relationship between the sizes of the parts of a garment, to each other and to the wearer, such as pockets sized to the garment, a collar sized to the neckline, or jacket length to skirt length.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.1] Balance: an even distribution of visual weight. It can be vertical, symmetrical (both sides match) or asymmetrical (the sides differ but still feel even).
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.2] Emphasis: a centre of interest that draws the eye to one area, created by a brooch, contrasting trim, a belt, a bright accessory, a bold hemline or a neckline. There should be only one centre of emphasis in an outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.3] Harmony (unity): the sense that all parts of a design work together and complement one another, for example colours, fabrics and style that suit each other, or one colour repeated.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.4] Rhythm or repetition: the repeated use of a colour, line or shape, such as a repeated button or stripe, which moves the eye through the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.5] Diagram: when asked to sketch an outfit, label the elements and principles used, such as line, shape, proportion, harmony and emphasis.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.b] Choosing the elements well lets a wearer look taller, slimmer or better balanced, and draw attention to a feature or away from one. This is the use of the elements that exam questions ask about directly.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.0] To look taller or slimmer, use vertical lines, one colour head to toe or tonal shades, dark colours such as navy and black that absorb light, V-necks, princess seams, a single long line from shoulder to hem and narrow shapes.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.1] To balance or add width, use horizontal stripes, light or bright colours and bulky textures. Contrasting top and bottom colours balance the shoulders and hips.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.2] To draw attention to a feature, put pattern or a bright colour near it, such as near the face for the upper body, or use a belt to define the waist.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.3] To draw attention away from an area, wear dark colour over it, because dark colours appear to reduce size.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.4] Colour also flatters the wearer's hair, eyes and skin; for example, blues can brighten the complexion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.5] Shape, fit and proportion matter too: choose lines that suit the figure. A short jacket with slim trousers lengthens the leg line.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.b] The way a garment is made creates its line, shape and emphasis. Here the focus is on the effect of each feature on the design.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.0] Seams join the parts of a garment, and their placement creates line and can add shaping to the body.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.1] Darts remove excess fabric to shape a garment around the bust, waist or hips. Pressing defines the darts.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.2] Princess seams shape the garment without a waist seam.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.3] Pleats, tucks and gathers add fullness and movement, and are used at the waist, sleeve or skirt, where they can add visual interest.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.4] Pockets, collars, buttons, trims and fastenings add line and emphasis, and they must be positioned in proportion to the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.5] Fabric choice affects the finished design: crisp fabrics hold a shape, as in tailoring, while soft fabrics drape. The fabric's texture and pattern must also suit the design lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.6] Design and colour can be applied to fabric by dyeing, screen, roller, stencil or transfer printing, embroidery and appliqué (fabric shapes stitched on).
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.b] A garment is evaluated against three criteria: how well its design serves its function, its comfort and its aesthetic appeal. Examiners often set these as headings for an outfit shown in a picture.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.0] Function: the garment must suit the purpose, activity, climate, weather and occasion it is intended for, such as freedom of movement for sport. Durability, easy care, useful pockets and fastenings that work also count.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.1] Comfort: the fabric (soft, absorbent, breathable or stretch), fit and ease of movement, weight, versatility in different weather and footwear determine whether the wearer is comfortable.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.2] Aesthetic appeal: the colour scheme, line, shape, texture, proportion, balance and emphasis, and any accessories, must work together to produce an attractive and pleasing overall look.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.3] A well-designed garment shows a clear relationship between its design features and the function they serve, for example a drawcord hood, reflective trim or a zip opening.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.4] Using colour and design to flatter the body: choose colours and lines that emphasise the wearer's best features and balance any areas they wish to de-emphasise.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.5] Other headings that appear are shape, proportion, design features, current fashion trend, lifestyle, cost, care and suitability for the occasion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.6] Method: for each heading make a point from the picture, then give the reason, such as the fabric, cut or colour.

CANDIDATES hom-clothing-selection-s2: 2. Influences on clothing
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.b] Clothing requirements are the needs an outfit must meet, and they vary between people and situations. Working out which needs matter most is the first step in choosing what to buy.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.0] Function: clothing must protect the body from weather, provide the movement an activity needs and be safe for the wearer's work or play. For example, a waterproof jacket needs a close-weave, water-repellent fabric so rain cannot soak through.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.1] Climate and season decide the weight of fabric, the amount of covering and the need for waterproof or insulating layers, because the clothing has to keep the body comfortable in the weather it will meet.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.2] Cost versus money available: a buyer weighs the price of a garment against their budget, and the purchase cost is balanced against durability and the number of times it will be worn, which is why a dearer, hardwearing garment can be better value.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.3] Availability: clothing can only be bought if it is stocked in the shops or online in the right size, colour and style, so what a person wears is limited by what they can actually find.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.4] Personal taste and body shape guide the choice of style, colour and fit, because people want clothes that suit them and feel comfortable.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.0.5] Occasion and activity define the level of formality, such as a school uniform, work wear, sportswear or a wedding outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.b] People do not choose clothes in isolation. The culture, beliefs and groups around them shape what feels right to wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.0] Culture shapes modesty and dress codes, and it inspires designs, because each culture has its own standards of what is suitable to wear. Traditional dress such as the sari and pashmina shawl, and traditional embroidery motifs, are examples.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.1] Religious and national traditions influence the clothing worn for ceremonies, festivals and rites of passage (the key events in life, such as a wedding or graduation).
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.2] Peer groups influence teenagers, who often adopt the styles and brands their friends wear to gain acceptance.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.3] Status: clothing signals a person's rank, wealth or profession, so what someone wears tells others about their position.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.1.4] Identification: uniforms show that a person belongs to a group or holds a role, such as the uniform of a nurse. The principle is that a uniform signals role.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.b] Most people see what is fashionable long before they see it in a shop. The media, admired public figures and designers decide which styles spread.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.0] Magazines, newspapers, television, film and advertising popularise fashion by showing what is worn, so people see new styles and want them.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.1] Young people copy the clothes worn by actors, presenters, musicians and sports stars they admire.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.2] Trendsetters are individuals or groups whose choices are copied by others, and they set the direction for the mass market. Examples are James Dean (jeans, white T-shirt, black leather jacket), Jackie Kennedy (A-line suit and pillbox hat) and Princess Diana.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.3] Fashion houses and designers launch collections at seasonal shows, which are then copied by high street retailers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.4] Fashion is the currently accepted style, and it changes over time, so clothing that was fashionable in one decade can look dated in the next.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.2.5] Trendsetters are people, fashion is the accepted style and a trend is a short-lived direction, so keep the three terms apart.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.3.b] Social conditions change what people want to wear, so designers and makers change the garments they produce.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.3.0] Social conformity, the wish to fit in with a group, drives demand for common styles, because people buy what others around them are wearing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.3.1] Changes in women's status and in male roles have altered the silhouettes and tailoring that designers offer, so garments such as women's trouser suits became common.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.b] Money decides both what consumers can spend and what it costs to make a garment, so the economy shapes design and construction.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.0] Prosperity encourages consumers to spend on fashion, including power dressing and luxury items, because they have more money to spare.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.1] In a downturn, consumers buy simpler, more sombre and lower-cost clothing, and manufacturers use cheaper materials to keep prices down.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.4.2] Production costs, including raw materials and labour, set the price a garment must be sold at.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.b] Technology changes how garments are designed and made, and how quickly new styles reach buyers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.0] Computer-aided design (CAD) allows designers to develop patterns and styles on screen before production, so ideas can be tested without making a sample.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.1] Advanced sewing machines, computerised cutting and mass production make garments faster and cheaper to make.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.2] New and high-tech fibres and blends, such as stretch fibres and microfibres, create fabrics with new properties.
[hom-clothing-selection-s2.5.3] Media and communication allow new styles to reach markets around the world very quickly, for example through fashion shows and images shared by phone.
