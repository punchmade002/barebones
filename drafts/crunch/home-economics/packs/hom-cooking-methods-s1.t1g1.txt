TARGET hom-cooking-methods-s1: 1. Physical and chemical changes during preparation and cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.b] A physical change alters the size, shape, state, colour or texture of food without forming new substances. Heat usually drives these changes, and each one has a food you can name as an example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.0] Coagulation: heat unfolds (denatures) egg, milk and fish proteins, which then bond into a set structure. Egg white turns from clear to white and opaque, fish flesh from translucent to opaque, and custard, scrambled egg and omelette set.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.1] Gelatinisation: starch granules absorb water and swell when heated in liquid, and can swell until they burst. This thickens sauces, gravies, custard and porridge.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.2] Tenderising: long, moist cooking converts collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) into gelatine, so tough cuts of meat become tender.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.3] Shrinkage: meat and fish lose water and melted fat during cooking, so they become smaller and lighter and fat melts out of meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.4] Increase in size: dried peas, beans and fruit, rice and pasta absorb water and swell during cooking. Whisked egg foam, sponge and bread also rise and increase in volume.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.5] Colour change: meat turns from red or pink to brown or grey as its myoglobin (the red pigment in meat) is heated. Green vegetables turn olive-brown with prolonged cooking or acid because the chlorophyll is altered, whereas carrots and other orange vegetables stay colourful.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.6] Softening: heat softens the cellulose and breaks down pectin (the 'glue' between cells) in plant cell walls, so vegetables and fruit become tender as cell water is lost.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.7] Increased digestibility and safety: heat destroys most micro-organisms and makes starch and protein easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.8] Loss of nutrients, because water-soluble vitamins dissolve in cooking water, is also counted as a physical change.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.b] A chemical change alters the molecular make-up of food and forms new substances with different colours, flavours and textures. Raising agents are a clear example, because they make a new gas that lifts the mixture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.0] Chemical raising agent: bicarbonate of soda reacts with an acid such as buttermilk and releases carbon dioxide gas, which makes soda bread rise. The same applies in cakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.1] Baking powder contains bicarbonate of soda plus an acid, so it releases carbon dioxide when it is moistened and heated.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.2] Biological raising agent: yeast feeds on sugar during fermentation and gives off carbon dioxide (and alcohol), which makes bread dough rise. The gas expands in the oven, and baking kills the yeast.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.3] Tenderising by acid or enzymes, for example in a marinade or with a meat tenderiser, is also accepted as a chemical change.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.4] Browning reactions are chemical changes too, because they make new coloured and flavoured substances: the Maillard reaction, caramelisation and dextrinisation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.b] Enzymic browning is a chemical change caused by an enzyme in damaged fruit and vegetables. It happens without heat, which separates it from the browning of toast or roast meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.0] Cutting, peeling or bruising breaks cell walls, so the enzyme polyphenol oxidase (called 'oxidase' in some marking schemes) meets phenolic compounds in the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.1] Oxygen from the air is needed: the enzyme oxidises the phenolic compounds, which then form brown pigments.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.2] Examples: sliced apple, banana, pear, avocado, potato and mushroom turning brown on standing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.3] Browning spoils the appearance, flavour and vitamin C content of fruit and vegetables. It is wanted in the making of black tea, where it is used deliberately.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.4] To stop oxygen reaching the surface, keep cut surfaces under water, sugar syrup, cling film, a layer of juice or a coating of dressing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.5] Acid such as lemon juice lowers the pH and slows the enzyme's action. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) reduces the first brown products back to colourless compounds.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.6] Heat inactivates the enzyme, so blanching vegetables in boiling water before freezing, or cooking them, stops browning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.7] Refrigeration slows enzyme activity but does not stop it. Working quickly and cutting just before serving also limits browning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.b] A fair test lets you see which treatment slows browning best, by changing one thing at a time and keeping everything else the same.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.0] Aim: to find which treatment best slows enzymic browning on a sliced apple.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.1] Hypothesis: lemon juice will slow browning more than an untreated slice, because acid slows polyphenol oxidase.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.2] Independent variable: the treatment applied to each slice, e.g. untreated (the control), dipped in lemon juice, dipped in sugar syrup, dipped in salt water or cold water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.3] Dependent variable: the degree of browning, rated on a 0 to 5 colour scale at fixed times.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.4] Controlled variables: the same apple variety and ripeness, slices of the same thickness cut with the same knife, the same dipping time, the same room temperature and the same time after cutting. These keep the test fair, so only the treatment changes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.5] Method: 1. Wash and dry the apple and cut it into slices of equal thickness. 2. Dip each treated slice for the same time, keeping one untreated slice as the control. 3. Lay all slices on labelled plates and record the colour of each every 5 minutes for 30 minutes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.6] Safety and hygiene: wash hands and the apple, use a clean board and a sharp knife held with a claw grip, and wipe up any spilled juice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.7] Expected result: the untreated control browns first and most, while the lemon juice slices stay palest for longest. This supports the hypothesis.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.8] Evaluation: a rating by eye is subjective and apples vary, so repeat the test with several apples and average the ratings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.b] Non-enzymic browning needs heat and does not involve enzymes. It has three processes, each involving a different food molecule: the Maillard reaction (protein with sugar), caramelisation (sugar alone) and dextrinisation (starch).
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.0] Maillard reaction: amino acids from protein react with reducing sugars such as glucose when food is heated in dry conditions, producing brown colour and savoury roasted flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.1] Maillard browning shows on roast meat and poultry skin, roast potatoes, toast and the crust of bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.2] Dry heat and a dry surface favour browning, so roasting, grilling, baking and frying brown food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.3] Little Maillard browning occurs in boiled, steamed or stewed food, because water holds the surface at about 100 °C, too cool for browning, so the food stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.4] Caramelisation: sugar (sucrose) melts and darkens under strong heat, forming caramel with a sweet, slightly bitter flavour. It needs no protein.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.5] Caramelisation examples: caramel sauce, crème caramel and toffee.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.6] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains called dextrins, which brown and give a crisp surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.7] Dextrinisation examples: toast and the crust of cakes and bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.b] Some nutrients dissolve in water or are destroyed by heat and air, so preparation and cooking can lower the nutritional value of food. Knowing why they are lost shows how to keep them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.0] Water-soluble vitamins, vitamin C and the B group, dissolve into cooking water and are lost when it is poured away.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.1] Vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and oxidation (exposure to air), so the longer vegetables are cooked and exposed to air, the more is lost.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.2] Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are more stable in cooking but drain away with the melted fat from grilled, roasted or fried food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.3] Soluble minerals such as potassium and some sugars dissolve into cooking water along with the water-soluble vitamins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.4] Cutting vegetables into small pieces increases the surface exposed to water and air, and soaking them before cooking increases loss. Cooking in a lot of water for a long time has the same effect.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.b] Most nutrient loss can be limited by handling vegetables sensibly before and during cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.0] Peeling thinly or cooking in the skin reduces loss, because nutrients are concentrated just beneath the skin. Peeling thickly increases loss.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.1] Holding cooked vegetables hot for long periods destroys further vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.2] Adding bicarbonate of soda to vegetables destroys vitamin C and thiamine, so avoid alkalis and copper pans.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.3] Steaming, stir-frying and microwaving use little water and short cooking times, so they conserve more nutrients. Preparing just before cooking, using a sharp knife, putting the lid on and serving soon after cooking also help.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.4] Cooking liquid from vegetables can be used in soups, sauces or gravies to recover some nutrients.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s5: 5. Properties
[hom-proteins-s5.0.b] Cooking and food preparation change protein in two linked steps. First the folded protein chain unfolds (denaturation), then the unfolded chains join up and set (coagulation). Both are permanent, and together they explain most of what happens to eggs, meat, fish and milk.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.0] Denaturation is the irreversible unfolding of the secondary and tertiary structure of a protein (the folded shape of its chain), without breaking its peptide bonds (the links between amino acids), so the chain keeps its amino acids but changes shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.1] Denatured chains lose the original shape and functional properties of the protein, because the function of a protein depends on its shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.2] Denatured chains can then link to one another, and this linking is coagulation.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.3] Coagulation is the setting of denatured protein into a solid or semi-solid mass, which is why a runny raw egg becomes firm when cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.4] Denaturation changes the texture and appearance of a food, and denatured protein is more digestible, so cooked protein foods are easier to digest than raw ones.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.5] Denaturation can be caused by heat, mechanical action (such as whisking), chemicals (such as acid and salt) and enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.6] Egg white coagulates from about 60 degrees C, turning from transparent to opaque and firm; the yolk sets at about 68 degrees C. These are approximate temperatures because eggs set over a range.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.7] Fish flesh changes from translucent to opaque as its protein coagulates during cooking, which is how you can tell fish is cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.8] Examples of coagulation in cookery: omelette, quiche, boiled egg, meringue, soufflé and lemon meringue pie.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.b] A foam is a mass of air bubbles held in a thin film. Egg white makes a good foam because its protein, albumen, can be stretched into a film around each bubble and then set by heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.0] Whisking or beating stretches and partly denatures the albumen chains, which unfold and arrange themselves around the air bubbles. This is mechanical action, not heat, that creates the foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.1] The protein film around each bubble holds the trapped air, forming a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.2] Gentle heat in cooking then coagulates the albumen around the bubbles, setting the foam permanently, which is why a baked meringue stays firm.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.3] Over-whisking denatures the protein too far and breaks the film, so the foam becomes grainy and collapses.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.4] Examples: meringue, pavlova, soufflé and whisked sponge cake.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.b] A gel is a liquid held in a soft solid. Some proteins, especially gelatine, can do this: they dissolve in hot liquid and set into a network as it cools.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.0] A gel forms when protein chains (gelatine) dissolved in a hot liquid cool and link into a network that traps the liquid, such as water.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.1] Gelatine is the main protein used to make gels in cookery.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.2] A firmer gel results from a higher concentration of gelatine, because more chains form a denser network.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.3] Examples: jelly, aspic, mousses and cheesecake.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.b] Dry heat cooks food without added liquid. It acts mainly on the surface protein, which is why roasted and grilled foods firm up and brown.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.0] Dry heat (roasting, baking, grilling and frying) denatures and coagulates the surface protein, causing shrinkage and firmness.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.1] Prolonged dry heat dries the muscle protein and makes meat tough and dry, so overcooked meat is chewy.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.2] Dry heat at high temperatures produces the Maillard reaction, which browns the surface of meat, bread and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.b] The golden-brown crust on bread and roast meat is not caused by sugar alone or by enzymes. It comes from protein and sugar reacting together under dry heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.0] The Maillard reaction is non-enzymic browning, which happens when the amino acids of protein react with a reducing sugar (a sugar such as glucose) on heating.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.1] It needs high heat and a dry surface with little moisture; it does not need enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.2] It produces brown colour and roasted, savoury flavour, as in a toasted bread crust, roast meat, grilled or toasted cheese and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.b] Moist heat cooks food in liquid or steam at lower temperatures. For meat it does something dry heat cannot: it changes the tough connective tissue into a soft jelly.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.0] Moist heat (boiling, stewing, poaching and braising) converts the collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) to gelatine, which tenderises meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.1] Collagen conversion needs long, slow cooking in moisture, so cheaper cuts of meat, which contain a lot of connective tissue, become tender.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.2] Prolonged high heat toughens muscle protein, so gentle moist heat is used for tender results.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.3] Heat breaks the weak bonds that hold the folded structure, which is why heat denatures protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.b] Whisking, beating and mincing physically stretch and break protein, so they change texture without any heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.0] Whisking, beating and mincing unfold protein chains; whisking egg white is the example that forms a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.1] Kneading bread dough develops the gluten (the protein of wheat flour) into an elastic network that traps the carbon dioxide from yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.2] Beating meat with a mallet breaks up the muscle fibres and tenderises it.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.3] Excessive agitation denatures protein so far that it becomes tough or curdled.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.b] Acids and salt change protein without any heating. That is why a splash of lemon juice curdles milk and a marinade softens meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.0] Acids such as lemon juice and vinegar denature protein; lemon juice added to milk curdles it, which is also how milk sours.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.1] A marinade of acid, such as vinegar, wine or lemon, tenderises meat by denaturing its surface protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.2] Alkalis also denature protein and change its texture.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.3] Cheese-making uses acid to help set the milk protein, and salt to increase the firmness of the curd (the solid part of the milk), which controls the texture of the cheese.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.4] The acid in the stomach denatures food protein before enzymes hydrolyse (break) it, so the chains are easier for digestive enzymes to reach.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.b] Enzymes are biological catalysts, and some of them work on protein. Cooks use them to set milk for cheese and to soften tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.0] Rennin, traditionally from the stomach of young calves, coagulates casein (the main protein of milk) for cheese-making.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.1] Papain, from papaya, is a proteolytic enzyme (one that breaks down protein) that tenderises tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.2] Proteolytic enzymes hydrolyse peptide bonds, so too much enzyme action makes protein soft and mushy.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.3] Enzymes in the digestive tract hydrolyse protein to amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.4] Heat inactivates enzymes, which is why cooking stops enzymic tenderising.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.b] This investigation shows denaturation in practice by comparing egg white that is left alone, heated and whisked.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.0] Aim: to show how heat and mechanical action denature egg white protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.1] Independent variable: the treatment (heating in a water bath, or whisking); dependent variable: the change in appearance and texture of the white.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.2] Controlled variables: the same volume of egg white, the same starting temperature and the same type of dish.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.3] Control: place an untreated sample of egg white in a labelled dish for comparison.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.4] Method: heat a second sample gently in a water bath until it sets, then whisk a third sample until stiff.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.5] Safety: use fresh eggs, wash hands after handling raw egg, and do not taste raw egg white, because raw egg can carry bacteria.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.6] Expected result: the heated white turns opaque and firm, and the whisked white forms a stable foam; the untreated white stays clear and runny.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.7] Conclusion: heat and whisking both denature the protein of egg white and change its appearance and texture.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.b] Sugars are the sweet, water-loving carbohydrates, and these two properties explain why they are used so widely in cooking. Starch is the contrast: it is neither sweet nor soluble in cold water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.0] All sugars are sweet, but to different degrees. Fructose is sweeter than sucrose (table sugar), and lactose (milk sugar) is the least sweet of the common sugars.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.1] On a scale where sucrose scores 100, the 2017 marking scheme gives fructose 170 and lactose 15. Learn the order (fructose, then sucrose, then lactose) and use the figures only as that scale.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.2] Sweetness is why sugar is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.3] Sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve readily in water, and solubility increases with temperature, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.4] A concentrated sugar solution is a syrup. Syrups are used to sweeten drinks and sauces, to make icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.5] Starch is different: it is insoluble in cold water, so it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension (tiny solid particles spread through the liquid). It is also not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.b] Starch behaves differently from sugar: it is insoluble in cold water and must be heated with a liquid before it thickens anything. It also takes in moisture from the air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.0] Gelatinisation: when starch is heated in a liquid, the granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, thickening it into a sol (a colloidal solution, where tiny particles stay spread through the liquid).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.1] On cooling, a concentrated starch mixture may set into a gel (a solid-like network that holds the liquid), which is why a cooled custard or sauce can become firm.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.2] Gelatinised starch thickens white sauces, custard, soups, gravies, risotto and fillings for pies and flans.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.3] To prevent lumps, the granules must be separated before they swell. Mix the starch with a little cold liquid first (a cornflour paste), or coat it in fat as in a roux.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.4] A roux is a mixture of fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added. The fat separates the starch granules, and the starch in the flour gelatinises as the liquid is stirred in, giving a base for white sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.5] Starch is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump if stored in damp air. Store them in airtight containers.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.6] Sugar, especially brown sugar, is also hygroscopic, so it can turn hard or lumpy if left open in damp air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.b] How a carbohydrate food is cooked decides what happens to it. Dry heat browns it, while moist heat softens it and thickens liquids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.0] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch makes the granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs: the starch breaks down into brown dextrins. This gives a brown colour and a crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.1] Dry heat on sugar causes caramelisation and the Maillard reaction, which brown the surface of the food and give it flavour and a crisp texture.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.2] Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch causes gelatinisation: the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid, thickening sauces and puddings. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.3] Moist heat softens the cellulose in vegetable cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest. The vegetable keeps its fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.4] Sugar dissolves in the cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when fruit is stewed, so the syrup carries the flavour of the fruit. Sugar also helps the fruit keep its shape.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.b] Enzymes (proteins that speed up chemical reactions) break complex carbohydrates down in stages until they are small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.0] 1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins breaking starch down into smaller polysaccharides and maltose. This enzyme stops working in the stomach acid, so no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.1] 2. Pancreatic amylase, in pancreatic juice released into the small intestine, completes the breakdown of the remaining starch to maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.2] 3. Maltase, sucrase and lactase, made by the wall of the small intestine and found in the intestinal juice, finish the job. Maltase splits maltose into two glucose units, sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.3] Only monosaccharides (single sugar units: glucose, fructose and galactose) are small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.4] Humans have no enzyme that digests cellulose, so it passes through the intestine as dietary fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.b] Sugar does much more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each property has a use, and exam answers pair the two.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.0] Sweetness: sweetens cakes, desserts and drinks. Solubility: dissolves to make syrups and icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.1] Preservation: sugar draws water out of micro-organisms, so they cannot grow and spoilage is slowed in jam, jelly, chutney and crystallised fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.2] Fermentation: sugar is the food for yeast, which produces carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.3] Aeration (trapping air): creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures such as sponge cakes. Whisking traps air in egg white, and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable, which makes meringue.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.4] Maillard reaction: sugar reacts with amino acids (the units that make up protein) when heated, a non-enzymic browning that gives brown colour and flavour to roast meat, toast, roast potatoes and baked goods. Caramelisation is different because it involves sugar alone.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.5] Gel formation: sugar, acid and pectin together set jam into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.6] Caramelisation gives crème caramel and toffee, crystallisation gives confectionery, and inversion is used in jam and boiled sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.b] Caramelisation, the Maillard reaction and dextrinisation all brown food, so they are easy to confuse. What differs is what is being heated and with what.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.0] Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry until it melts and browns, so it needs no protein, as in caramel sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.1] The Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids together, heated dry, so it browns foods that contain both, such as toast and roast meat.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.2] Dextrinisation is starch, not sugar, broken into brown dextrins by dry heat, which is why toast browns even though bread is mostly starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.3] Gelatinisation is a different change again: starch heated in a liquid (moist heat) swells and thickens it, while inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by acid or enzyme.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.b] Hydrolysis is the reaction that splits big sugars into small ones, and it is the same reaction that happens in digestion. Inversion is the form of it that cooks use on sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: the bond between two units is split and water is added across it. It is the reverse of condensation (joining units with the loss of water).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.1] A disaccharide reacts with water, with enzymes or with heat and acid, and splits into its monosaccharide units. Sucrose gives glucose and fructose, lactose gives glucose and galactose, and maltose gives two glucose units.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.2] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. It produces invert sugar, a mixture of glucose and fructose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.3] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise than sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.4] This is why invert sugar is used in jam and boiled sweets, and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.b] Heating and cooling sugar and starch changes their colour, flavour and texture in three different ways. Each is wanted in some foods and avoided in others.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.0] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a distinctive, pleasant flavour. Overheating makes it dark and bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.1] Caramel is used in caramel sauce, crème caramel, toffee and brûlée.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.2] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution) and the mixture cools, sugar crystals form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.3] Controlled crystallisation gives the texture of fudge, fondant and sweets, while unwanted large crystals make jam grainy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.4] Crystallisation is prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (which causes inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.5] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains of glucose units called dextrins, which are brown. The 2005 scheme names the brown compound pyrodextrin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.6] Dextrinisation gives toast and the crust of baked bread their brown colour and flavour. Browned flour used for a roux thickens less, because its starch has already been broken into shorter chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.7] Dextrinisation is a property of starch, not sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.b] Jam sets because of pectin, a substance in fruit cell walls. Making jam depends on getting the pectin out of the fruit and then giving it the right conditions to set.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.0] Pectin is extracted by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft. Acid, such as lemon juice, helps release the pectin from the cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.1] Fruit high in pectin includes cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit (especially the peel), gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants. Strawberries are low in pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.2] Unripe fruit contains more pectin than ripe fruit, so under-ripe apples are a good source of pectin for jam.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.3] A gel forms only when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions and the mixture is boiled and then cooled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.4] During cooling, the pectin molecules link into a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.5] Too little pectin, as in strawberries, gives a runny jam. Add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit to fix this.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.6] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.b] This simple test shows that starch does not thicken a cold liquid but does thicken it when heated.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.0] Aim: to show the effect of heat on starch mixed with a liquid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.1] Method: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water and observe that it does not dissolve and does not thicken. Then heat the mixture while stirring and observe that it thickens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.2] Variable: temperature is the variable that changes (cold, then heated).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.3] Control and comparison: the unheated cornflour and cold water mixture is compared with the heated one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.4] Result: cold, the starch settles as a cloudy suspension and the mixture stays thin; heated, the granules swell, burst and absorb the water, and the mixture thickens into a sol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.b] Heating sugar on its own, with no water, shows caramelisation happening step by step.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.0] Aim: to observe how granulated sugar changes when it is heated on its own, with no water added.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.1] Method: place a measured quantity of granulated sugar in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat, stirring gently as it melts, and note the colour and smell at regular intervals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.2] Variables: heating time is the independent variable; colour, smell and taste are the dependent variables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.3] Control: a sample of the same sugar left unheated, for comparison.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.4] Safety: molten sugar is extremely hot and causes severe burns, so use heatproof utensils and keep hands and skin away from the pan.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.5] Expected results: the colour darkens gradually from pale to golden, then deep brown, and the caramel smell strengthens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.b] These two tests show a change in starch caused by dry heat and a change in sucrose caused by acid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.0] Dextrinisation, aim: to show that dry heat changes starch. Method: toast white bread, or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.1] Dextrinisation, result: the browned starch is darker and thickens less than unbrowned starch, because its chains are shorter. Safety: the pan is hot.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.2] Inversion, aim: to show that acid changes sucrose. Method: boil a sucrose solution with a little lemon juice and cool it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.3] Inversion, result: the invert sugar does not crystallise as readily and is sweeter.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.4] A Benedict's test shows the invert sugar: reducing sugars give an orange-red colour, while sucrose does not.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s2: 2. Water-soluble vitamins C, B12 and folate
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.b] Vitamin C, vitamin B12 and folate are water-soluble vitamins, so they behave differently from vitamins that dissolve in fat. Knowing this explains why they are needed every day and why cooking can ruin them.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.0] Water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C) dissolve in water and are not stored in useful amounts in the body. Any excess is lost in urine, so they are needed regularly in the diet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.1] The exception is vitamin B12, which the liver stores, so a store builds up that can last for several years.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.2] Because they dissolve in water, these vitamins are lost into cooking water and are easily destroyed during storage, preparation and cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily allowance) is the daily amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It varies with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and smokers are advised more vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.b] Vitamin C is the most-asked vitamin in the exam, and it comes mainly from fruit and vegetables. Fresh, raw and in-season produce has the most.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.0] Citrus fruit (oranges, lemons, grapefruit), blackcurrants, rosehips, kiwi fruit and strawberries are rich sources of vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.1] Peppers, broccoli, sprouts, cabbage, tomatoes, new potatoes and peas are good vegetable sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.2] Fresh fruit juices and smoothies, and fortified drinks, also supply vitamin C, and a supplement can too. Any fruit or vegetable is accepted as a source in an answer.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.b] Vitamin C does several different jobs in the body, and exam questions usually ask for two or three of them. The key one is collagen, because so many other effects follow from it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.0] Vitamin C is needed to make collagen, the protein of connective tissue that gives strength to skin, blood vessels, gums, cartilage, bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.1] By supporting collagen formation, vitamin C speeds up the healing of wounds, because new tissue can be built.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.2] Vitamin C aids iron absorption. It converts non-haem iron (the iron in plant foods) from the ferric form (Fe³⁺) to the ferrous form (Fe²⁺), which is absorbed more readily.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.3] Vitamin C also aids calcium absorption, which marking schemes accept.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.4] Vitamin C is an antioxidant: it neutralises free radicals (damaging particles) and so protects cells from damage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.5] Vitamin C supports the immune system, helping the body resist infection, and it prevents scurvy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.b] If the diet lacks vitamin C for a long time, collagen cannot be made properly and the body's tissues start to fail. The deficiency disease is scurvy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.0] Scurvy develops after a severe and prolonged lack of vitamin C in the diet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.1] Weak collagen causes bleeding, sore and swollen gums, loose teeth, easy bruising and painful joints.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.2] Wounds heal slowly because new tissue cannot be formed properly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.3] Reduced iron absorption can lead to anaemia, with tiredness and pale skin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.4] The body becomes more susceptible to infection because its resistance is lowered.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.b] Vitamin C is easily lost, but the loss depends on method and time. Cooking does not destroy all of it: steaming, microwaving and short cooking keep most.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.0] Buy fresh produce and use it quickly, and eat it raw where possible.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.1] Wash vegetables before cutting rather than soaking them, prepare them just before cooking and cut them in large pieces, so less of the surface is exposed.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.2] Cook in a little boiling water for a short time with the lid on, or steam, microwave or stir-fry, so less vitamin C dissolves or is destroyed.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.3] Do not use bicarbonate of soda or copper pans, which destroy the vitamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.4] Serve at once rather than keeping the food hot, and use the cooking water in soups and sauces to recover the vitamin that dissolved into it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.b] Ascorbic acid is a very unstable vitamin, and its properties explain every rule for keeping it in food. Higher Level answers on vitamin C add these properties to sources, functions and deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.0] Vitamin C is a white, crystalline, acidic substance with a sweet-sour taste, and it is very soluble in water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.1] Because it is water-soluble, vitamin C is lost into cooking water when vegetables are boiled or soaked.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.2] Vitamin C is destroyed by prolonged heat, by light, by alkalis such as bicarbonate of soda, and by oxidation in air.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.3] The enzyme oxidase is released when plant cells are cut or bruised and destroys vitamin C, so cut vegetables should be cooked promptly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.4] Copper and brass pans destroy vitamin C, and it is also lost during long storage and dehydration.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.5] Vitamin C is an antioxidant, which means it acts as a reducing agent (it donates electrons), and this is how it changes ferric iron to ferrous iron.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.b] Vitamin B12 is unusual because only animal foods contain it naturally. Its absorption also needs a helper made in the stomach.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.0] Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods: liver and kidney, meat, fish and oily fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.1] Plant foods contain no natural vitamin B12, so vegans rely on fortified foods such as some breakfast cereals, plant milks and yeast extract, or on supplements.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.2] The lining of the stomach makes intrinsic factor, a protein that binds vitamin B12 so that it can be absorbed in the ileum (the end of the small intestine).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.3] The liver stores vitamin B12, and the store can last for several years, so deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.b] B12 is needed wherever cells are being made or nerves are working, which is why its deficiency affects both the blood and the nervous system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.0] Vitamin B12 works with folate to form healthy red blood cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.1] Vitamin B12 is needed for the synthesis of DNA, so it is needed for cell division, especially in cells that divide rapidly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.2] Vitamin B12 maintains the myelin sheath, the fatty layer that insulates nerve fibres and keeps nerve impulses working normally, so it keeps nerve cells healthy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.3] Vitamin B12 is also involved in the body's use of fatty acids and amino acids.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.b] A lack of B12 harms both the blood and the nerves. The cause can be the diet or a failure to absorb the vitamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.0] B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia, in which red blood cells are too large and immature and cannot carry oxygen effectively.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.1] Symptoms of this anaemia include tiredness, weakness, pale skin and breathlessness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.2] Nerve damage causes numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, poor balance and memory problems.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.3] A strict vegan diet without fortified foods or supplements can cause deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.4] Pernicious anaemia is the form of B12 deficiency caused by a lack of intrinsic factor, often through an autoimmune condition, which prevents B12 absorption even when the diet is adequate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.5] Failure to absorb B12 can also come from stomach or gut disease, and older people absorb less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.b] B12 is more stable than vitamin C, but it is not indestructible.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.0] Vitamin B12 is water-soluble, so some is lost into cooking water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.1] Vitamin B12 is a red crystalline compound that contains the mineral cobalt, which gives it the name cobalamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.2] Vitamin B12 is stable to heat in ordinary cooking but is destroyed by light and by strong acids and alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.b] Folate is the natural form of vitamin B9, and folic acid is the synthetic form. Folate is needed for making new cells, which matters most in early pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.0] Natural folate is found in dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, sprouts and cabbage, in pulses such as chickpeas, lentils and beans, and in asparagus, oranges, liver and yeast extract.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.1] Wholegrain and fortified breakfast cereals also supply folate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.2] Folic acid is the synthetic form used in supplements and in fortified foods such as breakfast cereals. It is not a different vitamin from folate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.3] Folate is needed to synthesise DNA and RNA, so it is essential for cell division and growth, including the growth of the foetus.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.4] Folate works with vitamin B12 to form red blood cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.5] In the first weeks of pregnancy, folate is essential for the formation of the neural tube, the early brain and spinal cord, and it protects against neural tube defects such as spina bifida and anencephaly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.b] The neural tube forms in the first weeks of pregnancy, before many women know they are pregnant, so folate advice is aimed at women before they conceive.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.0] A lack of folate causes megaloblastic anaemia, with the same large, immature red blood cells as B12 deficiency, and tiredness, weakness, pale skin, breathlessness and a sore tongue.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.1] Neural tube defects, such as spina bifida and anencephaly, can develop in the unborn baby if the mother lacks folate in early pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.2] Poor growth is also an effect of folate deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.3] Women who may become pregnant are advised, in HSE advice, to take a folic acid supplement before conception and during early pregnancy to reduce the risk of neural tube defects, as well as eating folate-rich foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.b] Folate is fragile in the kitchen, which is why fortified foods use the more stable synthetic form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.0] Folate is water-soluble and is sensitive to heat, light and oxygen, so it is destroyed by prolonged cooking and storage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.1] Folate is lost into cooking water during prolonged boiling.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.2] Synthetic folic acid is more stable than natural folate, which is why it is used in fortification.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-lipids-s4.0.b] As a fat is heated it passes through three different stages, each at a higher temperature than the last: it melts, then it smokes, then it can burst into flame. Knowing these temperatures explains how fats are chosen and used in cooking.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.0] Melting point is the temperature at which a solid fat turns to liquid. Fats are mixtures of triglycerides, so they melt over a range rather than at one sharp temperature, and solid fats melt at about 30–40 °C. Oils are already liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.1] Fats with a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points, because their kinked chains do not pack tightly. Fats with more saturated fatty acids, whose chains are straight and pack closely, have higher melting points.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.2] Smoke point is the temperature at which an overheated lipid begins to decompose and gives off a continuous blue haze. Smoke points are about 200 °C for solid fats and about 250 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.3] On decomposition the fat splits into glycerol and fatty acids, and the glycerol breaks down further to release acrolein, which has an acrid, irritating smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.4] Free fatty acids, impurities and food particles left in the oil lower its smoke point, and repeated use builds these up, so used or dirty fat smokes at a lower temperature. This is why frying fat is strained and replaced.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.5] A fat with a high smoke point suits frying, while butter, with a lower smoke point, burns.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.6] Flash point is the temperature at which the vapour from an overheated lipid ignites spontaneously and bursts into flame. Flash points are about 310 °C for solid fats and about 325 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.7] A fat that reaches its flash point is a serious fire hazard. Never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended, never heat fat until it smokes, and smother a fat fire with a fire blanket or lid, never water.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.b] This practical compares how common fats and oils behave on heating, and links the results to their fatty acids and to impurities. Care is needed because the fats become very hot.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.0] Aim: to compare the melting, smoke and flash points of common fats and oils, for example butter, lard, olive oil and sunflower oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.1] 1. Use a small amount of each fat or oil, so any smoke or fire is limited.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.2] 2. Heat each gently, preferably on a hot plate rather than a flame, which is safer for a class demonstration, and read the temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.3] 3. Record the order in which the fats melt, and which fats smoke first. Do not heat beyond the smoke point.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.4] Conclusion: link the order of melting points to how saturated the fatty acids are, and the point at which smoke appears to the fat's impurities.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.b] Oil and water do not mix normally, but they can be forced to, and in some foods they stay mixed. This is the basis of mayonnaise, butter, milk and many sauces.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.0] An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix (immiscible), such as oil and water, with one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.1] Vigorous shaking, whisking or blending forces the liquids together by breaking one liquid into droplets that spread through the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.2] A temporary emulsion, such as French dressing (oil and vinegar), separates again on standing because nothing keeps the droplets apart.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.3] A permanent (stable) emulsion, such as mayonnaise, hollandaise sauce, ice cream, cream, milk or butter, stays mixed because an emulsifying agent coats the droplets.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.4] An emulsifier has a hydrophilic (water-attracting) end and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) end, which sit at the boundary between the oil and the water.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.5] In an oil-in-water emulsion, oil droplets are dispersed in water, as in mayonnaise, milk and cream.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.6] In a water-in-oil emulsion, water droplets are dispersed in fat, as in butter, margarine and some spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.b] Making mayonnaise and a French dressing side by side shows the difference between a temporary and a permanent emulsion.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.0] Aim: to show emulsion formation and the effect of an emulsifier, using mayonnaise (egg yolk, oil, and vinegar or lemon juice) and a French dressing (oil and vinegar).
[hom-lipids-s4.3.1] 1. Make the mayonnaise by adding the oil to the egg yolk slowly while whisking, so the droplets are small and evenly spread.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.2] 2. Shake the oil and vinegar together for the French dressing, and leave both to stand.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.3] Result: the French dressing separates, but the mayonnaise stays stable because the egg yolk is the emulsifier. Comparing with and without an emulsifier shows this.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.b] Two kinds of additive help keep an emulsion together, and they do different jobs. An emulsifier makes the emulsion, while a stabiliser helps it stay that way.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.0] An emulsifying agent is a molecule with a hydrophilic (water-loving) end and a hydrophobic (water-hating, fat-loving) end. The hydrophilic end attaches to the water and the hydrophobic end to the oil, so the droplets are coated and cannot join together.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.1] Emulsifying agents include lecithin (found in egg yolk and soya; the food additive E322), mustard, and mono- and diglycerides such as glycerol monostearate (GMS; mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids are E471).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.2] The function of an emulsifier is to reduce the tension between oil and water so they mix, and to make and keep the emulsion stable.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.3] Stabilisers do not form the emulsion. They thicken the water phase and stop droplets joining together (coalescing).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.4] Examples of stabilisers are starch, pectin, gelatine and gums such as xanthan gum and guar gum. They are used in dressings, sauces, ice cream and low-fat spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.b] Some fats can be spread or shaped without being runny or rock hard. This property, called plasticity, is why butter and margarine behave so differently from oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.0] Plasticity is the ability of a fat to be shaped, spread or worked (creamed, rubbed in) at a given temperature without being fully liquid or fully hard.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.1] It is due to a mixture of solid and liquid fat within the fat: the solid fat forms a network that holds the liquid fat. The proportion of saturated fatty acids only decides how much of the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.2] Butter, lard, margarine and shortening are plastic over a range of temperatures. Oils are not plastic, a very unsaturated fat is too liquid to hold its shape, and a very saturated fat is hard and brittle when cold.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.3] Plastic fats are valued in pastry and cake making because they can be creamed, which traps air, or rubbed into flour and layered. Plasticity also gives butter and margarine their spreadability.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.b] Liquid oils can be turned into solid fats by changing their fatty acids. This process, hydrogenation, is how margarine is made from vegetable oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.0] Hydrogenation is the addition of hydrogen to the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, using a nickel catalyst under heat and pressure.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.1] It converts unsaturated fatty acids to saturated ones, so a liquid oil becomes a semi-solid or solid fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.2] It is used to make margarine and spreads, hard margarines, shortenings and baking fats from liquid vegetable oils. The fats are solid, plastic and more stable against oxidative rancidity.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.3] Partial hydrogenation forms trans fatty acids, so the process is now controlled. Full hydrogenation gives a hard saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.b] Rancidity is the spoilage of fats and oils, which gives them an unpleasant taste and smell. It happens in two different ways, with different causes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.0] Oxidative rancidity: oxygen from the air reacts at the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, which also breaks the chain. This produces aldehydes and ketones, which cause the rancid smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.1] Light, heat and traces of metal ions speed up oxidative rancidity. It affects polyunsaturated oils most because they have the most double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.2] Hydrolytic rancidity: water, together with the enzyme lipase from the food itself or from micro-organisms such as moulds and bacteria, breaks fats down into fatty acids and glycerol. The free fatty acids, for example butyric acid in butter, give the smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.3] Hydrolytic rancidity can occur in a freezer, because lipase is not destroyed by freezing. Blanching vegetables before freezing destroys these enzymes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.4] Fats are protected from rancidity when stored in airtight, opaque containers in a cool, dark place, kept dry, bought in small amounts and used before the date. Refrigerating or freezing, using less-unsaturated fats, adding antioxidants and not reusing frying oil repeatedly also help.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.b] Antioxidants are the main chemical defence against oxidative rancidity. They are added to fats and oils so that they keep their flavour for longer.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.0] An antioxidant is a substance that slows oxidation. It delays oxidative rancidity by reacting with oxygen or free radicals before they attack the double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin E (tocopherols, E306), present in some vegetable oils, and vitamin C (ascorbic acid, E300).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.2] Synthetic antioxidants added to fats include butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, E320) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, E321).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.3] Antioxidants are added to oils, margarines, spreads, snack foods and fried foods to keep their flavour and extend their shelf life. They appear in the ingredients list by name or E number under the additives rules (Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008).
