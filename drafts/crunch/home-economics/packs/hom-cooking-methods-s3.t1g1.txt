TARGET hom-cooking-methods-s3: 3. Choice of cooking method
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.b] Cooking methods fall into three groups according to what carries the heat to the food. The right choice depends on the food, the eater and the result you want.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.0] Moist methods use water, stock or steam as the medium. Dry methods use hot air or radiant heat (heat given off as rays from a hot element or flame) with little or no added liquid. Fat-based methods use fat or oil as the medium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.1] The food: tender cuts and delicate foods such as fish, eggs and vegetables suit quick methods, because they need little time to become tender. Tough, fibrous cuts need long, moist methods, which soften the fibres.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.2] Nutrition: steaming, stir-frying and grilling use little or no water and short times, so they conserve water-soluble vitamins. Boiling loses them into the cooking water unless that liquid is used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.3] Palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat): dry and fat-based methods brown and crisp the surface, adding colour and flavour, while moist methods give a soft, tender texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.4] Diet: steaming, poaching, boiling and grilling on a rack add little or no fat, which suits a diet that limits fat. Deep-fat frying adds fat and energy because the food absorbs fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.5] Consumer needs: an older person who has difficulty chewing or swallowing needs soft, moist, easily digested food, so a moist method is the better choice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.6] Time and equipment: a pressure cooker shortens cooking time, while stewing and braising are long, slow methods in a covered pan or oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.7] Cost: slow moist cooking makes an inexpensive cut of meat tender and palatable, so you do not have to buy an expensive cut.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.b] In moist methods the food is cooked in water, stock or steam. The temperature of the liquid is what separates them: boiling is hottest, simmering is just below it and poaching is lower again.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.0] Boiling: food is cooked in rapidly bubbling water at boiling point, 100 °C at sea level. It is quick and used for vegetables, pasta, rice, eggs and potatoes, but water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) into the water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.1] Simmering: liquid is kept just below boiling point, with small bubbles breaking the surface. It is gentler than boiling and is used for stocks, soups, stews and stewed fruit.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.2] Stewing: food is cooked slowly and gently in a small amount of liquid in a covered pan or casserole, on the hob or in the oven. It suits tough meat and vegetables, as in Irish stew, a traditional stew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.3] Stewing keeps nutrients because the liquid is served with the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.4] Steaming: food is cooked by the steam from boiling water without touching the water, in a steamer, on a plate over a pan or in a covered bowl in a pan. No fat is added, and nutrients and shape are kept. It suits fish, chicken, vegetables, rice and steamed puddings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.5] Poaching: food is cooked gently in liquid kept slightly below simmering point, about 85 °C, on the hob or in the oven. It suits delicate foods that would break up or toughen in fiercer heat, such as eggs, fish and fruit like pears.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.6] Braising: tough meat or vegetables are browned in fat, then cooked slowly with a little liquid in a covered dish, in the oven or on the hob. The long, slow, moist cooking tenderises tough cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.7] Pressure cooking: food is cooked in a sealed pan in which steam builds up pressure. This raises the temperature above 100 °C and shortens the cooking time. It is used for stocks, stews, tough meat and pulses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.b] Dry methods cook food with hot air or radiant heat rather than liquid. They brown the surface, which adds colour and flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.0] Roasting: meat, poultry, potatoes or vegetables are cooked uncovered in the oven in a roasting tin, usually with a little fat. The surface browns while the centre cooks by conduction and convection.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.1] Baking: food is cooked by the dry heat of an oven, mainly by convection (hot air circulating) and radiation. It is used for cakes, bread, pastry, fish, pasta and potato dishes, as with fish pie and lasagne.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.2] Grilling: food is cooked by radiant heat from an element or flame above or below it. The heat seals the surface, so it is a fast method. It suits tender cuts, steaks, chops, thin fish, sausages, rashers, tomatoes, mushrooms, peppers and kebabs.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.3] Barbecuing: this is grilling outdoors on a grid over burning charcoal or gas, using radiant heat, which gives a smoky flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.4] Grilling lowers the fat content of food, because fat drips away from it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.b] Grilling is fast and the heat is fierce, so food can burn outside while it is raw inside, or dry out. These rules prevent that.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.0] Preheat the grill to the required temperature so that the surface seals on contact.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.1] Oil the grid or the food to prevent sticking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.2] Use thin pieces, so that heat reaches the centre before the surface burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.3] Do not add salt before grilling, because salt draws moisture to the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.4] Turn the food with tongs rather than a fork, so that its juices stay inside instead of running out through holes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.5] Use the correct cooking time, since grilled food dries out and overcooks quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.b] Fat-based methods, usually called frying, cook food in hot fat or oil. They give a crisp, golden surface, but the amount of fat used varies a great deal from one method to another.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.0] Shallow frying: food is cooked in hot fat in a shallow pan, partly covered by the fat and turned once or twice, giving a crisp surface. It is used for eggs, fish, chops and pancakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.1] Deep-fat frying: food is fully immersed in hot oil in a deep pan or fryer, which seals the surface quickly. It is used for chips and fish in batter. The food absorbs some fat, so it is higher in fat and energy than grilled food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.2] Stir-frying: small, thin pieces are tossed and stirred constantly over high heat in a wok or large pan with a little oil. Because the cooking is so quick, it keeps colour, crispness and vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.3] Dry frying: food is cooked in a non-stick pan without added fat, using the fat already in foods such as bacon rashers and sausages.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.4] Pot roasting: meat is cooked in a little fat in a covered pan on the hob, so it cooks in its own juices and steam and stays moist. It is not done in the oven and not uncovered.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.b] A microwave oven is a fast method that suits reheating and cooking small amounts. It works differently from the other methods, so its advantages and limits are worth knowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.0] Microwave ovens cook and reheat quickly with little added fat and water, so vitamins are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.1] They do not brown food unless a grill or combination mode is used, so the surface stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.2] Containers must be microwave-safe, and metal must not be used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.b] Examiners often ask you to give dishes or foods for a method. These are the examples that are accepted.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.0] Baking: apple crumble, sponge, soda bread, fish pie, lasagne, pizza and muffins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.1] Grilling: kebabs, chicken satay, burgers, fish, rashers, sausages, tomatoes, mushrooms and bruschetta.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.2] Steaming: fish, chicken, rice, Christmas pudding (a traditional steamed pudding), fruit and vegetables. Roasting: meat, potatoes and vegetables.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.3] Frying has four types: dry, shallow, deep and stir-fry. The moist methods are boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.4] Classifying methods: moist covers boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking. Dry covers baking, grilling, roasting and barbecuing. Frying covers dry, shallow, deep and stir-frying.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.b] Steaming is a good choice for an older person because it is simple and gives soft food that is easy to eat. It also has weaknesses, so a full evaluation covers ease, nutrition and palatability.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.0] Ease: steaming needs little attention or equipment, a whole meal can cook in a tiered steamer, and it suits small portions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.1] Texture: the moist, soft food is easy to chew and swallow, which suits an older person with difficulty chewing or swallowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.2] Nutrition: steaming adds no fat, so it suits a diet that limits fat. Because the food does not sit in water, water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.3] Palatability: steamed food keeps its colour and moisture, but it can taste bland and does not brown. Overcooking spoils its texture and colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.4] Fish, chicken, rice and vegetables suit steaming; steamed puddings are a traditional example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.5] Herbs, lemon juice and a sauce add flavour without added salt, which suits a diet that limits salt.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s2: 2. Heat transfer and the principles of cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.b] Cooking is more than making food hot. Heat changes the food in several useful ways, and each change is a reason examiners expect you to give.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.0] Cooking makes food safe by destroying harmful micro-organisms, so food must be heated right through to the centre, because bacteria deep inside a thick piece can survive if the heat never reaches them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.1] Heat denatures protein (changes its structure), so that digestive enzymes can act on it more easily.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.2] Cooking gelatinises starch (the starch grains swell and burst in water and heat), which makes rice, potatoes and pasta easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.3] Cooking softens the cellulose in plant cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to chew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.4] Heat develops flavour and changes colour and texture, which makes food more appetising and gives variety in the diet.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.5] Cooking destroys enzymes and most spoilage micro-organisms, which extends the time food stays fit to eat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.b] Whatever the method, cooking is heat moving from a source into the food. The cook controls how much heat reaches the food, for how long, and whether moisture or fat is present.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.0] Too low a temperature or too short a time leaves food undercooked and unsafe, because the centre never gets hot enough; too high a temperature or too long a time dries, toughens and burns the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.1] Thickness governs cooking time: thin or small pieces cook quickly and evenly, while a large joint needs lower heat for longer so that the centre cooks before the outside burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.2] Moist methods prevent food drying out; dry methods need added fat, basting (spooning hot fat or juices over the food) or a covered dish to keep food juicy.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.3] Food type governs the method: cuts rich in connective tissue need long, slow cooking, while delicate foods need short cooking at gentle heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.4] Cooked food must be served promptly or cooled quickly, because the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C is where bacteria multiply fastest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.b] Conduction is heat transfer through a solid from one molecule to the next, without the material itself moving. It is the way heat passes from a hot pan into the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.0] As molecules at the hot end are heated they vibrate faster and pass energy to their neighbours, until the whole piece is hot.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.1] Metals such as aluminium, copper and steel conduct heat quickly, which is why saucepans have metal bases; glass, ceramic, wood, plastic and air conduct heat slowly (they are insulators), which is why handles and oven gloves are made from them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.2] Pan-based methods begin with conduction: the cooker heats the pan base, which heats the liquid or fat in the pan, and that then heats the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.3] Boiling, poaching, stewing, shallow frying and stir-frying all depend on conduction through the pan base.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.4] Searing a steak in a hot pan: heat conducts from the pan surface into the outer layers of the meat and then gradually towards the centre.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.5] Baking trays and cake tins conduct heat into the base of a cake, so the base sets before the top.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.6] A large solid piece of food takes longer to cook through, because the heat has to travel inwards from the surface molecule by molecule.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.7] Diagram: arrows from a gas flame through a pan base into the food, labelled as conduction, with the direction of heat flow marked.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.b] Convection is heat transfer through a liquid or gas by currents. The heated fluid expands and becomes less dense so it rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place and is heated in turn.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.0] Natural convection occurs in a pot of boiling water, in a stew and in the hot air of a conventional oven, because the heated fluid rises on its own and the cooler fluid sinks.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.1] Forced convection: the fan in a fan oven circulates the hot air, so food cooks faster and more evenly than in a conventional oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.2] Deep-fat frying: convection currents in the hot oil carry heat to every surface of the immersed food at once.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.3] Steaming: hot steam rises from boiling water and carries heat to the food, and it also condenses on cooler food, releasing its latent heat (the heat stored in steam) to cook it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.4] Examples: boiling, stewing, steaming, deep-fat frying, and baking or roasting in an oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.5] Diagram: a pot of water with arrows showing hot water rising from the base, cooling at the surface and sinking at the sides.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.b] Radiation is heat transfer by infrared rays that travel in straight lines from a hot source and heat the first solid surface they strike. No solid, liquid or gas is needed to carry it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.0] The surface struck by radiant heat absorbs it, so browning and crisping happen on that surface while the inside of the food cooks by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.1] Grilling uses an element or flame above or below the food to heat the surface, sealing and browning it; thin pieces cook quickly because the heat is intense.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.2] Barbecuing and toasting use radiant heat from glowing coals, gas flames or electric elements.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.3] Dark, dull (matt) surfaces absorb radiant heat more readily than shiny surfaces, which reflect it; this is why foil reflects heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.4] Radiant heat weakens with distance from the source, so the height of the grill rack is adjusted to control how much heat the food receives.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.5] Diagram: straight arrows from a grill element to the top surface of a piece of food, labelled as radiation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.b] In practice most cooking uses more than one type of heat transfer at once. Microwave cooking is a special case, because the heat is made inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.0] Roasting uses radiation from the oven walls and element, convection by the hot air, and conduction inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.1] Boiling uses conduction through the pan base, then convection in the water, which is at 100 °C when boiling.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.2] In microwave cooking, a magnetron produces microwaves (electromagnetic waves) that make the water molecules in the food vibrate, and this makes heat inside the outer layer of the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.3] Once the heat is made in the outer layer, it spreads inwards by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.b] Each type of food reacts to heat in its own way, so the principles above are applied differently to each one. The right choice gives food that is safe, tender and keeps its colour and nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.0] Meat: tough cuts, rich in collagen, need long, slow, moist cooking so that collagen converts to gelatine; tender cuts can be grilled or fried quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.1] Fish: its flesh has little connective tissue and sets quickly, so it needs short cooking at gentle heat; poaching, steaming, grilling, baking and frying all suit it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.2] Eggs: egg proteins set (coagulate) at moderate temperatures, so eggs and custards need gentle heat; high heat makes scrambled egg rubbery and custard curdle.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.3] Vegetables and fruit: short cooking in a little boiling water, or steaming, keeps colour, texture and water-soluble vitamins, which would otherwise dissolve into the cooking water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.4] Cereals, rice and pasta: starch needs water and heat to gelatinise, so they are cooked in plenty of boiling water or steam.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.5] Milk and cheese: milk scorches on direct heat and cheese turns stringy at high temperatures, so sauces containing them are cooked gently.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.6] Fats and oils: fat overheats, smokes and breaks down if heated too far, so frying oil must be watched, an oil with a high smoke point chosen for deep-fat frying, and it should not be reused too often.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.7] Pastry and cakes: the fat is kept cold when rubbed in, the dry heat of the oven sets the flaky structure, and the oven should not be opened early when baking a cake.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s1: 1. Physical and chemical changes during preparation and cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.b] A physical change alters the size, shape, state, colour or texture of food without forming new substances. Heat usually drives these changes, and each one has a food you can name as an example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.0] Coagulation: heat unfolds (denatures) egg, milk and fish proteins, which then bond into a set structure. Egg white turns from clear to white and opaque, fish flesh from translucent to opaque, and custard, scrambled egg and omelette set.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.1] Gelatinisation: starch granules absorb water and swell when heated in liquid, and can swell until they burst. This thickens sauces, gravies, custard and porridge.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.2] Tenderising: long, moist cooking converts collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) into gelatine, so tough cuts of meat become tender.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.3] Shrinkage: meat and fish lose water and melted fat during cooking, so they become smaller and lighter and fat melts out of meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.4] Increase in size: dried peas, beans and fruit, rice and pasta absorb water and swell during cooking. Whisked egg foam, sponge and bread also rise and increase in volume.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.5] Colour change: meat turns from red or pink to brown or grey as its myoglobin (the red pigment in meat) is heated. Green vegetables turn olive-brown with prolonged cooking or acid because the chlorophyll is altered, whereas carrots and other orange vegetables stay colourful.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.6] Softening: heat softens the cellulose and breaks down pectin (the 'glue' between cells) in plant cell walls, so vegetables and fruit become tender as cell water is lost.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.7] Increased digestibility and safety: heat destroys most micro-organisms and makes starch and protein easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.8] Loss of nutrients, because water-soluble vitamins dissolve in cooking water, is also counted as a physical change.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.b] A chemical change alters the molecular make-up of food and forms new substances with different colours, flavours and textures. Raising agents are a clear example, because they make a new gas that lifts the mixture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.0] Chemical raising agent: bicarbonate of soda reacts with an acid such as buttermilk and releases carbon dioxide gas, which makes soda bread rise. The same applies in cakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.1] Baking powder contains bicarbonate of soda plus an acid, so it releases carbon dioxide when it is moistened and heated.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.2] Biological raising agent: yeast feeds on sugar during fermentation and gives off carbon dioxide (and alcohol), which makes bread dough rise. The gas expands in the oven, and baking kills the yeast.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.3] Tenderising by acid or enzymes, for example in a marinade or with a meat tenderiser, is also accepted as a chemical change.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.4] Browning reactions are chemical changes too, because they make new coloured and flavoured substances: the Maillard reaction, caramelisation and dextrinisation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.b] Enzymic browning is a chemical change caused by an enzyme in damaged fruit and vegetables. It happens without heat, which separates it from the browning of toast or roast meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.0] Cutting, peeling or bruising breaks cell walls, so the enzyme polyphenol oxidase (called 'oxidase' in some marking schemes) meets phenolic compounds in the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.1] Oxygen from the air is needed: the enzyme oxidises the phenolic compounds, which then form brown pigments.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.2] Examples: sliced apple, banana, pear, avocado, potato and mushroom turning brown on standing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.3] Browning spoils the appearance, flavour and vitamin C content of fruit and vegetables. It is wanted in the making of black tea, where it is used deliberately.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.4] To stop oxygen reaching the surface, keep cut surfaces under water, sugar syrup, cling film, a layer of juice or a coating of dressing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.5] Acid such as lemon juice lowers the pH and slows the enzyme's action. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) reduces the first brown products back to colourless compounds.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.6] Heat inactivates the enzyme, so blanching vegetables in boiling water before freezing, or cooking them, stops browning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.7] Refrigeration slows enzyme activity but does not stop it. Working quickly and cutting just before serving also limits browning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.b] A fair test lets you see which treatment slows browning best, by changing one thing at a time and keeping everything else the same.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.0] Aim: to find which treatment best slows enzymic browning on a sliced apple.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.1] Hypothesis: lemon juice will slow browning more than an untreated slice, because acid slows polyphenol oxidase.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.2] Independent variable: the treatment applied to each slice, e.g. untreated (the control), dipped in lemon juice, dipped in sugar syrup, dipped in salt water or cold water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.3] Dependent variable: the degree of browning, rated on a 0 to 5 colour scale at fixed times.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.4] Controlled variables: the same apple variety and ripeness, slices of the same thickness cut with the same knife, the same dipping time, the same room temperature and the same time after cutting. These keep the test fair, so only the treatment changes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.5] Method: 1. Wash and dry the apple and cut it into slices of equal thickness. 2. Dip each treated slice for the same time, keeping one untreated slice as the control. 3. Lay all slices on labelled plates and record the colour of each every 5 minutes for 30 minutes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.6] Safety and hygiene: wash hands and the apple, use a clean board and a sharp knife held with a claw grip, and wipe up any spilled juice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.7] Expected result: the untreated control browns first and most, while the lemon juice slices stay palest for longest. This supports the hypothesis.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.8] Evaluation: a rating by eye is subjective and apples vary, so repeat the test with several apples and average the ratings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.b] Non-enzymic browning needs heat and does not involve enzymes. It has three processes, each involving a different food molecule: the Maillard reaction (protein with sugar), caramelisation (sugar alone) and dextrinisation (starch).
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.0] Maillard reaction: amino acids from protein react with reducing sugars such as glucose when food is heated in dry conditions, producing brown colour and savoury roasted flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.1] Maillard browning shows on roast meat and poultry skin, roast potatoes, toast and the crust of bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.2] Dry heat and a dry surface favour browning, so roasting, grilling, baking and frying brown food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.3] Little Maillard browning occurs in boiled, steamed or stewed food, because water holds the surface at about 100 °C, too cool for browning, so the food stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.4] Caramelisation: sugar (sucrose) melts and darkens under strong heat, forming caramel with a sweet, slightly bitter flavour. It needs no protein.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.5] Caramelisation examples: caramel sauce, crème caramel and toffee.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.6] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains called dextrins, which brown and give a crisp surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.7] Dextrinisation examples: toast and the crust of cakes and bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.b] Some nutrients dissolve in water or are destroyed by heat and air, so preparation and cooking can lower the nutritional value of food. Knowing why they are lost shows how to keep them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.0] Water-soluble vitamins, vitamin C and the B group, dissolve into cooking water and are lost when it is poured away.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.1] Vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and oxidation (exposure to air), so the longer vegetables are cooked and exposed to air, the more is lost.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.2] Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are more stable in cooking but drain away with the melted fat from grilled, roasted or fried food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.3] Soluble minerals such as potassium and some sugars dissolve into cooking water along with the water-soluble vitamins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.4] Cutting vegetables into small pieces increases the surface exposed to water and air, and soaking them before cooking increases loss. Cooking in a lot of water for a long time has the same effect.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.b] Most nutrient loss can be limited by handling vegetables sensibly before and during cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.0] Peeling thinly or cooking in the skin reduces loss, because nutrients are concentrated just beneath the skin. Peeling thickly increases loss.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.1] Holding cooked vegetables hot for long periods destroys further vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.2] Adding bicarbonate of soda to vegetables destroys vitamin C and thiamine, so avoid alkalis and copper pans.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.3] Steaming, stir-frying and microwaving use little water and short cooking times, so they conserve more nutrients. Preparing just before cooking, using a sharp knife, putting the lid on and serving soon after cooking also help.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.4] Cooking liquid from vegetables can be used in soups, sauces or gravies to recover some nutrients.

CANDIDATES hom-meal-planning-s2: 2. Dietary requirements through the life cycle
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.b] No two people need exactly the same diet. Energy and nutrient needs change with the stage of life and with circumstances, so every meal plan starts from the requirements of the person it is for.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.0] A balanced diet contains all the nutrients in the correct proportions for the individual's needs and good health, so what counts as balanced differs from person to person.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.1] Age: needs change at each life stage, with growth in childhood and adolescence, maintenance in adults and falling energy needs in older age.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.2] Sex: men generally need more energy than women because they have greater muscle mass and a larger body, while girls and women need extra iron once menstruation begins.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.3] Health status: illness, allergy, diabetes or a digestive disorder can change the foods a person is allowed to eat or how well their body absorbs nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.4] Pregnancy and breastfeeding increase the need for energy, protein, folate, iron and calcium, because the body is building a baby or making milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.5] Activity: energy needs rise with physical activity, and active people also need more fluid and carbohydrate. A sedentary adult needs less energy.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.6] Energy balance: energy taken in as food should match energy used by the body; any surplus is stored as body fat.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.b] A baby's first food and a young child's meals are planned around fast growth, a small stomach and teeth that are still forming. Pre-school guidelines are examined as short lists of practical rules.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.0] Breast milk supplies all the nutrients a baby needs for about the first six months.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.1] Breastfed babies, and babies taking little formula, are advised to take a vitamin D supplement from birth to 12 months, because breast milk contains little vitamin D.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.2] Weaning (moving from milk to solid food) begins at about six months, when the iron stored at birth starts to run low. Puréed meat and iron-fortified cereals supply iron.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.3] Salt should be kept low and sugar limited in foods for babies and young children, with none added to baby foods. Sugar harms developing teeth.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.4] Pre-school children have small, variable appetites, so regular balanced meals in small, manageable portions with nutritious snacks between suit them better than large meals. Attractive presentation helps.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.5] Young children need protein for growth, calcium and vitamin D for bones and teeth, iron for blood, and fruit and vegetables, following the Food Pyramid.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.6] Fussy eating is common. Offering new flavours one at a time in small portions, without forcing a child to eat, and involving the child in food preparation, helps them accept new foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.7] Sugary foods and drinks should be limited to protect developing teeth.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.b] School-age children need every nutrient in balance, and adolescents need even more during the growth spurt. Teenage eating habits, though, often work against meeting those needs.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.0] Children need every nutrient in balanced proportions, following the Irish Food Pyramid with portions suited to their age and activity. Protein is for growth, carbohydrate and fat for energy, calcium for bones and teeth, iron for blood, plus fibre and water, with nutritious snacks and less sugar and salt.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.1] Ways to include vegetables for children: stir-fries, cut shapes, grated or blended into sauces, smoothies, soups, and vegetables in lunchboxes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.2] Adolescents go through a growth spurt, roughly between the ages of 12 and 19, which increases the need for every nutrient.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.3] Girls need extra iron once they begin menstruating, because menstrual blood loss depletes iron stores. Fruit and vegetables supply fibre and vitamin C, and vitamin C improves the absorption of iron from plant foods, helping avoid anaemia.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.4] Calcium and vitamin D build bone mass during adolescence, so good intake now builds peak bone mass and reduces the risk of osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones) later.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.5] Active teenagers need high-energy starchy foods such as pasta, potatoes, rice and wholegrain cereals. Protein from meat, fish, eggs, dairy and pulses (animal and plant sources) supports growth and repair.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.6] High-salt, high-sugar and high-fat snacks and fizzy drinks add energy but few nutrients, so they should be limited. Irregular meals and skipped breakfasts are common in adolescence and make it harder to meet nutritional needs, so teenagers should eat three varied meals daily and avoid skipping meals, especially breakfast.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.7] Factors making a healthy diet difficult for teenagers: likes and dislikes, early exposure to fatty, salty and sugary foods, convenience and eating on the go, availability, cost, advertising and peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.b] Adult bodies are no longer growing, so the aim shifts to maintaining health and weight. The main guideline is to match what you eat to how active you are.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.0] Adults need to balance energy intake with activity to maintain a healthy weight, and to eat a balanced diet with all the nutrients in proportion.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.1] Daily starchy foods, fruit and vegetables supply the fibre, vitamins and minerals that support bowel health.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.2] Protein maintains muscle and repairs tissue, so a protein food is included at each main meal.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.3] Unsaturated oils should replace saturated fats, and sugar and salt should be kept low.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.4] Women of childbearing age need adequate folate and iron, because menstrual losses continue.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.5] A sedentary worker (one who sits most of the day) needs less energy, so should reduce fat and sugar, eat fibre-rich foods, get enough calcium, iron and vitamins, and limit salt.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.b] Older bodies burn less energy but still need nearly as many nutrients, so food must be nutrient-dense. Practical problems with teeth, appetite and money also shape what older people can eat.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.0] Energy needs fall with age because muscle mass and activity decline, so portions should be smaller but nutrient-dense (packed with nutrients per mouthful). Small, frequent, appetising meals suit reduced appetite, and fortified foods help.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.1] Protein remains important for maintaining muscle and repairing tissue, so older people should include a protein food at main meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.2] Calcium and vitamin D protect the bones, and calcium absorption decreases with age.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.3] Vitamin B12 absorption can fall in later life, so older people may need B12 from fortified foods or supplements.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.4] Fibre and fluid prevent constipation, which is common among older people who are less active. Iron and vitamin C are also needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.5] Salt should be reduced to help control blood pressure, and saturated fat reduced.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.6] Chewing and swallowing difficulties, or dentures, call for soft, moist and easy-to-chew foods. A limited budget and reduced mobility also affect shopping and cooking.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.7] Diet-related conditions common in older people include osteoporosis, diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure and high cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.b] A pregnant woman's diet must feed her own body and build a baby, so the need for particular nutrients rises while food safety matters more. This is the group examined most often, usually as nutrient, reason and food source.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.0] Folic acid (also called folate) taken before conception and in early pregnancy helps prevent neural-tube defects in the developing baby. Sources include green vegetables and fortified cereals, plus a supplement as advised.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.1] Iron supports the extra blood volume of pregnancy and the baby's iron stores; vitamin C eaten at the same meal improves iron absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.2] Calcium and vitamin D are needed for the baby's bones and teeth and to protect the mother's bones.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.3] Protein from lean meat, fish, fully cooked eggs and pasteurised dairy supports the growth of fetal tissue, the placenta and maternal tissues.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.4] Omega-3 fatty acids from cooked oily fish, such as salmon, support the development of the baby's brain and nervous system. Iodine and vitamins A, C, D, K and folate are also needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.5] Water is needed for blood plasma and fetal tissues, to carry nutrients and remove waste, and to prevent dehydration and constipation. Fibre also helps prevent constipation, which is common in pregnancy.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.6] Energy needs rise only modestly, mainly in later pregnancy, so a pregnant woman should not double her food intake. She should balance food with activity, follow the Food Pyramid and choose low-GI starchy foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.7] Breastfeeding women need extra energy, protein, calcium and fluid to produce milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.b] Some foods carry bacteria that can harm an unborn baby, so pregnancy guidelines restrict them. Any menu for a pregnant woman must follow these rules.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.0] Pâté, unpasteurised milk, soft mould-ripened cheese, and raw or undercooked meat, fish and eggs carry a risk of listeria or salmonella (food-poisoning bacteria) and should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.1] Alcohol should be avoided in pregnancy, and caffeine and salt should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.2] Taste changes and morning sickness can be managed with dry toast before rising and small, frequent meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.3] A sample one-day menu uses pasteurised dairy and fully cooked foods. Breakfast: fruit juice, porridge made with pasteurised milk and berries, wholemeal toast.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.4] Lunch: vegetable soup, wholemeal bread, yoghurt and fruit. Dinner: well-cooked lean chicken or fish with vegetables and potatoes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.b] A convalescent is someone recovering from illness. Their appetite is low but their body needs nutrients to repair itself, so food must be light, appealing and nourishing.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.0] Convalescents need small, appetising, easy-to-digest portions, because appetite is often reduced during recovery. Regular balanced meals are important.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.1] Protein from lean meat, fish, eggs and dairy supports tissue repair.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.2] Iron and vitamin C support recovery of blood levels, and vitamin C from fruit and vegetables aids iron absorption. Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and fluids keep the bowel working.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.3] Lightly seasoned meals are better tolerated; spicy, fried, oily and greasy foods should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.b] Active people burn more energy and lose fluid in sweat, so their diet is built around extra fuel and water.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.0] Active people need extra energy, mainly from complex carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, pasta, rice and potatoes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.1] Sweat losses mean active people need extra fluid, drunk before, during and after exercise.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.2] Protein supports muscle repair and growth after exercise, and fruit, yoghurt or wholegrain sandwiches supply energy between meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.b] Shift work and long or varied days disrupt normal mealtimes. Good planning keeps meals balanced even when no one is home at the usual time.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.0] Irregular shifts disrupt mealtimes, so people often skip meals or rely on fast food and vending machines, which should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.1] Planning ahead with a list, and keeping staples and some frozen or prepared ingredients, makes balanced meals easier.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.2] Batch cooking and freezing portions allows balanced meals to be eaten at staggered times without much last-minute preparation.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.3] Soups, casseroles and other reheatable portions suit people eating at different times.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.4] Packed lunches with fruit, wholegrain bread and yoghurt keep nutrition on track during long working days.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.5] Slow cookers and timers allow a meal to cook while the household is out. Portions should stay balanced, following the guidelines.
