TARGET hom-meal-planning-s5: 5. Meal management and planning
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.b] Ireland's national guide to healthy eating is the Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and the Healthy Eating Food Pyramid, published under Healthy Ireland. A balanced diet follows the pyramid, so it contains all the nutrients in the correct proportions.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.0] Vegetables, salad and fruit, together with wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice, make up the foundation shelves of the pyramid, so these are the foods eaten most often.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.1] Fruit and vegetables: five or more servings a day are recommended, and up to seven, because they supply vitamins, minerals and fibre.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.2] Milk, yoghurt and cheese, preferably the lower-fat versions, supply calcium and protein; three servings a day are given in menu answers.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.3] Meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans, pulses and nuts supply protein; two servings a day, and at least two portions of fish a week are advised, one of them oily fish.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.4] Fats, spreads and oils sit on a small shelf and are used in small amounts, with unsaturated oils preferred to saturated fat.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.5] The top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt. They are not needed for health, so they are limited to once or twice a week at most; sugary drinks are limited too, and water is the main drink.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.6] Salt intake is kept low by cooking with less salt and reading labels, and portion sizes are controlled so that energy intake is balanced with activity.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.0.7] When planning, choose wholegrain, lean meat and lower-fat dairy, vary the foods eaten, and use less salt and sugar.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.b] Good planning starts with who is being fed and ends with a plan for cooking. Meals are then chosen so that the whole day, not just one meal, matches the pyramid.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.0] Planning begins with the people being fed: their ages, health needs, activity, likes and dislikes, any special diets, and the number to feed.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.1] Each meal includes a starchy food, fruit or vegetables, and a protein food or dairy, so that all the pyramid groups are covered across the day.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.2] Breakfast is not skipped, because it supplies energy for the morning, and a packed lunch or snack supplies fruit and wholegrain foods between meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.3] Colour, flavour, texture and cooking method are varied, and the same food is not repeated in a day, so that meals are appetising and not repetitive.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.4] Portion sizes are matched to age, appetite and activity, and energy is balanced across the day.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.5] A three-meal menu sets out breakfast, lunch and dinner, often with a drink or snack. Name actual dishes, then show how they meet the person's needs.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.6] The steps in planning are: 1. decide the menus for the week. 2. Check the food and equipment already at home. 3. Write a shopping list. 4. Buy the food. 5. Schedule the cooking order and timing, after checking cooking time, budget and equipment.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.1.7] When a question names a person such as a pregnant woman, teenager, vegetarian, coeliac or person with CHD (coronary heart disease), apply that group's requirements to the menu.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.2.b] The cook's own ability decides which meals are realistic. Skill affects both what can be made and how consistent the results are.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.2.0] A skilled cook can adapt recipes, for example to add fibre, reduce fat or cater for a special diet, and can make dishes from raw ingredients.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.2.1] A less experienced cook may rely on convenience foods, so simple recipes with few stages suit them.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.2.2] Following a tested recipe with accurate measures gives consistent results and portion sizes.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.2.3] Nutrition knowledge, such as reading labels and knowing the guidelines, helps a cook choose healthy meals, and skills can be built by cooking with others.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.3.b] Time available on the day decides how elaborate a meal can be. Planning ahead lets a busy household still eat well.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.3.0] Dishes are chosen to suit the time available; quick dishes such as stir-fries, omelettes, soups and grills suit a limited preparation time.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.3.1] Batch cooking large quantities and freezing portions saves time on later days, because a meal is ready after reheating.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.3.2] Time can also be saved by preparing ahead, using convenience foods sensibly, and cooking one meal in stages.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.3.3] For families with irregular routines, keep a shopping list and staple stocks, and use bulk buying and special offers sensibly.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.3.4] For irregular routines, also keep some prepared or frozen ingredients and reheatable, freezable batches, use a slow cooker or timer, and pack meals, while keeping the diet balanced and portions sensible.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.b] The equipment in the kitchen decides which cooking methods are possible. The right appliances also save both time and energy.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.0] Equipment available decides what can be cooked: oven, hob, grill, microwave, pressure cooker, slow cooker, food processor, blender and freezer. A lack of equipment limits the methods that can be used.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.1] A microwave, pressure cooker, slow cooker or food processor reduces cooking and preparation time; microwave reheating also saves energy.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.2] A freezer permits batch cooking and bulk buying, because portions and large purchases can be stored until needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.3] Frozen foods keep their nutritional and sensory quality, and frozen fruit and vegetables are frozen at peak ripeness, retain most of their nutrients and reduce waste.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.4] Frozen foods also save time and money, add variety and out-of-season foods, allow portion control and make emergency meals possible.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.4.5] Freezers should be kept at -18 °C, and cooked food should not be left between 5 °C and 63 °C, the range in which bacteria multiply quickly.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.b] A limited budget does not have to mean a poor diet if meals are planned before shopping. Low-income families have limited choice, so careful planning matters most for them.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.0] Set a budget, plan menus before shopping, and write a shopping list and keep to it. This prevents impulse buying and waste.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.1] Compare prices, including unit prices, and use special offers sensibly. Bulk buying suits only foods that will be used or frozen.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.2] Seasonal produce is usually cheaper and fresher than out-of-season imports.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.3] Own-brand products are often cheaper than branded equivalents; compare labels and unit prices.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.4] Pulses, eggs and tinned fish are inexpensive sources of protein, as are less expensive cuts of meat, and growing food or batch cooking can also cut costs.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.5] Cheap processed foods are often high in salt, sugar and fat, so budget plans should still include fresh produce, wholegrains and milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.6] Foods are chosen for nutritive value, cost, availability and season, suitability for the people (age, health), preparation time and storage, and labels and dates are checked.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.5.7] Food is stored so that it stays safe and usable: in the fridge, freezer or cupboard, with a vegetable drawer for vegetables, and with stock rotation.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.b] Food that is thrown away is money wasted. Planning, correct storage and understanding dates on packs all cut waste and the food bill.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.0] Plan meals and shop with a list, check what is already at home, and buy only what is needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.1] A use-by date relates to food safety and food must not be eaten after it; a best-before date relates to quality, and food may still be safe after it.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.2] Stock is rotated so that older items are used first.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.3] Food is stored correctly, and food that will not be used in time is frozen before its use-by date.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.4] Leftovers are planned into the next day's meals, for example turning a roast into soup or a pie, and the right portions are cooked; scraps can be composted.
[hom-meal-planning-s5.6.5] Reducing waste saves money and reduces environmental harm, such as landfill and greenhouse gas emissions.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s4: 4. Dietary guidelines
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.b] Dietary guidelines are official advice, based on scientific evidence, on the types and amounts of food that keep a population healthy. They turn nutrition science into practical advice for the general healthy population, and are revised as evidence changes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.0] Evidence comes from nutrition research, national food consumption surveys (which record what people actually eat) and health data on diet-related diseases.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.1] Expert committees review this evidence and turn it into practical advice on foods rather than nutrients, setting the types of food to eat and the amounts to limit. The guidelines are reviewed as new evidence appears.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.2] The Department of Health publishes the healthy eating guidelines for Ireland and the Food Pyramid, which is one of the tools used to explain them (the current pyramid is called 'Healthy Food for Life', 2016).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.3] The HSE supports the guidelines, and the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) and safefood are other Irish bodies in this field.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.4] The guidelines are written for the general healthy population, so they are not a substitute for advice for individuals with special medical needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.5] The aims are to make sure people get enough nutrients, to prevent deficiency, and to reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, some cancers and osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.6] One consistent message also helps consumers, schools and food planners, because everyone is working from the same advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.b] The Irish guidelines can be given as about eight principles. Together they say which foods to eat most, which to eat in moderation and which to keep small.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.0] Eat plenty of vegetables, salad and fruit: about five to seven portions a day, spread across meals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.1] Base meals on starchy foods, choosing wholegrain bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.2] Include protein from lean meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans, lentils and nuts, with fish eaten regularly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.3] Choose milk, yoghurt and cheese in lower-fat forms, as they supply calcium and HBV protein (high biological value protein, which contains all the essential amino acids).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.4] Use fats and oils sparingly, and prefer unsaturated oils such as rapeseed and olive oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.5] Limit foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, which are not needed for health and should be occasional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.6] Drink water as the main drink, and keep alcohol within recommended limits.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.7] Eat regular meals and prepare food with methods that add little fat, such as steaming, grilling and baking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.b] Each guideline is linked to a health benefit or a health risk. Examiners want the guideline and its reason together.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.0] Fruit and vegetables supply fibre, vitamins, minerals and antioxidants (substances that protect cells from damage), which protect against chronic disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.1] Wholegrain starchy foods give more fibre and nutrients than refined white flour products, and promote bowel health because fibre helps prevent constipation and bowel disorders.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.2] Saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.3] Excess salt is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of stroke and heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.4] Sugar adds energy without nutrients, contributes to obesity, and causes tooth decay.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.b] An RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) is a planning value: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of nearly all healthy people in a group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.0] An RDA is set for each nutrient and differs by age, sex and life stage, such as growth, pregnancy, lactation (breastfeeding) or adolescence.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.1] RDAs are used to plan menus and to check that a diet supplies enough of each nutrient over the day, by comparing the day's nutrient total with the RDA. They also guide food-labelling and supplement standards and institutions such as schools and hospitals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.2] An RDA is a guide for a group; it is not a precise target for an individual.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.3] Intake far above the RDA does not give extra benefit and can be harmful for some nutrients, so supplements should be used with care.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.4] Nutrients that are often low in Irish diets, such as iron and calcium, are the ones to check against the RDA when planning a menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.b] A food composition table is a reference book of food values. It lets you turn a menu into numbers, so you can check it against the RDAs and the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.0] A food composition table gives the nutrient and energy content of a food, usually per 100 g of edible food. Irish tables are produced from national food analysis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.1] Tables state whether values are for raw or cooked food, so the matching version must be used because cooking changes the nutrient content.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.2] Method: for each food, work out the grams eaten, divide by 100, then multiply by the value per 100 g.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.3] Example (sample data): 150 g of a food with 3 g fibre per 100 g supplies 150 / 100 x 3 = 4.5 g fibre.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.4] Add the results for all foods in the menu to give the day's total for each nutrient and for energy, then compare the total with the RDA. Where a total is low or high, adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.b] A computer nutrition program does the same job as a food composition table, but automatically. It is quick to use, though it is only as good as the information put in.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.0] Nutrition programs contain a built-in food composition database; the user enters each food and the quantity eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.1] The program totals the energy and nutrients for the day and compares them with the guidelines or RDAs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.2] Check that the portion size entered matches the food as eaten, and that the correct cooking method is selected, because the results depend on correct portions and the right cooking state.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.3] A program shows which nutrients are low or high, which helps the planner adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.4] Advantages are that it is fast and accurate and holds many foods and recipes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.b] The Food Pyramid turns the healthy eating guidelines into the types and relative amounts of food needed each day. Foods with similar nutrients are grouped on shelves, with the base shelf eaten most and the top shelf least.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.0] The current Irish pyramid has six shelves. At the base are vegetables, salad and fruit, with wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice above them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.1] Next come milk, yogurt and cheese, then meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (including lean choices), then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, to be used sparingly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.2] The top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt. These are not needed for health, have no recommended servings, and should be occasional. Water is the main drink.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.3] Servings: five to seven servings of vegetables, salad and fruit a day, three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese (five for ages 9 to 18), and three to five servings of wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.4] The number and size of servings from the other shelves are adjusted for age, sex, health, activity level and life stage, for example children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.5] To plan meals, take most foods from the large lower shelves, some from the middle and very little from the top, and vary the foods within each shelf. Include vegetables or fruit at every meal, and choose wholemeal and lower-fat versions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.6] Spread the choices across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks so the day covers every shelf and matches the person's portions. This supports energy balance and prevents both deficiency and excess.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.7] The pyramid guides what to eat and roughly how much, not an exact diet, and it does not replace advice for medical conditions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.8] Diagram: draw the Food Pyramid, label each shelf with the main foods on it, and mark the base shelf and the top shelf.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.b] A serving (portion) is a standard measure of food used to plan and count intake against the pyramid. It makes diets easy to compare, because everyone counts in the same units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.0] A portion is a standard amount of food used for planning, counting and comparing diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.1] One piece of fruit, such as an apple, or one serving of vegetables counts as one portion of fruit or vegetables.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.2] One glass of milk counts as one portion of milk, yoghurt or cheese in the dairy group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.3] Portion size should be adjusted to the person's age and activity: teenagers and active adults need larger portions from the starchy foods shelf than older, less active adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.4] Serving sizes and numbers rise for growing teenagers and active people and fall for older, less active adults. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra servings of some groups.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.b] Food supplements are concentrates of important nutrients. They can fill a gap in a diet, but they cannot do everything that food does.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.0] Food supplements are concentrates of nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, omega-3 fatty acids (polyunsaturated fatty acids) and amino acids, sold in tablet, capsule or powder form.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.1] They may be used to fill gaps where diet is restricted, where allergies or intolerances exclude foods, or where a person has special needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.2] Folic acid prevents neural tube defects (defects of the baby's brain and spine) in a developing baby when taken before conception and in early pregnancy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.3] Iron supplements can treat anaemia caused by low iron intake, under medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.4] Older people may absorb nutrients less efficiently, so they may need supplements, particularly vitamin D.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.5] Their popularity is growing for reasons such as busy lifestyles, poor diets, media and advertising, a wish to prevent illness, special needs (vegans, restricted diets, illness), sports performance and convenience.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.6] Supplements cannot replace a varied diet, because food supplies fibre and other nutrients that supplements do not contain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.7] High doses of some vitamins and minerals can be harmful, especially fat-soluble vitamins, so supplements should not be taken above the recommended levels. They can also cost a lot, so take medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.b] Healthy eating is only part of staying healthy. The lifestyle guidelines cover activity, mental wellbeing and habits that harm the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.0] Regular physical activity: build activity into daily life, such as brisk walking, cycling, sport or active housework, and include muscle-strengthening and weight-bearing exercise (which loads the bones, such as walking or running).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.1] Physical activity improves cardiovascular fitness, maintains muscle and bone, supports weight control and mental wellbeing, and may raise protective HDL cholesterol (the protective form of cholesterol).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.2] Mental health: get adequate sleep and rest, keep a realistic workload, and use stress management, relaxation and social contact.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.3] Avoid tobacco and second-hand smoke, which damage the lungs and blood vessels and greatly raise the risk of cancer and coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.4] Limit alcohol: it supplies empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrients), harms the liver and judgement, and must never be used to manage stress.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.5] Drink plenty of water and keep a healthy body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.6] Regular health checks and good personal hygiene complete the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.7] Together these habits reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension (high blood pressure) and heart disease.

CANDIDATES hom-meal-planning-s2: 2. Dietary requirements through the life cycle
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.b] No two people need exactly the same diet. Energy and nutrient needs change with the stage of life and with circumstances, so every meal plan starts from the requirements of the person it is for.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.0] A balanced diet contains all the nutrients in the correct proportions for the individual's needs and good health, so what counts as balanced differs from person to person.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.1] Age: needs change at each life stage, with growth in childhood and adolescence, maintenance in adults and falling energy needs in older age.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.2] Sex: men generally need more energy than women because they have greater muscle mass and a larger body, while girls and women need extra iron once menstruation begins.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.3] Health status: illness, allergy, diabetes or a digestive disorder can change the foods a person is allowed to eat or how well their body absorbs nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.4] Pregnancy and breastfeeding increase the need for energy, protein, folate, iron and calcium, because the body is building a baby or making milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.5] Activity: energy needs rise with physical activity, and active people also need more fluid and carbohydrate. A sedentary adult needs less energy.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.6] Energy balance: energy taken in as food should match energy used by the body; any surplus is stored as body fat.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.b] A baby's first food and a young child's meals are planned around fast growth, a small stomach and teeth that are still forming. Pre-school guidelines are examined as short lists of practical rules.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.0] Breast milk supplies all the nutrients a baby needs for about the first six months.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.1] Breastfed babies, and babies taking little formula, are advised to take a vitamin D supplement from birth to 12 months, because breast milk contains little vitamin D.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.2] Weaning (moving from milk to solid food) begins at about six months, when the iron stored at birth starts to run low. Puréed meat and iron-fortified cereals supply iron.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.3] Salt should be kept low and sugar limited in foods for babies and young children, with none added to baby foods. Sugar harms developing teeth.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.4] Pre-school children have small, variable appetites, so regular balanced meals in small, manageable portions with nutritious snacks between suit them better than large meals. Attractive presentation helps.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.5] Young children need protein for growth, calcium and vitamin D for bones and teeth, iron for blood, and fruit and vegetables, following the Food Pyramid.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.6] Fussy eating is common. Offering new flavours one at a time in small portions, without forcing a child to eat, and involving the child in food preparation, helps them accept new foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.7] Sugary foods and drinks should be limited to protect developing teeth.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.b] School-age children need every nutrient in balance, and adolescents need even more during the growth spurt. Teenage eating habits, though, often work against meeting those needs.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.0] Children need every nutrient in balanced proportions, following the Irish Food Pyramid with portions suited to their age and activity. Protein is for growth, carbohydrate and fat for energy, calcium for bones and teeth, iron for blood, plus fibre and water, with nutritious snacks and less sugar and salt.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.1] Ways to include vegetables for children: stir-fries, cut shapes, grated or blended into sauces, smoothies, soups, and vegetables in lunchboxes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.2] Adolescents go through a growth spurt, roughly between the ages of 12 and 19, which increases the need for every nutrient.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.3] Girls need extra iron once they begin menstruating, because menstrual blood loss depletes iron stores. Fruit and vegetables supply fibre and vitamin C, and vitamin C improves the absorption of iron from plant foods, helping avoid anaemia.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.4] Calcium and vitamin D build bone mass during adolescence, so good intake now builds peak bone mass and reduces the risk of osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones) later.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.5] Active teenagers need high-energy starchy foods such as pasta, potatoes, rice and wholegrain cereals. Protein from meat, fish, eggs, dairy and pulses (animal and plant sources) supports growth and repair.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.6] High-salt, high-sugar and high-fat snacks and fizzy drinks add energy but few nutrients, so they should be limited. Irregular meals and skipped breakfasts are common in adolescence and make it harder to meet nutritional needs, so teenagers should eat three varied meals daily and avoid skipping meals, especially breakfast.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.7] Factors making a healthy diet difficult for teenagers: likes and dislikes, early exposure to fatty, salty and sugary foods, convenience and eating on the go, availability, cost, advertising and peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.b] Adult bodies are no longer growing, so the aim shifts to maintaining health and weight. The main guideline is to match what you eat to how active you are.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.0] Adults need to balance energy intake with activity to maintain a healthy weight, and to eat a balanced diet with all the nutrients in proportion.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.1] Daily starchy foods, fruit and vegetables supply the fibre, vitamins and minerals that support bowel health.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.2] Protein maintains muscle and repairs tissue, so a protein food is included at each main meal.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.3] Unsaturated oils should replace saturated fats, and sugar and salt should be kept low.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.4] Women of childbearing age need adequate folate and iron, because menstrual losses continue.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.5] A sedentary worker (one who sits most of the day) needs less energy, so should reduce fat and sugar, eat fibre-rich foods, get enough calcium, iron and vitamins, and limit salt.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.b] Older bodies burn less energy but still need nearly as many nutrients, so food must be nutrient-dense. Practical problems with teeth, appetite and money also shape what older people can eat.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.0] Energy needs fall with age because muscle mass and activity decline, so portions should be smaller but nutrient-dense (packed with nutrients per mouthful). Small, frequent, appetising meals suit reduced appetite, and fortified foods help.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.1] Protein remains important for maintaining muscle and repairing tissue, so older people should include a protein food at main meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.2] Calcium and vitamin D protect the bones, and calcium absorption decreases with age.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.3] Vitamin B12 absorption can fall in later life, so older people may need B12 from fortified foods or supplements.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.4] Fibre and fluid prevent constipation, which is common among older people who are less active. Iron and vitamin C are also needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.5] Salt should be reduced to help control blood pressure, and saturated fat reduced.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.6] Chewing and swallowing difficulties, or dentures, call for soft, moist and easy-to-chew foods. A limited budget and reduced mobility also affect shopping and cooking.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.7] Diet-related conditions common in older people include osteoporosis, diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure and high cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.b] A pregnant woman's diet must feed her own body and build a baby, so the need for particular nutrients rises while food safety matters more. This is the group examined most often, usually as nutrient, reason and food source.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.0] Folic acid (also called folate) taken before conception and in early pregnancy helps prevent neural-tube defects in the developing baby. Sources include green vegetables and fortified cereals, plus a supplement as advised.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.1] Iron supports the extra blood volume of pregnancy and the baby's iron stores; vitamin C eaten at the same meal improves iron absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.2] Calcium and vitamin D are needed for the baby's bones and teeth and to protect the mother's bones.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.3] Protein from lean meat, fish, fully cooked eggs and pasteurised dairy supports the growth of fetal tissue, the placenta and maternal tissues.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.4] Omega-3 fatty acids from cooked oily fish, such as salmon, support the development of the baby's brain and nervous system. Iodine and vitamins A, C, D, K and folate are also needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.5] Water is needed for blood plasma and fetal tissues, to carry nutrients and remove waste, and to prevent dehydration and constipation. Fibre also helps prevent constipation, which is common in pregnancy.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.6] Energy needs rise only modestly, mainly in later pregnancy, so a pregnant woman should not double her food intake. She should balance food with activity, follow the Food Pyramid and choose low-GI starchy foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.7] Breastfeeding women need extra energy, protein, calcium and fluid to produce milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.b] Some foods carry bacteria that can harm an unborn baby, so pregnancy guidelines restrict them. Any menu for a pregnant woman must follow these rules.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.0] Pâté, unpasteurised milk, soft mould-ripened cheese, and raw or undercooked meat, fish and eggs carry a risk of listeria or salmonella (food-poisoning bacteria) and should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.1] Alcohol should be avoided in pregnancy, and caffeine and salt should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.2] Taste changes and morning sickness can be managed with dry toast before rising and small, frequent meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.3] A sample one-day menu uses pasteurised dairy and fully cooked foods. Breakfast: fruit juice, porridge made with pasteurised milk and berries, wholemeal toast.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.4] Lunch: vegetable soup, wholemeal bread, yoghurt and fruit. Dinner: well-cooked lean chicken or fish with vegetables and potatoes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.b] A convalescent is someone recovering from illness. Their appetite is low but their body needs nutrients to repair itself, so food must be light, appealing and nourishing.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.0] Convalescents need small, appetising, easy-to-digest portions, because appetite is often reduced during recovery. Regular balanced meals are important.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.1] Protein from lean meat, fish, eggs and dairy supports tissue repair.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.2] Iron and vitamin C support recovery of blood levels, and vitamin C from fruit and vegetables aids iron absorption. Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and fluids keep the bowel working.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.3] Lightly seasoned meals are better tolerated; spicy, fried, oily and greasy foods should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.b] Active people burn more energy and lose fluid in sweat, so their diet is built around extra fuel and water.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.0] Active people need extra energy, mainly from complex carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, pasta, rice and potatoes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.1] Sweat losses mean active people need extra fluid, drunk before, during and after exercise.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.2] Protein supports muscle repair and growth after exercise, and fruit, yoghurt or wholegrain sandwiches supply energy between meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.b] Shift work and long or varied days disrupt normal mealtimes. Good planning keeps meals balanced even when no one is home at the usual time.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.0] Irregular shifts disrupt mealtimes, so people often skip meals or rely on fast food and vending machines, which should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.1] Planning ahead with a list, and keeping staples and some frozen or prepared ingredients, makes balanced meals easier.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.2] Batch cooking and freezing portions allows balanced meals to be eaten at staggered times without much last-minute preparation.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.3] Soups, casseroles and other reheatable portions suit people eating at different times.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.4] Packed lunches with fruit, wholegrain bread and yoghurt keep nutrition on track during long working days.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.5] Slow cookers and timers allow a meal to cook while the household is out. Portions should stay balanced, following the guidelines.

CANDIDATES hom-meal-planning-s4: 4. Modified diets
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.b] Coronary heart disease (CHD) is the narrowing of the coronary arteries, the vessels feeding the heart muscle, by fatty deposits. The aim of the CHD diet is to lower LDL cholesterol and blood pressure and to reduce the strain on the heart and arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.0] Replace saturated animal fats with mono- and polyunsaturated oils such as olive, rapeseed and sunflower oil, because saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol, the type that clogs arteries. Use low-cholesterol products where possible.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.1] Choose low-fat dairy products and lean meat, and trim visible fat from meat before cooking. Eat more fish and skinless chicken in place of fatty red meat.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.2] Eat oily fish such as salmon, mackerel and sardines, which supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.3] Increase soluble fibre from oats, beans, lentils, fruit and vegetables, which helps lower LDL cholesterol. Eat more fruit and vegetables in general.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.4] Reduce salt, which helps keep blood pressure down, and use herbs, spices and lemon juice for flavour.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.5] Reduce refined carbohydrate and sugar, such as white bread, sugary foods and sugary drinks, and reduce alcohol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.6] Use low-fat cooking methods: grill, bake, steam or boil food rather than frying it, avoid creamy sauces, and skim fat from stock and gravy.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.7] A sample day's menu: porridge with low-fat milk and fruit; a wholegrain sandwich with chicken and salad; grilled oily fish with vegetables and potatoes; low-fat yoghurt or fruit.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.b] Cholesterol is the substance behind the CHD diet. The body needs some of it, but the type and amount in the blood decide whether it helps or harms the arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.0] Cholesterol is a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell, made mainly by the liver and also obtained from animal foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.1] Cholesterol forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed for some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.2] Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is associated with saturated fat and deposits cholesterol in artery walls, hardening them. This is why LDL is called the harmful cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.3] High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is associated with unsaturated fat and carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.4] Lifestyle changes that reduce CHD risk include maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, avoiding excess alcohol, exercising regularly and reducing stress.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.b] Some foods are designed to do more than feed you. These functional foods can be added to a CHD menu to help protect the heart.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.0] A functional food has health benefits beyond its basic nutritive value, so it does more than supply energy and nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.1] Plant sterol and stanol spreads, yoghurts, cheese spreads and drinks reduce cholesterol absorption and so lower LDL cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.2] Omega-3 enriched eggs and milk supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.3] Prebiotic and probiotic milk and yoghurt improve digestion.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.4] Fortified foods have vitamins and minerals added to them, so they supply nutrients the diet may otherwise lack.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.b] A lacto-vegetarian eats no meat, poultry, fish or eggs, but includes milk and dairy products. Without meat, careful choices are needed to get enough protein, iron, calcium and vitamins.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.0] Protein comes from milk, cheese and yoghurt (high biological value protein), and from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products such as tofu and textured vegetable protein (TVP), and mycoprotein. Combining plant proteins across the day gives a good range of amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.1] Calcium comes from milk, yoghurt and cheese, and from green leafy vegetables, sesame seeds and fortified soya products. Vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus help calcium absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.2] Iron comes from green leafy vegetables, pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit and fortified cereals; vitamin C from fruit and vegetables at the same meal improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.3] Vitamin B12 comes from dairy products, vitamin B group from wholegrains, and vitamin D from fortified spreads and cereals and from sunlight. Omega-3 fats come from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.4] Some cheeses are made with animal rennet, and some mycoprotein products contain egg white, so labels must be checked.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.5] Eggs are excluded, so the diet relies on dairy and pulses for protein and on fortified foods for nutrients that eggs would normally supply.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.6] Other types of vegetarian: lacto-ovo includes eggs and dairy, ovo includes eggs, and pesco includes fish. Some sources also allow eggs in a lacto-vegetarian diet, but the strict definition is dairy yes, eggs no.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.7] People become vegetarian for religious, cultural, moral, health, economic or environmental reasons. A sample day: orange juice, porridge with milk and wholemeal toast; vegetable soup, brown bread, yoghurt and fruit; vegetarian lasagne or a lentil dish with green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.b] A vegan diet excludes all animal-derived foods, including meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and honey. Because dairy and eggs are gone too, a vegan must plan protein, calcium, iron and vitamin B12 from plant and fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.0] Protein comes from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products, vegan mycoprotein, seitan and tempeh; eating pulses with grains or nuts over the day provides all the essential amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.1] Vitamin B12 has no reliable plant source, so it must come from fortified foods such as soya milk, cereals and yeast extract, or from a supplement.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.2] Calcium comes from fortified soya milk and flour, calcium-set tofu, sesame seeds in tahini, nuts, vegan cheese, and broccoli, spinach and other dark green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.3] Iron comes from pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit, dark green vegetables and fortified cereals; vitamin C from peppers, tomatoes, berries and citrus improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.4] Vitamin D comes from fortified foods and sunlight, and omega-3 fats from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.5] Hidden animal ingredients can turn up in stock cubes, some spreads, some bread and sweets (gelatine) and in cheese made with rennet, so choose labelled vegan products and check for gelatine, milk powder, egg, rennet or honey.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.6] A vegan menu example uses vegan mycoprotein mince, lentils or beans, vegetables and fruit, which excludes animal products and still supplies protein, iron, calcium and fortified B12.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.b] Coeliac disease is a lifelong intolerance to gluten, the protein found in wheat, barley and rye. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.0] Gluten damages the villi, the finger-like lining of the small intestine, so food is not properly absorbed and deficiencies of iron, calcium and folate can develop.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.1] Avoid bread, cakes, biscuits, pastries, pizzas and pies made with wheat, ordinary pasta and couscous, seitan, and processed foods such as sausages, some soups, sauces and gravies. Wheat, barley, rye and malt are all sources of gluten.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.2] Build meals around naturally gluten-free staples such as rice, corn (maize), potatoes, buckwheat, soya products and lentils, plus meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fruit and vegetables. Gluten-free versions of bread, pasta and flour are also available.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.3] Check ingredient lists for hidden gluten in stock, sauces, malt products and processed meats, and look for the gluten-free symbol or declaration on products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.4] A gluten-free claim means the food as sold contains no more than 20 mg of gluten per kilogram (Regulation (EU) No 828/2014, FSAI guidance). Wheat and oats used must still appear in the ingredients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.5] Avoid cross-contamination in the kitchen, for example by using a separate toaster and utensils, so crumbs of gluten do not reach safe food. Oats are naturally gluten-free but often contaminated, so use only oats labelled gluten-free.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.6] Gluten-free breads and cereals are often low in fibre, so choose brown rice, beans and other high-fibre gluten-free foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.7] Replace the iron, calcium and folate normally supplied by wheat products with other naturally gluten-free or fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.b] The diabetic diet aims to keep blood glucose stable, maintain a healthy weight and reduce the risk of heart disease. It does not mean no carbohydrate; it means carbohydrate that is low-GI, regular and controlled.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.0] Regular meals and snacks at set times prevent large swings in blood glucose; skipping meals is avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.1] Low-glycaemic-index (GI) carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, oats, pasta and pulses release glucose slowly, so blood glucose rises gradually. High-fibre foods and plenty of vegetables also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.2] Refined sugar, sugar-sweetened soft drinks, sweets and sugar-laden processed foods are avoided or limited, and low-calorie sweeteners can replace sugar.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.3] Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and low-sugar fruits are chosen, as high-sugar fruit should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.4] Saturated fat and salt are reduced, and unsaturated oils are used instead, because diabetes raises the risk of heart disease. Keeping a healthy weight and exercising also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.5] Insulin dose is matched to the carbohydrate eaten in people using insulin, so meal timing and portions must be consistent.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.b] People on modified diets need suitable foods that are easy to find and easy to use. Manufacturers and retailers both respond to this.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.0] Manufacturers produce specialised ranges such as gluten-free, diabetic, vegetarian and coronary heart disease products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.1] Manufacturers give detailed labels and packet recipes, and offer smaller portion sizes for people who need them.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.2] Retailers provide specialist shops or sections, designated shelves and wider ranges of special-diet foods, so customers can find suitable products quickly.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.3] Retailers run in-store cookery demonstrations to show customers how to use special-diet products, and provide information and websites.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.b] Labels let people on special diets check quickly whether a food is suitable. What the label shows, and how far it can be trusted, is what exams focus on.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.0] The name of the food and ingredients must be listed in descending order of weight, so the main ingredients are easy to identify.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.1] Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, allergens must be highlighted in the ingredient list.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.2] Nutrition information shows the energy, fat, saturated fat, sugar, salt and fibre content of the food.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.3] Symbols and claims show gluten-free, vegetarian and vegan status and whether a food is fortified, functional, low-fat or reduced-sugar, which helps people with specific needs choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.4] Labels give cooking and storage instructions, the net quantity, the use-by or best-before date, the origin and any GM ingredients, helping people choose foods safely.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.5] Labels are useful but must be read critically: claims can mislead, small print can hide gluten, and 'may contain' warnings need checking.

CANDIDATES hom-meal-planning-s1: 1. Food choices
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.b] Nobody picks food in a vacuum. A food choice results from several pressures acting together, and each one can be named and explained on its own, with an example.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.0] Culture: the shared customs and traditions of a group decide which foods are normal, acceptable or celebratory, and certain foods are linked with a particular country, tradition or festival.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.1] Travel, television and immigration spread foods from one culture to another, so people often eat dishes that did not belong to their own country's tradition.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.2] Religion: some faiths restrict foods, so a person's beliefs can rule certain foods out. Jews and Muslims do not eat pork, and some Hindus follow a vegetarian diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.3] Eating patterns: the number and timing of meals, snacking, eating out and whether the family eats together all shape what is bought and eaten.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.4] Irregular family routines change what is bought and cooked, because people who eat at different times tend to rely on quick foods rather than a shared cooked meal.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.5] Sensory aspects: taste, smell, appearance, texture and temperature decide whether a food is accepted, and unfamiliar flavours are often refused at first.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.6] Likes and dislikes, the way food is presented and early exposure to fatty, salty and sugary food shape preferences, which is why children who grow up on these foods often keep choosing them.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.b] The remaining factors are about knowledge, the body, the shops and the money in a person's pocket. Name each factor, then explain it with an example in a sentence.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.0] Nutritional awareness: knowledge of healthy eating guidelines and of food labels steers people towards lower-fat, lower-sugar and higher-fibre products, while low awareness leads to poorer choices.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.1] Health status: allergies and intolerances, coeliac disease, diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure and weight management limit or direct what a person can safely eat.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.2] Because of these conditions people buy foods such as lactose-free, gluten-free, low-cholesterol and allergy-safe products.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.3] Availability: seasonal produce, the range stocked by local shops, the distance to a supermarket, access to a car and the foods sold in a school canteen or vending machine determine what is on offer.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.4] Finance: income sets the budget. People with more money buy expensive foods more often, eat out and meet foreign foods on holidays.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.5] People on low incomes tend to buy cheaper and more processed foods, own brands, special offers and inexpensive protein such as pulses, eggs and tinned fish.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.6] Marketing and advertising: packaging, brand image, special offers, shelf placement, endorsements by celebrities or influencers and social media encourage purchases, and advertising to children and teenagers is especially influential.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.b] Two further influences are examined even though they are not separate syllabus factors. They fit under eating patterns, availability, nutritional awareness and culture.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.0] Convenience and lifestyle: lack of time or cooking skill increases the use of ready meals and takeaways, so busy households choose food that is quick to prepare.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.1] Eating food away from home covers restaurants, cafés, hotels, takeaways, delicatessens and pub food. More people do it because they have more money and choice, less time and less cooking skill.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.2] Health and wellbeing: people choose foods for health reasons, such as less fat, salt and sugar and more fibre, which is an example of nutritional awareness at work.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.3] Responsible living: people also choose foods for ethical and environmental reasons, such as organic, Fairtrade, local and seasonal foods, less food waste, animal welfare and sustainability. These choices reflect personal values and culture.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.b] Irish eating habits have changed a great deal over the last century. These changes describe the whole population over time, so they are trends such as more convenience food and less home cooking.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.0] Potatoes and bread have been partly replaced by pasta, rice and a wider range of breads, because a wider food range has become available.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.1] Convenience, ready-meal and processed foods have become more common, bringing more salt, sugar, saturated fat and additives and less fibre into the diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.2] Eating out, takeaways and fast food have increased, as has eating on the go. This includes eating food away from home in restaurants, cafés, hotels, takeaways, delicatessens and pubs.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.3] Meals have become more irregular: family members often eat at different times, so the main meal, now in the evening, is less often eaten together.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.4] Foreign holidays, television, the internet, travel, immigration and food bloggers have introduced new cuisines and ingredients to Irish diets, such as ethnic cuisines.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.5] Microwaving, stir-frying and barbecuing are now common cooking methods in Irish homes, and people also have less time for cooking at home.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.6] Greater health and nutritional awareness has encouraged more fruit, vegetables, wholegrain, organic, functional and fortified foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.7] Intermittent fasting and plant-based eating are recent trends, reflecting people's interest in health.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.b] Very young children do not choose for themselves. Parents decide, and they weigh up several things.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.0] Parents make most food choices for pre-school children, weighing nutrition and the food pyramid, cost, allergies and health, and the degree of processing.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.1] They also consider a portion size suited to a small child, ease of handling, packaging and labelling, and family habits.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.2] Young children often refuse new foods at first and may need to be offered a food many times before accepting it. This reluctance is called food neophobia.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.3] Involving children in preparation and presenting food attractively encourages them to try new foods, because the food becomes familiar and appealing.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.b] Teenagers start to make their own food decisions, and the influences on them are different from those on young children.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.0] Teenagers' choices are influenced by peer groups, advertising and social media, independence and a wish to eat out.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.1] They have money of their own from pocket money or part-time work, which gives them the buying power to choose for themselves.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.2] Body image, convenience and busy lives also play a part in what teenagers choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.3] Many teenagers prefer fatty, salty and sugary foods, which they have often been exposed to since early childhood.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.4] A teenager may choose a vegetarian diet for cultural, religious, moral or ethical reasons such as animal welfare, or for health, environmental, economic or family reasons, or because of peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.5] Culture and religion affect young people through foods linked with cultures, religious restrictions, travel, television, immigration and multicultural influences.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.b] Students in school or living away from home share many teenage influences, but time, money and facilities limit what they can choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.0] Students in post-primary school choose according to personal preference, cost, nutritional awareness, advertising and peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.1] Health status and allergies, and activity level, also affect their choices.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.2] The range sold in the canteen or vending machines, and whether there are facilities to reheat food, decide what is practical.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.3] Students living away or on a budget face the same factors, with limited time, money and cooking skill, so they tend to choose quick, cheap foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.b] Higher Level exams give a table or chart and ask you to comment on it. A clear routine earns the marks.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.0] Describe the overall trend first, then support it with specific figures read from the data.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.1] Compare two groups, such as parents and children or boys and girls, by stating which group is higher and by how much.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.2] Suggest reasons for differences by drawing on the factors that shape food choices, because the data only shows what people choose, not why.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.3] Relate the results to healthy eating guidelines, saying whether consumption meets them or falls short, for example whether five or more portions of fruit and vegetables a day (Department of Health Food Pyramid) are being reached.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.4] Comment on what the results mean for planning meals or for health and health education.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.8.b] These are practical contexts that help you practise applying the factors.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.8.0] Comparing meal patterns between countries, such as when the main meal is eaten and how many meals are normal.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.8.1] A theme day or week on the foods of a culture or period, such as a traditional Irish breakfast.

CANDIDATES hom-personal-finance-s2: 2. Household expenditure and budgeting
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.b] Expenditure is the money a household spends, and income sets the limit on it. Spending that goes beyond income leads to debt, so spending must be matched to income, and many factors shape how a household divides its money.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.0] Low-income households spend most of their income on essentials such as housing, food and heat, and a larger proportion on food than richer households do, so they have little or no discretionary spending (spending on non-essentials) or saving.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.1] Higher-income households spend more in total and a bigger share on discretionary items such as leisure, holidays and eating out, so they have more discretionary income (money left after essentials) and can also save and pay for credit.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.2] Family size and composition matter, for example the number of children and whether it is a one-parent or two-parent household, because each extra person adds to food, clothing and housing needs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.3] The stage of the family life cycle changes priorities: young children add costs for food, clothing, childcare and schooling, while teenagers cost more for food, clothing and education.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.4] Once children are independent and at retirement, spending falls and shifts towards health, leisure and heating; older children can also help decide and manage spending.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.5] Employment affects money, travel time and cost, and meal and sleep patterns. Working outside the home adds travel, convenience food and childcare costs, and shift work changes meal and sleep patterns and transport costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.6] Urban and rural location change patterns, for example through transport costs and housing costs: rural households depend more on cars, while urban rents and mortgages are higher. Heating costs also differ.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.7] Other influences include socio-economic class and the gender of the earner, personal values and priorities, health or special needs, advertising and peer pressure (the influence of friends and others the same age).
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.b] Not all spending is equally urgent. Households divide it into what they must pay to manage family life and what they can choose to spend once the necessities are covered.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.0] Essential expenditure is spending on necessities needed to manage family life, such as housing (rent or mortgage), utilities (electricity, heating), food, clothing, transport, insurance, medical bills, loan repayments and household bills.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.1] Discretionary expenditure is spending on non-essentials, made only after essential expenditure and savings have been provided for.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.2] Examples of discretionary expenditure: holidays, entertainment, alcohol, cosmetics, eating out, leisure and gym membership, and streaming services.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.3] Fixed essential costs stay the same amount at regular times each week or month, such as rent or a mortgage repayment, insurance and telephone bills, so they are easy to plan for.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.4] Irregular essential costs vary in amount or timing, such as clothing, doctor's bills, school expenses and car repairs, so a budget must set money aside for them. Some are also seasonal, such as heating, Christmas and back to school.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.5] Whether an item is essential depends on the household. The same item can be essential for one household and discretionary for another, for example a car for a rural family compared with a city family.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.6] Credit repayments are fixed commitments in the budget, and credit used for essentials adds to future spending because it must be paid back.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.b] A budget is a plan that sets expected net income against planned spending for a period such as a week, month or year. Following a series of steps means essentials are paid first and money is left for savings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.0] 1. List all income, using net (take-home) income, never gross, because net income is the money actually available to spend. Include wages, welfare, maintenance and any other income.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.1] 2. List planned fixed, irregular and discretionary expenditure for each week or month, including credit repayments, and choose the time period the budget will cover.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.2] 3. Plan essential needs first, such as rent or mortgage and household bills, before any discretionary spending, so the necessities are always covered.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.3] 4. Allocate money for short-term and long-term savings, and for emergencies, so unexpected costs do not have to be borrowed.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.4] 5. Allow for personal spending and special occasions such as Christmas and holidays, and for changes in circumstances, so these annual costs do not cause a shortfall.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.5] 6. Keep bills and receipts, and record one month of actual spending to find where overspending occurs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.6] 7. Compare income with expenditure and adjust. A surplus (income greater than spending) can be saved; a deficit (spending greater than income) means cutting discretionary spending, raising income or seeking advice, rather than borrowing.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.7] 8. Review the budget regularly, and change it when family circumstances or income change, so it stays realistic.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.b] When a budget shows overspending or a deficit, a household can cut costs without going without essentials. The savings come from shopping smarter and using less.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.0] Use a shopping list and stick to it, because this avoids impulse buying of items you did not plan to purchase.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.1] Choose own-brand goods and compare prices between shops, since similar goods can cost much less.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.2] Buy second-hand or in charity shops, use sales, and buy food in season, when it is cheaper and plentiful.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.3] Cook from scratch and choose cheaper cuts of meat, because convenience foods and prime cuts cost more.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.4] Cut energy use and switch supplier to reduce utility bills.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.5] Cancel unused subscriptions, such as gym membership or streaming services, so you are not paying for things you do not use.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.b] A budget does more than record numbers. It shows a household where its money goes and helps it avoid problems before they happen.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.0] Budgeting shows where money goes, and identifies overspending and impulse buying before they lead to unplanned debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.1] Budgeting reduces reliance on credit and supports a good credit record, because bills and repayments are planned for and paid on time.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.2] A budget gives financial security and reduces the worry and stress caused by unpaid bills, because it ensures bills are paid.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.3] A budget that allows some saving can provide for emergencies and cover unexpected events, such as job loss or an urgent repair.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.4] Budgeting helps a household reach its goals, because money is set aside for them.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.5] Budgeting builds money-management skills that transfer to independent living.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.b] A sample budget shows how a net income can be divided between headings so that every euro is planned. The first sample is a family of two adults and two children with a combined net income of 900 euro a week.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.0] Housing, the largest fixed essential cost, is allocated about a quarter of income (25 per cent): 225 euro.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.1] Food is allocated about a quarter of income (25 per cent): 225 euro, because food needs rise with growing children.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.2] Household bills are allocated about 15 per cent of income: 135 euro. Education is allocated about 10 per cent: 90 euro, to cover school costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.3] Clothing, leisure, savings, health and discretionary spending are each allocated about 5 per cent: 45 euro each, with savings kept for emergencies.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.4] A second sample, 800 euro a week for the same type of family, gives 200 euro for housing, 200 for food, 120 for household bills, 80 for education and 40 for each of the five 5 per cent headings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.5] The totals must add up to the net income, so the plan allocates the full net income, leaves no money unplanned, and is reviewed each month.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.6] The percentages are a planning guide for this sample, not a rule, and should be adjusted to the family's actual fixed costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.7] A lower income cannot cover the same percentages, so essentials come first while discretionary spending and savings shrink; a higher income allows more savings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.b] Households that are in debt, or at risk of it, can get free help from MABS. It advises and negotiates for them but does not lend money or pay their debts.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.0] MABS is a free, confidential and independent service for people in debt or at risk of debt, funded by the State through the Citizens Information Board.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.1] MABS helps a household work out its income and spending, and draws up a realistic budget and repayment plan (schedule) with the family.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.2] MABS negotiates with lenders, including moneylenders and mortgage lenders, on behalf of households in debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.3] MABS offers a mortgage arrears service, and advises on personal insolvency (when a person cannot pay their debts) and income maximisation, which means checking and claiming entitlements to State supports.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.4] MABS advises on financial products and interest rates, and points to cheaper sources of credit such as credit unions.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.5] MABS also teaches money-management skills, so households can manage their budgets themselves in future.

CANDIDATES hom-resource-management-s1: 1. Family resource management
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.b] Every family has more things it wants to do than it has time, money or energy for. Resource management is how a family decides what to do with what it has.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.0] Family resource management is using the resources a family has wisely and efficiently to achieve its goals. Resource management is the planned use of the resources available.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.1] A resource is anything a family can draw on to meet its needs and wants, such as a parent's time, a cooker or a weekly wage.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.2] Resources are limited, so choices have to be made about how they are used. This scarcity is why management is needed.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.3] Goals are what the family is trying to achieve, and they give management its direction. Short-term goals include this week's meals or a family outing; long-term goals include buying a home, education or retirement.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.b] Managing resources is not only about saving money. The purpose has three parts, and a full answer names all three.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.0] The first purpose is to use available resources efficiently in order to achieve goals, so that the family gets the most from what it has.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.1] The second purpose is to improve the quality of family life, so that home is more comfortable and less stressful for everyone.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.2] The third purpose is to enable each family member to reach their full potential, because well-managed time, money and support leave room for learning, work and interests.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.3] Good management reduces waste of time, money, food and energy, and gets the best value from limited resources.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.4] It also avoids conflict and stress by agreeing who does what, so members know what is expected of them.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.5] Example of efficient management: a weekly meal plan with a shopping list saves money and time and reduces food waste, because the family buys only what it will use. Other examples are budgeting, planned childcare, sharing household tasks, or resolving a conflict by agreeing a timetable.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.b] Families draw on four kinds of resource, and only one of them is money. Naming each type with an example shows you understand what the family has to work with.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.0] Human resources: the time, energy, skills and knowledge of family members, such as one parent's cooking skills or a teenager's free afternoon.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.1] Material resources: equipment, food, ingredients, clothing, furniture and other household goods, such as a washing machine or a freezer.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.2] Economic resources: income, savings and credit available to the family.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.3] Environmental resources: the physical surroundings and community facilities that support family life, such as space, daylight and local services like schools, transport and health care.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.4] Resources other than money matter as much as money. A family with little money can still manage well by using time, skills, knowledge and good planning.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.b] A management system is a planned, step-by-step way of turning a need or goal into a result using the resources available. In other words, it turns what you have into what you wanted.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.0] A management system lets a family decide what needs doing, who does it, when it is done and which resources it needs.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.1] Everyday tasks that use a management system include meal planning, budgeting, housework, childcare and shopping. Money and home management, problem solving and decision-making are also uses of the management process.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.2] One-off events such as a birthday party, a holiday or a house move also use a management system, as does a problem such as choosing an appliance.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.3] Systems work best when the goals, standards and responsibilities are agreed in advance and tasks are shared, so that members know what is expected of them.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.4] Systems are not fixed: they are adjusted when circumstances change, such as a new job, a job loss, a move or the arrival of a baby.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.5] A system is a cycle, not a one-off. The result is checked against the goal, and what was learned improves the next attempt. This checking is called feedback.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.b] When a family uses a management system well, the benefits show up in everyday life: in time, money, workload, relationships and the ability to cope with change.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.0] Time is used well. Planned routines make sure meals, laundry and school preparation are done on time, with less last-minute stress.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.1] Money is used well. Meal plans and shopping lists reduce impulse buying and the amount of food that is wasted, and budgeting helps the family avoid debt.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.2] Work is shared fairly. Shared tasks spread the workload so no single member carries the whole household, and each member learns skills.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.3] Goals and standards agreed by all members give the family a shared direction, support communication and reduce conflict.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.4] Energy, water and food are used carefully, so the cost of running the home is lower and there is less waste.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.5] Efficient use of time and equipment frees time for leisure, rest and relationships, so quality of life improves.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.6] The family copes better with change and emergencies because it has a plan and some resources in reserve.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.b] The family is a small group that pools, allocates and controls its own resources. Because it sets goals and takes responsibility for results, it acts as a managerial unit.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.0] Each member contributes resources such as earnings, time or skills, and each member benefits from the shared pool of the household.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.1] The family sets goals, makes decisions, allocates resources and takes responsibility for the results.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.2] Adults usually plan, earn and coordinate; children and teenagers take on tasks and share in decisions as they grow older. This means management duties are shared and change over time.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.3] Family goals may be short term, such as a weekly shop or a family outing, or long term, such as buying a home or saving for education.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.4] Management requires balancing individual wants against the needs and goals of the household as a whole, so the unit works best when decisions take everyone's views into account.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.5] Article 41 of the Irish Constitution recognises the family as the natural, primary and fundamental unit group of society.
