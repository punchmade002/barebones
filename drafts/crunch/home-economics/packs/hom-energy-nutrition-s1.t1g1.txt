TARGET hom-energy-nutrition-s1: 1. Energy requirements and the role of energy
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.b] Every activity the body carries out, from breathing to running, needs energy, and that energy comes from the food and drink we take in. Knowing what energy is for, and how it is measured, is the starting point for working out how much a person needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.0] Energy is what the body needs to carry out every function: breathing, circulating blood, nerve and brain activity, growth and repair, movement and keeping warm.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.1] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal, often called "calories"). 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, and food labels give both units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.2] The body releases energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein, and from alcohol, through chemical reactions, and uses it for every body function.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.3] Energy not needed straight away is stored, because the body cannot waste it: a small store as glycogen (a stored form of carbohydrate) in the liver and muscles, and the large store as fat in adipose tissue (fatty tissue under the skin and around organs).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.4] Three main functions of energy are basal metabolism (keeping the body working at rest), growth and physical activity. Marking schemes also credit the working of the internal organs and the production of heat to maintain body temperature.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.b] Even when you are lying still, your body is working hard to keep you alive. The energy for this hidden work is called the basal metabolic rate, and it is the biggest part of what most people use each day.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.0] BMR is the minimum amount of energy the body needs to stay alive when completely at rest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.1] It pays for the functions that continue at rest, such as breathing, the heartbeat and circulation of blood, and the work of the brain, liver, kidneys and other internal organs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.2] It also pays for maintaining body temperature, and for the continuing renewal of cells.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.3] BMR is measured with the person lying at complete rest and awake, in a warm room, about 12 hours after eating (fasting). These conditions mean no movement, cold or digestion adds extra energy use to the result.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.4] For most people BMR is the largest single part of the energy used in a day, more than the energy used for activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.b] Two people lying side by side will not burn exactly the same amount of energy. BMR varies with the amount and type of body tissue a person has and with the hormones controlling the speed of their metabolism.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.0] Age: BMR per kilogram is highest in infancy and childhood because of rapid growth, and it falls with age as lean muscle tissue is lost.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.1] Gender: men generally have a higher BMR than women of the same weight, because men have a higher proportion of lean muscle tissue to fat. Do not say it is simply because they are men.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.2] Body size and weight: the larger the body, the more tissue there is to maintain, so the higher the BMR.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.3] Body composition: lean muscle tissue is more active than fat, so it uses more energy at rest. A muscular person has a higher BMR than a person of the same weight with more fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.4] Hormones: the thyroid gland controls the speed of metabolism (the chemical reactions in the body). An overactive thyroid, producing too much thyroid hormone, raises BMR, and an underactive thyroid lowers it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.5] Health: fever, infection and recovery from injury raise BMR, because the body is working harder to fight illness or repair itself.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.6] Pregnancy and lactation (breastfeeding) raise the requirement, and in a cold climate the body works to stay warm, which raises it too; a hot climate has a smaller effect.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.7] Activity does not change BMR itself. It is a separate part of the total energy the body uses in a day.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.b] Building a new body takes energy as well as nutrients. This is why people who are growing quickly need a lot of energy for their size.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.0] Energy is needed to build new body tissue, especially muscle, bone, blood and organs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.1] Infants and young children need a high amount of energy for their size because they are growing quickly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.2] Teenagers have adolescent growth spurts (short periods of very rapid growth) which raise the requirement sharply.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.3] Teenage boys generally need more energy than girls at this stage because of their greater body size and muscle.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.4] After growth stops, adult energy is needed for the maintenance and repair of tissue, not for new growth.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.b] Physical activity is the most variable part of daily energy use, so it is the factor an individual can change most easily.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.0] The energy used in activity depends on the type, intensity, duration and frequency of the activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.1] Occupational activity: a manual worker, such as a builder or farm worker, or an athlete uses far more energy than someone in a desk job such as an office worker.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.2] Leisure activity: walking, cycling, gardening and playing sport add to daily energy use.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.3] Sedentary lifestyle (little activity and much sitting) means low energy output, so less food energy is needed to meet the body's needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.b] A person's daily energy requirement is more than their BMR. It adds the energy for activity and for dealing with food, and then rises or falls with circumstances such as climate, pregnancy and health.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.0] Total daily energy use is made up of BMR, plus the energy for physical activity, plus the small amount used to digest, absorb and process food (the thermic effect of food). BMR on its own is not the total requirement.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.1] Occupation: manual and athletic work increases the requirement, while sedentary work lowers it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.2] Climate: in cold conditions the body burns more energy to keep its temperature stable, partly through the muscular work of shivering, so energy needs are higher than in a warm climate.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.3] Pregnancy: extra energy is needed for the growth of the baby and the placenta and for the mother's added tissue, especially in the later months.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.4] Lactation: extra energy is needed to make breast milk.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.5] Health: fever, illness, burns and recovery from injury or surgery raise energy needs. Prolonged bed rest lowers the activity part of the requirement.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.6] The factors examiners list are age, gender, body size or weight, occupation, level of physical activity, climate, pregnancy, lactation, health or illness, growth spurts, sedentary lifestyle and BMR.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.7] When a person is described, such as a teenager who plays sport, a builder, an office worker, a pregnant woman or an older retired person, name the factor, say whether it raises or lowers the requirement, and give the reason.
