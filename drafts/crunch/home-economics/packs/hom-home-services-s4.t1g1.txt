TARGET hom-home-services-s4: 4. Water
[hom-home-services-s4.0.b] Water for the public mains is taken from rivers, lakes or underground sources, and it is not safe to drink as it comes. It is treated at a treatment plant in stages, each removing a different problem. Today the public water service is run by Uisce Éireann (formerly Irish Water, renamed from 2023).
[hom-home-services-s4.0.0] Stage 1, screening: water passes through coarse and fine screens that remove floating debris such as leaves and twigs, and large solids, so they do not block or damage the later stages.
[hom-home-services-s4.0.1] Stage 2, coagulation and sedimentation: chemicals are added to bind fine dirt into larger clumps, which sink to the base of the tank and are removed, because very fine particles would otherwise stay suspended.
[hom-home-services-s4.0.2] Stage 3, filtration: water passes through filter beds of sand, gravel and stones to remove the remaining small particles.
[hom-home-services-s4.0.3] Stage 4, chlorination (disinfection): chlorine is added to kill bacteria and other micro-organisms. A small amount stays in the water to keep it safe as it travels through the pipes.
[hom-home-services-s4.0.4] Stage 5, fluoridation: fluoride is added to many public supplies to help protect teeth from decay. It is carried out under the Health (Fluoridation of Water Supplies) Act 1960.
[hom-home-services-s4.1.b] Once treated, water must be checked and held safely until it is needed. It then reaches houses through mains pipes.
[hom-home-services-s4.1.0] Stage 6, testing: samples are tested regularly for quality and purity before the water is released, so any fault is caught before it reaches homes.
[hom-home-services-s4.1.1] Stage 7, storage: treated water is held in a covered, high-level reservoir so it cannot become contaminated and so that pressure is available for distribution, because water from high ground flows down with force.
[hom-home-services-s4.1.2] Distribution: clean water flows from the reservoir through a mains pipe to houses, with manholes or valves at regular intervals for inspection and repair.
[hom-home-services-s4.2.b] Not every home is on a public main. Rural homes can get their water in three ways, and the choice affects how safe the water is.
[hom-home-services-s4.2.0] Rural homes may use the public mains, a group water scheme, or a private well or spring.
[hom-home-services-s4.2.1] Private supplies need to be protected from contamination, filtered and treated, and tested, because nobody else is treating or checking the water before it reaches the tap.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.b] Water travels from the public main to the attic through a chain of pipes with a valve to control it. Each part has its own name and job.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.0] Mains pipe: the public pipe under the road, which carries clean water from the public main to the area.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.1] Service pipe: connects the mains pipe to the house, so it brings water into the house.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.2] Stop cock (stop valve): a valve on the service pipe, outside the house near the boundary or under the kitchen sink, that turns the water off at the main. A second stop cock may be found inside the house.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.3] The stop cock is closed to isolate the supply, for example in a burst pipe, during a repair or when the house is left empty.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.4] Rising main: a pipe that carries water from the stop cock up to the cold water storage tank in the attic.
[hom-home-services-s4.3.5] Drinking water tap: the kitchen cold tap is usually fed directly from the rising main, so drinking water is fresh from the main and does not pass through the storage tank.
[hom-home-services-s4.4.b] The attic tank stores water and keeps it under control, so the house has a supply even when the mains is busy. Two safety parts stop it from over-filling.
[hom-home-services-s4.4.0] Cold water storage tank (cistern): a tank in the attic that stores water for the bathroom taps, the WC cisterns and the hot water cylinder. It is placed high so that gravity gives pressure, and it is covered and insulated. Its water is not for drinking.
[hom-home-services-s4.4.1] Ball valve (ball cock, float valve): a float on an arm closes the inlet when the water reaches the set level, so the tank is not over-filled. The same valve is in the WC cistern.
[hom-home-services-s4.4.2] Overflow pipe (warning pipe): leaves the tank and goes through the wall to outside, carrying water away if the ball valve fails. It also warns that something is wrong and stops flooding of the attic and ceilings.
[hom-home-services-s4.4.3] Distribution pipes: carry cold water from the tank to the bathroom taps, the WC cistern and the feed to the hot water cylinder. The cylinder is fed from the tank and heated by the boiler or immersion.
[hom-home-services-s4.4.4] Diagram: a labelled cold water system showing the mains pipe, service pipe, stop cock, rising main, attic tank with ball valve and overflow, distribution pipes, taps, WC, hot water cylinder and kitchen tap.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.b] Putting the parts together, the system fills and delivers water automatically with no pump, using height and a float valve.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.0] 1. Water enters through the service pipe, passes the stop cock and rises through the rising main into the attic tank.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.1] 2. The ball valve closes when the tank reaches its set level, so the tank is never over-filled.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.2] 3. Water is stored in the tank and flows out by gravity to the taps, cistern and cylinder when a tap is opened or a cistern refills.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.3] 4. The ball valve then opens as the level drops and refills the tank.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.4] Water pressure depends on the height of the tank above the outlet, so a tank in the attic gives enough pressure at lower-level taps without a pump.
[hom-home-services-s4.5.5] If the ball valve fails, water leaves through the overflow pipe, which carries it outside so that it does not damage or leak into the ceilings below.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.b] Water expands when it freezes, so cold weather can split pipes and tanks. The damage often shows only when the thaw comes and the water starts to leak.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.0] Water freezes at 0 °C and expands as it turns to ice, so pipes and tanks in cold spaces such as the attic and external walls can split.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.1] Lag the cold water storage tank with an insulated lid or jacket, and lag the pipes in the attic and in external walls with pipe insulation, because these are the coldest places.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.2] Keep the heating on at a low setting in very cold weather or when away, rather than switching it off, so the building remains warm.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.3] Keep the attic hatch closed and repair draughts near pipes, so that cold air does not reach them.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.4] Open the doors of cupboards containing pipes on external walls so that warm air reaches them.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.5] In severe frost, allow a tap to drip or run slowly, because moving water is less likely to freeze.
[hom-home-services-s4.6.6] Know where the stop cock is, so that you can turn the water off quickly if a pipe bursts.
[hom-home-services-s4.7.b] A frozen pipe is a warning sign. The aim is to thaw it safely before it splits, and to avoid causing a fire or a burst.
[hom-home-services-s4.7.0] Turn off the water at the stop cock, so that nothing floods if the pipe has already split.
[hom-home-services-s4.7.1] Open the nearest tap, so that melting water and pressure have somewhere to go.
[hom-home-services-s4.7.2] Thaw the pipe gently from the tap end with a warm cloth or a hot water bottle; never use a naked flame or a blowtorch, because they can damage the pipe and start a fire.
[hom-home-services-s4.7.3] After thawing, check the pipes and joints for leaks before turning the water back on.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.b] Water is a finite resource, and treating and pumping it uses energy, so saving it cuts costs and emissions. Each saving method works because it stops water being used or wasted.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.0] Bathroom: take a short shower instead of a bath, because a shower uses far less water than filling a bath. Water-saving or aerated showerheads cut the flow further.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.1] Turn off the tap while brushing teeth and shaving, because running water is wasted when nothing is being washed.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.2] Fit a dual-flush WC cistern so that the half-flush can be used for liquids, and fit cisterns with reduced-volume flushes or a cistern displacement device where possible.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.3] Kitchen: wash vegetables and small amounts of dishes in a basin or a plugged sink rather than under a running tap.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.4] Laundry: use washing machines and dishwashers only on full loads, or on economy programmes for lightly soiled items, so that fewer washes are needed.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.5] Do not overfill the kettle, since only the water needed should be heated, which also saves energy.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.6] Repair leaks and dripping taps promptly, because a constant drip wastes a large volume over a year. Check the overflow pipe for dripping.
[hom-home-services-s4.8.7] Garden and reuse: collect rainwater from the roof in a butt for watering plants and washing outdoor surfaces. Rainwater harvesting for flushing toilets, grey-water reuse, and sensor or aerator taps also save water.
[hom-home-services-s4.9.b] Hot water costs both water and the energy to heat it, so cutting hot water use saves twice.
[hom-home-services-s4.9.0] Heating less hot water saves both water and energy, for example by timing the immersion, using solar heating and insulating the hot water cylinder.
[hom-home-services-s4.9.1] Shorter showers and full washing loads also use less heated water, so they reduce fuel costs as well as water use.

CANDIDATES hom-environmental-issues-s4: 4. Environmentally responsible practices in the home
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.b] Everyday household activities use energy, water and materials, and they produce emissions and waste. How big the impact is depends on how much energy an activity uses and which fuel supplies that energy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.0] Space heating and hot water account for the largest share of energy use in most homes, so savings on heating usually matter more than savings elsewhere.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.1] Electricity used in the home is generated partly by burning fossil fuels, which releases carbon dioxide at the power station, so using less electricity cuts emissions.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.2] Water used in the home must be pumped and treated, and hot water also needs energy to heat it, so saving water saves energy as well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.0.3] Packaging, food waste and discarded appliances all add to the waste that is landfilled (buried in a landfill site) or recovered (reused or recycled).
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.b] Heating is the biggest energy use in most homes, so the best savings come from stopping heat escaping and from heating only when and where it is needed. Heat leaves through the roof, walls, floor, windows and gaps.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.0] Insulation laid on the floor of the roof space (attic) is one of the most effective measures for an existing house, because warm air rises and escapes through the roof.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.1] Cavity wall insulation fills the gap between the two leaves of a masonry wall, reducing heat loss through the walls.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.2] Draught-proofing seals gaps around doors, windows, letterboxes and floorboards, which stops warm air escaping and cold air entering. Keep controlled ventilation where fuel is burned.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.3] Double or triple glazing reduces heat loss through windows, and closing thick curtains at night adds a further layer of insulation.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.4] A lagging jacket on the hot water cylinder and insulated (lagged) pipes reduce heat lost while water is stored and moved.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.5] Reflective panels or foil behind radiators on external walls send heat back into the room rather than into the wall.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.1.6] An annual boiler service keeps combustion efficient and safe, so less fuel is burned for the same heat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.b] Heating only when and where it is needed saves fuel, and renewable sources reduce reliance on fossil fuels. Controls and cleaner fuels also cut the emissions released when fuel is burned at home.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.0] A room thermostat and a programmer ensure the heating runs only when it is needed, and timers on the immersion heater do the same for hot water. Lowering the set temperature, even slightly, reduces fuel use.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.1] Thermostatic radiator valves control the temperature of individual radiators, so unused rooms can be kept cooler.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.2] Renewable heat sources, such as heat pumps, solar thermal panels and stoves burning wood from sustainably managed forests, reduce reliance on fossil fuels.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.3] Burning fuels at home releases carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and smoke, which harm the environment.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.2.4] Emissions can be reduced by using smokeless fuel, an efficient boiler, heating controls and renewable heat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.b] A washing machine's energy use is mostly about heat, so the choices you make about temperature, load size and drying matter far more than the machine itself.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.0] Heating the water accounts for most of the energy used on a hot wash, so lower temperatures save energy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.1] A cold or 30 °C wash uses far less energy than a 60 °C or 90 °C wash, and modern detergents clean well at lower temperatures, so a hotter wash is not usually needed.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.2] A full load uses almost the same energy and water as a part load, so run the machine only when it is full.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.3] An eco programme runs at lower temperatures or with less water, using less energy per load.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.4] A higher spin speed removes more water before drying, which reduces the time a dryer must run.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.5] A tumble dryer is one of the most energy-intensive appliances in the home, so line drying saves significant energy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.6] Using the correct dose of detergent avoids residue and reduces the amount of product that enters drains.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.3.7] Wash clothes only when needed, and repair and reuse them rather than replacing them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.b] Food has an environmental cost before it reaches the plate and after it is thrown away. Wasting food wastes everything used to produce it and creates a greenhouse gas in landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.0] Food waste sent to landfill decomposes without oxygen (anaerobically) and releases methane, a greenhouse gas.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.1] Food production uses land, water, fertiliser and energy, so wasted food wastes all the resources used to grow, transport and package it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.2] Plan meals, shop with a list, check dates and stock, and buy only what will be eaten. Use leftovers and freeze surplus food promptly.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.3] Store food correctly: keep the fridge at or below 5 °C and freeze food at -18 °C or below, which slows bacterial growth and spoilage and so cuts waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.4.4] Compost food and garden waste or use the brown bin, so it is not sent to landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.b] What you choose to buy and eat, and how it is packaged and transported, also affects the environment. Small changes in the weekly shop add up.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.0] Vegetables, pulses and cereals generally need less land, water and energy to produce than meat and dairy, so eating more plant-based foods lowers the environmental impact of the diet.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.1] Seasonal and local food avoids the energy used for long-distance transport and for heated or refrigerated growing and storage.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.2] Buy loose fruit and vegetables and products with minimal packaging, and buy in bulk to reduce packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.3] Use reusable bags and containers for bulk and counter purchases, so fewer single-use bags are thrown away.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.4] Buy refills and products in recycled or reusable packaging, and choose products with the Ecolabel.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.5.5] Cutting the number of shopping trips reduces the fuel used for travel.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.b] The energy label lets you compare appliances before you buy, so you can choose the one that will use the least energy over its life.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.0] The EU energy label rates an appliance from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient) for energy efficiency, and this rescaled scale replaced the older A+++ to D scale in 2021, so do not compare old and new ratings.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.1] The label also shows the appliance's annual or per-cycle energy consumption and its noise level.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.2] For washing machines and dishwashers, the label shows water use as well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.3] Choose a high rating and the right capacity for the household, because an oversized appliance uses more energy than its load needs.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.6.4] Other criteria to look for are appliances that are repairable and durable, have little packaging, and offer eco and low-temperature programmes.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.b] Even an efficient appliance wastes energy if it is used badly. Most savings come from small habits, which are easy to name in an exam answer.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.0] Standby is not off: the appliance still draws electricity continuously, so switch appliances off at the socket, or use a switched power strip.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.1] LED bulbs use much less power than incandescent bulbs and last much longer. Older schemes say CFL for the energy-saving bulb.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.2] Use the oven only when needed, avoid preheating for longer than necessary, and use a microwave, slow cooker or air fryer for small quantities. Use lids on pans.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.3] Boil only the water needed in a kettle, so no energy is spent heating water you do not use.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.4] Clogged filters and dirty condenser coils make fridges and dryers use more energy, so keep them clean and keep door seals sound.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.5] A fridge set colder than necessary wastes energy. Keep the door closed and do not overpack it, and a freezer that is not kept full uses more energy to stay cold.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.7.6] Use full loads and eco or cold cycles in washing machines and dishwashers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.b] Making an appliance take longer to wear out avoids the energy and materials needed to manufacture a new one. When it is finally finished with, how you get rid of it matters too.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.0] Keeping a working appliance in use through servicing, repair or second-hand sale avoids the energy and materials needed to manufacture a replacement.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.1] Sell or donate a working appliance rather than replacing and discarding it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.8.2] Dispose of old appliances through the retailer take-back or a recycling centre, rather than putting them in the bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.b] Saving water saves the energy used to pump, treat and heat it. Most savings come from fixing leaks and changing habits in the bathroom and garden.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.0] Showers use less hot water than baths, so short showers save both water and the energy needed for heating.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.1] Turning off the tap while brushing teeth and fixing dripping taps and leaks save water that would otherwise be wasted. Aerator or sensor taps and efficient showerheads reduce flow further.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.2] A dual-flush toilet allows the smaller flush to be used for liquid waste, saving water with each flush.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.3] Collecting rainwater in a butt provides free water for the garden, and grey water (used water from baths, sinks and washing machines) can be reused.
[hom-environmental-issues-s4.9.4] Insulating the cylinder and pipes and timing the immersion save water-heating energy, and solar thermal heating can supply hot water.
