TARGET hom-consumer-rights-s4: 4. Consumer protection legislation
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.b] The Consumer Rights Act 2022 gives consumers statutory rights (rights set by law) and remedies when they buy goods, services or digital content from traders. It applies to contracts made on or after 29 November 2022 and replaced the earlier law on sales and services for consumer contracts.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.0] A trader is a person who supplies goods, services or digital content in the course of business, so the Act applies to shops, online sellers and service providers alike.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.1] A consumer is an individual acting for purposes that are not part of their business, so someone buying a washing machine for their home is a consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.2] Statutory rights apply automatically to every contract between a trader and a consumer, whether or not the trader mentions them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.3] A trader cannot remove statutory rights through terms and conditions, small print or notices; a 'no refunds' sign does not override the Act.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.4] Digital content, such as downloaded software, music or films, is covered by the same standards of quality, fitness and description as goods, because it is bought in the same way and can be just as faulty.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.b] Goods must meet four standards at the time they are supplied, and the consumer is entitled to all of them whatever the trader says.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.0] Satisfactory quality: goods must be of a standard a reasonable person would consider satisfactory, taking account of price and description. This includes being durable, safe, free from minor defects, and having an acceptable appearance and finish.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.1] Fit for purpose: goods must be fit for the purposes for which goods of that type are normally used, and for any particular purpose the buyer made known to the trader, for example asking for a coat that is waterproof.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.2] As described: goods must match any description given by the trader, the label or the advertisement, so a 'leather' bag that is plastic does not meet this right.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.3] Correspond to sample or model: goods must match a sample or model shown to the consumer before purchase.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.4] A fault that is present when the goods are supplied is the trader's responsibility, even if it appears only later.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.5] The retailer is responsible for faulty goods and must deal with the complaint, even if the fault was made by the manufacturer. The consumer approaches the trader, not the manufacturer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.b] A service is measured against what a competent provider would deliver and against what the trader said or agreed, so the consumer is protected even though there is no physical product to inspect.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.0] Reasonable care and skill: a service must be carried out with the care and skill a competent provider would show, in other words to a high standard by a suitably qualified person.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.1] Materials: any materials used in a service must be of satisfactory quality and fit for their purpose, so a repair using poor parts is a breach even if the work itself was careful.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.2] Time: where no time was agreed, the service must be carried out within a reasonable time, so a trader cannot leave a job half-finished for months.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.3] Price: where no price was agreed in advance, the consumer pays only a reasonable price for the service, which protects them from an inflated bill afterwards.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.4] Matching what was said or agreed: the service must match what the trader told the consumer or agreed with them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.b] When goods or services do not meet their statutory standard, the remedies are applied in a set order: the consumer starts with the gentlest fix and moves to the next only if that fails.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.0] First remedy for goods: repair or replacement at the trader's cost, within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience to the consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.1] Second remedy for goods: a price reduction or a refund, where repair or replacement is not possible, is not carried out within a reasonable time, or causes significant inconvenience.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.2] Short-term right to reject: the time limit for rejecting faulty goods outright is 30 days, so they can be rejected within 30 days of supply (delivery) and the consumer receives a full refund, without having to accept a repair first.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.3] Services that are not performed to the required standard must be repeated by the trader, or the consumer can ask for a price reduction.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.4] A manufacturer's guarantee is extra to statutory rights and cannot reduce them. A statutory right is set by law and applies automatically, whereas a guarantee is a voluntary promise from the manufacturer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.b] The Act protects buyers from faulty goods and services, not from every disappointment, so exam scenarios often turn on spotting when no remedy is owed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.0] There is no statutory right to a refund merely because the buyer changed their mind or dislikes the colour. Any refund, exchange or credit note in that case is the shop's own policy, a favour rather than a legal duty.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.1] Goods bought in a sale are covered by the same statutory rights as goods bought at full price, so a sale does not weaken the consumer's position.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.2] Exception: a fault that the trader pointed out before the sale is not covered by the statutory rights for that fault.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.3] A consumer who was not told of a fault before purchase keeps the full statutory remedies for it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.4] Misuse or ordinary wear and tear is not a fault for which the trader is liable, because the goods were not faulty when supplied.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.5] Proof of purchase, such as a receipt, bank statement or order confirmation, is needed to show the goods came from this trader.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.b] The Consumer Protection Act 2007 is about how traders behave in advertising, pricing and selling, rather than what can be claimed for a faulty purchase. It prohibits unfair commercial practices and protects consumers from false and misleading claims, and it replaced the Consumer Information Act 1978.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.0] Misleading actions: giving false or deceptive information about a product, its price, its origin or the trader's identity, which could lead a consumer to buy something they would not otherwise choose.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.1] Misleading omissions: leaving out or hiding material information that a consumer needs in order to make a decision.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.2] Aggressive practices: harassment, coercion, undue influence or pressure that limits the consumer's freedom to choose.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.3] Prohibited practices: a list of practices banned in all cases, such as falsely claiming that a product has an approval it does not have, or using a quality mark without permission.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.4] The Act is enforced by the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC), which investigates and can prosecute offences. It does not settle an individual consumer's dispute with a trader.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.5] The consumer benefits from protection against false advertising, honest price information, and a state body that takes action.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.b] Price claims must be accurate, including claims about reductions and comparisons, because a price is often what persuades a consumer to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.0] False or misleading information about the price of goods or services is prohibited.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.1] A claimed previous price or a claimed reduction must be accurate; a false or misleading reduction, such as a 'was' price the item never sold at, is an unfair practice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.2] A recommended retail price must not be used misleadingly to suggest a saving the consumer would not in fact make.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s3: 3. Consumer responsibility: information and rights
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.b] A purchase involves several parties, and each has a different relationship to the consumer. Knowing who is who tells you where to go when something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.0] The consumer is an individual who buys goods or services for personal use, not for business, so a shopper buying a kettle for the kitchen is a consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.1] The retailer or trader sells goods or services in the course of business and is the consumer's first point of contact for a complaint, because the consumer's contract is with the seller.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.2] Under consumer law the retailer is responsible for faults in goods, even when the manufacturer made the fault, and must deal with the complaint.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.3] The manufacturer makes the product and is the other party to any manufacturer's guarantee (a written promise to repair or replace a faulty product).
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.4] A guarantee is a contract between the manufacturer and the consumer. It is usually offered in addition to the consumer's statutory rights (rights set by law), not instead of them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.5] The supplier or distributor moves goods from the manufacturer to the retailer, so the consumer rarely deals with this party directly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.b] Informed decisions rest on reliable information. It comes from the product itself, from the seller and from independent sources, and each should be checked against the others.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.0] Labels, instructions and care labels give the contents or ingredients, how to use and care for the product, and warnings about hazards, so they should be read before buying and using.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.1] Unit pricing shows the price per kilogram or per litre, which allows comparison between different pack sizes.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.2] Advertising and sales material is useful but should be checked against the product label and independent information, because the seller wants to make the sale.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.3] Independent consumer publications and testing reports compare products on quality and value for money. The Consumers' Association of Ireland publishes comparative findings in Consumer Choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.4] Citizens Information gives free, confidential information on consumer rights, so a consumer can ask about a problem without cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.5] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission publishes guidance on consumer rights and on unfair trading.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.6] Terms and conditions, the returns policy and guarantee wording should be read before purchase, as they set out what the seller offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.7] Marks and symbols are information too: quality marks such as the Bord Bia Quality Mark, the CE mark (the manufacturer declares the product meets EU safety requirements), the energy rating label on appliances, and safety and hazard symbols.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.8] Reviews, word of mouth and online comparison sites are useful but may be biased, so check more than one source.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.b] A consumer's part in a transaction is to be informed, careful and honest. These are the consumer's duties, which are different from the rights the consumer is entitled to.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.0] Know your rights and the laws that protect consumers: the basic rights and remedies under consumer law apply whether or not the seller mentions them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.1] Be informed about goods and services before buying, and compare value, not only price, by weighing price against quality, durability and the cost of running or maintaining the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.2] Read and understand labels and terms before buying, including ingredients, dates, guarantee conditions and cancellation terms.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.3] Follow the manufacturer's instructions and care labels; a fault caused by misuse is unlikely to be the trader's responsibility.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.4] Keep receipts and proof of purchase, since they show when, where and what was bought, and are needed for a claim.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.b] The consumer's duties continue after the sale. A responsible consumer also acts quickly when something goes wrong and plans spending so that problems are less likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.0] Report faults promptly, stop using a faulty or unsafe product, and complain where appropriate, as delay can make a problem harder to prove.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.1] Give accurate, truthful information when complaining, including a clear description of the problem and the redress (the remedy) wanted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.2] Plan purchases and set a budget: check whether a product is needed before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.3] Resist pressure selling and impulse buying, and do not be rushed by sales talk, because a quick decision leaves no time to compare or check.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.4] Check the total cost, including interest and charges, before agreeing to credit or easy-pay arrangements, because the instalments can add up to far more than the cash price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.5] Use resources responsibly and consider the environment: reduce waste, and reuse and recycle packaging.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.b] Traditional buying principles still shape how a consumer should approach a purchase, even though the law now gives buyers more protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.0] Caveat emptor is a Latin principle meaning 'let the buyer beware': it places the duty of checking goods on the buyer, so the buyer had to inspect goods before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.1] Modern consumer law limits caveat emptor by giving statutory rights that apply automatically, but inspecting goods and asking questions before purchase remains the best protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.2] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income; a wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.3] Vague claims such as 'the best' or 'new' that lack supporting evidence should be questioned before they influence a purchase, because they sound impressive but promise nothing that can be checked.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.b] Consumer rights are what a buyer is entitled to expect. Papers list them in slightly different ways, so learn the rights below as the common set.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.0] Right to safety: goods and services must not endanger health or life.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.1] Right to honest and truthful information about goods and services, so that buyers can decide on accurate facts.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.2] Right to choice: a range of goods and services is available to choose from.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.3] Right to value for money: goods and services should be of a quality that matches the price paid, and goods should be of satisfactory quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.4] Right to redress: a consumer whose goods or service are faulty can seek repair, replacement or a refund.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.5] Right to services completed to a high standard, by a qualified person, using good materials.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.6] There is no right to a refund simply for a change of mind in a shop purchase; a retailer may offer an exchange or a credit note as goodwill.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.7] Rights and responsibilities are linked: each right is only as useful as the consumer's knowledge and action, so a right to information works only if labels are read.
