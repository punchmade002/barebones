TARGET hom-food-safety-s4: 4. Uses of micro-organisms in food production
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.b] Not all micro-organisms spoil food. Some are added on purpose, because their feeding changes the food in useful ways, and this process is called fermentation.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.0] Fermentation is the breakdown of organic substances, such as sugars, by bacteria or yeast, usually in the absence or shortage of oxygen. It is used to make many foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.1] The by-products of fermentation include carbon dioxide, energy, acids (such as lactic acid and acetic acid) and alcohol, which is why fermented foods taste and behave differently from the raw ingredients.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.2] In lactic acid fermentation, bacteria turn sugars such as lactose (the sugar in milk) into lactic acid. This is the basis of yoghurt and cheese.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.3] In alcoholic fermentation, yeast turns sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. This is the basis of bread, beer and wine.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.b] Milk contains the sugar lactose, which lactic acid bacteria feed on. Their acid changes the milk's flavour and texture, which is how milk becomes cheese or yoghurt.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.0] Starter cultures are lactic acid bacteria added to milk to begin fermentation. They ferment the lactose in milk into lactic acid, giving cheese and yoghurt their sour flavour and texture.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.1] In cheese-making, the lactic acid works together with rennet to curdle the milk and set the curd, so acid on its own is not enough. Ripening by further bacteria or moulds then develops the flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.2] Yoghurt is made by fermenting milk, typically with Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus. The lactose becomes lactic acid, so the milk thickens and tastes sour. Some yoghurts add other strains.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.3] Probiotic (functional) yoghurts and drinks contain live bacteria that may benefit the intestinal flora (the helpful bacteria living in the gut) when eaten, so they are examples of functional foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.b] Moulds are fungi that grow as fine threads. In a few foods a particular mould is added deliberately, and each gives the food its own flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.0] Blue-veined cheese: the mould Penicillium roqueforti grows through the cheese, producing blue-green veins and a sharp flavour. Cashel Blue, made in Co. Tipperary, is an Irish example; Roquefort and Stilton are others.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.1] Surface-ripened soft cheese: Penicillium camemberti grows on the outside and gives the white rind of Camembert and Brie.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.2] Soy sauce is produced by the mould Aspergillus oryzae, which breaks down the protein and starch in soya beans by fermentation, giving the sauce its savoury flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.b] Yeast is a single-celled fungus, not a bacterium. It ferments sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and different foods make use of different parts of that result.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.0] In bread-making (including sourdough), yeast ferments the sugars in the dough and produces carbon dioxide, which makes the dough rise. This is why yeast is called a biological raising agent.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.1] The alcohol produced in the dough evaporates during baking, so bread is not alcoholic.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.2] In brewing, yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (barley that has been germinated) to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide, giving beer.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.3] In wine-making, yeast ferments the sugars in grape juice into alcohol. Spirits are distilled from fermented liquids.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.4] Vinegar is made by acetic acid bacteria, such as Acetobacter, which oxidise (add oxygen to) alcohol to form acetic acid.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.b] Micro-organisms are also used in meat products and as foods in their own right. Some provide protein or vitamins rather than flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.0] Salami, pepperoni and chorizo are made by adding bacterial starter cultures to cured meat. The bacteria ferment its sugars to lactic acid, which flavours the product and helps to preserve it.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.1] Quorn is a meat substitute made from mycoprotein, which comes from the fungus Fusarium venenatum grown by fermentation in large fermenters. It is high in protein and fibre and low in saturated fat.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.2] Mycoprotein is a novel protein food, meaning a new kind of food produced by fermentation rather than from animals. It is from a fungus, not from yeast, bacteria or plants.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.3] Mushrooms and truffles are large fungi that are eaten directly as food.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.4] Yeast extracts and yeast-based food supplements are used as sources of B vitamins, and bacteria are also used in food supplements.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.b] Using micro-organisms on purpose gives foods qualities that the raw ingredients do not have.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.0] Fermentation adds flavour and changes texture, for example the sourness of yoghurt, the veins of blue cheese and the rise of bread dough.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.1] Fermented foods can have a longer keeping time, because acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid are produced, for example in salami and vinegar.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.2] Micro-organisms improve nutritional value, since yeast extracts supply B vitamins and mycoprotein supplies protein.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.3] They add variety to the diet, from cheeses, yoghurts and breads to meat substitutes such as Quorn.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s1: 1. Moulds and yeasts
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.b] Moulds are one of the micro-organisms that spoil food. Knowing what kind of living thing a mould is explains how it feeds and why it grows where it does.
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.0] Moulds are multicellular, filamentous fungi: their bodies are made of many cells joined in thread-like filaments.
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.1] Moulds are not plants because they have no chlorophyll (the green pigment plants use to make their own food), and they are not bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.2] Moulds are saprophytes, which means they feed on dead organic matter such as bread, cheese, jam, fruit and decaying plant material. They digest it outside their bodies and then absorb the digested products.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.b] A mould's visible body is built from thread-like filaments. Each part has a name and a job, and the parts are the labels exams ask for on a diagram.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.0] Hyphae (singular hypha): thread-like filaments that grow over and into the food and absorb its nutrients, which is how the mould feeds.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.1] Mycelium: the interwoven mass of hyphae that forms the visible growth, such as the fluffy or powdery layer on bread.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.2] Sporangiophore: an upright hypha that rises from the mycelium and carries a sporangium at its tip, lifting the spores up so they can escape into the air.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.3] Sporangium: a round, spore-filled case at the tip of a sporangiophore; it bursts open when ripe and releases spores into the air.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.4] Conidia: in Penicillium, and also in Aspergillus, spores are borne in chains at the tips of branched conidiophores (spore-bearing hyphae) instead of inside a round sporangium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.5] Diagram: a mould growing on a food surface, showing the mycelium spreading across the food, a sporangiophore rising from it and a sporangium at its tip; label hypha, mycelium, sporangiophore and sporangium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.b] Mould spores are almost everywhere, which is why food left out so often goes mouldy. Knowing where they come from shows how they reach food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.0] Moulds are saprophytes: they feed on dead organic matter, so they grow on decaying food and plant material.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.1] Spores are carried in the air, on dust, on food packaging and on unclean utensils, and they settle on food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.2] Soil and damp indoor surfaces act as reservoirs (stores) from which spores spread to food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.3] Already-mouldy food is also a source: a mouldy item releases spores that spread to food stored beside it.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.4] Rhizopus is the common bread mould; it grows on bread, fruit and other moist starchy or sugary foods.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.b] Mould spores only grow into a mould when six conditions are met. Each one gives a way to stop growth, and each has a reason you can give.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.0] Food: moulds feed on the organic matter in food, such as bread, cheese, jam and fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.1] Warmth: most moulds are mesophiles (they prefer moderate temperatures); Penicillium grows best at 20 to 25 °C, and cold slows growth.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.2] Moisture: moulds need moisture to grow, so frozen and very dry foods are less at risk.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.3] Oxygen: moulds are aerobic (they need oxygen), so they grow on the surface of food, such as jam, or through open-textured foods such as bread.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.4] pH: moulds prefer slightly acidic conditions, around pH 4 to 6; strong acids inhibit their growth.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.5] Time: moulds need about 24 to 48 hours to establish visible growth, because the spores must germinate and the mycelium must spread.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.6] Freezing at −18 °C inactivates mould growth, but it does not kill the mould, so growth can start again after thawing.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.b] One type of mould is often asked about in detail. Penicillium is a good choice because its features are easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.0] Penicillium is a saprophytic mould with a green-blue, powdery appearance, which comes from its chains of conidia.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.1] It grows on bread, jam, cheese and fruit, and favours temperatures of 20 to 25 °C.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.2] Penicillium reproduces asexually only, by conidia, so it never forms a zygospore.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.3] Heat above 75 °C destroys Penicillium, whereas freezing only inactivates its growth.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.b] Other moulds can be given as your named example. Their differences are in colour, spore structure and whether they reproduce sexually.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.0] Rhizopus (the black bread mould) is saprophytic, with fluffy white mycelium and black pin-head sporangia that hold its spores.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.1] Rhizopus reproduces both sexually and asexually, so it can form a zygospore as well as spores.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.2] Mucor is saprophytic, with white hyphae and grey sporangia, and reproduces both sexually and asexually.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.3] Aspergillus is saprophytic and black or green; it favours 20 to 25 °C and reproduces asexually only, by conidia.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.b] Moulds reproduce by releasing spores, tiny cells that spread through the air. Some moulds can also reproduce sexually, which gives a tough resting stage.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.0] Asexual reproduction, first stage: once the mycelium is established, a hypha (the sporangiophore) grows upwards and its head forms a sporangium, or in some moulds chains of conidia.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.1] When the sporangium ripens it bursts, releasing spores into the air, so they travel to new food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.2] A spore that lands on suitable food germinates (starts to grow) and develops a new mycelium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.3] Sexual reproduction (Rhizopus and Mucor): two compatible hyphae grow beside each other and fuse.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.4] The dividing wall between the fused hyphae breaks down and a thick-walled zygospore forms.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.5] The zygospore protects the spores until conditions are suitable; then it germinates, hyphae grow out and spores are released.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.b] Yeasts are single-celled fungi. Each cell is a single oval or round unit, larger and more regular in shape than a bacterium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.0] Yeast is a eukaryote, meaning its cell has a true nucleus; it is a fungus, not a bacterium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.1] Cell wall: a rigid outer layer that gives the cell its shape, so it holds together, and protects it.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.2] Cell membrane: a thin layer inside the cell wall that controls which substances pass in and out, so the cell takes in food and keeps unwanted substances out.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.3] Nucleus: a membrane-bound structure containing the genetic material, so the cell is a eukaryote, for example unlike a bacterium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.4] Vacuole: a fluid-filled storage space within the cytoplasm.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.5] Granular cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents of the cell, containing food reserves stored as granules.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.6] Diagram: a yeast cell labelled cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, vacuole and granular cytoplasm.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.b] Yeasts live wherever sugar and moisture are found. They reproduce in a different way from moulds, by budding rather than by spores.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.0] Yeasts occur naturally on the skins of fruit, especially grapes, and in soil, in the air, on plant surfaces and on sugary foods.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.1] Yeasts need sugar as food, moisture, warmth and time to grow.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.2] Yeast is facultative, meaning it can do either: it respires with oxygen, or ferments sugar when oxygen is absent.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.3] In fermentation, yeast converts sugar into carbon dioxide and alcohol.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.4] Reproduction is asexual, by budding: a small bud grows from the parent cell, enlarges and breaks away as a new cell.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.5] Yeast spoils sugary foods such as juices, jam and syrups by fermenting them. Baker's and brewer's yeast is Saccharomyces cerevisiae.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.b] Bacteria are prokaryotes: single cells with no true nucleus, so their genetic material lies free in the cytoplasm. This is the main contrast with fungi, whose cells have a nucleus.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer, made of peptidoglycan, that gives the bacterial cell its shape and protects it.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.1] Cell membrane: controls the movement of substances in and out of the cell.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.2] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like interior where the cell's chemical reactions take place.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.3] Ribosomes: small structures in the cytoplasm where proteins are made, so the cell can build and repair itself.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.4] Flagella: whip-like tails that rotate to move the bacterium; not all bacteria have them.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.5] Nucleoid: the region containing the single circular loop of DNA, lying free in the cytoplasm; plasmids are small extra loops of DNA.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.6] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, where one cell divides in two; under ideal conditions some divide about every 20 minutes.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.7] Diagram: a bacterial cell labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, flagella and nucleoid.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s2: 2. Food poisoning bacteria
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.b] Most bacteria in food are harmless or even useful, so only a few cause illness. Food poisoning is illness caused by eating food contaminated with pathogenic (disease-causing) bacteria or their toxins.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.0] Six environmental factors affect bacterial growth: food, moisture, warmth, oxygen requirement, pH and time. Protein-rich, moist foods are high risk because they give bacteria what they need to grow.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.1] Most food poisoning bacteria favour a near-neutral pH, neither strongly acid nor strongly alkaline.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.2] Under good conditions a bacterium divides by binary fission (splitting in two) about every 20 minutes, so numbers rise very quickly and a few bacteria can become millions within hours.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.3] The danger zone, about 5 to 63 °C, is the range in which food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest. Below 5 °C growth slows down, and above 63 °C most bacteria stop multiplying and thorough cooking destroys them.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.b] Micro-organisms are grouped by the temperature at which they grow best. This explains why the danger zone sits around body temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.0] Psychrophiles grow best at about −5 to 20 °C, in cold conditions such as refrigerated food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.1] Mesophiles grow best at about 20 to 45 °C. Most food poisoning bacteria are mesophiles, which is why the danger zone is close to body temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.2] Salmonella has an optimum (best) growth temperature of about 37 °C, which is normal body temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.3] Thermophiles grow best at about 45 to 75 °C, in hot environments.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.4] The danger zone, about 5 to 63 °C, is the range in which mesophilic food poisoning bacteria multiply rapidly.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.b] Micro-organisms also differ in their need for oxygen. This decides which foods and storage conditions suit each one.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.0] Aerobic micro-organisms require oxygen to survive and grow; moulds are aerobic.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.1] Anaerobic micro-organisms thrive without oxygen, so sealed foods suit them; Clostridium botulinum is an example.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.2] Facultative micro-organisms adapt to either aerobic or anaerobic conditions, so they can grow with or without oxygen; Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus and yeast are facultative.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.b] Salmonella is a common infectious food poisoning bacterium, meaning the illness comes from eating the living bacteria themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.0] Structure: a rod-shaped, Gram-negative bacterium that does not form spores (tough resting cells). It reproduces by binary fission.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.1] Habitat and sources: the intestines of humans, animals and birds; faeces; polluted water; unwashed hands, which carry it onto food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.2] High-risk foods: raw or undercooked poultry, meat, fish and eggs; unpasteurised milk and dairy products.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.3] Growth: needs food, moisture and time, and favours a near-neutral pH. It is a mesophile with an optimum of about 37 °C, and it is facultative.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.4] Incubation period: usually about 12 to 36 hours after eating contaminated food, with a wider range of 12 to 72 hours.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.5] Symptoms: diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and fever.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.b] Staphylococcus aureus is a common toxic food poisoning bacterium. It makes a poison in the food as it grows, and the poison causes the illness.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.0] Structure: a Gram-positive, spherical cell (a coccus) that forms grape-like clusters; it does not form spores.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.1] Habitat and sources: the skin, nose and throat of humans and their hair; infected cuts and boils. It spreads to food by handling.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.2] High-risk foods: cooked meats, poultry, ham, egg products, cream-filled cakes, custard and egg or potato salads made with mayonnaise.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.3] Growth: a mesophile that grows at about 37 °C and is facultative. It tolerates salt and sugar better than most bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.4] Toxin: it produces an enterotoxin (a toxin that acts on the gut) in food. The toxin is heat-stable, so it is not destroyed by normal cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.5] Incubation period: short, usually about 2 to 4 hours, within a wider range of about 1 to 6 hours.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.6] Symptoms: sudden nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhoea.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.b] Clostridium botulinum is a toxic food poisoning bacterium that produces a very potent neurotoxin (a poison that attacks the nerves). It is rare but dangerous.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.0] Structure: a rod-shaped, Gram-positive, spore-forming bacterium that is an obligate anaerobe, meaning it can only grow where there is no oxygen.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.1] Habitat and sources: soil and water, from which its spores reach vegetables and other foods.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.2] High-risk foods: home-canned low-acid vegetables, badly processed canned or vacuum-packed foods, cured meats, and honey, which must not be given to babies under one year.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.3] Growth: it multiplies in sealed, low-acid foods where no oxygen is present, because these are the conditions it needs.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.4] Toxin: a neurotoxin that blocks nerve signals to muscles, causing paralysis. The toxin is destroyed by boiling for about 10 minutes, but the spores survive ordinary cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.5] Incubation period: typically 12 to 36 hours after eating contaminated food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.6] Symptoms: double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing and speaking, muscle weakness, paralysis and breathing difficulties; it can be fatal.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.b] A question may ask for any common strain, so it helps to know a few more with one typical high-risk food each.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.0] Listeria is infectious. It is found in pâté, coleslaw, soft cheese and smoked fish, and it grows at fridge temperature. It is serious for pregnant women, newborns and the elderly.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.1] E. coli is infectious. High-risk foods are undercooked minced meat, polluted water and unpasteurised milk.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.2] Campylobacter is infectious. High-risk foods are raw poultry and unpasteurised milk.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.3] Bacillus cereus is linked to cooked rice and reheated rice.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.4] Clostridium perfringens is toxic. High-risk foods are stews, gravies and large cooked joints that cool slowly.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.b] Food poisoning falls into two types, depending on whether the harm comes from the bacteria or from their toxin. Incubation period is the time between eating contaminated food and the appearance of the first symptoms.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.0] Infectious food poisoning: caused by eating food containing large numbers of living pathogenic bacteria, which multiply in the gut.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.1] Infectious food poisoning: the bacteria release endotoxins, which are made inside their cells and released when the cells die.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.2] Infectious food poisoning: endotoxins and bacteria are easily destroyed by proper cooking and reheating, so the risk comes from undercooked food. Incubation is usually more than 12 hours; examples are Salmonella, E. coli, Campylobacter and Listeria.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.3] Toxic food poisoning: caused by eating food that already contains exotoxin, a poison produced by bacteria as they grow in the food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.4] Toxic food poisoning: exotoxins are hard to destroy and may need boiling for 30 minutes, so cooking may not make the food safe. Symptoms appear quickly, often within 2 hours; examples are Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium botulinum and Clostridium perfringens.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.5] In one line each: infectious means the bacteria themselves are eaten, onset is slower and cooking destroys them; toxic means the toxin is eaten, onset is fast and the toxin survives cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.b] Outbreaks usually trace back to a few handling failures that let bacteria reach food or multiply in it. Each one links to the growth conditions bacteria need.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.0] Undercooked poultry or minced meat allows surviving bacteria to cause illness.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.1] Cooked food left at room temperature allows bacteria to multiply in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.2] Cross-contamination from raw meat or poultry transfers bacteria to food that is eaten without cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.3] Food handlers with poor personal hygiene or illness, such as not washing hands after using the toilet, transfer bacteria to food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.4] Contaminated water or unpasteurised dairy products introduce bacteria before any cooking takes place.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.5] Slow cooling of large batches, inadequate reheating and preparing food too far in advance also give bacteria time to grow or survive.
