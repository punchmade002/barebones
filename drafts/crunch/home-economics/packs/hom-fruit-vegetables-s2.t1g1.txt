TARGET hom-fruit-vegetables-s2: 2. Selection, storage and spoilage
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.b] Shoppers can buy fruit and vegetables in several forms, and each has a place in the diet. The best choice depends on cost, time, storage space and how soon the produce will be used.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.0] Fresh: choose firm produce with no bruising, cuts, mould, wilting or discolouration, because damage shortens keeping time. In-season produce is usually cheaper and has the best flavour; buy usable quantities and shop often for the most perishable items.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.1] Frozen: picked and frozen soon after harvest, so frozen fruit and vegetables retain most of their nutrients. They are convenient, create little waste, are available all year and can be cooked from frozen. Check the pack is solid, undamaged and not frosted, and that it has been kept frozen.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.2] Canned: cooked and sealed in tins or jars, so the product has a long shelf life and is ready to use. Check the liquid: brine adds salt (canned vegetables often contain added salt) and syrup adds sugar, while juice-packed or water-packed options exist. Check cans for dents, bulges and leaks.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.3] Dried: most of the water is removed, so the produce is light and keeps for long periods. Dried fruit such as raisins and apricots is concentrated in sugar and energy, and dried pulses need soaking or cooking.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.4] Other choices also exist: organic, Fairtrade, loose or pre-packed, in-season or imported (out-of-season) produce, and exotic fruit.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.5] Pre-prepared (convenience) produce is washed, peeled, cut or ready to cook, such as salad bags, stir-fry mixes and fruit salad pots. It saves time and skill and leaves little waste.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.6] The disadvantages of pre-prepared produce are that it costs more, has a shorter shelf life once prepared than whole produce, and some vitamin C is lost through cutting.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.0.7] When buying pre-prepared produce, check the use-by or best-before date, cost and value for money, that the packaging is undamaged and unopened, that there is no discolouration or sliminess, that the quantity suits the household, the storage and cooking instructions, and the nutritional information on the label.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.1.b] Two shoppers can make different choices from the same shelf, because their circumstances differ. Cost, skills, time and storage space decide how much is bought fresh, frozen or pre-prepared.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.1.0] Cost: buying in season is cheaper, loose produce is cheaper than packaged, and buying direct from growers or markets can save money.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.1.1] Buying in bulk only makes sense if there is freezer or preserving capacity, because fresh produce left over will spoil.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.1.2] Skills and time: the household's cooking skills and the time available decide how much is bought fresh and how much pre-prepared.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.1.3] Storage space (fridge, freezer, cool dry store) limits the quantity that can be bought and kept in good condition.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.b] Shoppers judge quality by looking, feeling and smelling, and the EU sets grading classes so that buyers know what standard they are getting. Fresh fruit and vegetables sold in the EU must meet marketing standards.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.0] Quality signs when selecting fresh produce: firmness, fresh colour, crispness, no bruising or blemish, no mould or sprouting, uniform size (for even cooking), fresh-looking stalks and leaves, a pleasant smell, and heavy for its size.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.1] Extra class: the best (superior) quality, uniform in shape, size and colour, and free from defects.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.2] Class I: good quality, with minor defects allowed provided they do not affect general appearance, keeping quality or presentation; it is free from bruising and has only slight defects.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.3] Class II: reasonably good, acceptable quality, with more defects of shape or colour allowed, but still fit for consumption. It is sound and good for cooking and processing.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.4] The grading standards are the EU marketing standards for fresh produce, set in Delegated Regulation (EU) 2023/2429.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.5] Fresh produce must show its country of origin under these marketing standards and, where the standard requires it, the class and variety.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.2.6] Irish produce may carry the Bord Bia quality assurance mark, which shows it has met an assured standard. This mark is a quality assurance scheme and is not the same thing as an EU class.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.3.b] Fruit and vegetables are still alive after harvest, so they keep changing while they wait to be sold and eaten. This is why storage guidelines exist.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.3.0] Produce keeps respiring (using up its own food reserves), losing moisture and ripening after harvest, so quality and vitamin C fall with time.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.3.1] For this reason, buy little and often, use produce soon, and rotate older stock to the front so it is used first.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.3.2] Fresh vegetables should be refrigerated at about 5 degrees Celsius or below and used as soon as possible, because vitamin C declines during storage. This applies to refrigerated salad and vegetables, not to produce such as potatoes and onions that is kept in a cool store.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.b] Different produce needs different conditions. The aim is to slow moisture loss, ripening and rot without damaging the produce.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.0] Salad and most fresh vegetables: remove any plastic wrapping and damaged items, then store in the refrigerator in the salad or vegetable drawer with air circulating, and use quickly. A sealed bag traps condensation, which speeds rot.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.1] Potatoes: store in a cool, dark, dry, ventilated place such as a vegetable rack or paper sack. Light turns them green and produces bitter solanine, which is harmful, so discard green or sprouting parts or tubers.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.2] Onions and root vegetables: store in a cool, dry, dark, ventilated place, because damp conditions encourage rot. Keep the tops of roots trimmed.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.3] Apples and bananas give off ethylene, a gas that speeds the ripening of nearby produce, so keep them away from other fruit and vegetables.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.4] Bananas and other tropical fruit, and hard fruit, ripen at room temperature, away from ethylene producers. Soft fruit and berries are then refrigerated, and berries are washed only just before eating.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.4.5] Keep fresh produce away from raw meat and fish to prevent cross-contamination, wash produce before use, and clean the fridge regularly.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.5.b] Processed produce keeps longer, but only if the storage conditions are right. The rules protect both quality and safety.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.5.0] Frozen: store at minus 18 degrees Celsius or below, wrapped in moisture-proof packaging, and use within the date. Do not refreeze after thawing.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.5.1] Canned: keep in a cool, dry place. Once a can is opened, transfer the contents to a covered container in the fridge and use within a day or two.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.5.2] Dented, swollen (bulging) or leaking cans are discarded, because they may be unsafe.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.5.3] Dried fruit is kept in a sealed container in a cool, dry place.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.b] Enzymes (proteins in the produce that speed up chemical reactions) stay active after harvest. They cause two kinds of spoilage: ripening that goes too far, and browning of cut surfaces.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.0] Enzymes remain active after harvesting and continue the ripening process until the produce becomes over-ripe, soft and eventually decays.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.1] When cells are cut, bruised or peeled, the enzyme oxidase is released and reacts with oxygen in the air, causing enzymic browning, as seen in apple, potato and banana.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.2] Enzymic browning also destroys some vitamin C and carotene.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.3] Cold slows the enzymes, so refrigeration slows ripening and browning.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.4] Blanching before freezing (brief boiling water, then cold) denatures the enzymes, which means their shape is destroyed so they stop working. Unblanched vegetables continue to deteriorate even in a freezer.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.5] Acid such as lemon juice lowers the pH and slows browning.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.6.6] Excluding air, for example with water, sugar syrup or wrapping, stops oxygen reaching the cut surface.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.7.b] Fresh produce is mostly water, so losing it changes how it looks and feels. Loss is fastest in warm, dry, uncovered storage.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.7.0] Moisture is lost through respiration and evaporation, especially in warm, dry, uncovered storage.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.7.1] Loss of water causes cells to lose turgor (the firmness that water pressure gives cells), so leafy vegetables wilt, roots shrivel and skins wrinkle.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.7.2] Produce loses mass and becomes limp, with poorer texture and appearance.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.7.3] Cool, humid storage such as a salad drawer, and suitable packaging, slow moisture loss.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.7.4] Covering cut produce and not leaving produce in warm rooms also reduces loss.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.b] Micro-organisms are tiny living things such as bacteria, yeasts and moulds. They use the produce as food, and their growth makes it unfit to eat.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.0] Bacteria, yeasts and moulds come from soil, water, dirty hands and equipment, pests, damaged produce and cross-contamination.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.1] Bruises and cuts give micro-organisms access to nutrients and moisture inside the produce.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.2] Warmth, moisture, food (the produce itself), time and a suitable pH allow micro-organisms to multiply rapidly.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.3] Bacteria cause soft, slimy rot and unpleasant odours; moulds form coloured fuzzy growth, and some produce harmful mycotoxins (poisons).
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.4] Yeasts ferment the sugars in fruit, giving a sour or alcoholic smell and bubbles.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.5] Prevention combines good hygiene (clean hands, equipment and surfaces), removing damaged items, separating produce from raw meat, careful washing, refrigeration, correct storage temperatures and prompt use. Washing alone is not enough, because soil-borne contamination can persist.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.8.6] Other causes of spoilage are physical damage, such as bruising or crushing in transport, and pests.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.9.b] Businesses that handle produce are bound by hygiene law, and public bodies oversee food safety. Grading is set at EU level.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.9.0] Food businesses must follow the hygiene rules in Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, given effect in Ireland by the European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s2.9.1] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), established under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998, enforces food law through service contracts and gives food safety guidance on handling and storing produce.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s5: 5. Enzymes and food spoilage
[hom-food-safety-s5.0.b] Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions, and food is full of them. Plant and animal foods contain their own enzymes, which keep working after harvest or slaughter, so food can spoil without any micro-organisms being involved.
[hom-food-safety-s5.0.0] Enzymes occur naturally in fruit, vegetables, fish and meat. Because they keep working after the food is picked or killed, they slowly change its colour, texture and flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s5.0.1] Each enzyme has an active site, a shaped region into which its specific substrate (the one substance it acts on) fits to be broken down. This is why each enzyme acts on only one kind of substance.
[hom-food-safety-s5.0.2] Enzymes work best in warmth and moisture, so removing water from food leaves them unable to act.
[hom-food-safety-s5.0.3] Heat denatures enzymes, which means it changes the shape of the active site so that the substrate no longer fits and the enzyme stops working. Cooking therefore inactivates them.
[hom-food-safety-s5.0.4] Low temperatures slow enzyme activity without destroying it, so enzymes resume working when the food warms up again.
[hom-food-safety-s5.1.b] Fruit does not stop changing when it is picked. Its ripening enzymes carry on working, and if they are left long enough they turn good fruit into spoiled fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s5.1.0] Ripening enzymes continue to act after fruit is picked, converting starch to sugar and softening the cell walls, so the fruit becomes sweeter and softer.
[hom-food-safety-s5.1.1] As enzymes change colour, texture and flavour, fruit moves from ripe to over-ripe and then decays, becoming soft and mushy before it rots.
[hom-food-safety-s5.1.2] Over-ripening: a hard, under-ripe banana becomes soft and sweet, and then black, as enzymes continue to break it down.
[hom-food-safety-s5.2.b] Cut an apple and leave it, and the flesh turns brown. This is enzymic browning, and it needs three things: damaged cells, the enzyme and oxygen from the air.
[hom-food-safety-s5.2.0] When the surface of apples, bananas or potatoes is cut, peeled or bruised, enzymes are released from the damaged cells.
[hom-food-safety-s5.2.1] Polyphenol oxidase, also called oxidase, acts on phenolic compounds in the presence of oxygen.
[hom-food-safety-s5.2.2] The reaction forms brown pigments on the cut surface, which is why cut apples and potatoes discolour.
[hom-food-safety-s5.2.3] Browning changes the appearance and flavour of the food but is not a safety hazard.
[hom-food-safety-s5.3.b] Enzymes also break down fish, fats and vegetables, even when the food is stored in the cold. The damage shows up as off-odours, rancidity, poor colour and texture, and lost nutrients, and it shortens shelf life.
[hom-food-safety-s5.3.0] Lipase in fish and oily foods breaks down fats (triglycerides) into fatty acids and glycerol, causing rancidity (the stale, unpleasant taste of spoiled fat) and off-flavours.
[hom-food-safety-s5.3.1] Enzymes in fish continue to break down the flesh even at low temperatures, producing odour.
[hom-food-safety-s5.3.2] Vegetables that are not blanched before freezing keep their enzymes active during storage, which spoils colour, texture and flavour in the freezer.
[hom-food-safety-s5.3.3] Oxidase in the cell walls of plants is activated by oxygen and can destroy thiamine (vitamin B1), vitamin C and carotene, so some nutritive value is lost.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.b] Control works by inactivating enzymes, slowing their action or excluding oxygen. In other words, enzymes are controlled by destroying them (heat), changing their conditions (acid, cold, dryness) or removing a reactant (oxygen).
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.0] Heat: blanching, a brief immersion of vegetables in boiling water or steam followed by rapid cooling, inactivates enzymes before freezing; cooking denatures them.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.1] Acid: adding lemon juice or vinegar to cut apples lowers the pH and inactivates the enzymes that cause browning.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.2] Low temperature: refrigeration and freezing slow enzyme activity but do not stop or destroy it, so enzymes become active again on thawing. This is why frozen vegetables need blanching first.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.3] Sulphur dioxide: used as a preservative in dried fruit and in fruit and vegetable juices, it prevents browning and inhibits enzyme action.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.4] Removing oxygen: vacuum packing, covering cut surfaces with water or sugar syrup, or treating cut fruit with an antioxidant such as ascorbic acid (vitamin C) prevents oxidase from browning the food.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.5] Drying: removing water makes enzymes inactive because they need it to act, which preserves herbs and dried fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s5.4.6] Irradiation is also listed as a control for enzymic spoilage.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s3: 3. Micro-organisms and the control of microbial spoilage
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.b] Spoilage is any change that makes food unfit or unpleasant to eat, such as an off smell, taste, colour or texture. Several causes are at work, but micro-organisms are the main one.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.0] Micro-organisms (bacteria, moulds and yeasts) are the main cause of spoilage, and they are the cause this subsection deals with.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.1] Enzyme action (the food's own chemicals continuing to work) causes changes such as browning and over-ripening.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.2] Physical damage and bruising break the surface of food, which lets other spoilage in.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.3] Moisture loss makes food shrink, wrinkle or harden, for example a dried-out piece of fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.4] Rancidity is the going-off of fats, which gives them an unpleasant smell and taste.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.5] Chemical contamination, for example by pesticides, antibiotics or metals, spoils food and can make it unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.6] Spoilage micro-organisms mainly make food unpleasant, so spoiled food is not always unsafe. Pathogens (disease-causing micro-organisms) can make food unsafe even when it looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.b] Bacteria are the micro-organisms that spoil most protein and moist foods, and they change food in several ways.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.0] Putrefaction: bacteria break down protein in meat, fish and eggs, producing foul odours and a slimy surface.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.1] Souring: lactic acid bacteria ferment the lactose (milk sugar) in milk, producing lactic acid that curdles the milk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.2] Bacteria that survive in food can produce toxins (poisons), which can make food unsafe even when the food looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.3] The foods at greatest risk are high-moisture, high-protein foods such as meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk and cooked rice, because bacteria find both water and nutrients in them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.b] Moulds and yeasts spoil foods that bacteria find harder to use, especially acidic and sugary ones.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.0] Moulds grow visibly on food as fluffy, powdery or coloured patches, for example on bread, cheese, fruit and jam, and some produce mycotoxins (poisons made by moulds) that are harmful if eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.1] Mould spores (tiny reproductive cells) spread through the air, which is how they land on food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.2] Yeasts ferment the sugars in fruit juices, jams, syrups and fruit, producing carbon dioxide, alcohol and off-flavours, and sometimes bubbling or swollen containers.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.3] Mould and yeast growth is mostly on the surface of food, where oxygen is available, but moulds also grow through open-textured foods such as bread.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.4] Acidic, sugary foods favour moulds and yeasts because they tolerate acid better than most bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.b] Micro-organisms spoil food only when the conditions they need are present. Remove or change one of them and growth slows or stops.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.0] Food: micro-organisms need nutrients, particularly protein and carbohydrate, as a source of energy and for building new cells.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.1] Moisture: their chemical reactions and nutrient absorption take place in solution, so dry foods support little growth and high-moisture foods are high risk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.2] Warmth: most spoilage and poisoning micro-organisms multiply fastest in the danger zone (5 to 63 °C), while cold slows growth, freezing stops it without killing them, and heat kills them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.3] Oxygen: aerobic micro-organisms such as moulds need oxygen, while anaerobic bacteria grow in sealed packs from which oxygen is absent, and facultative ones can adapt to either.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.4] pH (how acid or alkaline food is): most bacteria prefer a near-neutral pH, whereas moulds and yeasts tolerate acid. Lactic acid bacteria are an exception because they tolerate acid, but strongly acidic foods are generally safer.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.5] Time: micro-organisms need time to multiply, and some bacteria can double in number in about 20 minutes under ideal conditions (binary fission, where one cell divides into two).
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.b] Control of spoilage works either by destroying micro-organisms, keeping them away from food, or restricting one of the conditions they need. The first two principles deal with the micro-organisms themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.0] Heat treatments kill micro-organisms: cooking, pasteurisation, which uses a lower temperature for a set time, and sterilisation, which destroys all micro-organisms and their spores. Canning and bottling also use heat.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.1] Irradiation destroys micro-organisms and parasites in food by exposing it to controlled radiation, without making the food radioactive.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.2] Irradiation can extend shelf life and slow ripening and sprouting in fruit and vegetables, with little effect on colour, flavour or texture.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.3] Preventing micro-organisms reaching the food in the first place uses hygiene, clean equipment, covering food, sealed packaging, sterile containers and separating raw and cooked food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.b] The other principles leave micro-organisms alive but take away something they need. Most foods use more than one, and applying each to a named food is the usual exam task.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.0] Low temperature: refrigeration slows the growth of micro-organisms, and freezing inactivates them without destroying them, so growth resumes on thawing.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.1] Removing moisture: drying, salting, sugaring and concentrating reduce the water available to micro-organisms. Dissolved salt or sugar draws water out of microbial cells by osmosis, which dehydrates them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.2] Acidity: pickling in vinegar, or adding lemon juice, lowers the pH to a level that most bacteria cannot tolerate. Chutney and fermentation acids work the same way.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.3] Removing oxygen: vacuum packing and canning exclude air, preventing growth of aerobic micro-organisms, and modified-atmosphere packs replace the air around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.4] Preservatives: chemicals such as sulphur dioxide, benzoates and nitrites inhibit the growth of micro-organisms. Natural preservatives are salt, sugar, vinegar, alcohol and smoke.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.5] Combining principles: jam, for example, uses heat, sugar, acid and sealing together, so several principles protect the same food.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s1: 1. Physical and chemical changes during preparation and cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.b] A physical change alters the size, shape, state, colour or texture of food without forming new substances. Heat usually drives these changes, and each one has a food you can name as an example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.0] Coagulation: heat unfolds (denatures) egg, milk and fish proteins, which then bond into a set structure. Egg white turns from clear to white and opaque, fish flesh from translucent to opaque, and custard, scrambled egg and omelette set.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.1] Gelatinisation: starch granules absorb water and swell when heated in liquid, and can swell until they burst. This thickens sauces, gravies, custard and porridge.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.2] Tenderising: long, moist cooking converts collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) into gelatine, so tough cuts of meat become tender.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.3] Shrinkage: meat and fish lose water and melted fat during cooking, so they become smaller and lighter and fat melts out of meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.4] Increase in size: dried peas, beans and fruit, rice and pasta absorb water and swell during cooking. Whisked egg foam, sponge and bread also rise and increase in volume.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.5] Colour change: meat turns from red or pink to brown or grey as its myoglobin (the red pigment in meat) is heated. Green vegetables turn olive-brown with prolonged cooking or acid because the chlorophyll is altered, whereas carrots and other orange vegetables stay colourful.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.6] Softening: heat softens the cellulose and breaks down pectin (the 'glue' between cells) in plant cell walls, so vegetables and fruit become tender as cell water is lost.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.7] Increased digestibility and safety: heat destroys most micro-organisms and makes starch and protein easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.0.8] Loss of nutrients, because water-soluble vitamins dissolve in cooking water, is also counted as a physical change.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.b] A chemical change alters the molecular make-up of food and forms new substances with different colours, flavours and textures. Raising agents are a clear example, because they make a new gas that lifts the mixture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.0] Chemical raising agent: bicarbonate of soda reacts with an acid such as buttermilk and releases carbon dioxide gas, which makes soda bread rise. The same applies in cakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.1] Baking powder contains bicarbonate of soda plus an acid, so it releases carbon dioxide when it is moistened and heated.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.2] Biological raising agent: yeast feeds on sugar during fermentation and gives off carbon dioxide (and alcohol), which makes bread dough rise. The gas expands in the oven, and baking kills the yeast.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.3] Tenderising by acid or enzymes, for example in a marinade or with a meat tenderiser, is also accepted as a chemical change.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.1.4] Browning reactions are chemical changes too, because they make new coloured and flavoured substances: the Maillard reaction, caramelisation and dextrinisation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.b] Enzymic browning is a chemical change caused by an enzyme in damaged fruit and vegetables. It happens without heat, which separates it from the browning of toast or roast meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.0] Cutting, peeling or bruising breaks cell walls, so the enzyme polyphenol oxidase (called 'oxidase' in some marking schemes) meets phenolic compounds in the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.1] Oxygen from the air is needed: the enzyme oxidises the phenolic compounds, which then form brown pigments.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.2] Examples: sliced apple, banana, pear, avocado, potato and mushroom turning brown on standing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.3] Browning spoils the appearance, flavour and vitamin C content of fruit and vegetables. It is wanted in the making of black tea, where it is used deliberately.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.4] To stop oxygen reaching the surface, keep cut surfaces under water, sugar syrup, cling film, a layer of juice or a coating of dressing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.5] Acid such as lemon juice lowers the pH and slows the enzyme's action. Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) reduces the first brown products back to colourless compounds.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.6] Heat inactivates the enzyme, so blanching vegetables in boiling water before freezing, or cooking them, stops browning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.2.7] Refrigeration slows enzyme activity but does not stop it. Working quickly and cutting just before serving also limits browning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.b] A fair test lets you see which treatment slows browning best, by changing one thing at a time and keeping everything else the same.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.0] Aim: to find which treatment best slows enzymic browning on a sliced apple.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.1] Hypothesis: lemon juice will slow browning more than an untreated slice, because acid slows polyphenol oxidase.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.2] Independent variable: the treatment applied to each slice, e.g. untreated (the control), dipped in lemon juice, dipped in sugar syrup, dipped in salt water or cold water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.3] Dependent variable: the degree of browning, rated on a 0 to 5 colour scale at fixed times.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.4] Controlled variables: the same apple variety and ripeness, slices of the same thickness cut with the same knife, the same dipping time, the same room temperature and the same time after cutting. These keep the test fair, so only the treatment changes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.5] Method: 1. Wash and dry the apple and cut it into slices of equal thickness. 2. Dip each treated slice for the same time, keeping one untreated slice as the control. 3. Lay all slices on labelled plates and record the colour of each every 5 minutes for 30 minutes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.6] Safety and hygiene: wash hands and the apple, use a clean board and a sharp knife held with a claw grip, and wipe up any spilled juice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.7] Expected result: the untreated control browns first and most, while the lemon juice slices stay palest for longest. This supports the hypothesis.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.3.8] Evaluation: a rating by eye is subjective and apples vary, so repeat the test with several apples and average the ratings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.b] Non-enzymic browning needs heat and does not involve enzymes. It has three processes, each involving a different food molecule: the Maillard reaction (protein with sugar), caramelisation (sugar alone) and dextrinisation (starch).
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.0] Maillard reaction: amino acids from protein react with reducing sugars such as glucose when food is heated in dry conditions, producing brown colour and savoury roasted flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.1] Maillard browning shows on roast meat and poultry skin, roast potatoes, toast and the crust of bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.2] Dry heat and a dry surface favour browning, so roasting, grilling, baking and frying brown food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.3] Little Maillard browning occurs in boiled, steamed or stewed food, because water holds the surface at about 100 °C, too cool for browning, so the food stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.4] Caramelisation: sugar (sucrose) melts and darkens under strong heat, forming caramel with a sweet, slightly bitter flavour. It needs no protein.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.5] Caramelisation examples: caramel sauce, crème caramel and toffee.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.6] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains called dextrins, which brown and give a crisp surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.4.7] Dextrinisation examples: toast and the crust of cakes and bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.b] Some nutrients dissolve in water or are destroyed by heat and air, so preparation and cooking can lower the nutritional value of food. Knowing why they are lost shows how to keep them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.0] Water-soluble vitamins, vitamin C and the B group, dissolve into cooking water and are lost when it is poured away.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.1] Vitamin C is also destroyed by heat and oxidation (exposure to air), so the longer vegetables are cooked and exposed to air, the more is lost.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.2] Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are more stable in cooking but drain away with the melted fat from grilled, roasted or fried food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.3] Soluble minerals such as potassium and some sugars dissolve into cooking water along with the water-soluble vitamins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.5.4] Cutting vegetables into small pieces increases the surface exposed to water and air, and soaking them before cooking increases loss. Cooking in a lot of water for a long time has the same effect.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.b] Most nutrient loss can be limited by handling vegetables sensibly before and during cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.0] Peeling thinly or cooking in the skin reduces loss, because nutrients are concentrated just beneath the skin. Peeling thickly increases loss.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.1] Holding cooked vegetables hot for long periods destroys further vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.2] Adding bicarbonate of soda to vegetables destroys vitamin C and thiamine, so avoid alkalis and copper pans.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.3] Steaming, stir-frying and microwaving use little water and short cooking times, so they conserve more nutrients. Preparing just before cooking, using a sharp knife, putting the lid on and serving soon after cooking also help.
[hom-cooking-methods-s1.6.4] Cooking liquid from vegetables can be used in soups, sauces or gravies to recover some nutrients.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s2: 2. Freezing
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.b] Freezing preserves food by lowering its temperature far enough to stop enzyme action and microbial growth, and by turning free water into ice that micro-organisms cannot use. It is popular because no high heat is needed, so food stays close to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.0] Low temperature slows enzyme reactions (enzymes are the natural chemicals in food that make it spoil or change) and stops micro-organisms from reproducing, but it does not kill most of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.1] Frozen food is not sterile: micro-organisms become dormant (inactive) and start growing again when the food thaws, so frozen food is never safe indefinitely.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.2] Turning free water into ice removes the water micro-organisms need, because ice is unavailable to them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.3] Pure water freezes at 0 °C, but food freezes below this because its dissolved sugars and salts lower its freezing point.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.4] Water expands by about 9 per cent when it freezes, which is why liquids need headspace (an empty gap at the top) in their containers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.5] Freezing itself does little harm to nutritive value, and colour, flavour and texture are kept better than with most other methods because no high heat is used, except in blanching; most nutrient losses happen during blanching and thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.b] The speed of freezing decides how big the ice crystals are, and crystal size decides how much damage the food suffers. Quick freezing makes small crystals; slow freezing makes large ones.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.0] Quick freezing takes place at about −25 °C (the 2013 Higher Level scheme gives −25 °C to −40 °C) in the fast-freeze section of a freezer, so the food passes through the freezing range rapidly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.1] Quick freezing forms small ice crystals within the cells, which causes little damage to the cell walls, so there is little drip (juice lost) on thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.2] For this reason quick freezing retains the texture, colour, flavour and nutritive value of food better than slow freezing does.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.3] Slow freezing takes place at a higher temperature than −25 °C, as in an ordinary freezer compartment, where ice forms gradually.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.4] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals, mostly between the cells, that pierce and rupture cell walls, allowing juices to escape.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.5] As a result slow-frozen food is limp and soft when thawed, and it loses colour and flavour and drip containing soluble vitamins B1 and C and minerals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.6] The fast-freeze setting should be switched on about 3 to 4 hours before food is placed in the freezer, so the freezer is cold enough to freeze the new food quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.7] Diagram: draw two cells side by side, one with small crystals inside an intact cell wall (quick) and one with large crystals and a ruptured wall (slow).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.b] Blanching is a brief heat treatment given to vegetables before freezing. Enzymes keep working slowly even at freezer temperatures, so vegetables would spoil in storage without it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.0] 1. The vegetables are plunged into boiling water for a short, set time that depends on the vegetable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.1] 2. They are plunged immediately into ice-cold water for the same length of time, which stops the cooking so the vegetables do not go soft.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.2] 3. They are drained and dried, because surface water left on them would form ice crystals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.3] Blanching inactivates enzymes that would otherwise cause loss of colour, flavour, texture and nutrients during frozen storage.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.4] Blanching also cleans the vegetable, destroys some micro-organisms on its surface, softens it slightly so it packs well, and keeps its bright colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.5] Blanching loses some water-soluble vitamins, particularly vitamin C and vitamin B1, into the water and through heat. Blanch small batches in plenty of fast-boiling water so the water returns to the boil quickly and the timing stays short and accurate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.6] Fruit is not blanched; soft fruit is open-frozen on trays or packed in sugar or sugar syrup. Some foods, such as soups, sauces and casseroles, are frozen cooked after cooling.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.b] Good results at home depend on following a set of steps, and each step has a reason. Most of them aim either to freeze the food quickly or to keep air and moisture away from it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.0] Choose fresh, best-quality food in perfect condition and freeze it soon after harvest or purchase, because freezing keeps quality but cannot improve it. Wash, peel, trim or cut as needed, blanch vegetables, and cool cooked food quickly before it goes into the freezer.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.1] Portion food into usable (family-sized) quantities so that only what is needed is thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.2] Open-freeze (tray-freeze) sticky or delicate items, such as strawberries or raspberries, or items to be kept separate, such as sausages and burgers: spread them in a single layer on a tray, freeze hard, then bag them so that they stay separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.3] Pack food in strong, moisture-proof and vapour-proof freezer bags, foil or rigid plastic containers, and remove as much air as possible so that the food does not dry out. Leave headspace in containers holding liquids to allow for expansion as the food freezes.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.4] Seal tightly and label with contents and date. Place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze compartment, as the freezer instructs, and keep them apart from already frozen food until frozen, so they freeze quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.5] Do not overload the freezer: freeze no more than about one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24 hours, because overloading raises the temperature and slows freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.6] Store frozen food at −18 °C or colder, using the oldest first. The safe storage time depends on the food: fatty foods and oily fish keep for a shorter period, and fatty foods stored too long can turn rancid.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.7] Foods that freeze well include peas, beans, carrots, broccoli, apples, strawberries, meat, fish, bread, cakes, soups, sauces and prepared meals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.8] Foods that freeze poorly include lettuce, whole tomatoes and other high-water salad foods, bananas, melons, pears, whole eggs in the shell, low-fat cream and mayonnaise-based foods, which go limp, watery or separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.b] Problems with frozen food usually come from air reaching it or from careless thawing. Both are preventable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.0] Freezer burn shows as dry, discoloured, tough patches where air has reached the surface of the food, caused by dehydration and oxidation, especially in protein foods such as meat and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.1] Airtight packaging with the air removed prevents freezer burn.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.2] Thaw food in a fridge, or cook it from frozen where the food allows, such as vegetables, so that the food stays below the danger zone while it thaws. Thaw meat and poultry fully before cooking unless the pack says otherwise.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.3] Thawed food should be used promptly and not refrozen as it is, because quality declines and micro-organisms may have multiplied. Raw food thawed in the fridge can be cooked and then frozen as a cooked dish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.4] In a freezer fault or power cut, keep the door closed and do not open it, because frozen food keeps for a time if the cold is held in.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.b] Commercial freezing is faster and colder than home freezing, so ice crystals are smaller and quality is higher. Food is frozen soon after harvesting or catching, often on the farm or on a factory ship. Home freezers are not as cold or as fast, so home freezing does not match these methods.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.0] Air blast freezing: a very cold air stream at about −30 °C is blown over food, often on a conveyor belt or through a tunnel; suitable for most foods, such as fruit, vegetables, meat and bakery products.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.1] Plate (contact) freezing: food is pressed between two hollow metal plates cooled by refrigerant; suitable for thin, flat foods such as fish fillets, flat pieces of meat and burgers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.2] Flow (fluidised bed) freezing: cold air is blown up through a perforated conveyor belt so that small pieces float and freeze individually, staying free-flowing and not sticking together; suitable for peas, sweetcorn and berries.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.3] Cryogenic freezing: food is exposed to liquid nitrogen or carbon dioxide at extremely low temperature, which freezes it almost instantly; suitable for delicate or high-value foods such as strawberries, prawns and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.4] The cold chain keeps frozen food at −18 °C or colder in cold stores, refrigerated lorries and supermarket freezer cabinets until it reaches the home.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.5] When buying frozen food, check that the pack is intact with no ice crystals or frost inside, that it is below the load line on the cabinet, and take it home quickly in a cool bag.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.b] Freezing helps a household plan meals, save money and cut waste. These benefits are the points examiners expect when they ask about frozen foods in meal planning.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.0] Freezing surplus food before it spoils reduces household waste, and leftovers can be frozen for reuse as ready-made portions.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.1] Bulk cooking and freezing portions saves time and fuel, since a large batch is cooked once.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.2] A freezer stock of meals and ingredients provides emergency food and convenience on busy days, including for households with different eating times.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.3] Buying in bulk or when produce is in season is more economical, and freezing keeps it for a longer period (a longer shelf life).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.4] Frozen foods provide a wide variety of vegetables, fish and fruit all year, including items not available fresh locally.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.5] Freezing retains colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value well compared with heat processing; frozen vegetables keep more vitamin C than canned ones, as one past-paper table shows for peas (25 mg fresh, 17 mg frozen, 8 mg canned).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.6] Fruit and salad foods go soft after thawing because of cell damage, so they suit cooking better than eating raw.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.7] Labelling each pack with contents, date and portion size lets older items be used first, which helps planned menus.
