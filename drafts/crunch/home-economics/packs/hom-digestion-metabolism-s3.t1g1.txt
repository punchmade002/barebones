TARGET hom-digestion-metabolism-s3: 3. Digestion and absorption of lipids
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.0.b] Fat is eaten mostly as triglycerides, which are too big to absorb. Digestion is hydrolysis, the splitting of a molecule using water, done by enzymes (specific biological catalysts that work at about 37 °C), and lipase is the enzyme that does this for fat.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.0.0] A triglyceride is one glycerol molecule bonded to three fatty acid chains by ester bonds, so there are three bonds for the enzyme to break.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.0.1] Lipase hydrolyses the ester bonds of triglycerides, splitting them into fatty acids and glycerol. Monoglycerides and diglycerides (glycerol with one or two fatty acids still attached) form as intermediate products on the way.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.0.2] Fats do not dissolve in water, so lipase can act only at the surface of a fat droplet, and a larger droplet surface area speeds digestion. This is why fat must be emulsified (broken into tiny droplets) first.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.0.3] Diagram: a triglyceride showing one glycerol backbone and three fatty acid chains, with the ester bonds labelled.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.1.b] Very little fat is digested before it reaches the small intestine, so the early stages of the canal matter mainly for how long fat stays in them.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.1.0] The mouth carries out little or no lipid digestion.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.1.1] Gastric lipase (lipase made in the stomach) hydrolyses only a small proportion of the triglycerides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.1.2] Fat slows the emptying of the stomach, so it stays there for longer than carbohydrate or protein, which is why a fatty meal keeps you feeling full.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.2.b] Bile is a digestive secretion that works on fat without being an enzyme. Knowing where it is made, stored and released helps you place it correctly in the sequence.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.2.0] The liver produces bile, which is stored and concentrated in the gall bladder and released through the bile duct into the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.2.1] Bile contains bile salts and bile pigments such as bilirubin, and it contains no enzymes, so it cannot chemically digest fat.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.2.2] Diagram: the liver, gall bladder, bile duct, pancreas, pancreatic duct and duodenum, with the flow of bile and pancreatic juice labelled.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.3.b] Bile does three jobs, and the most important is preparing fat for lipase. Emulsification is a physical change, not a chemical one.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.3.0] Bile salts emulsify fat, breaking large fat globules into very small droplets. An emulsion is tiny droplets of one liquid dispersed in another, and no bonds are broken, so this is not hydrolysis.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.3.1] Emulsification increases the surface area of the fat, so lipase can act on far more of it at once.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.3.2] Bile is alkaline, so it neutralises the acid chyme (the partly digested food) arriving from the stomach and creates a suitable pH for the pancreatic and intestinal enzymes to work.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.3.3] Bile also carries waste products such as bilirubin out of the body.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.4.b] Most fat is digested in the small intestine, where lipase from two sources meets fat that bile has already emulsified.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.4.0] Pancreas (acting in the duodenum): it secretes pancreatic juice containing lipase. The substrate is triglycerides in the emulsified droplets, and the products are fatty acids and glycerol.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.4.1] Small intestine: its wall secretes intestinal juice containing lipase, which acts on the same substrate, triglycerides, to give fatty acids and glycerol.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.4.2] Liver and gall bladder: they supply bile, which has no enzyme and emulsifies fat into droplets.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.4.3] Lipase hydrolyses triglycerides into fatty acids and glycerol, with some monoglycerides and diglycerides as intermediate products.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.b] The digested fat is absorbed through the villi, the finger-like projections of the small intestine wall. Each villus has a capillary network and a lacteal (a lymph capillary), and the route a product takes depends on its size.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.0] Short-chain fatty acids and glycerol are small and water-soluble, so they pass directly into the capillaries of the villi and do not need to be rebuilt. The hepatic portal vein carries them to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.1] Long-chain fatty acids and monoglycerides diffuse into the cells lining the villi, where they are rebuilt into triglycerides. Fat is therefore not absorbed as whole triglycerides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.2] The triglycerides are coated with protein (and other lipids) to form chylomicrons, which are too large to enter the capillaries.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.3] Chylomicrons pass into the lacteals, the lymph capillaries at the centre of each villus.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.4] Lymph carries the chylomicrons through the lymphatic system into the thoracic duct, which empties into the blood near the heart. The fat reaches the general circulation without first passing through the liver; the liver later removes what is left of the chylomicrons from the blood.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.5.5] Diagram: a villus cross-section showing the capillary network, the lacteal and the two routes.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.b] Tracing a fat molecule from the mouth to the blood brings the stages together in order.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.0] 1. Mouth and stomach: little or no lipid digestion takes place.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.1] 2. Duodenum: bile emulsifies the fat and pancreatic lipase hydrolyses it.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.2] 3. Villi: the products are absorbed. Long-chain fat enters the lacteals, while short-chain products enter the capillaries.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.3] 4. Lymphatic system: lymph carries the fat from the lacteals into the thoracic duct and then into the bloodstream.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.4] 5. Hepatic portal vein: carries the short-chain fatty acids and glycerol, absorbed directly into the capillaries, to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.5] 6. General circulation: the blood distributes fat around the body.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.6.6] Diagram: the digestive system, labelled mouth, stomach, liver, gall bladder, pancreas, small intestine and hepatic portal vein.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.b] Once absorbed, fat is moved around the body, broken down again at the tissues and either used for energy or stored.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.0] Lipids travel in the blood as lipoproteins (lipids packed with protein): chylomicrons carry dietary fat, and very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) carry fat made in the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.1] Lipoprotein lipase, an enzyme at the body tissues, hydrolyses the triglycerides in lipoproteins into fatty acids and glycerol, which the cells take up.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.2] Muscle cells oxidise fatty acids in respiration to release energy. Fat is the most concentrated energy source, giving 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.3] Glycerol is converted into glucose in the liver or enters glycolysis (the first stage of respiration).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.4] Surplus fatty acids are rebuilt into triglycerides and stored in adipose (fat) tissue under the skin and around organs, which gives insulation, protection of organs and an energy reserve.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s3.7.5] The liver converts surplus carbohydrate (and protein) into fatty acids and triglycerides, which it exports as VLDL.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s2: 2. Triglycerides and the classification of lipids
[hom-lipids-s2.0.b] Lipids are the fats and oils in our food. Most of the lipid we eat is in one form, the triglyceride, so knowing what a triglyceride is made of explains most of this topic.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.0] Fats are solid at room temperature and oils are liquid, but both are lipids. Most dietary lipid is in the form of triglycerides.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.1] Fats and oils (triglycerides) are made of carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) only. They contain no nitrogen, so, unlike protein, they are not a source of nitrogen.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.2] Lipids are made up of fatty acids and glycerol, which join together to form the triglyceride.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.3] Other lipids exist, such as phospholipids like lecithin and sterols like cholesterol, but triglycerides are the lipids studied here.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.4] In digestion the enzyme lipase, made in the pancreas and carried in pancreatic juice, breaks triglycerides down into fatty acids and glycerol.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.b] Most fats and oils in food are triglycerides: one glycerol molecule combined with three fatty acid molecules. The name tells you this, because tri means three.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.0] A triglyceride contains only carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) atoms, with no nitrogen.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.1] Glycerol is an alcohol, with the formula C₃H₈O₃. It carries three hydroxyl (–OH) groups, one on each of its three carbon atoms, which gives three places for a fatty acid to attach.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.2] The three fatty acids in one triglyceride can be the same or different, and may be saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated. This mixture decides whether the fat is solid or liquid.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.3] The carboxyl group of each fatty acid joins to one hydroxyl group of glycerol, forming an ester bond (–COO–).
[hom-lipids-s2.2.b] Each fatty acid is joined to glycerol by the same reaction, repeated three times. The result is a glycerol backbone with a fatty acid chain hanging from each of its carbons.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.0] Each ester bond forms by condensation, a reaction in which two molecules join and one molecule of water (H₂O) is eliminated.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.1] Three water molecules are removed when one glycerol and three fatty acids combine to form one triglyceride, because three ester bonds are made.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.2] A triglyceride is therefore a glycerol backbone with three fatty acid chains hanging from it.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.3] Digestion runs this in reverse: lipase breaks the ester bonds by adding water back, which is hydrolysis, releasing the fatty acids and glycerol.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.4] Diagram: a glycerol backbone with three fatty acid chains attached by ester (–COO–) links, labelling glycerol, the fatty acid chains and the ester bonds, and marking the three water molecules released during formation.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.b] One way to sort lipids is by where they come from. There are three classes: animal, vegetable (plant) and marine.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.0] Animal fats come from land animals, for example butter, lard, suet, dripping, cheese, egg yolk and the fat in meat.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.1] Vegetable (plant) oils come from seeds, nuts and fruits, for example olive, sunflower, corn, soya and rapeseed oils, and avocado oil.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.2] Marine lipids come from fish and other sea creatures, for example the oil in oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout, and fish-liver oils such as cod-liver oil.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.3] Palm oil and coconut oil come from plants but are high in saturated fat, so they behave like animal fats in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.b] The second way to sort lipids is by the type of fatty acid they contain. Every fat and oil is a mixture, so the class depends on which type is present in the largest proportion.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.0] A lipid is classed as saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated according to which type of fatty acid makes up the largest proportion of its fatty acids.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.1] Olive oil is classed as monounsaturated because most of its fatty acids have one double bond. Rapeseed oil and avocado are also mainly monounsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.2] Sunflower, corn and soya oils are classed as polyunsaturated because most of their fatty acids have more than one double bond. The oils of oily fish are also mainly polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.3] Butter, lard, suet, dripping, meat fat, cheese, coconut oil and palm oil are classed as saturated because saturated fatty acids form the largest proportion of their fatty acids.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.4] Most fats contain a mixture of all three fatty acid types, so classification depends on the predominant type. Butter has some monounsaturated fat and olive oil has some saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.5] The fatty acid type explains the state: a lipid that is mostly saturated is a fat, solid at room temperature, while one that is mostly unsaturated is an oil.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s1: 1. Fatty acids
[hom-lipids-s1.0.b] Fatty acids are the building blocks of fats and oils (lipids). They are sorted into three classes by the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain, and that count decides how the fat behaves.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.0] A fatty acid is a long chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. It has a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end and a methyl group (–CH₃) at the other.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.1] Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds between carbon atoms, so every carbon carries its full quota of hydrogen atoms. Saturated means saturated with hydrogen.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.2] Monounsaturated fatty acids have one double bond (C=C) in the carbon chain, so two neighbouring carbons each hold one hydrogen fewer.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.3] Polyunsaturated fatty acids have two or more double bonds in the carbon chain.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.4] Saturated fats are generally solid at room temperature, while fats rich in monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids are generally liquid oils. Coconut oil and palm oil are plant oils that are rich in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.5] Examples: stearic acid (or butyric acid) is saturated, oleic acid is monounsaturated, and linoleic acid (or linolenic acid) is polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.b] The shape of the carbon chain explains why some fats are solid and others are liquid. Higher Level questions ask you to describe and draw each type.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.0] Saturated fatty acids contain only single C–C bonds and are fully saturated with hydrogen. The chain is straight and flexible, so the chains pack tightly together and the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.1] Each cis C=C double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid puts a kink (bend) in the chain, so the molecules pack less tightly and the fat stays liquid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.2] A monounsaturated fatty acid has one double bond and so one kink. A polyunsaturated fatty acid has two or more double bonds, so it has one kink for each cis double bond and is liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.3] Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, with the first double bond between the third and fourth carbon atoms counted from the methyl (omega) end. Omega-6 fatty acids have their first double bond at the sixth carbon from the methyl end.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.4] Diagram: draw a zig-zag or a simple row of carbons for a saturated, a monounsaturated and a polyunsaturated fatty acid. Label the –COOH (carboxyl) end, the –CH₃ (methyl) end and mark each C=C double bond.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.b] A double bond can hold its two hydrogen atoms in two different arrangements. The arrangement changes the shape of the chain and how the fat acts in the body.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.0] Cis and trans describe the hydrogen atoms at one double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid, not how many double bonds there are.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.1] In a cis fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on the same side of the carbon chain (both above or both below), so the chain bends.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.2] In a trans fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight, like a saturated fatty acid.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.3] Most naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids are cis, for example those in plant oils such as cooking oils and in oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.4] Trans fatty acids form when oils are partially hydrogenated in food manufacture, so they are found in some processed foods such as biscuits, pastries and fried takeaway foods. Small natural amounts occur in the fat of ruminant animals, in dairy fat and in beef and lamb fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.b] Most fatty acids can be made by the body, but a few cannot. These essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated and must come from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.0] Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that the body cannot manufacture (or cannot make in enough quantity), so they must be obtained from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.1] Linoleic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) and linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid) are the two essential fatty acids most often named.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.2] Arachidonic acid is also counted as essential by examiners. Strictly the body forms it only from linoleic acid, when enough linoleic acid is eaten.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.3] Essential fatty acids are needed for growth, for the structure of cell membranes, for the nervous system and brain, and for the immune response.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.4] The body uses essential fatty acids to make prostaglandins, hormone-like compounds that regulate blood clotting, blood pressure and inflammation.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.5] Good sources include vegetable oils such as sunflower, corn, soya and rapeseed oil, nuts and seeds, and oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.b] Omega-3 fatty acids are a family of essential polyunsaturated fats with several health benefits. They come from a limited range of foods, so it helps to know the sources by name.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.0] Omega-3 fatty acids help reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, circulatory disease and the formation of blood clots, and they can help raise HDL cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.1] Omega-3 fatty acids are linked with healthy brain activity and reduced inflammation, and they may reduce the risk of certain cancers.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.2] Oily fish are the main animal source: salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.3] Plant sources include walnuts, flaxseed (linseed), soya beans and rapeseed (canola) oil.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.4] Some eggs and milk are enriched with omega-3 by changing the diet of the hens or cows, and fish-oil supplements are also accepted as a source.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.b] The type of fatty acid in the diet affects the amount of cholesterol carried in the blood. This is why the balance of saturated, unsaturated and trans fats matters for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.0] Cholesterol travels in the blood bound to proteins, forming lipoproteins (carriers of cholesterol). LDL and HDL are these carriers, not types of cholesterol itself.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.1] LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol through the blood to the tissues and can deposit it on artery walls, where it narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis) and raises the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.2] HDL (high-density lipoprotein) removes cholesterol from the circulation, carrying it away from the tissues to the liver for removal.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.3] Saturated fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.4] Unsaturated cis fatty acids, both monounsaturated and polyunsaturated, reduce LDL cholesterol and help to raise HDL cholesterol, so they are generally good for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.5] Trans fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of CHD. They are thought to do more harm than saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.6] Monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats can lower total cholesterol when they replace saturated fats in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.7] Essential fatty acids must be eaten because the body cannot make them, and omega-3 fatty acids add the heart and brain benefits.
