TARGET hom-education-s4: 4. Equality of opportunity in education
[hom-education-s4.0.b] Equality of opportunity means every student has a fair chance to access, take part in and benefit from education, whatever their background. It is about the chance, not about guaranteeing that everyone ends up with the same result.
[hom-education-s4.0.0] Equal access means every student can enrol and attend; equal outcomes means students finish with similar results. The two are different, because equal access does not automatically lead to equal outcomes.
[hom-education-s4.0.1] Every person should have a fair chance to enter, take part in and progress through education, whatever their gender, family background, income, ability, disability, ethnicity or where they live.
[hom-education-s4.0.2] Students begin school from different starting points, so equal treatment alone may not give equal opportunity.
[hom-education-s4.0.3] Equity means giving extra support in proportion to need, so that students at a disadvantage can reach the same level of achievement as others.
[hom-education-s4.0.4] Free education helps: the State funds primary and second-level education, and the Free Fees Initiative pays third-level tuition for eligible undergraduates. Even so, some groups still have lower participation and results, so the State adds targeted supports.
[hom-education-s4.0.5] Barriers to equality of opportunity include gender, socio-economic status, disability, family background and location.
[hom-education-s4.0.6] The Equal Status Acts 2000 to 2018 prohibit discrimination in education, in schools, colleges and access to courses, on grounds that include gender, civil status, family status, age, disability, race, religion, sexual orientation and membership of the Traveller community. There is an exemption for single-sex schools.
[hom-education-s4.0.7] The Education (Admission to Schools) Act 2018 bars schools from using religion as a selection criterion when they are oversubscribed (the minority-faith-school exception remains). The Education Act 1998 sets out equality of access as a goal of the system.
[hom-education-s4.1.b] Gender inequity in education has changed direction over time. Girls once had fewer opportunities, but today the inequity lies mainly in the subjects students choose and the careers that follow.
[hom-education-s4.1.0] In the past girls had fewer opportunities, for example fewer girls stayed on to the Leaving Certificate or went to third level. Today girls' participation and attainment at second and third level are, on average, higher than boys'.
[hom-education-s4.1.1] Boys are more likely than girls to leave school early, to have lower literacy levels at second level and to achieve lower grades on average. Reasons include peer pressure against being seen to work hard, lower expectations and less engagement with some subjects and teaching methods.
[hom-education-s4.1.2] Subject choice can be gendered: fewer girls than boys take subjects such as Physics, Engineering, Construction Studies, Technology and Design and Communication Graphics, and fewer boys take subjects such as Home Economics, Languages and Art.
[hom-education-s4.1.3] Single-sex schools can limit the subjects on offer, for example a girls' school without technical subjects or a boys' school without Home Economics, so students may not get the chance to choose them.
[hom-education-s4.1.4] Subject choice affects later course and career choices, which is why there are fewer women in engineering and IT and fewer men in caring and teaching roles at primary level.
[hom-education-s4.1.5] Teacher and parent expectations can differ by gender, and stereotypes held by parents, teachers and peers, textbooks, career guidance and a lack of role models can influence the subjects and courses students consider.
[hom-education-s4.1.6] Schools can widen opportunity by encouraging students to choose subjects on the basis of ability and interest rather than gender stereotypes.
[hom-education-s4.1.7] Other responses include less stereotyped textbooks and resources, mixed-gender access to subjects, encouragement into non-traditional subjects (for example promoting STEM for girls), gender balance in career guidance and equality legislation.
[hom-education-s4.2.b] Socio-economic status (SES) is a family's position based on income, occupation and education, also called social class. It shapes a student's chances because it affects what a family can pay for, what it expects and what support it can give.
[hom-education-s4.2.0] Students from higher groups (professional, managerial) are more likely to complete second level and go on to third level. Students from lower socio-economic groups are more likely to leave school early and less likely to progress to third level, so they are under-represented at third level.
[hom-education-s4.2.1] School costs such as books, equipment, uniforms, trips, subject charges, school bags and technology, transport and, at third level, accommodation and living costs can exclude low-income families.
[hom-education-s4.2.2] Partial relief comes from the back-to-school clothing and footwear allowance, book rental schemes and grants, school meals and school transport schemes, free fees and SUSI grants, and the Back to Education Allowance with its Cost of Education Allowance add-on for participants with dependent children.
[hom-education-s4.2.3] These supports reduce costs, but they do not remove the wider gap in resources between families.
[hom-education-s4.2.4] Higher-status families usually offer higher expectations and aspiration, more help with homework and course choices, a quiet place to study, grinds and extra-curricular activities, and social contacts and knowledge of the system, so their children start with advantages.
[hom-education-s4.2.5] Parents with higher levels of education are more likely to have high expectations for their children and to be able to help with schoolwork. Lower-status families may face a cycle of poverty and low expectations, poor housing or crowding, and pressure to earn.
[hom-education-s4.2.6] School factors add to this. Some schools use streaming (grouping students by ability), and students in lower-status groups may be placed in lower streams or sets, which can limit the subject level, grades and courses open to them.
[hom-education-s4.2.7] Teacher expectations and the locality of a school (a disadvantaged area, with fewer resources and more early leaving) can reinforce the effects of socio-economic status.
[hom-education-s4.2.8] Responses include DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools), which gives extra support to schools in disadvantaged communities, HEAR, which gives reduced-points third-level entry to students from disadvantaged backgrounds, and the financial supports above.
[hom-education-s4.3.b] A disadvantaged student is one at risk of low achievement because of several causes working together, not just low income. Knowing who is at risk, what holds them back and what helps lets you answer both the short and the long questions.
[hom-education-s4.3.0] Groups at risk of educational disadvantage include students from low-income and unemployed families, Travellers, Roma, students from migrant, asylum-seeking or refugee families, students with disabilities or special educational needs, homeless young people, young people in care, those in disadvantaged areas and lone-parent households.
[hom-education-s4.3.1] Students from migrant families may need language support in English or Irish (English as an additional language), and their qualifications from other countries may not be recognised.
[hom-education-s4.3.2] Students living in disadvantaged areas may face poor housing, poor health, lack of study space and limited access to after-school activities, which makes it harder to study and take part.
[hom-education-s4.3.3] Other barriers are poverty, discrimination, low expectations, lack of role models and, in some communities, negative experiences of school. These lead to low attendance and early leaving.
[hom-education-s4.3.4] The DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) programme identifies schools serving disadvantaged communities and gives them extra staffing and supports. These include the school completion programme, homework clubs, mentoring, school meals, home school community liaison and enhanced book grants.
[hom-education-s4.3.5] Other responses to name are language support, learning support and special needs assistants (SNAs), Traveller and Roma education supports, grants and allowances, school meals and transport, and the Home School Community Liaison scheme.
[hom-education-s4.4.b] Leaving school early closes doors that are hard to reopen. Knowing who is at risk, why students leave and what follows explains why the State invests in keeping them in school.
[hom-education-s4.4.0] An early school leaver is a young person who leaves school before completing senior cycle, that is before the Leaving Certificate or an equivalent programme.
[hom-education-s4.4.1] Early school leaving is more likely among students with poor attendance and truancy, low literacy and numeracy, or learning difficulties, because they fall behind and lose interest.
[hom-education-s4.4.2] Bullying, family problems, poverty and the cost of staying on, and negative peer pressure all increase the risk of leaving school early.
[hom-education-s4.4.3] Other causes are a cycle of poverty and low parental expectation, living in a disadvantaged area, dislike of school or a lack of suitable programmes, the pull of jobs, pregnancy, and health or addiction problems.
[hom-education-s4.4.4] Leaving school early means few or no qualifications, which limits access to further and third-level education and training and to skilled employment.
[hom-education-s4.4.5] Later in life early school leaving is linked with unemployment or low-paid unskilled work, welfare dependence, poverty, low self-esteem, poor health, social exclusion and a higher risk of crime.
[hom-education-s4.4.6] The effects spread: the next generation is also affected through the cycle of poverty, and the State loses skills and pays more in welfare.
[hom-education-s4.4.7] Responses include the School Completion Programme, the Home School Community Liaison scheme, DEIS, the Educational Welfare Service of Tusla (which deals with school attendance), JCSP and LCA, Youthreach, the Back to Education Allowance and VTOS.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s4: 4. Social institutions
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.b] Every society has basic needs, such as raising children, passing on knowledge and organising work. A social institution is the lasting, organised way a society meets one of them, which is why institutions matter for understanding family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.0] A social institution is an established, organised set of norms (accepted rules of behaviour), roles and values built around a basic need of society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.1] The five main social institutions are the family, education, religion, the economy (work) and government (the State). Health and the media are sometimes added to this list.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.2] An institution is not a building or a single organisation. A school is an organisation within the institution of education, so the school down the road is part of the institution, not the institution itself.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.3] Institutions last across generations, while individual groups come and go. Because they carry norms and values, pass on culture and give structure to social life, each one is an agent of socialisation (a means by which people learn how to behave in society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.4] Institutions are linked, so what happens in one affects the others. For example, the economy affects family life, and education prepares people for work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.5] Functionalism is the view that each institution performs functions that help keep society stable. This view describes what institutions do; it does not label any institution good or bad.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.6] A manifest function is an intended and recognised result of an institution, for example the school's function of educating young people.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.7] A latent function is an unintended and often hidden result, for example a school providing supervised care for children while parents are at work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.b] The family is the first and most basic institution, because it is where every person's care and learning begins. Over time other institutions take over or add to some of its work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.0] The family reproduces the population and provides for the care of children, older people and dependants (people who rely on others for support).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.1] The family socialises children, passing on language, norms, values and skills, so that children learn how to take part in society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.2] The family is the basic economic unit of consumption (using goods and services) and provides economic support through shared income and household work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.3] The family provides emotional security and a sense of belonging and identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.4] Other institutions adopt or supplement family functions. Schools take on much of children's education, the State provides welfare and health services, employers provide income, and religious bodies provide moral guidance.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.5] Examples of supplementing a family function: parenting courses support the emotional function, schooling from preschool to third level supports the educational function, welfare payments support the economic function, and medical services support the physical function.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.b] Education is how a society deliberately passes on what it knows, and it also shapes children's behaviour and future work. It works both in the classroom and in the everyday life of the school.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.0] Education transmits knowledge, skills and the culture of a society from one generation to the next. It does this formally through the curriculum (the subjects taught), from primary to third level.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.1] It also teaches informally through the hidden curriculum, the unwritten lessons of school life such as rules, punctuality, respect for authority, sharing and teamwork.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.2] Education socialises children, reinforcing the behaviour and values learned at home. It promotes social cohesion by teaching shared history, language and civic values, such as the Irish language and Irish history in Irish schools.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.3] Education prepares people for work through literacy and numeracy, qualifications, specialist skills and career guidance. Work experience comes through Transition Year, the Leaving Certificate Applied, the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme and college.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.4] Education sorts and allocates people to positions in the economy by means of qualifications. It can support social mobility (moving to a higher social position) by opening access to skilled and professional occupations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.5] In Ireland, education runs through primary, post-primary, further and higher education. The Leaving Certificate is the state examination at the end of second level, and most students use it for entry to third-level courses.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.b] Education is meant to give everyone a fair chance, but a family's circumstances can affect how far a child gets. Supports exist to narrow that gap.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.0] Socio-economic status (a family's income, occupation and social position) affects chances in education. Higher-status families often have more educational opportunities and go on to third level more often.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.1] Costs of books, uniforms and equipment can exclude low-income families, because they cannot afford what a child needs to take part fully.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.2] Grants, book rental schemes and allowances help families meet these costs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.3] DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) supports aim to reduce the gap for pupils at risk of being left behind.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.b] Religion is the institution that answers questions about meaning and right and wrong, and it gives people a community to belong to. Describe it neutrally: Ireland has many faiths and people of no religion.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.0] Religion provides a system of beliefs and values that gives meaning to life and explains events that cannot be explained by science alone.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.1] Religion provides moral codes that guide behaviour and reinforce many of a society's norms and values, for example rules against stealing or killing.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.2] Religious rituals mark key stages of life, such as birth, first communion or confirmation, marriage and death.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.3] Religious institutions provide community networks and social support to their members, so people feel they belong.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.4] Churches and other religious bodies have supported education, health care and charity. In Ireland many primary schools still have a religious patron.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.5] The influence of religion on Irish society has changed, with declining influence of the Church, more civil ceremonies and a more secular society (one not guided by religion).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.b] The economy is the institution that decides how a society produces and shares what people need. Work is how most families connect to it, which is why economic change reaches family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.0] The economic institution organises the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.1] Employment is the main way the economy allocates status and income to individuals and families, and income decides a family's standard of living.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.2] Economic institutions include businesses, banks, markets and the systems of trade through which goods move between producers and consumers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.3] Work is where people take on adult roles and learn workplace norms (the accepted behaviour at work).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.4] Economic institutions shape family life, for example by determining whether one or both parents must work outside the home, and so hours of work and childcare needs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.5] Economic changes affect family income and roles. Unemployment lowers living standards and strains relationships, while growth of service industries creates jobs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.6] Families are consumers, buying goods and services with the income that work provides.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.b] Government is the institution with the power to make rules for everyone and to provide services that individuals cannot organise alone. Its structure spreads that power so no single body holds all of it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.0] The political institution, the State, maintains order, makes laws, collects taxes and provides public services, including social welfare, health, education, housing and protection of the vulnerable.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.1] Government is typically divided into three branches: the legislature, which makes laws; the executive, which carries them out; and the judiciary (the courts), which interprets and applies them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.2] This division is called the separation of powers, and it prevents any one branch from holding unchecked authority.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.3] In Ireland the Oireachtas, the national parliament, consists of the President, Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann. The Government is the executive, and the courts are the judiciary.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.4] Local authorities provide local services such as housing, planning and libraries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.5] Citizens take part in government by voting in elections and referendums, electing representatives to the Dáil in general elections.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.6] The State supports the family in each function: welfare payments for the economic function, health services for the physical function, and schooling for the educational function.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.7] Child protection is part of this support. The State's agency for children is Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, which has exercised the Child Care Act 1991 functions since 2014.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.b] Health services and the media are often added to the five main institutions because they also shape everyday life. Treat them as additions, since the list of institutions is not fixed.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.0] The health institution promotes health and provides care and treatment for illness and injury.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.1] In Ireland the Health Service Executive (HSE) provides public health care.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.2] The media, including newspapers, radio, television and online platforms, inform the public and entertain it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.3] The media also shape public opinion and attitudes and transmit norms and values, which makes them both an institution and an agent of socialisation.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.4] The legal system (courts and law) is part of government, so it needs no separate institution.

CANDIDATES hom-education-s2: 2. Factors that influence educational achievement
[hom-education-s2.0.b] Achievement is the result of a student's ability combined with the effort, attitude and health they bring to learning. Ability is only one factor among several, so two students of equal ability can get very different results.
[hom-education-s2.0.0] Intellectual ability (intelligence) affects how quickly a student learns and the level at which they can perform, but ability alone does not decide results, because work and support matter too.
[hom-education-s2.0.1] Learning difficulties, such as dyslexia (a difficulty with reading and spelling), can lower achievement if the student is not supported.
[hom-education-s2.0.2] Work ethic, meaning commitment to homework, revision, attendance and class participation, has a direct effect on results because learning builds up through regular practice.
[hom-education-s2.0.3] Attitude and motivation matter: a student who values learning and sets personal goals tends to achieve more, while one who sees school as pointless will often disengage.
[hom-education-s2.0.4] Poor health, including illness, tiredness, lack of sleep and poor nutrition, reduces concentration and attendance, so the student misses or fails to take in lessons. Mental health and stress matter too.
[hom-education-s2.0.5] Choosing subjects and a level that match a student's interests and abilities improves engagement and results. At third level, choosing the wrong course can lead to poor results or dropping out.
[hom-education-s2.0.6] Part-time work during term time (at second and third level) reduces study time and can leave a student tired in class.
[hom-education-s2.1.b] A student's family shapes their expectations, their resources and the conditions in which they study. Some of these influences come from the family's attitudes, others from money and circumstances.
[hom-education-s2.1.0] Parental attitude: parents who value education, take an interest in homework and school, and have high expectations tend to raise their child's achievement.
[hom-education-s2.1.1] Parents' own level of education shapes the expectations they hold for their children, their understanding of the school system and how much help they can give with schoolwork and choices.
[hom-education-s2.1.2] Family size: in larger families, parents' time, attention and money for each child are often more limited, and older children may have caring duties.
[hom-education-s2.1.3] Family finances and socio-economic background affect access to books, uniform, a computer, extra tuition, school trips and activities. They can also decide whether a student must work part-time or leave school early.
[hom-education-s2.1.4] Home environment: a quiet place to study, books, a computer and internet access, a routine for homework and the absence of overcrowding all support homework and revision. Without them, study is difficult.
[hom-education-s2.1.5] Family circumstances such as separation, bereavement, illness, addiction in the home, conflict or unemployment cause stress that can lower concentration and attendance.
[hom-education-s2.1.6] A cycle of poverty and low expectations can pass down through generations, because children who see little value in education may carry the same expectations into their own lives.
[hom-education-s2.2.b] Students spend much of the day with people their own age, so the peer group (the friends and classmates a student mixes with) has a strong influence on how they behave in school.
[hom-education-s2.2.0] Positive peers encourage effort and study, because students tend to copy the behaviour of the group they belong to.
[hom-education-s2.2.1] Negative peer pressure encourages truancy (staying away from school without permission), disruption and early leaving, so a student can disengage in order to fit in.
[hom-education-s2.2.2] Bullying damages self-confidence and can cause a student to miss school or stop engaging with learning.
[hom-education-s2.3.b] The school a student attends affects what they can learn and how they feel about learning. Both the atmosphere of the school and the resources it has to work with count.
[hom-education-s2.3.0] School attended and environment: the school's ethos (its values and outlook), discipline, atmosphere and expectations of its pupils, together with the range of subjects offered and whether it is single-sex or mixed. A supportive, safe environment helps achievement.
[hom-education-s2.3.1] Teacher expectations shape pupils' attainment, because low expectations of a student can limit the goals they set themselves. Teacher quality also affects how well students learn.
[hom-education-s2.3.2] School resources, including class size, access to technology and equipment, laboratories, libraries and a guidance counsellor, affect what students can learn.
[hom-education-s2.3.3] Supports such as learning support teachers, special needs assistants (SNAs) and exam accommodations help pupils with special needs to achieve.
[hom-education-s2.4.b] Where a school is, how often a student attends, and the supports on offer all affect results. Poor attendance in particular leaves gaps that are hard to close.
[hom-education-s2.4.0] The locality of a school matters: schools in socially disadvantaged areas often face higher rates of early school leaving, fewer after-school facilities and less access to resources.
[hom-education-s2.4.1] Students may also live far from school or lack transport, which can hurt attendance. Good local amenities, libraries and community supports help.
[hom-education-s2.4.2] Poor school attendance leads to gaps in learning and lower attainment, and it is linked with early school leaving.
[hom-education-s2.4.3] Programmes and supports can raise achievement for students who might otherwise drop out: Transition Year, LCA, LCVP, JCSP, work experience, access routes to third level (such as HEAR and DARE) and exam accommodations (RACE).
[hom-education-s2.5.b] Many of the same factors apply to students in college, though the details change because students are older and often live away from home.
[hom-education-s2.5.0] Correct choice of course matters, because the wrong course can lead to poor results or dropping out.
[hom-education-s2.5.1] Cost of fees, accommodation and travel, together with family situation, can limit what a student can afford, so working part-time may be needed, which cuts study time.
[hom-education-s2.5.2] Home and college environment affect study: a student needs a suitable place to work and a college that supports them.
[hom-education-s2.5.3] Positive or negative peers influence a student's effort, and parental attitude still affects their motivation.
[hom-education-s2.5.4] Locality and distance from home affect travel time and cost, and college supports help students who are struggling.
[hom-education-s2.5.5] Ability and work ethic remain key, because college students must study independently.

CANDIDATES hom-education-s3: 3. The provision of education in Ireland
[hom-education-s3.0.b] Pre-school (early childhood) education is provided by private, community and voluntary services before a child starts primary school. It is not compulsory, but it gives children a head start, so the State funds some of it.
[hom-education-s3.0.0] The Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) scheme provides a free pre-school year to children before they enter primary school, delivered through approved providers. It is a government scheme, so it costs families nothing.
[hom-education-s3.0.1] Early Start is a one-year preventative pre-school programme for three- and four-year-olds, run in selected primary schools in disadvantaged areas. It aims to give children a positive start and prevent later school failure.
[hom-education-s3.0.2] Early Start is staffed by primary teachers working with a child care assistant, so the programme is led by trained teachers.
[hom-education-s3.0.3] The Access and Inclusion Model (AIM) supports children with disabilities to take part in the free pre-school programme, so they are not left out.
[hom-education-s3.0.4] Naíonraí are pre-schools where children learn through the Irish language.
[hom-education-s3.0.5] Montessori schools, which are generally privately run, use trained teachers and encourage children to learn through play at their own pace.
[hom-education-s3.0.6] Playschools give children of a similar age the opportunity to play together and learn to share, take turns and follow group rules.
[hom-education-s3.0.7] Pre-school builds language, social skills and the routines a child needs for the transition to primary school, so children settle in more easily when they start.
[hom-education-s3.1.b] Primary school is the first stage of compulsory education. Exams ask for the ages involved, the types of school to choose from and the alternatives open to parents.
[hom-education-s3.1.0] Children may enrol in primary school from age four and must begin by age six, because school is compulsory from six.
[hom-education-s3.1.1] Primary education is an eight-year cycle, from junior infants to sixth class, and most pupils finish between the ages of 12 and 14.
[hom-education-s3.1.2] Primary schools are state-funded or private. A state-funded school is paid for by the State but run by its patron (the religious body, Educate Together or other group that runs it), so it is not state-run and not private.
[hom-education-s3.1.3] State-funded schools may be religious, multidenominational or non-denominational. Educate Together is an example of a non-denominational school, and it is not a private school.
[hom-education-s3.1.4] Specialist types include Educate Together schools, Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools, where subjects are taught through Irish), Montessori schools and single-sex schools.
[hom-education-s3.1.5] Parents may educate a child at home instead of enrolling them in a school. Home-educated children are registered with Tusla.
[hom-education-s3.2.b] Second-level education follows primary school and is made up of several programmes. Each has a set length, a particular kind of curriculum and its own form of assessment, which is how exams ask you to describe them.
[hom-education-s3.2.0] Junior Cycle is a three-year programme that uses classroom-based assessments and ends with a state examination at the end of third year, marked by the State Examinations Commission. Its award is the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement, which replaced the old Junior Certificate.
[hom-education-s3.2.1] Transition Year is an optional year between Junior Cycle and Leaving Certificate with no state examination. It focuses on personal development, work experience and self-directed learning (students taking charge of their own learning).
[hom-education-s3.2.2] The Leaving Certificate Programme is a two-year programme that ends with state examinations at the end of sixth year.
[hom-education-s3.2.3] Leaving Certificate subjects are offered at higher and ordinary level, and foundation level exists only in Irish and mathematics. Grades are converted to points for third-level entry.
[hom-education-s3.2.4] Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA) is a two-year modular programme (made up of separate units) with practical, written and oral assessment. It suits students who prefer practical learning and can lead to Post Leaving Certificate courses.
[hom-education-s3.2.5] The Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP) has two link modules: Preparation for the World of Work and Enterprise Education. Work experience is part of Preparation for the World of Work.
[hom-education-s3.3.b] Work experience is a placement in a workplace. It lets a student try out working life while still at school, and the benefits are a common exam question.
[hom-education-s3.3.0] Work experience is part of the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (inside the Preparation for the World of Work link module) and is a standard element of Transition Year.
[hom-education-s3.3.1] A placement lets students test their career preferences and see what a job actually involves, so they can make a better choice about their future.
[hom-education-s3.3.2] Students learn workplace co-operation and how working life differs from school.
[hom-education-s3.3.3] Work experience develops communication, teamwork, planning, organisation, punctuality and problem solving.
[hom-education-s3.3.4] Students learn the expectations of a workplace, including timekeeping, appearance and health and safety.
[hom-education-s3.3.5] Completing a placement builds confidence and responsibility, and gives the student a work record for a curriculum vitae.
[hom-education-s3.4.b] Third level is education after second level. It includes degree courses in colleges and further education courses, and students need a way in and a way to pay.
[hom-education-s3.4.0] Third-level institutions include universities, technological universities and institutes of technology, which offer full-time degree and diploma courses. TU Dublin, created under the Technological Universities Act 2018, is an example of a technological university.
[hom-education-s3.4.1] Most full-time undergraduate places are allocated through the Central Applications Office (CAO) using Leaving Certificate points, which are based on the grades a student earns.
[hom-education-s3.4.2] Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses are full-time further education courses, usually delivered by Education and Training Boards in second-level schools and colleges. They provide training with work experience and can give access to third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.3] The Student Universal Support Ireland (SUSI) scheme provides means-tested grants (based on family income) towards the costs of approved third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.4] The Free Fees Initiative pays tuition for eligible undergraduates, so students pay only a student contribution.
[hom-education-s3.4.5] The Higher Education Access Route (HEAR) and the Disability Access Route to Education (DARE) are reduced-points entry routes for students from disadvantaged backgrounds or with disabilities, so these students can enter with fewer points.
[hom-education-s3.5.b] Adult and second-chance education lets people return to learning at any age, whatever their earlier educational level.
[hom-education-s3.5.0] Adult education courses are available full-time, part-time, modular, day, evening and weekend, and by distance learning, so adults can fit study around work and family.
[hom-education-s3.5.1] Second-chance education gives people who left school early a route to qualifications, such as Youthreach.
[hom-education-s3.5.2] Education and Training Boards (ETBs) run adult, community and further education courses; they replaced the Vocational Education Committees in 2013.
[hom-education-s3.5.3] SOLAS, the Further Education and Training Authority, funds and co-ordinates further education and training programmes across the country.
[hom-education-s3.5.4] Adult literacy schemes provide basic reading, writing and numeracy courses for adults.
[hom-education-s3.5.5] Adult education builds skills for work, supports career change, and helps adults keep pace with new workplace technology.
[hom-education-s3.6.b] Students with special educational needs are supported through a combination of planning, specialist staff and school settings. The aim is that each student gets the help that suits their need.
[hom-education-s3.6.0] An Individual Education Plan (IEP) sets out the goals, teaching approach and supports for a student with special needs, drawn up with the school and the parents.
[hom-education-s3.6.1] The National Council for Special Education (NCSE) plans and co-ordinates special educational provision, and Special Educational Needs Organisers allocate supports to schools.
[hom-education-s3.6.2] Special Needs Assistants (SNAs) provide care and support in class for students who have care needs or who need help to take part in lessons.
[hom-education-s3.6.3] Special education teachers (formerly called learning support and resource teachers) give withdrawal teaching, taking students out of class individually or in small groups, to students with learning difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.6.4] Special classes in mainstream schools, such as autism classes, have a reduced number of pupils per teacher, so each pupil gets more attention.
[hom-education-s3.6.5] Special schools provide for students with significant general or specific learning difficulties, physical disabilities, or sensory impairments.
[hom-education-s3.6.6] Visiting teachers advise and support students who are blind or vision impaired, or deaf or hard of hearing, and the teachers who teach them.
[hom-education-s3.6.7] Assistive technology, such as text-to-speech software, helps students with reading or writing difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.7.b] Beyond the school, specialist professionals assess needs and provide therapy, and the examinations themselves are adjusted for students who need it.
[hom-education-s3.7.0] Educational psychologists assess students' needs and recommend supports, which helps the school decide what help to give.
[hom-education-s3.7.1] The Health Service Executive (HSE) provides speech and language therapy and occupational therapy to children with special needs.
[hom-education-s3.7.2] The State Examinations Commission provides reasonable accommodations in state examinations, such as a reader, a scribe or extra time, so that a student's disability does not stop them showing what they know.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s2: 2. Role, status, socio-economic groupings, social mobility
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.b] Everyone occupies positions in society, and each position carries a certain standing in other people's eyes. Sociologists call such a position a status, and one person holds several at the same time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.0] A status is a social position, such as mother, teacher, employee or citizen. One person holds several at once, for example daughter, student, goalkeeper and citizen, so a status describes one part of who a person is, not the whole person.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.1] An ascribed status is given at birth or without choice, for example sex, age, ethnic origin or the family into which a person is born.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.2] An achieved status is gained through effort, choice or ability, for example a qualification, a job or a marriage.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.3] Being a parent or a spouse is usually treated as achieved because it follows from choices a person makes. Social class at birth is ascribed, but class in adulthood is mostly achieved through occupation.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.4] A master status is a status that dominates how others see a person and outweighs the person's other statuses, for example being a serving prisoner.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.5] Status can be based on occupation, education, wealth, family background or achievement. In a society that rewards achievement a person can raise their status, which links directly to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.6] Societies that allocate status mainly by birth are described as closed; societies that allocate it mainly by achievement are described as open.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.b] A status tells you where a person stands; a role tells you how they are expected to behave there. Roles are learned through socialisation (the process of learning the ways of a society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.0] A role is the set of behaviours and expectations attached to a status: the rights, duties and behaviour considered appropriate for a person according to their position in society. Examples include teacher, student, adult, child and parent.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.1] Status and role go together. A teacher (the status) is expected to prepare lessons, mark work and treat pupils fairly (the role). Likewise, the role of a student includes attending class, completing homework and respecting the teacher.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.2] A person plays many roles at once, such as mother, employee, daughter and club member.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.3] Roles change over the life course: a person moves from the role of child to adolescent to adult, and each stage brings different expectations. They also change over time, for example gender roles within the family.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.4] Role conflict occurs when the demands of two roles are incompatible, so meeting one means failing the other.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.5] For example, a parent who is also a manager is asked to work late when a child is ill, or a person is expected to be both a loyal friend and an honest employee.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.6] Role overload occurs when a person has too many roles and cannot meet all the demands placed on them, which reduces leisure time and increases stress. For example, a working parent also cares for an elderly relative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.7] Role conflict and role overload are most common where people hold several demanding roles at once.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.8] A role model is a person whose behaviour others copy, such as a parent, teacher or sports star.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.b] People in a society are not all in the same economic and social position. Socio-economic groups sort people into categories according to that position, and they show that society is arranged in layers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.0] A socio-economic group (also called a social class) is a category of people with a similar economic and social position, defined mainly by occupation and linked to income, education, wealth, housing and lifestyle.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.1] Social stratification is the division of a society into layers, a hierarchy of unequal ranks. A socio-economic scheme is one way of describing those layers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.2] In Ireland the Central Statistics Office (CSO) groups the population into social classes for the census, using the occupation of each person. The groups run from professional workers, managerial and technical, non-manual, skilled manual and semi-skilled to unskilled, with a further group for all others gainfully occupied and unknown.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.3] Occupation, not education, decides which class a person is placed in, so a qualification affects the class only through the job it leads to. Level of education is one of the strongest factors separating socio-economic groups, because it governs access to skilled and professional work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.4] Examples of occupations, one per group: doctor or solicitor (professional); manager or technician (managerial and technical); factory worker (semi-skilled); labourer (unskilled). Electrician, plumber, carpenter or motor mechanic are skilled manual occupations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.5] Factors that determine socio-economic group are occupation, income, education level, wealth, housing and family background.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.6] Socio-economic group affects life chances, meaning a person's likelihood of access to opportunities such as educational attainment, health, housing and income. It also shapes housing choices and household expenditure.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.7] Higher socio-economic groups are more likely to complete second level and go on to third level, while lower groups face higher early school leaving and lower expectations. This affects equality of opportunity in education.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.b] People do not always stay in the socio-economic group they start in. Social mobility is the movement of an individual or a group between socio-economic groups, enabled by education, income and occupation. It does not mean geographical mobility, which is moving house or country.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.0] Upward mobility is movement to a higher socio-economic group, for example a labourer's child who becomes a teacher, or a skilled worker who becomes a manager after completing a degree.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.1] Downward mobility is movement to a lower socio-economic group, for example after the loss of a skilled job with no replacement at the same level.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.2] There are two time frames for measuring social mobility. Intergenerational mobility compares a person's position with the position of their parents.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.3] Intragenerational mobility, sometimes called career mobility, compares a person's position at different points in their own working life, for example from apprentice to manager.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.4] Structural mobility is movement caused by changes in the occupational structure, such as the growth of professional and service jobs and fewer farm jobs, so that many people move even when their relative positions do not change.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.5] Exchange mobility is movement in both directions that balances out, so that the overall pattern of relative positions stays the same.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.6] Factors that help people move up include education and qualifications, skills and training, promotion in a job, a good income, free second-level education, access to third level (grants, free fees schemes), equality legislation, ambition and family support. Education is the main route to upward mobility in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.7] Barriers to mobility include low family income, poor access to education and third-level education, early school leaving, discrimination, unemployment and living in an area with few employment opportunities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.8] Social mobility is more likely in an open society, where status is achieved, than in a closed society, where status is ascribed.
