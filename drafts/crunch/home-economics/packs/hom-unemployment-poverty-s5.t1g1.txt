TARGET hom-unemployment-poverty-s5: 5. Causes and effects of poverty
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.b] Poverty persists where income from work is low or missing and where the costs of living absorb whatever income there is. The reasons fall into economic ones, about money and jobs, and social ones, about family, health and society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.0] Unemployment: a household with no earned income depends on State payments, which are normally lower than wages, so it has less to live on. Recession and high unemployment are the usual first reason given.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.1] Long-term unemployment erodes skills, work habits and confidence, which makes re-entry to the labour market (the pool of available jobs) harder the longer it lasts.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.2] Low pay: unskilled and minimum-wage work, and part-time or insecure work, often leaves full-time workers unable to afford an acceptable standard of living. These are called the working poor.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.3] Limited education: early school leavers and people with few qualifications or skills are concentrated in low-paid and insecure work, or in no work at all.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.4] Lone parenthood and large families: one income must cover food, housing and childcare for several people, and childcare costs can stop a lone parent working at all.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.5] High housing and living costs: rent, mortgage repayments, a shortage of affordable housing, energy, transport and childcare take a large share of low incomes, so little is left for other needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.6] Social problems: addiction and gambling divert money from essentials and lead to debt, for example when spending on alcohol, drugs or gambling replaces spending on food or heating. Ill health and disability can also stop people working.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.7] Economic change: recession and industrial decline mean the closure of a major employer removes jobs and the local businesses that depended on its workers' spending. Globalisation (production moved abroad) and automation (production mechanised) reduce demand for unskilled labour, mainly in manufacturing.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.0.8] Inequality in society: wealth and power are unequally shared, and some groups face discrimination, which limits their access to jobs, housing and services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.1.b] Disadvantage is passed from one generation to the next, so poverty repeats itself unless something interrupts the pattern. The cycle of poverty is poverty handed from parents to children through limited education, work and income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.1.0] Children raised in low-income households often have less access to good education, health care and social networks than children from better-off families, as well as poorer housing and diet.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.1.1] Disadvantage such as poor study conditions, low expectations and financial pressure can lead these children to leave school early with few qualifications, and then into unemployment or low-paid work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.1.2] Their own children are then born into the same financial conditions, so the pattern repeats across generations.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.1.3] Each stage reinforces the next: low income means poorer housing and diet, which affect health and school attendance, which in turn limit qualifications and later earnings.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.1.4] Breaking the cycle requires action at a weak point where it repeats, for example early-years education, family support and access to training and employment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.b] Social policy is the set of State decisions on welfare payments, housing, education, health, minimum pay and employment. These decisions affect how many people are poor and for how long.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.0] These decisions determine who is poor and for how long, because they set the income, housing and training available to each household.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.1] Policy can reduce poverty through welfare payments, the national minimum wage, free or subsidised health care, housing supports and education measures for disadvantaged areas.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.2] A minimum wage sets a legal floor for pay, so it directly determines what a full-time low-paid worker earns.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.3] Policy can also keep people poor if it is inadequate or badly designed. Critics argue that welfare payments set below the cost of basic needs keep recipients in poverty, and that a gap between welfare and wages is a problem.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.4] Social policy can perpetuate poverty where losing benefits when a person takes a job makes low-paid work financially unattractive.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.5] Other critics argue that generous payments reduce the incentive to seek work and so sustain dependence on the State. This is a contested view, so present it as a criticism.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.6] Housing policy affects poverty directly: the supply and cost of social and private rented housing decide how much of a low income is left after rent, and a failure to supply affordable homes keeps people poor.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.2.7] Support that is poorly targeted or changes often leaves gaps, so families move in and out of poverty rather than escaping it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.b] Deprivation is the lack of resources, services and opportunities that are normal in society. The cycle of deprivation is the way deprivation across several areas of life together, income, education, housing, health and services, reinforces itself and is passed on.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.0] In families, low income, low education and ill health limit the support parents can give children for learning, and children inherit the same limits.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.1] Children in such families often have little contact with people in employment, so they grow up with few examples of working life and low expectations, and repeat the pattern.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.2] In geographical areas, unemployment, welfare dependence and poor housing are concentrated in the same place, creating employment blackspots, often after a major industry closes. Some disadvantaged urban estates and some rural areas are examples.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.3] Services in those areas, such as shops, buses, schools and health centres, close as spending falls, and the area gets a poor reputation, making it less attractive to employers and families.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.4] Deprivation reinforces itself: an area losing population, employers and skilled workers loses the tax base and local spending that could sustain new employment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.3.5] Responses must work on the place as well as the household, for example community development, community education, transport, local training, job creation and local services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.4.b] The poverty trap is a situation in which taking up work brings little or no gain in net income, so a person stays on welfare. It is a problem of benefit withdrawal.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.4.0] A person on welfare who takes a low-paid job loses benefits or supports, and may pay tax and PRSI, so net income (what they keep) rises little or not at all, and may even fall. They can be no better off than on welfare.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.4.1] Supports often withdrawn as earnings rise include housing assistance, a medical card and, in some cases, child-related payments.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.4.2] The costs of working, such as transport, childcare and work clothing, reduce the gain further.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.4.3] When welfare is financially more rewarding than low-paid work, people have little incentive to take employment, which keeps them dependent on welfare.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.4.4] Payments reduced gradually as earnings rise, rather than withdrawn all at once, lessen the trap. In-work payments such as the Working Family Payment, a tax-free weekly payment for low-paid working parents, are designed to help low-paid working families.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.5.b] Poverty affects a person's mind, health and place in society, and these effects can then make it harder to escape poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.5.0] Stress, worry and fear about money can lead to depression and poor mental health.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.5.1] Loss of status and self-esteem, and a feeling of worthlessness, can reduce confidence to seek work or training.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.5.2] Social isolation and exclusion, because the cost of leisure, travel and social activities is beyond reach.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.5.3] Poor diet, and poorly heated or damp housing, increase the risk of illness, and ill health can prevent people from working.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.5.4] Some people turn to alcohol, drugs or gambling as a means of escape, which adds to debt and poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.b] When a household cannot meet its basic needs, the strain falls on the whole family, and children can carry the effects into adult life.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.0] Families may be unable to afford adequate food, clothing, heating and housing, and fall into rent, mortgage or utility arrears and debt. In the worst cases, they lose their home and become homeless.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.1] Financial pressure increases conflict and relationship strain, and can contribute to family breakdown.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.2] Children may be unable to take part in school trips, sport, clubs or activities their peers attend, which leads to embarrassment and affects belonging and self-esteem.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.3] Children without a quiet place to study, books or internet access may fall behind in school, and poor diet and health can affect their concentration.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.4] Children growing up in poverty are more likely to leave school early with few qualifications, which limits their later earnings and risks poverty as adults, the cycle of poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.5] Poorer diet and access to health care can affect children's physical health and development.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.6.6] Loss of a home forces children to move school and loses the stability that supports their learning.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.7.b] Poverty does not stay within households. Where it is concentrated, it affects whole neighbourhoods and the public finances.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.7.0] Higher demand for welfare, health and housing services increases the cost to the State.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.7.1] Concentrated poverty can contribute to crime, anti-social behaviour and vandalism in an area.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.7.2] Falling spending in poorer areas leads to shop closures and fewer local services, which reduces jobs further.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s5.7.3] Poverty reduces the skills and productive capacity available to the economy, because people who are unemployed or poorly educated cannot contribute fully.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s4: 4. Concepts of poverty
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.b] Poverty is more than having a low income. It is a lack of the income and resources needed for basic needs and for normal participation in society, and it is examined through two concepts.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.0] Poverty means being without enough income and resources (material, cultural and social) for an acceptable standard of living.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.1] It means inadequate food, clothing, shelter and warmth, because there is not enough income or resources to pay for them.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.2] It also means being unable to take part in socially acceptable activities that most people take for granted, which is why poverty brings social exclusion (being shut out from ordinary community life).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.3] Poverty includes the lack of opportunities, comforts and self-respect that other people enjoy, so it cannot be measured by income alone.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.4] Poverty is examined through two concepts: absolute poverty and relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.b] Absolute poverty is the most severe form: a person cannot afford the things needed to stay alive and healthy.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.0] Absolute poverty is income too low to meet basic physical needs for food, clothing, warmth and shelter, so health and survival are threatened.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.1] The standard is a fixed minimum, set regardless of how other people in society live. It does not change with the living standards of the surrounding society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.2] Absolute poverty is rare in a developed country like Ireland, but homeless people, and people living without proper housing, clothing or medical care, may experience it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.3] In Ireland the main concern is relative poverty rather than absolute poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.b] Relative poverty is judged against the living standards of the society in which a person lives, so it exists even in a wealthy country.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.0] A person in relative poverty lives below the standard of living that society regards as basic, so cannot take part in activities that are normal for others.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.1] The poverty line is the level of income below which a household is counted as poor: the estimated minimum income for basic food, clothing and shelter and an acceptable standard of living. A household below it is at risk of poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.2] In Irish official statistics the Central Statistics Office (CSO) sets the line at 60% of median equivalised household income. The median is the middle income, and equivalised means adjusted for household size and composition.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.3] Because the poverty line moves as incomes change, relative poverty can persist even when incomes rise.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.4] Examples of relative poverty: being unable to afford a warm waterproof coat, two pairs of strong shoes, adequate heating for the home, or a meal with meat, chicken or fish (or a vegetarian equivalent) every second day.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.5] Photographs of the same type of home or setting from different times and places show that the accepted standard of living changes, which is why poverty is relative.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.6] A child's clothing or a family's home in a photograph from another time or place may look very different from today's norm, yet be an ordinary standard for its own society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.b] Governments need numbers to track poverty. Two official measures are used, and the second is stricter than the first.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.0] The at-risk-of-poverty rate is the share of people whose income is below the poverty line (60% of median income). It is another way of describing relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.1] Enforced deprivation means being unable to afford basic items that most people regard as normal, such as those in the examples of relative poverty. It is not by choice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.2] The CSO counts a household as deprived if it lacks two or more of 11 listed items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.3] Consistent poverty means being both below the poverty line and in enforced deprivation, lacking at least two of the 11 items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.4] Consistent poverty is narrower and more severe than the at-risk-of-poverty rate, because it counts only people who both have a low income and cannot afford the basics.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.5] The CSO publishes these measures every year from the Survey on Income and Living Conditions (SILC), an EU-wide household survey. Social Justice Ireland also publishes a count of people in poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.b] The level of poverty in Ireland has followed the economy and unemployment. It is also not spread evenly across the population.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.0] Poverty fell from the 1960s and 1970s onwards as living standards rose, but rose again during the recession of the 1980s, when unemployment was high.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.1] Poverty fell during the Celtic Tiger period of economic growth in the 1990s and early 2000s, because employment grew.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.2] Poverty rose again after the 2008 downturn as unemployment soared and living costs rose, and rose further with high housing and living costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.3] The COVID-19 pandemic increased the risk of poverty for people who lost work or income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.4] Today poverty is a real problem for a sizeable minority of people, with children and some adult groups at higher risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.5] Poverty is not spread evenly. Risk differs by household type, by labour-market status, by region (urban black spots and some rural areas) and by age.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.b] Some groups face a higher risk of poverty because of low income, limited skills, or caring responsibilities. Each group below has its own reason for being at risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.0] Lone-parent households, particularly those without paid work, because they have one income and childcare costs make it hard to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.1] Unemployed people and long-term unemployed people, who depend on welfare or low-paid work; welfare payments are lower than wages, so they may be too low for their needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.2] Low-paid workers (the working poor), including those on the minimum wage who may not qualify for state supports, and people in insecure or part-time work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.3] People with disabilities or long-term illness, who may be unable to work or face extra costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.4] Older people, especially those living alone on a State pension in poorly insulated or substandard homes that are costly to heat, which can lead to illness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.5] Large families, because there are more children to feed and clothe on one income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.6] Travellers and other ethnic minorities, who face discrimination, lower education and employment, and poorer accommodation, including in the labour market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.7] Homeless people, who face isolation and poor health; asylum seekers, who have a restricted right to work while awaiting a decision; and refugees, who may have low income and language barriers and lack skills and qualifications.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.b] Education and opportunity also shape who is at risk. Two further groups are linked to low qualifications and low pay.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.0] Early school leavers and people with few qualifications are at risk because they face low pay and poor job prospects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.1] People in households in a cycle of deprivation lack education and opportunity, so low income passes from one generation to the next.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s3: 3. Effects of unemployment
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.b] Work gives a person income, purpose and a place in society, so losing it hits a person's money, status and wellbeing at the same time. These are individual effects, felt by the person who lost the job.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.0] Financial insecurity: lost income forces people to cut spending and lower their living standard, and to rely on savings, credit or welfare payments, which are lower than a wage.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.1] Debt: unpaid bills and loans build up, which can lead to mortgage or rent arrears (payments missed) and, in serious cases, repossession of the home or homelessness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.2] Loss of identity and status: work gives identity, purpose and social standing, so unemployed people may feel undervalued, worthless, a failure or guilty, and lose self-esteem.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.3] Stress, anxiety and depression: long-term unemployment can create feelings of hopelessness, and stress and depression can in turn affect a person's health.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.4] Social isolation: losing work removes daily contact with colleagues, and having less money for leisure reduces participation in social activities.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.5] Loss of daily routine can lead to boredom.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.6] Addiction: some people turn to alcohol, drugs or gambling to cope, which adds to their difficulties.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.7] Loss of skills over time and falling confidence make it harder to get a job, so a person can slip into long-term unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.b] Young people who cannot find work are at the start of adult life, so unemployment delays the steps that normally follow a first job.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.0] Delayed independence: without a wage, a young adult may have to remain at the parental home instead of moving out.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.1] Delay in starting a family or buying a home, because there is no steady income to save or borrow against.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.2] Risk of emigrating in search of work, which takes the young person away from family and friends.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.b] When an adult loses a job, the whole household feels it. These are family effects, felt by the people who live together and share the household income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.0] Reduced income lowers the family's standard of living, so it may struggle to afford food, clothing, heating and other household bills, and it relies on welfare payments.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.1] Debt and housing risk: arrears on rent, mortgage or utilities build up, and families that fall behind on rent or mortgage repayments face a risk of losing the home and of homelessness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.2] Financial pressure, changed roles within the household and the stress of one or both adults being at home cause arguments and strain relationships.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.3] Relationship strain can lead, in some cases, to separation or family breakdown.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.4] Children may have to give up activities such as sports, music lessons or school trips, and may feel different from classmates or be teased.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.5] Family members may emigrate for work, which breaks up the family's support network.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.6] Reliance on welfare payments can shape family finances for long periods.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.b] Children grow up in the household, so the effects of unemployment can follow them into school and into their own working lives.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.0] Children's educational opportunities suffer when a family cannot pay for books, technology or a quiet place to study.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.1] Children of unemployed parents have fewer working role models (people whose working life they can copy), which can lower their expectations of work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.2] Intergenerational unemployment: children raised in a household with no one in work may come to expect unemployment, so the disadvantage is repeated when they reach working age.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.3] Financial pressure at home can lead to early school leaving, which reduces the qualifications children take into the labour market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.b] Unemployment is also a cost to the whole community and the State, because jobless people stop paying into the system and start drawing on it. These are society effects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.0] Cost to the State: higher spending is needed on social welfare payments and related supports such as medical cards, fuel and housing supports, and taxes may have to rise to pay for it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.1] Loss of income tax and social insurance (PRSI) income means less money for public services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.2] Loss of production and skills: the economy's output falls and unused skills are wasted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.3] Crime, antisocial behaviour and vandalism can follow in areas with high long-term unemployment. This is an association with those areas, not a rule about unemployed people.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.4] Emigration of working-age and skilled workers reduces the population, the skills base and future tax income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.5] The black economy grows, as unpaid or undeclared work avoids tax and PRSI, so the State loses that income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.6] Social exclusion and inequality: people cut off from work, income and social networks become harder to reintegrate, and the gap widens between those in work and those without.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.7] Pressure on health and community services: more people use mental health, addiction and counselling services, and local authorities, voluntary bodies and community groups take on more work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.b] A black spot is an area of very high unemployment. When many people in one place lose their jobs, the effects feed on each other and the whole area declines.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.0] Falling local spending closes shops and services, which causes further job losses in the area.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.1] Population loss reduces the number of users of local schools and transport, making services harder to keep open, because people move away in search of work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.2] Black spots acquire a poor reputation, which deters investment and new businesses.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.3] People still in work feel it too: they face job insecurity and fear of redundancy, and where an area empties there is less local demand for their products and services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.6.b] The levels are connected, and an answer is stronger when it shows how one effect leads to the next.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.6.0] Effects chain from one level to the next: lost income (individual) leads to arrears and strain (family) and, multiplied across a town, to closed shops and falling tax income (society).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.6.1] The same effect can sit at more than one level, but give it once, at the level the question asks about. For example, depression is an individual effect, while the cost of treating it falls on society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.7.b] Marks go to answers that cover every level the question names, with each effect named and explained.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.7.0] Cover each level asked for: at least one effect for the individual, the family and society, then further effects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.7.1] Give each point a name and a short explanation of why it follows, for example lost income, so debt.
