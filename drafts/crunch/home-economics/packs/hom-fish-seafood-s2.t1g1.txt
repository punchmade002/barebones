TARGET hom-fish-seafood-s2: 2. Selection, spoilage and storage
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.b] Fish spoils quickly, so you judge it by sight, touch and smell, and by how reliable the seller is. A good buyer checks each sign in turn, because a fish that fails on one is usually failing on others.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.0] Eyes should be bright, clear and bulging (plump); sunken, cloudy or dull eyes show the fish is old.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.1] Gills should be bright red; grey or brown gills show the fish is not fresh.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.2] Flesh should be firm and elastic, springing back when pressed; soft flesh that keeps the dent is stale.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.3] Skin and scales should be moist and shiny, with tight, unbroken scales and no bruising or breaks in the skin.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.4] Smell should be mild and fresh, like the sea; a strong or unpleasant smell means the fish is spoiling.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.5] Shellfish should be alive and heavy for their size. Mussels should be closed, because an open mussel that does not close when tapped is dead and is discarded.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.6] Smoked fish should look glossy; frozen fish should be solid with its packaging intact and no signs of thawing or freezer burn; canned fish should be in an undamaged, undented can within its date.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.7] Buy from a reliable, hygienic retailer where the fish is displayed on ice or refrigerated, check the date on packaged fish, and buy fish in season, when it is better and cheaper. Medium-size fish have the best flavour.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.0.8] Get the fish home quickly, using a cool bag or ice, and refrigerate it straight away so it does not warm up on the way.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.1.b] Buyers also rely on official checks on the fish they buy. This is background, not a buying guideline.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.1.0] The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority (set up in 2007 under the Sea-Fisheries and Maritime Jurisdiction Act 2006) carries out official controls on fish and shellfish from production to distribution, under a service contract with the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI).
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.2.b] Fish spoils faster than meat, so it must be used or processed as soon as possible after it is caught. Its flesh is soft, high in water and has little connective tissue, and its fat and enzymes act quickly. There are three main causes.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.2.0] Oxidative rancidity: the fats in the fish, especially the polyunsaturated fat of oily fish, react with oxygen in the air, giving an unpleasant smell and taste.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.2.1] Bacterial action starts before death: a fish caught in a net struggles and uses up its glycogen stores (the sugar store in the flesh).
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.2.2] When glycogen is present, it converts to lactic acid, which is a preservative. If lactic acid is not present, the fish goes off quickly.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.2.3] Bacteria work on the flesh and produce trimethylamine, a strong-smelling nitrogen compound, which is what gives spoiled fish its smell.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.2.4] Enzymic activity: enzymes in the flesh cause deterioration even at low temperatures, so spoilage continues, slowly, even in the fridge.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.b] Storage cannot stop spoilage, only slow it, so fresh fish keeps for a short time only. Good storage keeps it cold and keeps it from contaminating other food.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.0] Chilling slows bacterial growth and enzyme action but does not stop them, so fresh fish is kept chilled, below 5 °C, packed on crushed ice until it is used.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.1] Raw fish is stored on the lowest shelf of the fridge in a covered container, so that drips cannot contaminate other food.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.2] Raw fish is kept apart from cooked and ready-to-eat foods to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.3] Covering the fish stops it drying out and stops its smell passing to other food.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.4] Fresh fish is used on the day of purchase or as soon as possible after it.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.3.5] Oily fish deteriorates faster than white fish because its fat goes rancid; this also limits how long it keeps frozen.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.b] Freezing is the best way to keep fish for longer. At freezer temperature the water in the flesh is held as ice, so micro-organisms cannot grow and enzyme action is inactivated. It slows or stops spoilage but does not kill all bacteria, which can grow again after thawing.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.0] Freeze only fish that is very fresh and of best quality; freezing cannot improve fish that is already poor in quality.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.1] Turn the freezer to its fast-freeze setting 3 to 4 hours before freezing the fish, so the fish freezes quickly.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.2] Freeze fish in usable quantities, so that only the amount needed is thawed.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.3] Do not freeze more than about a tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24 hours, so that the food already frozen stays frozen.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.4] Wrap fish tightly in moisture-proof packaging or vacuum pack it, to prevent freezer burn (dried-out patches) and oxidation, because air in the packaging lets oily fish go rancid.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.5] Label each package with the name of the fish and the date, so you can use it within the time on the pack.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.6] Store frozen fish at −18 °C or below.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.4.7] Thaw frozen fish slowly in the fridge and do not refreeze it once thawed raw.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.5.b] Freezing changes fish very little, which is why it is so widely used, but it has costs too.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.5.0] Colour and flavour are largely unchanged and nutrients are well kept.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.5.1] Texture may change, and some B vitamins and extractives (flavour compounds) are lost on thawing.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.5.2] Poor wrapping causes freezer burn, and air in the packaging lets oily fish go rancid.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.5.3] Advantages: freezing is simple and safe, loses little nutrient, and allows bulk buying and out-of-season fish.
[hom-fish-seafood-s2.5.4] Disadvantages: it needs packaging and freezer running costs.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s3: 3. Micro-organisms and the control of microbial spoilage
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.b] Spoilage is any change that makes food unfit or unpleasant to eat, such as an off smell, taste, colour or texture. Several causes are at work, but micro-organisms are the main one.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.0] Micro-organisms (bacteria, moulds and yeasts) are the main cause of spoilage, and they are the cause this subsection deals with.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.1] Enzyme action (the food's own chemicals continuing to work) causes changes such as browning and over-ripening.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.2] Physical damage and bruising break the surface of food, which lets other spoilage in.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.3] Moisture loss makes food shrink, wrinkle or harden, for example a dried-out piece of fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.4] Rancidity is the going-off of fats, which gives them an unpleasant smell and taste.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.5] Chemical contamination, for example by pesticides, antibiotics or metals, spoils food and can make it unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.6] Spoilage micro-organisms mainly make food unpleasant, so spoiled food is not always unsafe. Pathogens (disease-causing micro-organisms) can make food unsafe even when it looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.b] Bacteria are the micro-organisms that spoil most protein and moist foods, and they change food in several ways.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.0] Putrefaction: bacteria break down protein in meat, fish and eggs, producing foul odours and a slimy surface.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.1] Souring: lactic acid bacteria ferment the lactose (milk sugar) in milk, producing lactic acid that curdles the milk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.2] Bacteria that survive in food can produce toxins (poisons), which can make food unsafe even when the food looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.3] The foods at greatest risk are high-moisture, high-protein foods such as meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk and cooked rice, because bacteria find both water and nutrients in them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.b] Moulds and yeasts spoil foods that bacteria find harder to use, especially acidic and sugary ones.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.0] Moulds grow visibly on food as fluffy, powdery or coloured patches, for example on bread, cheese, fruit and jam, and some produce mycotoxins (poisons made by moulds) that are harmful if eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.1] Mould spores (tiny reproductive cells) spread through the air, which is how they land on food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.2] Yeasts ferment the sugars in fruit juices, jams, syrups and fruit, producing carbon dioxide, alcohol and off-flavours, and sometimes bubbling or swollen containers.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.3] Mould and yeast growth is mostly on the surface of food, where oxygen is available, but moulds also grow through open-textured foods such as bread.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.4] Acidic, sugary foods favour moulds and yeasts because they tolerate acid better than most bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.b] Micro-organisms spoil food only when the conditions they need are present. Remove or change one of them and growth slows or stops.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.0] Food: micro-organisms need nutrients, particularly protein and carbohydrate, as a source of energy and for building new cells.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.1] Moisture: their chemical reactions and nutrient absorption take place in solution, so dry foods support little growth and high-moisture foods are high risk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.2] Warmth: most spoilage and poisoning micro-organisms multiply fastest in the danger zone (5 to 63 °C), while cold slows growth, freezing stops it without killing them, and heat kills them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.3] Oxygen: aerobic micro-organisms such as moulds need oxygen, while anaerobic bacteria grow in sealed packs from which oxygen is absent, and facultative ones can adapt to either.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.4] pH (how acid or alkaline food is): most bacteria prefer a near-neutral pH, whereas moulds and yeasts tolerate acid. Lactic acid bacteria are an exception because they tolerate acid, but strongly acidic foods are generally safer.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.5] Time: micro-organisms need time to multiply, and some bacteria can double in number in about 20 minutes under ideal conditions (binary fission, where one cell divides into two).
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.b] Control of spoilage works either by destroying micro-organisms, keeping them away from food, or restricting one of the conditions they need. The first two principles deal with the micro-organisms themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.0] Heat treatments kill micro-organisms: cooking, pasteurisation, which uses a lower temperature for a set time, and sterilisation, which destroys all micro-organisms and their spores. Canning and bottling also use heat.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.1] Irradiation destroys micro-organisms and parasites in food by exposing it to controlled radiation, without making the food radioactive.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.2] Irradiation can extend shelf life and slow ripening and sprouting in fruit and vegetables, with little effect on colour, flavour or texture.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.3] Preventing micro-organisms reaching the food in the first place uses hygiene, clean equipment, covering food, sealed packaging, sterile containers and separating raw and cooked food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.b] The other principles leave micro-organisms alive but take away something they need. Most foods use more than one, and applying each to a named food is the usual exam task.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.0] Low temperature: refrigeration slows the growth of micro-organisms, and freezing inactivates them without destroying them, so growth resumes on thawing.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.1] Removing moisture: drying, salting, sugaring and concentrating reduce the water available to micro-organisms. Dissolved salt or sugar draws water out of microbial cells by osmosis, which dehydrates them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.2] Acidity: pickling in vinegar, or adding lemon juice, lowers the pH to a level that most bacteria cannot tolerate. Chutney and fermentation acids work the same way.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.3] Removing oxygen: vacuum packing and canning exclude air, preventing growth of aerobic micro-organisms, and modified-atmosphere packs replace the air around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.4] Preservatives: chemicals such as sulphur dioxide, benzoates and nitrites inhibit the growth of micro-organisms. Natural preservatives are salt, sugar, vinegar, alcohol and smoke.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.5] Combining principles: jam, for example, uses heat, sugar, acid and sealing together, so several principles protect the same food.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s2: 2. Freezing
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.b] Freezing preserves food by lowering its temperature far enough to stop enzyme action and microbial growth, and by turning free water into ice that micro-organisms cannot use. It is popular because no high heat is needed, so food stays close to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.0] Low temperature slows enzyme reactions (enzymes are the natural chemicals in food that make it spoil or change) and stops micro-organisms from reproducing, but it does not kill most of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.1] Frozen food is not sterile: micro-organisms become dormant (inactive) and start growing again when the food thaws, so frozen food is never safe indefinitely.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.2] Turning free water into ice removes the water micro-organisms need, because ice is unavailable to them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.3] Pure water freezes at 0 °C, but food freezes below this because its dissolved sugars and salts lower its freezing point.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.4] Water expands by about 9 per cent when it freezes, which is why liquids need headspace (an empty gap at the top) in their containers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.5] Freezing itself does little harm to nutritive value, and colour, flavour and texture are kept better than with most other methods because no high heat is used, except in blanching; most nutrient losses happen during blanching and thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.b] The speed of freezing decides how big the ice crystals are, and crystal size decides how much damage the food suffers. Quick freezing makes small crystals; slow freezing makes large ones.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.0] Quick freezing takes place at about −25 °C (the 2013 Higher Level scheme gives −25 °C to −40 °C) in the fast-freeze section of a freezer, so the food passes through the freezing range rapidly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.1] Quick freezing forms small ice crystals within the cells, which causes little damage to the cell walls, so there is little drip (juice lost) on thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.2] For this reason quick freezing retains the texture, colour, flavour and nutritive value of food better than slow freezing does.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.3] Slow freezing takes place at a higher temperature than −25 °C, as in an ordinary freezer compartment, where ice forms gradually.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.4] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals, mostly between the cells, that pierce and rupture cell walls, allowing juices to escape.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.5] As a result slow-frozen food is limp and soft when thawed, and it loses colour and flavour and drip containing soluble vitamins B1 and C and minerals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.6] The fast-freeze setting should be switched on about 3 to 4 hours before food is placed in the freezer, so the freezer is cold enough to freeze the new food quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.7] Diagram: draw two cells side by side, one with small crystals inside an intact cell wall (quick) and one with large crystals and a ruptured wall (slow).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.b] Blanching is a brief heat treatment given to vegetables before freezing. Enzymes keep working slowly even at freezer temperatures, so vegetables would spoil in storage without it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.0] 1. The vegetables are plunged into boiling water for a short, set time that depends on the vegetable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.1] 2. They are plunged immediately into ice-cold water for the same length of time, which stops the cooking so the vegetables do not go soft.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.2] 3. They are drained and dried, because surface water left on them would form ice crystals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.3] Blanching inactivates enzymes that would otherwise cause loss of colour, flavour, texture and nutrients during frozen storage.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.4] Blanching also cleans the vegetable, destroys some micro-organisms on its surface, softens it slightly so it packs well, and keeps its bright colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.5] Blanching loses some water-soluble vitamins, particularly vitamin C and vitamin B1, into the water and through heat. Blanch small batches in plenty of fast-boiling water so the water returns to the boil quickly and the timing stays short and accurate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.6] Fruit is not blanched; soft fruit is open-frozen on trays or packed in sugar or sugar syrup. Some foods, such as soups, sauces and casseroles, are frozen cooked after cooling.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.b] Good results at home depend on following a set of steps, and each step has a reason. Most of them aim either to freeze the food quickly or to keep air and moisture away from it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.0] Choose fresh, best-quality food in perfect condition and freeze it soon after harvest or purchase, because freezing keeps quality but cannot improve it. Wash, peel, trim or cut as needed, blanch vegetables, and cool cooked food quickly before it goes into the freezer.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.1] Portion food into usable (family-sized) quantities so that only what is needed is thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.2] Open-freeze (tray-freeze) sticky or delicate items, such as strawberries or raspberries, or items to be kept separate, such as sausages and burgers: spread them in a single layer on a tray, freeze hard, then bag them so that they stay separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.3] Pack food in strong, moisture-proof and vapour-proof freezer bags, foil or rigid plastic containers, and remove as much air as possible so that the food does not dry out. Leave headspace in containers holding liquids to allow for expansion as the food freezes.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.4] Seal tightly and label with contents and date. Place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze compartment, as the freezer instructs, and keep them apart from already frozen food until frozen, so they freeze quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.5] Do not overload the freezer: freeze no more than about one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24 hours, because overloading raises the temperature and slows freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.6] Store frozen food at −18 °C or colder, using the oldest first. The safe storage time depends on the food: fatty foods and oily fish keep for a shorter period, and fatty foods stored too long can turn rancid.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.7] Foods that freeze well include peas, beans, carrots, broccoli, apples, strawberries, meat, fish, bread, cakes, soups, sauces and prepared meals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.8] Foods that freeze poorly include lettuce, whole tomatoes and other high-water salad foods, bananas, melons, pears, whole eggs in the shell, low-fat cream and mayonnaise-based foods, which go limp, watery or separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.b] Problems with frozen food usually come from air reaching it or from careless thawing. Both are preventable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.0] Freezer burn shows as dry, discoloured, tough patches where air has reached the surface of the food, caused by dehydration and oxidation, especially in protein foods such as meat and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.1] Airtight packaging with the air removed prevents freezer burn.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.2] Thaw food in a fridge, or cook it from frozen where the food allows, such as vegetables, so that the food stays below the danger zone while it thaws. Thaw meat and poultry fully before cooking unless the pack says otherwise.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.3] Thawed food should be used promptly and not refrozen as it is, because quality declines and micro-organisms may have multiplied. Raw food thawed in the fridge can be cooked and then frozen as a cooked dish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.4] In a freezer fault or power cut, keep the door closed and do not open it, because frozen food keeps for a time if the cold is held in.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.b] Commercial freezing is faster and colder than home freezing, so ice crystals are smaller and quality is higher. Food is frozen soon after harvesting or catching, often on the farm or on a factory ship. Home freezers are not as cold or as fast, so home freezing does not match these methods.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.0] Air blast freezing: a very cold air stream at about −30 °C is blown over food, often on a conveyor belt or through a tunnel; suitable for most foods, such as fruit, vegetables, meat and bakery products.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.1] Plate (contact) freezing: food is pressed between two hollow metal plates cooled by refrigerant; suitable for thin, flat foods such as fish fillets, flat pieces of meat and burgers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.2] Flow (fluidised bed) freezing: cold air is blown up through a perforated conveyor belt so that small pieces float and freeze individually, staying free-flowing and not sticking together; suitable for peas, sweetcorn and berries.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.3] Cryogenic freezing: food is exposed to liquid nitrogen or carbon dioxide at extremely low temperature, which freezes it almost instantly; suitable for delicate or high-value foods such as strawberries, prawns and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.4] The cold chain keeps frozen food at −18 °C or colder in cold stores, refrigerated lorries and supermarket freezer cabinets until it reaches the home.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.5] When buying frozen food, check that the pack is intact with no ice crystals or frost inside, that it is below the load line on the cabinet, and take it home quickly in a cool bag.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.b] Freezing helps a household plan meals, save money and cut waste. These benefits are the points examiners expect when they ask about frozen foods in meal planning.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.0] Freezing surplus food before it spoils reduces household waste, and leftovers can be frozen for reuse as ready-made portions.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.1] Bulk cooking and freezing portions saves time and fuel, since a large batch is cooked once.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.2] A freezer stock of meals and ingredients provides emergency food and convenience on busy days, including for households with different eating times.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.3] Buying in bulk or when produce is in season is more economical, and freezing keeps it for a longer period (a longer shelf life).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.4] Frozen foods provide a wide variety of vegetables, fish and fruit all year, including items not available fresh locally.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.5] Freezing retains colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value well compared with heat processing; frozen vegetables keep more vitamin C than canned ones, as one past-paper table shows for peas (25 mg fresh, 17 mg frozen, 8 mg canned).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.6] Fruit and salad foods go soft after thawing because of cell damage, so they suit cooking better than eating raw.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.7] Labelling each pack with contents, date and portion size lets older items be used first, which helps planned menus.
