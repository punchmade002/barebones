TARGET hom-food-additives-s5: 5. Legal control of additives in the EU
[hom-food-additives-s5.0.b] Food additives are substances added to food for a technical purpose, such as keeping it fresh or improving its colour. Because they are added on purpose, the European Union controls their use by law under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives.
[hom-food-additives-s5.0.0] The regulation sets rules for preservatives, colourings, emulsifiers and antioxidants, among other additives such as sweeteners, so every additive group the syllabus names is covered.
[hom-food-additives-s5.0.1] As an EU regulation, it applies directly in Ireland, as in every Member State, without needing a separate Irish Act. This is why the same rules apply to a food sold anywhere in the EU.
[hom-food-additives-s5.0.2] Its purposes are to protect consumer health, to allow a single market in food, and to make sure additives are used only where there is a technological need and consumers are not misled.
[hom-food-additives-s5.0.3] Two related EU rules sit alongside it: flavourings have their own regulation, Regulation (EC) No 1334/2008, and vitamins and minerals added to food are covered by Regulation (EC) No 1925/2006.
[hom-food-additives-s5.1.b] The first control is a gate: an additive cannot be used unless it has been checked and approved. This stops untested substances reaching food.
[hom-food-additives-s5.1.0] An additive may be used only if it appears on the approved list for the EU (the Union list), and only in the foods and at the maximum levels that the list permits.
[hom-food-additives-s5.1.1] Each additive must be tested for safety before it is approved, so every approved additive has been tested.
[hom-food-additives-s5.1.2] The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) assesses the safety of the additive and sets an acceptable daily intake, the amount that can be eaten daily without harm. The European Commission then authorises the additive.
[hom-food-additives-s5.1.3] Approved additives are re-evaluated as scientific knowledge changes, so an approval is not permanent and an additive can be reviewed again.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.b] Each approved additive is given an E number, a code that shows it has passed the EU approval process. The E stands for Europe, and the number also gives a rough guide to what the additive does.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.0] Each approved additive is assigned an E number, except flavourings, which are not given E numbers.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.1] E100 to E199 are colourings, for example E150 caramel and E102 tartrazine. E200 to E299 are preservatives, for example E202 potassium sorbate and E220 sulphur dioxide.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.2] E300 to E399 are antioxidants, for example E300 vitamin C and E320 BHA; the range also includes acidity regulators.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.3] E400 to E499 are thickeners, stabilisers and emulsifiers, for example E471 mono- and diglycerides and E440 pectin. E500 to E599 are acidity regulators and anti-caking agents.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.4] E600 to E699 are flavour enhancers, for example E621 monosodium glutamate. E900 to E999 include sweeteners and glazing agents, for example E950 acesulfame K, E951 aspartame, E954 saccharin and E955 sucralose.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.5] The ranges are an approximate guide, with exceptions. For example, lecithin (E322) is an emulsifier but sits in the antioxidant range, so the class word on the label tells you what an additive does.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.6] An E number is shown on the label after the class name, for example preservative: E220, or the additive's name can be used instead, for example preservative: sulphur dioxide.
[hom-food-additives-s5.2.7] The label tells the consumer what the additive is, what it does and that it is approved. A consumer can also read the label to avoid additives they react to.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.b] Approval is not a free pass. The regulation sets conditions that every additive must meet, and it bans some additives from some foods altogether.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.0] An additive must not present a health hazard to the consumer, because protecting health is the first aim of the law.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.1] An additive must not mislead the consumer about the food, for example about its nature or quality.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.2] An additive must not be used to disguise poor quality or faulty processing, because it must serve a reasonable technological need.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.3] An additive must not reduce the nutritive value of the food.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.4] Each additive must be used in the smallest quantity needed to achieve its effect, so no more than necessary is added.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.5] Colourings are not permitted in fresh fruit, fresh vegetables, fresh meat, poultry or fish, because colour would hide how fresh these foods really are.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.6] Colourings are not permitted in baby food, and sweeteners are not permitted in food for infants and young children. Preservatives, BHA and BHT are also not allowed in baby food.
[hom-food-additives-s5.3.7] The label must show the class name of the additive followed by its specific name or E number, for example preservative: sulphur dioxide or preservative: E220. Flavourings are declared by name rather than by an E number.
[hom-food-additives-s5.4.b] The EU regulation sets the rules, but Irish bodies make sure they are followed. This is a short section of an answer, but one enforcement sentence can add marks.
[hom-food-additives-s5.4.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), set up under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998, is responsible for the safe use of food additives in Ireland and coordinates enforcement.
[hom-food-additives-s5.4.1] Official agencies working under FSAI service contracts check compliance through inspection and sampling. They include the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, the HSE Environmental Health Service and Public Analyst Laboratories, and local authorities.
[hom-food-additives-s5.4.2] The FSAI also advises ministers, regulators, industry and consumers.

CANDIDATES hom-food-additives-s4: 4. Classes of food additives
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.b] A food additive is a natural or artificial substance added to food during manufacture to improve colour, taste, texture, appearance, keeping qualities or nutritive value, or to aid processing. Because each additive is added for a particular job, additives are sorted into classes by that job.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.0] Additives are grouped into classes by their function: colourings, flavourings (including flavour enhancers), sweeteners, preservatives, antioxidants, nutritional supplements and, at Higher Level, physical conditioning agents.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.1] Each class has natural examples (from plants, animals or minerals) and artificial examples (made by chemical synthesis), and the function of the additive, not its origin, is the basis for its class.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.2] Natural does not mean safer: both natural and artificial additives must be approved before they can be used in food.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.3] Settings: examine a food label to find the additives used, identify the class of each from its name or E number (a code given to an approved additive), and suggest the reason it was added.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.4] Additives have disadvantages: some people react to them (allergy, intolerance, or a hyperactivity warning on certain colours), and they may mask poor-quality food or encourage a diet of processed foods.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.5] Two further disadvantages are specific to one additive each: sulphur dioxide destroys thiamine (vitamin B1), and polyols (bulk sweeteners) can cause diarrhoea if eaten in excess.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.b] Processing, such as heating or canning, can fade or change the natural colour of food, and shoppers judge food by its look. Colourings are added to put this right.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.0] Function: colourings restore colour lost in processing, make food more attractive and give a consistent, uniform appearance from batch to batch.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.1] Natural colourings include caramel (E150), chlorophyll (E140), carotene (E160a), cochineal (E120) and turmeric (E100, also called curcumin).
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.2] Artificial colourings include tartrazine (E102), a yellow dye used in soft drinks and sweets.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.3] Colourings are used in foods such as sweets, soft drinks, desserts, baked goods and cheese products.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.4] Tartrazine and some other azo colours (a group of synthetic dyes) must carry a warning that they may affect activity and attention in children, which is one reason some people avoid them.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.b] Some foods taste bland to begin with and others lose flavour during processing. Flavourings put taste back or add it, while flavour enhancers work differently by making a flavour already there seem stronger.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.0] Function: flavourings add flavour to bland food, replace flavour lost in processing, and (as enhancers) strengthen an existing flavour.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.1] Natural flavourings include herbs, spices, sugar and salt, used in foods such as jam, tinned beans, cereals, meat products, sauces, stock cubes, cheese and butter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.2] Artificial flavourings are chemical compounds made by synthesis, such as esters and aldehydes; ethyl acetate gives a rum flavour, amyl acetate a pear flavour, benzaldehyde a cherry flavour, and maltol is another example. They are used in baked goods and cakes.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.3] Flavour enhancers (in the E600 to E699 range) improve an existing flavour and make it seem stronger; monosodium glutamate (E621) is the most common example.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.4] Monosodium glutamate is used in soups, sauces, stock cubes and prepared meals.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.5] Flavourings in the narrow sense carry no E number, but flavour enhancers do, so do not say that every additive has an E number.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.b] Bacteria, yeasts and moulds spoil food. Preservatives slow this down so that food stays safe and edible for longer.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.0] Preservatives inhibit the growth of micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds), which delays spoilage and extending shelf life.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.1] Natural preservatives include salt, sugar, vinegar and smoke, which are accepted as natural preservatives.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.2] Chemical preservatives include sulphur dioxide (E220) and potassium sorbate (E202), used in soft drinks, jams, baked goods, dried fruit and wine.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.3] Sulphur dioxide destroys some thiamine (vitamin B1), so its use reduces the nutritive value of the food.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.4] Sulphur dioxide must be declared on the label as an allergen, because some people react to it.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.b] Sweeteners give a sweet taste, but they fall into very different groups. Nutritive sweeteners supply energy, intense sweeteners replace sugar with almost no energy, and bulk sweeteners (polyols) sweeten while also adding body.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.0] Nutritive sweeteners such as sugar, honey, glucose syrup and fructose supply energy as well as sweetness.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.1] Intense sweeteners (high-intensity, non-nutritive sweeteners) are many times sweeter than sugar, so only a tiny amount is needed and they contribute negligible energy.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.2] Intense sweeteners include saccharin (E954), aspartame (E951), acesulfame K (E950) and sucralose (E955).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.3] Because the quantity used is so small, intense sweeteners do not provide sugar's bulk, browning, preservation or texture, so a bulking agent may also be needed.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.4] Intense sweeteners are used in low-energy and diet soft drinks, tabletop sweeteners, yoghurts, desserts and foods for people reducing their sugar intake.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.5] Products containing aspartame carry a label warning for people with phenylketonuria, as aspartame is a source of phenylalanine (an amino acid they cannot process).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.6] Bulk sweeteners, or polyols, include sorbitol (E420), mannitol (E421) and xylitol; they are less intensely sweet, are used in larger amounts, give bulk and texture, and also act as humectants (they hold moisture in food).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.7] Polyols are found in sugar-free confectionery and chewing gum, and excessive amounts can have a laxative effect (loosen the bowel).
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.b] Intense sweeteners are used wherever sugar must be cut, but that does not make the food automatically healthy or suitable for everyone.
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.0] Artificial sweeteners are useful in slimming and diabetic products because they reduce sucrose, although the energy and portion size of the whole product still matter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.1] Sweeteners are not permitted in food for infants and young children.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.b] Fats and oils go rancid (stale and unpleasant-tasting) when oxygen attacks them. Antioxidants take up the oxygen first so the fat is protected.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.0] Antioxidants react with oxygen, so it cannot attack the fats and oils in food, preventing oxidative rancidity.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin C (E300, ascorbic acid) and vitamin E (E306, tocopherols).
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.2] Artificial antioxidants include BHA (E320) and BHT (E321).
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.3] Antioxidants are used in fats, cooking oils, crisps, biscuits, stock cubes, cheese spread, chewing gum, tinned fish, fruit drinks and jams.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.4] By slowing rancidity, antioxidants extend shelf life and reduce food waste, because less food spoils before it can be eaten.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.b] Some additives are added for nutrition rather than for how the food looks, tastes or keeps.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.0] Nutritional supplements are vitamins, minerals and other nutrients added to food to raise or restore its nutrient content.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.1] They are the additives used for fortification, so the food supplies more of a nutrient than it naturally contains.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.2] Unlike sweeteners, preservatives and colourings, their purpose is nutritional rather than technological.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.b] Physical conditioning agents are additives that change or maintain the physical properties of food, such as texture, consistency, moisture, stability and appearance. Emulsifiers and stabilisers work as a pair to keep mixtures like mayonnaise smooth.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.0] An emulsifier holds two immiscible liquids (liquids that do not mix), such as oil and water, together so that they do not separate.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.1] An emulsifier molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) head that attaches to water and a hydrophobic (oil-loving) tail that attaches to oil, so it links the two phases.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.2] Examples are lecithin (E322) and mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471).
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.3] Emulsifiers are used in mayonnaise, ice cream, sauces, margarine and chocolate.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.4] A stabiliser prevents an emulsion (a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix) from separating over time; alginates (E401), carrageenan (E407), guar gum (E412) and pectin (E440) are examples, with stabilisers and thickeners used in ice cream, sauces and desserts.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.5] Diagram: an emulsifier molecule with a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail, shown at the boundary between an oil droplet and water.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.b] These agents protect the texture of food by controlling moisture, lumping and firmness. One function in a single clause is enough when you are asked to name an agent and give its function.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.0] A humectant attracts and retains water, preventing food from losing moisture, drying out and hardening; sorbitol and mannitol are examples.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.1] Humectants are used in marzipan, cakes and sweets, so that they stay soft.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.2] An anti-caking agent prevents lumps forming in dried or powdered foods such as cake mixes and dried milk; polyphosphates (E450 to E452) are an example that also increases water retention.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.3] A setting agent helps a food to set; pectin aids the setting of jams.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.4] A firming agent keeps fruit and vegetables firm during processing.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.b] Some agents control what happens in the pan, in the packet or on the processing line, rather than in the food itself.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.0] Anti-foaming agents prevent scum and froth forming on the surface of food as it boils.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.1] Anti-spattering agents keep water droplets apart in cooking oils, preventing spatter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.2] Buffers maintain (control) the pH of a food at the level required for processing or taste.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.3] Packaging gases, such as nitrogen, are sealed into sachet foods to replace air and prevent oxidation.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.4] Release agents form a coating, so food does not stick: it stops foods from sticking to each other or to equipment.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s9: 9. Food legislation
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.b] Food law protects consumers by making sure food is safe and honestly described. It comes from two sources, the EU and Ireland, and the two work together.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.0] An EU regulation applies directly in every member state, so it is not an Irish law. Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 and Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 are EU regulations, not Irish Acts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.1] Ireland also makes national regulations (called statutory instruments) to give an EU regulation effect, which means they set out how it is enforced and which bodies enforce it.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.2] The Oireachtas (the Irish parliament) passes Acts, such as the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.3] The purpose of all of this law is consumer protection: food that is safe to eat and described honestly.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.b] Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs is an EU regulation, which applies directly in every member state. It lays down hygiene rules for food businesses at all stages after primary production (the growing or rearing of the raw food). Ireland gives effect to it through national regulations.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.0] The European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006, amended in 2009 and 2010, give effect to Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 in Ireland.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.1] The Regulations set hygiene standards for food premises, equipment, food handling, water supply, waste and the personal hygiene of food handlers, so that every step where food could be contaminated is covered.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.2] They require food businesses to put in place and operate a food safety system based on HACCP (a system that finds the points where food could become unsafe and controls them).
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.3] They require staff to be trained and supervised in food hygiene and to follow hygiene procedures, so that the people handling food know how to prevent contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.4] They require food premises to be registered with the relevant competent authority (the official body in charge of checking them).
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.5] They permit authorised officers, for example environmental health officers, to inspect premises, and to close them or have food destroyed where public health is immediately threatened.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.6] How this protects the consumer: food is prepared in clean premises by trained staff under a system that prevents contamination, and unfit food can be taken off sale.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.b] Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on the provision of food information to consumers is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014. It applies from 13 December 2014, and the nutrition declaration from 13 December 2016. It lists the information that must appear on pre-packed food, so that shoppers can choose safely.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.0] A label must give the name of the food and a list of ingredients in descending order of weight at the time of manufacture, so the main ingredient comes first.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.1] Fourteen named allergens must be emphasised wherever they appear in the list of ingredients, so that people with an allergy can spot them at once.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.2] A label must give the net quantity of the food, and any special storage conditions or instructions for use where needed.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.3] A label must give the name and address of the food business operator, so the food can be traced to a named business.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.4] Country of origin or place of provenance must be given where leaving it out would mislead the consumer, and for certain meats and fresh produce.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.5] Drinks over 1.2% alcohol must show their alcoholic strength.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.6] A nutrition declaration gives the energy value, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt of the food.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.7] Labelling must not mislead the consumer about the nature, identity, properties, origin or method of production of the food. Nutrition and health claims are regulated separately.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.b] Two parts of the label matter most for safety: the allergens and the date mark. Both help the consumer avoid harm, but they work in different ways.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.0] The 14 allergens that must be emphasised are cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above a set level), lupin and molluscs.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.1] A use-by date must appear on highly perishable food, because such food can become unsafe to eat. After this date the food must not be eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.2] A best-before date (the date of minimum durability) shows the date until which the food keeps its quality, so food past this date is a matter of quality, not safety.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.b] Alongside the rules on hygiene and labelling, there is a general framework that sets the principles for all food law. In Ireland this is the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998. Across the EU it is Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, known as general food law.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998 provides the Irish legal framework for food safety. It set up the FSAI and gave it the functions of coordinating food safety law and its enforcement.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.1] The 1998 Act also names the official agencies that carry out controls under service contracts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.2] Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 sets the general principles and requirements of food law across the EU.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.3] Under general food law, food that is unsafe must not be placed on the market, and responsibility for safety rests with the food business.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.4] Food businesses must be able to trace food one step back to their supplier and one step forward to their customers, so that unsafe food can be found and withdrawn quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.5] General food law set up the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.6] Risk analysis under general food law has three parts: risk assessment (scientific, carried out by EFSA), risk management (decisions by the authorities) and risk communication. The precautionary principle may apply where the science is uncertain.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.7] The result is a common high level of protection of health and consumer interests from farm to fork, with fast withdrawal of unsafe food.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.b] Rules only protect consumers if someone checks that businesses follow them. Regulation (EU) 2017/625 sets out the rules for official controls, which are checks by public authorities that food businesses comply with food law at every stage of the food chain.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.0] Regulation (EU) 2017/625 is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Official Controls in Relation to Food Legislation) Regulations 2020.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.1] Official controls include inspection, sampling and analysis, and audit (a formal check of a business's records and systems).
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.2] Official controls are regular and risk-based, so that higher-risk businesses are checked more often. They are carried out without prior warning in most cases.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.3] Inspectors can take samples of food for testing as part of an official control.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.4] In Ireland the FSAI is the competent authority with overall responsibility. The official agencies (HSE, DAFM, SFPA and local authorities) carry out controls on its behalf under service contracts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.5] How this protects the consumer: independent checks on all food businesses, with enforcement action such as improvement notices, closure and withdrawal where the law is broken.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s8: 8. National agencies in food safety
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.b] Consumers cannot see how hygienically their food was produced, so the state sets up bodies to check it on their behalf. In Ireland one central body coordinates the system and other bodies do the inspecting.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.0] National agencies protect consumers by setting and enforcing food safety standards, inspecting food businesses, testing food and acting when food is unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.1] Food businesses must be monitored independently, because a business cannot be relied on to judge its own hygiene and customers have no way of checking it themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.2] An outbreak or contamination can cross counties and countries, so a national system is needed to trace it and respond quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.3] The Irish system has one central body, the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), which coordinates, and a group of official agencies that carry out the inspecting.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.b] The FSAI is the state body at the centre of food safety in Ireland. It sets the direction and coordinates the work, while others carry out most of the inspections.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) is the state body responsible for food safety in Ireland, established under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.1] The FSAI operates under the Department of Health, the government department responsible for health policy. The FSAI advises the Minister on food safety, but the Department itself does not inspect food premises.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.2] It co-ordinates the enforcement of food safety legislation in Ireland, working through the official agencies, and it audits their work rather than inspecting most premises itself.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.3] It protects consumers by ensuring that food is safe to eat and that food information is accurate.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.4] It works with food businesses to make sure food is produced safely, for example through guidance and training on hygiene and HACCP systems (the system of identifying and controlling hazards in food production).
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.5] It takes action, through the agencies, when a food premises is in breach of food regulations, because it is the agencies that can inspect and enforce on the ground.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.b] Beyond coordinating enforcement, the FSAI informs the public, handles emergencies and processes applications for new food products. It has one clear limit: it is about safety, not promotion.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.0] It gives consumers and food businesses authoritative advice and publishes food safety information, so that both groups can rely on one trusted source.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.1] It carries out scientific work, which gives its advice and decisions an evidence base.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.2] It processes applications for novel foods and for new methods of food processing, so that new products are assessed before they reach consumers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.3] It also handles food alerts, product withdrawals and recalls, works with the agencies on traceability and leads on food incidents.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.4] It does not promote Irish food at home or abroad; that is the role of Bord Bia (the Irish Food Board), which is why an exam statement that the FSAI promotes Irish food is false.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.b] The FSAI does not carry out most food controls itself. Official agencies do this work under service contracts with the FSAI, which means the FSAI can coordinate one national system without employing every inspector.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.0] The official agencies are the Health Service Executive (HSE), the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority and the local authorities.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.1] Official agencies carry out inspections, sampling and analysis of food in the places they cover, and they enforce the rules on the FSAI's behalf.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.2] The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority carries out the official food safety controls on fish and shellfish, from production and landing through processing to distribution, but not retail.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.3] Local authorities carry out food controls under contract to the FSAI in their own areas, mainly veterinary inspections of food premises that handle meat and other animal products.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.b] The HSE is the national health service, and its Environmental Health Service does most of the day-to-day inspection of food premises. Its staff are environmental health officers, who work for the HSE and not for the FSAI.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.0] Environmental health officers (EHOs), working in the HSE's Environmental Health Service, carry out the inspections of food premises, including restaurants, cafés, shops, hospital kitchens, manufacturers and wholesalers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.1] The service registers new food premises, so that every business handling food is known to the authorities before it starts trading.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.2] It inspects food premises and farms.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.3] It handles hygiene complaints and issues closure orders and enforcement notices where food premises are unsafe, which stops a risky business from continuing to sell food.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.4] It ensures food safety at public gatherings and events.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.5] It monitors compliance with food, labelling, additives and contaminants legislation, and takes samples of food for testing.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.6] It responds to rapid alerts and recalls, and it investigates food poisoning outbreaks.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.7] It educates and advises food businesses on compliance with the law.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.b] Inspectors can see a kitchen, but only a laboratory can tell what is in a food. The Public Analyst Laboratories provide that testing, and they analyse rather than inspect.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.0] The Public Analyst Laboratories are run by the HSE.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.1] They analyse food and water, including samples taken by environmental health officers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.2] They test food for compliance with food law and labelling law, for example its composition, additives, contaminants and microbiological quality (the bacteria in it).
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.3] They test samples submitted by members of the public, for a fee.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.4] Their results help ensure that food sold to consumers is safe and wholesome, and they give the evidence needed for legal action.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.b] This Department looks after food at the farm end of the food chain, where animals and animal products come from. Its controls follow food from the farm to the shop.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.0] It monitors food safety in meat and meat products, milk and milk products, and eggs and egg products.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.1] It monitors the use of pesticides in food production, so that residues (traces left on or in food) do not reach consumers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.2] It enforces EU controls on the import of animal-based foods from countries outside the EU.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.3] It is responsible for the registration and traceability of cattle, pigs and sheep, so that an animal can be traced if a problem is found.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.4] It ensures correct hygiene procedures are followed from the dairy farm, to the dairy, through to the retailer and the consumer.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.b] Because the inspecting is shared between several bodies, they need common ways of tracking and removing unsafe food. The FSAI coordinates all of these.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.0] The FSAI and the official agencies work together on inspections, sampling, alerts, product withdrawals and recalls.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.1] Traceability allows an unsafe product to be traced back to its supplier (one step back) and forward to the businesses that received it (one step forward).
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.2] A product withdrawal removes a product from the supply chain, whereas a product recall also collects it from consumers by asking them to return it.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.3] Rapid alerts pass warnings about unsafe food quickly to the agencies that must act on them, so that action can be taken before more people are harmed.
