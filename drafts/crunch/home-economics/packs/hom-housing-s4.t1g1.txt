MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-housing-s4: 4. Housing provision
[hom-housing-s4.0.b] A home that suits one household can be unworkable for another. Each group needs its own mix of space, location, access and services, and in an exam you should say who each requirement serves and why.
[hom-housing-s4.0.0] Families need enough bedrooms for every member, adequate storage, a well-equipped, ventilated and well-lit kitchen, and a living room for relaxing and watching television, so that everyone has space for their own needs.
[hom-housing-s4.0.1] A larger family also needs more than one bathroom, and a hot-press (an airing cupboard for the hot water tank and linen) helps with laundry.
[hom-housing-s4.0.2] Families with young children need a safe, enclosed back garden so the children can play outdoors without danger, and safe stairs. Teenagers need space to study, and families with a car or sports equipment may need a garage or shed.
[hom-housing-s4.0.3] Families also need to be close to schools, childcare, shops, parks and public transport, which cuts the time and cost of daily journeys for school-going children and working parents.
[hom-housing-s4.0.4] Single people usually need smaller, affordable accommodation, such as an apartment, bedsit, small townhouse or a room in a shared house, because they have one income. It should be close to work, college or public transport, with privacy, security and space for study.
[hom-housing-s4.1.b] For these groups the home itself must be adapted, because stairs, steps and standard fittings can make an ordinary house unsafe or unusable.
[hom-housing-s4.1.0] Older people need single-storey living living, or a ground-floor bedroom and bathroom, or a stairlift where there are stairs, because climbing stairs becomes difficult. They need a level or ramped entrance and non-slip floors with safe lighting to prevent falls.
[hom-housing-s4.1.1] Older people also need a home that is warm, well insulated and easy to heat, an alarm or emergency call system, and nearness to shops, health services, public transport and community and family support. Sheltered housing (accommodation designed for older people) is one option.
[hom-housing-s4.1.2] People with disabilities need adjustments that depend on the disability. For mobility: ramps for wheelchair or walking-aid users, wide doorways and hallways, lowered worktops, lowered switches and sockets at reachable heights, a raised toilet with grab rails, an accessible bathroom or shower, and a lift or stairlift.
[hom-housing-s4.1.3] For a hearing disability, flashing lights are linked to the doorbell and alarms, so the person is alerted without hearing a sound. For a sight disability, audible or tactile alarms and good lighting help, and communication or alarm systems are needed for emergencies.
[hom-housing-s4.2.b] Homeless people first need somewhere safe to stay tonight, and the aim is then a permanent home with support.
[hom-housing-s4.2.0] Homeless people need emergency shelter in hostels or emergency bed and breakfast accommodation, ideally regular rather than a nightly queue for a bed, with washing and laundry facilities, privacy and dignity, a safe place to store possessions and a communal space (kitchen or day room) to reduce isolation.
[hom-housing-s4.2.1] For homeless people the next step is rent-assisted private accommodation or local authority housing, and then a permanent home with support.
[hom-housing-s4.2.2] Serviced halting sites (sites with water and other services) exist for Travellers who live in mobile homes or caravans.
[hom-housing-s4.3.b] The State and voluntary bodies provide homes for people who cannot afford to buy or rent privately. Several routes exist, and each works differently.
[hom-housing-s4.3.0] Local authority housing is rented housing for households on a lower income who cannot afford a home. It is allocated by need through a social housing assessment and a waiting list, with priority given to households unable to house themselves.
[hom-housing-s4.3.1] Local authorities consider household size, income, current accommodation, age and disability. Families, people with disabilities and older people are often given priority, so single people can wait longer.
[hom-housing-s4.3.2] Voluntary housing is provided by approved housing bodies (non-profit voluntary organisations), often for specific groups such as older people, people with disabilities or homeless people. The tenant's landlord is the approved housing body.
[hom-housing-s4.3.3] Co-operative housing is built by a group of members who pool the costs of the site, builder and solicitor, share costs and manage the housing on non-profit principles. A local authority may provide a low-cost site for a co-operative whose members are on its waiting list.
[hom-housing-s4.3.4] The Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) is a local authority support for private rented housing: the local authority pays the private landlord and the tenant pays a contribution. The Rental Accommodation Scheme (RAS) is where the local authority arranges long-term private rented homes for households with a long-term need.
[hom-housing-s4.3.5] Cost rental means homes let at a rent based on cost rather than profit, for households above social housing income limits. Do not confuse it with social housing (let at subsidised rent) or with affordable housing (sold below market price).
[hom-housing-s4.3.6] Shared equity and affordable purchase: under the First Home scheme the State and participating lenders take an equity share in a new home, which the buyer can buy out later. Local authorities also sell affordable homes below market price.
[hom-housing-s4.3.7] Adaptation grants help older people and people with disabilities to adapt a home so they can stay in it, and specific provision exists for homeless people, older people and Travellers. Demand for social housing exceeds supply, so applicants can wait for a number of years for a home.
[hom-housing-s4.4.b] Distribution is about where homes are and in what tenure (the way a home is held: owned, rented privately or rented from a social landlord). It is separate from whether those homes meet need.
[hom-housing-s4.4.0] By tenure, homes are owner-occupied (with a mortgage or owned outright), privately rented or socially rented from a local authority or approved housing body. Owner-occupation is the largest tenure in Ireland, followed by private renting and social housing.
[hom-housing-s4.4.1] By sector, private housing is built by developers and sold or let on the open market, while social housing is built and allocated by local authorities and approved housing bodies for people who cannot afford a home.
[hom-housing-s4.4.2] By location, urban areas have higher density, more apartments and greater demand, while rural areas have one-off houses and bungalows and, in some places, vacant homes.
[hom-housing-s4.4.3] Strong demand in cities and commuter areas pushes up prices and rents, while some rural areas have vacant housing but fewer jobs and services, so empty homes do not solve the shortage where people want to live.
[hom-housing-s4.5.b] A home can exist and still be a poor place to live. Quality is judged by structure and repair, insulation and heating, damp and ventilation, lighting, space for the household, safety and facilities.
[hom-housing-s4.5.0] A Building Energy Rating (BER) certificate rates a home's energy efficiency from A, the most efficient, to G, the least efficient, so buyers and tenants can compare running costs.
[hom-housing-s4.5.1] Older housing stock is often poorly insulated and costly to heat, which can cause fuel poverty (being unable to afford to heat the home adequately). New homes must meet the Building Regulations.
[hom-housing-s4.5.2] Damp, poor ventilation and inadequate lighting reduce the quality of accommodation and can affect health. Cold and overcrowding also harm health, and school and work performance.
[hom-housing-s4.5.3] Rented housing must meet legal minimum standards under the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019, which local authorities enforce.
[hom-housing-s4.6.b] Buying and renting both cost money, but at different times and with different returns. Comparing them means looking at initial costs, ongoing costs and what you are left with.
[hom-housing-s4.6.0] Buying has initial costs: a deposit, legal fees, lender, valuation and survey fees, stamp duty on the purchase, and furnishing.
[hom-housing-s4.6.1] Buying has ongoing costs: mortgage repayments, mortgage protection and home insurance, property tax, maintenance and repairs, and service charges for apartments. It builds equity (the part of the home you own), may rise in value and gives security.
[hom-housing-s4.6.2] Renting has initial costs of a deposit (a month's rent is the usual) and rent in advance, and an agency fee where an agent is used. Ongoing costs are rent, contents insurance and utilities.
[hom-housing-s4.6.3] The tenant has no repair costs but builds no equity, so rent is sometimes called dead money. Renting needs less upfront capital and gives mobility, with lower upfront costs than buying.
[hom-housing-s4.6.4] In high-demand urban areas, rent can be as high as, or higher than, mortgage repayments on a similar home, so renting for years can cost more than buying, but buying requires a deposit that many people cannot save.
[hom-housing-s4.6.5] Rural homes are often cheaper to buy than urban ones, but residents may have to travel further to reach schools, shops and jobs.
[hom-housing-s4.7.b] Adequacy asks whether the housing on offer matches what households need. It goes beyond counting dwellings.
[hom-housing-s4.7.0] Adequacy means housing must be enough in number, affordable, secure, suitable, accessible, in good condition and near employment, transport, schools, healthcare and shops, for each type of household.
[hom-housing-s4.7.1] Strengths include a wide variety of house types and tenures, social housing through local authorities, approved housing bodies and co-operatives, cost rental, rent supports (HAP and RAS), supports for first-time buyers, adaptation grants, and better standards through the Building Regulations and the BER.
[hom-housing-s4.7.2] Weaknesses include high prices and rents in cities, waiting lists for social housing, homelessness, poor access for some people with disabilities and older people, poor stock in places, vacant property, and uneven distribution between urban and rural areas.
[hom-housing-s4.7.3] Emergency accommodation does not offer the stability of a permanent home, and long stays in it are a weakness.
[hom-housing-s4.7.4] Low-income families, lone parents, single people (lower priority for social housing), homeless people, Travellers, older people, people with disabilities, students and new arrivals (refugees and asylum seekers) may not get housing suited to their needs.
[hom-housing-s4.7.5] Local authority building, voluntary and co-operative schemes increase supply, and adaptation grants help particular groups to stay in their homes.
[hom-housing-s4.7.6] Renovating vacant property and planning mixed, serviced developments are further ways to improve adequacy, along with more social, affordable and cost rental homes and planning for need.
[hom-housing-s4.8.b] A house is only as useful as what is around it. Amenities and services meet daily needs, and an estate without them isolates its residents.
[hom-housing-s4.8.0] Schools, childcare and playgrounds serve children and reduce long journeys for families.
[hom-housing-s4.8.1] Shops, post offices, banks, health services, pharmacies and community centres provide necessities and social contact, which builds a sense of community.
[hom-housing-s4.8.2] Public transport gives access to employment and services, so a development without transport isolates its residents.
[hom-housing-s4.8.3] Parks, sports grounds and play areas promote exercise, leisure and community interaction.
[hom-housing-s4.8.4] Street lighting improves safety, and regular refuse collection keeps the area hygienic.
[hom-housing-s4.8.5] Accessible healthcare, pharmacies and services for older people or people with disabilities support independence.
[hom-housing-s4.8.6] Setting: a group housing study of a local area such as a townland, village or housing estate, recording which amenities are present and which are missing.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s1: 1. Housing choices
[hom-housing-s1.0.b] A household chooses a home by weighing what it needs and can afford against what is available in the place it wants to live. No single factor decides alone, so a good answer shows how several of them pull against each other.
[hom-housing-s1.0.0] Socio-economic factors include income (which decides whether a household can afford a deposit or rent), employment, household size and the stage of the family life cycle, such as a young couple, young children, teenagers, an empty nest or an older couple whose children have left home.
[hom-housing-s1.0.1] Each stage of the family life cycle needs a different amount of space, which is why a growing family and an older couple choose differently.
[hom-housing-s1.0.2] Cost decides the size, type and location of home a household can afford. The deposit and running costs matter as much as the purchase price or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.0.3] Legal and lender fees, stamp duty, repayments, insurance, maintenance, heating and travel to work must all be counted, not just the headline price. Parental help with a deposit is also an economic factor.
[hom-housing-s1.0.4] Special needs, such as those of older people or people with a disability, may call for single-storey living, step-free access or a stairlift, because stairs and steps can make a home unusable.
[hom-housing-s1.1.b] Once a household knows what it needs and can afford, it looks at where homes are, what kinds exist and whether any are on offer.
[hom-housing-s1.1.0] Location is shaped by proximity to work, schools, shops, childcare, public transport, health services and family or friends. Urban areas offer services and jobs, while rural areas offer space and quiet. Safety, noise and risks such as flooding or pollution also count.
[hom-housing-s1.1.1] Personal preference covers layout, aspect (the direction the home faces), view, garden size and whether the household wants to own or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.1.2] Type and size of home: a household chooses between a bungalow, two-storey, detached, semi-detached, terraced, townhouse, duplex, apartment or bedsit. It also weighs room for the family, a possible attic conversion or extension, parking, space to work from home, and energy efficiency, shown by the BER.
[hom-housing-s1.1.3] Availability, meaning the supply of suitable homes at a given time and place, limits choice. In some urban and commuter areas private supply is short, which raises prices and rents, and social housing waiting lists are long because demand exceeds supply.
[hom-housing-s1.1.4] Setting: a case study of a student, a young couple or a retired couple, asking which of these factors matters most to them. For example, a student may rank low cost and closeness to college first, while a retired couple may rank step-free access and nearby health services first.
[hom-housing-s1.2.b] The way new homes are planned and built changes over time. Examiners want you to recognise the current trends and say what each means for households.
[hom-housing-s1.2.0] Mixed estates plan detached, semi-detached, terraced and apartment or duplex units together, so that different household types live in the same community.
[hom-housing-s1.2.1] New developments include shared open space and play areas, and in larger estates a crèche or shops, so families have facilities on their doorstep. Many estates and complexes have a gated or controlled entrance.
[hom-housing-s1.2.2] Refurbishment and inner-city redevelopment bring vacant, derelict and older buildings back into residential use. This includes the restoration of period homes, attic conversions and extensions, which add space without using new land.
[hom-housing-s1.2.3] Apartment living and higher-density development grow in cities and towns, using less land per household. Townhouses are built on small urban sites for the same reason.
[hom-housing-s1.2.4] An eco-house (an energy-efficient, environmentally friendly or passive home) is an emerging trend. They have high insulation and renewable heating, and may use timber frame or other modern methods of construction, so they cost less to heat.
[hom-housing-s1.2.5] Housing development follows employment. Demand rises where jobs grow, which pulls people towards commuter towns and suburbs around cities, and commuting distance becomes a cost to the household. Older marking schemes also name satellite towns and one-off rural houses.
[hom-housing-s1.2.6] Working from home creates demand for a study or home-office space, which affects the size and layout of home a household looks for.
[hom-housing-s1.3.b] Housing policy is the government's plan for how enough suitable homes are provided and who is helped. The current national plan is Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025 to 2030, published by the Government in November 2025. It rests on activating supply and supporting people.
[hom-housing-s1.3.0] Increasing the supply of new homes, with investment in the infrastructure (water, wastewater and roads) that housing needs, because homes cannot be built where these services are missing.
[hom-housing-s1.3.1] Social housing is built by local authorities and approved housing bodies, and social and affordable housing is delivered by these together with voluntary and co-operative housing organisations.
[hom-housing-s1.3.2] Affordable homeownership supports include the Starter Homes programme, and cost rental homes are let at rents based on the cost of providing them.
[hom-housing-s1.3.3] Measures to reduce and prevent homelessness include the Housing First programme, and rented accommodation must be registered, so that rented homes are on a register.
[hom-housing-s1.3.4] National rent controls protect tenants, and modern methods of construction (factory-built components) speed up building.
[hom-housing-s1.3.5] Sustainable communities are a policy aim: new housing should be supported by shops, schools, transport, parks and other infrastructure and services. Higher-density living and increased housing supply in urban areas aim to make more homes available at affordable cost.
[hom-housing-s1.3.6] Energy-efficient homes are promoted through the Building Regulations, BER ratings and SEAI grants for upgrading existing homes.
[hom-housing-s1.3.7] Accommodation for Travellers is planned by each local authority under a Traveller Accommodation Programme, required by the Housing (Traveller Accommodation) Act 1998.
[hom-housing-s1.3.8] Policy affects a household's choice because it changes what is built and where, what supports and grants are open to a buyer or tenant, and how much a tenant is protected.
[hom-housing-s1.4.b] Buying makes the household an owner-occupier. It gives control and a possible asset, but it demands money up front and a long commitment.
[hom-housing-s1.4.0] Advantage: a home may rise in value, so it can act as an investment, and ownership builds equity (the part of the home's value the owner owns).
[hom-housing-s1.4.1] Advantage: security of tenure, meaning the right to stay in the home, because an owner cannot be asked to leave by a landlord.
[hom-housing-s1.4.2] Advantage: freedom to choose the design and type of home, to alter and decorate it, and to decide whether to share it with others.
[hom-housing-s1.4.3] Advantage: mortgage repayments (the regular payments on a loan taken to buy the home) may cost less than rent over the long term, and grants or other supports may apply to some purchases.
[hom-housing-s1.4.4] Disadvantage: a large deposit and a long financial commitment are needed before the purchase can be completed.
[hom-housing-s1.4.5] Disadvantage: legal, lending, valuation and survey fees and stamp duty add to the initial cost on top of the price.
[hom-housing-s1.4.6] Disadvantage: the owner pays for all maintenance, repairs and insurance, and property tax where it applies, because there is no landlord to carry them.
[hom-housing-s1.4.7] Disadvantage: the value may fall, leaving the owner owing more than the home is worth, and buying ties the household to one location, so moving is slow and expensive.
[hom-housing-s1.5.b] A tenant pays a landlord (the owner who lets the home) for the right to live in a property, under a tenancy. Renting asks for little money at the start but gives no ownership.
[hom-housing-s1.5.0] Advantage: little capital is needed, usually only a deposit and rent paid in advance.
[hom-housing-s1.5.1] Advantage: mobility, so a household can move when work or family circumstances change.
[hom-housing-s1.5.2] Advantage: the landlord is normally responsible for structural repairs and maintenance, so the tenant has no repair bills.
[hom-housing-s1.5.3] Advantage: a wider choice of location and accommodation, from apartments and houses to shared houses and digs.
[hom-housing-s1.5.4] Advantage: tenants have legal protections, including notice rules before a tenancy can be ended.
[hom-housing-s1.5.5] Disadvantage: rent paid gives no ownership or return, often described as dead money. The rent can also be as high as a mortgage repayment on a similar home, and there is no long-term security if the landlord sells.
[hom-housing-s1.5.6] Disadvantage: an agency fee, a deposit and rent in advance add to the initial cost.
[hom-housing-s1.5.7] Disadvantage: the tenant must insure the contents of the home.
[hom-housing-s1.5.8] Disadvantage: facilities may be poor or shared, and the tenant has no say over changes to the property.
[hom-housing-s1.6.b] The private rented sector is made up of homes let by private landlords rather than by the State. Its standards and registration are regulated to protect tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.0] Private rented housing (houses, apartments, flats and rooms) is provided by individual landlords and businesses, mainly in cities and larger towns. Rent depends on demand and supply, location, size, quality and amenities, and quality varies.
[hom-housing-s1.6.1] Landlords must register tenancies with the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), which helps the state track rented housing and its standards. The RTB also deals with disputes between landlords and tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.2] Rented homes must meet the minimum standards in the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019, enforced by local authorities, who inspect rented homes. The property must be structurally sound and in good repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.3] Every room must have adequate ventilation and lighting, hot and cold water must be provided, and there must be heating and fire safety. Gas and electricity supplies must be safe.
[hom-housing-s1.6.4] Bathrooms, showers and other sanitary facilities must be in good working order and kept in a proper state of repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.5] Private renting is popular with students, young workers and people who are saving for a deposit to buy a home. It gives flexibility to people in temporary work or who want to move, and the State helps some tenants with rent through social housing supports.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s2: 2. Housing finance
[hom-housing-s2.0.b] Few buyers can pay for a home from savings alone, so most borrow. The amount a buyer must borrow is the purchase price minus the deposit, plus any costs of buying that the buyer cannot pay from savings.
[hom-housing-s2.0.0] The factors that determine the amount of finance required are the purchase price (which depends on the size, type and location of the house, and whether it is new or second-hand) and the buyer's own savings and deposit.
[hom-housing-s2.0.1] Additional costs of buying add to the total needed, so a buyer must budget for more than the price: solicitor's (legal) fees for the transfer of ownership, and stamp duty, a tax paid on the purchase.
[hom-housing-s2.0.2] Further costs are the valuation and survey carried out for the lender (to check what the house is worth and what condition it is in), an application or lender fee, land registry fees (to record the new owner), home insurance, and any fitting-out, furnishing or repair costs.
[hom-housing-s2.0.3] A deposit is the buyer's own money paid towards the purchase price. Lenders require a minimum percentage deposit, set by Central Bank rules, and supply the balance as a mortgage, so saving a deposit is a main barrier for first-time buyers.
[hom-housing-s2.0.4] A mortgage is a long-term loan to buy property, repaid with interest in regular (usually monthly) instalments over a term of many years. Interest is the cost of borrowing the money.
[hom-housing-s2.0.5] A mortgage is secured on the house itself, which means that if repayments are not made the lender can take possession of the property and sell it to recover the loan. This is called repossession.
[hom-housing-s2.1.b] A buyer usually combines several sources of money. Some are the buyer's own, some are borrowed from a lender, and some come from the State or family.
[hom-housing-s2.1.0] The buyer's own savings are the first source, and they make up the deposit that lenders require before they will lend the balance.
[hom-housing-s2.1.1] A mortgage from a bank or other lending institution is the main source of finance, because it provides the large sum that savings cannot cover.
[hom-housing-s2.1.2] A local authority home loan is government-backed and is for people who qualify but cannot get a commercial loan.
[hom-housing-s2.1.3] A credit union loan is usually a smaller amount, for example to help with a deposit or home improvements.
[hom-housing-s2.1.4] Help from parents or family, as a gift or a loan, is often used towards the deposit.
[hom-housing-s2.1.5] State supports for buyers include the First Home shared equity scheme (the State and participating lenders take a share in a new home, which the buyer can buy back), the Help to Buy incentive for first-time buyers of new homes, and affordable purchase schemes run by local authorities.
[hom-housing-s2.2.b] A lending institution assesses whether the applicant can repay the loan and whether the property is a suitable security for it. Each condition below protects the lender by making sure the loan can be repaid, or the property sold if it is not.
[hom-housing-s2.2.0] Borrowing limit: the loan is capped at a set multiple of the applicant's gross income (income before tax), so the borrower is not lent more than can be repaid. The multiple is set by Central Bank rules and changes.
[hom-housing-s2.2.1] Loan-to-value limit: the loan cannot exceed a set share of the property's value, so the buyer must provide the balance as a deposit. The rules differ for first-time buyers and others.
[hom-housing-s2.2.2] Secure, continuous employment: the applicant must be in stable work so that repayments can be met over the term. The self-employed supply accounts instead.
[hom-housing-s2.2.3] Proof of income: payslips, a P60 (the yearly summary of pay and tax), and tax and bank statements show the lender what the applicant earns.
[hom-housing-s2.2.4] Good financial record: a clean credit history with no missed repayments or unpaid debts, a record of regular saving and reasonable spending habits. Existing debts and commitments reduce the amount lent.
[hom-housing-s2.2.5] Term of the loan: repayments are spread over a long period, but an older applicant is usually offered a shorter term so the loan ends before retirement.
[hom-housing-s2.2.6] Property: the lender has it valued and surveyed, and it must be in good condition and good value as security. The lender checks the title deeds through a solicitor, and the buyer must have buildings insurance for the term.
[hom-housing-s2.2.7] Mortgage protection insurance, plus proof of identity and address so the lender can confirm who the borrower is.
[hom-housing-s2.3.b] A mortgage is a large debt that may last decades, and the borrower could die before it is repaid. A mortgage protection policy is a life assurance policy that protects the lender's loan and the borrower's family if the borrower dies during the term.
[hom-housing-s2.3.0] Mortgage protection is a term life assurance policy for the length of the mortgage and the amount of the loan. Life assurance means premiums (regular payments) are made and a sum is paid out when the insured person dies.
[hom-housing-s2.3.1] If the borrower dies during the term, the insurer repays the outstanding mortgage balance to the lender, so the family keeps the home.
[hom-housing-s2.3.2] Without cover, the remaining debt would fall on the borrower's family, who could be forced to sell the home.
[hom-housing-s2.3.3] Life assurance in general can run for life, whereas mortgage protection runs for the term only, and the sum insured falls as the loan is repaid (a decreasing term policy).
[hom-housing-s2.3.4] Under the Consumer Credit Act 1995 a lender must make sure that the borrower has mortgage protection, with exceptions such as where cover cannot be obtained because of age or health.
[hom-housing-s2.3.5] The borrower may buy mortgage protection from the lender's group scheme, an insurer or a broker, and cannot be forced to buy it from the lender.
[hom-housing-s2.3.6] Mortgage protection does not pay the repayments if the borrower is ill, disabled or made redundant, because it pays only on death. Income protection is a separate policy.
[hom-housing-s2.3.7] Annuity and pension-linked mortgages require a mortgage protection policy to be taken out with the loan.
[hom-housing-s2.3.8] An endowment mortgage does not need a separate protection policy, because its life assurance policy already covers the loan.
[hom-housing-s2.4.b] Mortgages differ in how the capital (the sum borrowed) and the interest are repaid. Annuity, endowment, pension-linked and tracker mortgages are the main types to be able to describe.
[hom-housing-s2.4.0] Annuity (repayment) mortgage: each repayment pays part of the interest and part of the capital borrowed, so the amount owed falls over time. Early repayments are mostly interest. It is the most common type, the rate may be fixed, variable or a combination, and it needs mortgage protection.
[hom-housing-s2.4.1] Endowment mortgage: the borrower pays only interest on the loan, plus a premium on a life assurance (endowment) policy designed to repay the loan when it matures. The policy yield may not fully cover the loan, and no separate protection policy is needed. It is no longer widely available.
[hom-housing-s2.4.2] Pension-linked mortgage: interest is paid on the loan and contributions are paid into a pension scheme, and the loan is repaid from the pension lump sum on retirement. It needs mortgage protection, is rare today, and was popular with self-employed people because of tax relief on pension contributions.
[hom-housing-s2.4.3] Tracker mortgage: the interest rate follows a published benchmark rate, the European Central Bank (ECB) rate, plus a fixed margin, so repayments rise and fall with the ECB rate. Trackers are no longer offered to new borrowers, but you may be asked to describe one.
[hom-housing-s2.4.4] Interest-only mortgage: the borrower pays only the interest for a period, so the capital is still owed at the end of that period. It is rarely offered.
[hom-housing-s2.4.5] Current account (offset) mortgage: savings held in a linked current account reduce the balance on which interest is charged. It is rarely offered.
[hom-housing-s2.4.6] Local authority mortgages and loans are also accepted as types of home finance.
[hom-housing-s2.5.b] Interest is the cost of borrowing, so the rate on a mortgage decides how much the borrower pays each month and in total. A borrower can choose how the rate behaves and how long the loan lasts.
[hom-housing-s2.5.0] A fixed rate is set for an agreed period of a number of years, so repayments are the same during that period and the buyer is protected from rises. The buyer misses out if rates fall, and ending early may carry a charge.
[hom-housing-s2.5.1] A variable rate changes with the lender's rate, which tends to follow market and ECB rates, so repayments rise when rates rise and fall when rates fall. This means there is risk for the borrower.
[hom-housing-s2.5.2] A combination (split) mortgage divides the loan between a fixed portion and a variable portion.
[hom-housing-s2.5.3] A fixed rate is often higher than the variable rate at the start, because the lender takes on the risk of rate changes.
[hom-housing-s2.5.4] A longer loan term gives smaller repayments but more interest in total, because interest is charged for more years.
[hom-housing-s2.5.5] A shorter loan term means higher repayments but less interest paid in total.

CANDIDATES hom-family-caring-s3: 3. Older people in the family
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.b] Independence means an older person keeps control over daily decisions and activities, which supports dignity, health and wellbeing. It is not the same as being alone: it means choosing how to live, with support where it is needed.
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.0] Dignity and self-esteem: making their own decisions protects an older person's sense of worth and identity.
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.1] Physical health: staying active and managing everyday tasks maintains strength, mobility and balance, so they stay able for longer.
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.2] Mental health: making choices and having a purpose reduces the risk of depression and loneliness.
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.3] Family roles: an independent older person needs less care, so the load on family members is lighter and shared more fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.4] Living in their own home lets older people keep their routines, friends and community ties.
[hom-family-caring-s3.0.5] An older person can be independent and still accept help, such as a home support worker, because independence is about who makes the decisions.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.b] Most older people want to stay in their own community for as long as they can. Small changes to the home, the right housing choice and regular contact with other people all make that possible.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.0] Adapting the home: handrails, ramps, non-slip flooring, good lighting, a level-access shower, and a stairlift or downstairs bedroom, so that stairs and bathrooms stay safe. Local authority housing adaptation grants help older people and people with a disability with the cost.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.1] Sheltered housing: self-contained units with a warden or manager on hand and shared communal facilities, so help is close without giving up a home of their own.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.2] Bungalow living: a single-storey home removes the difficulty of stairs.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.3] Granny flat: a self-contained unit built within or attached to a family member's house, giving privacy with family support nearby.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.4] Downsizing to a smaller house reduces maintenance, cleaning and running costs.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.5] Social contact: active retirement groups, community and voluntary groups, and visits from neighbours and relatives build a social circle and prevent isolation.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.6] Meals on Wheels and similar community meal services deliver meals to the home for older people who find cooking difficult, and home support (home help) assists with everyday tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s3.1.7] Personal alarm systems let an older person call for help in an emergency while living alone, which makes living alone safer.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.b] Many older people need some help without wanting to leave home. State and voluntary supports provide that help, and each one protects a different kind of independence.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.0] Home support (home help): the HSE's Home Support Service gives help with personal care and everyday tasks to people living at home, so they can stay in their own home. Home care packages exist for higher needs.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.1] Public health nurses visit older people at home to address minor health problems and advise on care.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.2] A medical card gives free GP visits and subsidised prescribed medicines, with a small charge per item, to people who qualify. It is granted on income (a means test) or on medical need or hardship.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.3] The Free Travel scheme (Travel Pass) makes public transport affordable and allows older people to keep going out, which protects their social life.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.4] The State pension gives a regular income, either contributory or non-contributory (means-tested), which supports financial independence.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.5] Other means-tested social welfare payments provide income support to older people with insufficient means.
[hom-family-caring-s3.2.6] Voluntary and community bodies such as Age Action, ALONE, Active Retirement Ireland and Meals on Wheels groups, along with family, neighbours and friends, provide visits, shopping, meals and company.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.b] Where an older person lives affects how independent they can be and how much the family has to do. Each option has advantages and disadvantages, and the best choice differs from person to person.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.0] Remaining in their own home (with adaptations and supports): advantages are familiarity, independence and community ties; disadvantages are maintenance costs, isolation and the cost of adaptations.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.1] Living with a family member: advantages are close support and companionship; disadvantages are loss of privacy, possible generation conflict, growing dependence and strain on the carer.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.2] Granny flat or extension: advantages are privacy with family support nearby; disadvantages are the cost of building and shared space tensions. Planning rules should be checked first.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.3] Sheltered housing: advantages are a self-contained unit, security, contact with neighbours and a warden; disadvantages are communal rules and limited choice of location.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.4] Residential care in a nursing home: advantages are 24-hour nursing and personal care for high needs; disadvantages are loss of the home and of independence, and high cost.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.5] The Nursing Homes Support Scheme, known as Fair Deal and run by the HSE, helps pay for long-term care: the resident contributes according to income and assets and the HSE pays the balance.
[hom-family-caring-s3.3.6] The choice depends on health, mobility, finances, family support and, above all, the older person's own wishes.
[hom-family-caring-s3.4.b] A real decision about where an older relative should live is best made by gathering evidence and involving the person concerned.
[hom-family-caring-s3.4.0] 1. Find out the relative's health, mobility, finances and wishes by talking with them.
[hom-family-caring-s3.4.1] 2. List the options, such as own home with adaptations, living with family, a granny flat, sheltered housing and a nursing home.
[hom-family-caring-s3.4.2] 3. For each option, record the advantages, disadvantages and cost, including any grants or Fair Deal support.
[hom-family-caring-s3.4.3] 4. Compare the options against the relative's needs and wishes, and recommend the one that keeps the most independence safely.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.b] Different generations grew up in different worlds, so their attitudes and expectations often clash. This is generation conflict, and it affects families at every stage.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.0] Generation conflict is disagreement between members of different generations because their attitudes, values and experiences differ.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.1] Parents and adolescents disagree over curfews, friends, technology and independence.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.2] Adults and their own parents may disagree over care, living arrangements, spending or the older person's role in the household.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.3] Older people may judge younger generations by their own youth without accounting for social changes such as technology and working life.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.4] Changing moral values and lifestyle choices, such as attitudes to marriage and religion, can divide generations.
[hom-family-caring-s3.5.5] When an older person loses independence, decisions made for them can cause frustration and conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.b] Conflict between generations rarely disappears by itself. The aim is for each side to feel heard and to agree arrangements everyone can live with.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.0] Good communication avoids confrontation and lets each generation state its view and listen to the other.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.1] Clear expectations: everyone should know what is expected of them and where the boundaries lie.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.2] Patience and understanding: recognising that values change over time reduces hostility.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.3] Respect for experience: younger people can value the wisdom older generations have gained, and older people can accept change.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.4] Compromise and negotiation: shared arrangements, such as visiting times or care responsibilities, are agreed together.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.5] Early action: problems are addressed before they grow, rather than being ignored.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.6] Regular family meetings create an atmosphere in which family members can talk openly.
[hom-family-caring-s3.6.7] Role-play practises these skills, for example a case study of an older person who resists moving into a granny flat.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.b] Grandparents are often an important part of family life. They contribute in many ways, and the family has duties towards them in return.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.0] Childcare: collecting grandchildren from school or minding them while parents work reduces childcare costs for the family.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.1] Emotional support: grandparents listen to and reassure children and adults during stressful times.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.2] Transmitting values: older members pass on values, traditions, culture and family history, giving children identity.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.3] Practical help: grandparents assist with homework, meals and household maintenance.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.4] Financial help: grandparents may help adult children with occasional costs, such as a deposit for a house.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.5] Advice and experience: older people help with decisions and act as positive role models.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.6] Responsibilities: respecting parents' rules and boundaries, communicating honestly and not undermining parental decisions.
[hom-family-caring-s3.7.7] As they age, the family has a duty in return to provide transport, care, company, money and emotional support to the older person.
[hom-family-caring-s3.8.b] Sharing a home brings grandparents and grandchildren closer, but it also changes family life for everyone.
[hom-family-caring-s3.8.0] Advantages: time with grandchildren, relaxed relationships because grandparents do not usually have to discipline, and practical help in the home.
[hom-family-caring-s3.8.1] Disadvantages: loss of privacy, differing views on child-rearing, and an extra caring burden as the grandparents age.
