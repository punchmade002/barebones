TARGET hom-dairy-eggs-s6: 6. Eggs: selection, storage, properties and culinary uses
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.b] Eggs are a fresh, perishable food, so choosing well and reading the carton tells you how safe, fresh and traceable they are. Most of what a buyer needs to know is printed on the carton or stamped on the egg.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.0] Choose eggs with clean, unbroken shells from a clean shop, because a cracked shell lets bacteria in. Eggs sold as Class A are not washed.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.1] Eggs are graded by class and by size, which is small, medium or large (and extra large). Shop eggs are Class A.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.2] Check the best-before date on the carton, and use the eggs by it. It is set at no more than 28 days after the egg was laid.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.3] The carton shows the country of origin, and the name, address and registration number of the producer or packer. It also shows the week number (the week the eggs were packed), the class and the size.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.4] The farming method is stated: organic, free-range, barn or caged. Methods differ in animal welfare and price, while nutrition is much the same, though free-range eggs may contain more of some vitamins (B12, niacin).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.5] Each egg is stamped with a producer code, so it can be traced back to its farm and county. The first digit gives the method of production (0 organic, 1 free-range, 2 barn, 3 caged), then IE for Ireland, then a code for the producer.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.6] The Bord Bia Quality Assurance mark confirms that the producer meets its requirements. Many Irish eggs carry it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.0.7] Labels must be clear, legible, indelible and not misleading, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect in Ireland. Organic eggs come from hens reared to organic standards, with no routine antibiotic or growth-hormone treatment and feed free of artificial pesticides and fertilisers.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.b] Salmonella (a food poisoning bacterium) can be in a hen's gut and, rarely, in the egg itself. Controlling it on the farm lowers the risk of food poisoning, so egg producers follow a quality assurance scheme.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.0] Ireland has an EU-approved national salmonella control plan for laying hens, to keep the national flock healthy and the eggs safe.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.1] Incoming hens (young hens, called pullets) must be certified as salmonella free, so infection is not brought onto the farm.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.2] Laying hens are checked for salmonella each month, so any infection is found early.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.3] All feed for laying hens is heat-treated, which kills bacteria in the feed.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.4] Management systems ensure full traceability of every egg, so a problem egg can be tracked back to its source.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.5] All producers and suppliers are inspected and approved.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.1.6] Every system is independently inspected before the Bord Bia Quality mark is awarded, so the mark shows the checks have been passed.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.b] An egg's shell is porous and its quality falls as it ages, so good storage keeps it fresh and safe. The rules follow from the shell and the egg's inner structure.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.0] Store eggs in the fridge at 4°C, in the egg compartment rather than the door, because the door is warmer and the eggs are shaken each time it opens.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.1] Store them pointed end down, away from strongly flavoured foods, because the shell is porous and the eggs absorb smells. Keep them away from cooked foods.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.2] Do not wash the shell, because washing removes the protective cuticle (the thin natural coating) and speeds staling.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.3] Use eggs within the best-before date.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.4] Leftover egg white can be kept in a sealed container in the fridge for 2 to 4 days.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.5] Leftover egg yolk can be kept covered with water in a sealed container for up to 4 days.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.6] Wash hands after handling raw eggs. Do not use cracked or dirty eggs in foods that are not cooked, and cook eggs until firm for vulnerable groups, because raw egg dishes such as mayonnaise and mousse carry a risk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.2.7] A fresh egg sinks and lies flat in water because its air cell is small, while an old egg tilts or floats. A fresh egg also has a thick white and a high, round yolk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.b] Coagulation is the setting of egg protein when heated, which thickens and binds food. It is why a runny egg becomes solid when cooked.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.0] Egg white coagulates at about 60 to 65°C; egg yolk coagulates at about 65 to 70°C.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.1] The protein chains untwist, or denature, and straighten, then bond together around small pockets of water, which traps the water so the egg sets.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.2] Overheating makes the protein clump tighter and squeeze out its water, causing curdling and a tough, rubbery texture. Gentle, low heat and stirring give a creamy custard, while high heat curdles it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.3] Culinary uses, thickening: custards and quiche. Adding egg to a hot liquid slowly prevents lumps.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.4] Culinary uses, binding: burgers, fishcakes, meatballs and stuffings, where the set egg holds the mixture together. Coating: egg and breadcrumbs on fish, and chicken goujons.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.5] Culinary uses, glazing: egg wash on scones, pastry and bread gives gloss and colour. Clarifying: egg white traps solid particles in consommé.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.3.6] Culinary uses, cooking as a dish: boiled, poached, scrambled, fried and baked eggs, and omelettes. Enriching: rice pudding and mashed potato.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.b] Aeration means trapping air in a food. Eggs do this well, which is how cakes and meringues become light.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.0] Whisking or beating egg, the white especially, traps air as many small bubbles.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.1] The heat from whisking partly unfolds (denatures) the egg protein, which forms a thin film that lines up around each air bubble and sets slightly, giving a foam.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.2] This foam is temporary, so it should not be left standing or it collapses. It is stabilised permanently by further heating, as the protein coagulates in the oven, or by a setting agent such as gelatine. Sugar also helps stabilise it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.3] Whole eggs and sugar whisked together aerate sponge cakes and Swiss rolls. Egg and fat creamed together also aerate cake mixtures.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.4] Whisked egg whites make meringues, soufflés, mousses, pavlova and the topping on lemon meringue.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.4.5] For the best foam, use a clean, grease-free bowl and a whisk, because fat or yolk stops the foam forming. Use whites at room temperature and add sugar gradually.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.b] Oil and water do not mix by themselves. Egg yolk can hold them together, which is why it is used in sauces and dressings.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.0] An emulsion is a permanent mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix, such as oil and vinegar, with one broken into tiny droplets spread through the other.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.1] Lecithin, found in the yolk, is a natural emulsifier (a substance that joins liquids that would otherwise separate).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.2] The hydrophilic (water-loving) head of the lecithin molecule attaches to water, and the hydrophobic (water-hating, oil-loving) tail attaches to oil.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.3] This holds the oil and vinegar together and prevents them separating.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.4] To make an emulsion, add the oil drop by drop to the yolk while whisking, because adding it too fast makes it curdle.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.5] Culinary uses: mayonnaise (oil and vinegar), hollandaise and similar sauces (butter and lemon juice), and salad dressings.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.5.6] Culinary uses: blending the fat and sugar in a cake mixture so the batter does not curdle.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.b] Cooking changes an egg's appearance, texture, safety and nutrients. Some changes are wanted and some are signs of overcooking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.0] Colour changes: the white turns from transparent to opaque white, as its protein coagulates (sets). The egg white becomes insoluble when heated.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.1] Heat kills bacteria, including salmonella, so thoroughly cooked eggs are safer.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.2] Vitamin B is lost during cooking, though most other nutrients change little.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.3] Overcooking makes eggs tough and rubbery, and harder to digest, because the protein curdles. Digestibility is best with light cooking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.4] Iron in the yolk and sulphur in the white react to form a green-grey ring around the yolk of an overcooked hard-boiled egg. Cooling the egg quickly in cold water reduces it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s6.6.5] Different cooking methods and times give soft, medium and hard results.

CANDIDATES hom-dairy-eggs-s5: 5. Eggs: structure, nutritional significance and contribution to the diet
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.b] The outside of the egg is built to protect the liquid contents while still letting the egg breathe. The air cell inside it is a useful clue to how old the egg is.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.0] Shell: hard and porous, made mainly of calcium carbonate. It protects the contents, and the tiny pores let gases pass in and out.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.1] The outer coating of the shell (the cuticle, or bloom) reduces the entry of bacteria.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.2] Shell colour, white or brown, depends on the breed of hen, so it does not affect the nutritive value.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.3] Shell membranes: two thin membranes, an outer and an inner one, line the shell and help keep bacteria out.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.4] Air cell (air space): a pocket between the two membranes at the blunt, broad end of the egg. It grows as the egg ages because water evaporates through the shell, so a bigger air cell means an older egg.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.5] Water test for freshness: a fresh egg sinks and lies flat in water because its air cell is small, while an old egg floats because its air cell is large.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.0.6] Diagram: a cross-section of an egg, labelling the shell, shell membranes and air cell.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.b] Inside the shell are two liquid parts, the white and the yolk. Cords and membranes hold the yolk in the centre so it is cushioned from knocks.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.0] Albumen (white): the transparent liquid around the yolk. It is mainly water (about 90%) and protein (albumin, ovalbumin and globulin), and it is fat free.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.1] The albumen has two layers, thick and thin. The thick white is the sign of a fresh egg.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.2] Chalazae: two twisted cords of albumen at each end of the yolk that anchor the yolk in the centre of the egg. They are not the same thing as the germinal disc.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.3] Yolk: the yellow centre, held in the vitelline membrane. It holds the fat, lecithin, cholesterol, protein (vitellin and livetin), the fat-soluble vitamins and minerals, including iron.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.4] Yolk colour depends on the hen's feed, because of carotenoid pigments (yellow-orange colouring) in it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.5] Germinal disc (blastodisc): a small pale spot on the surface of the yolk. It would develop into a chick if the egg were fertilised, but eggs sold in shops are normally unfertilised. It is not a blood spot or an embryo.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.1.6] Diagram: a labelled cross-section of the egg showing the shell, outer and inner shell membranes, air cell, thick and thin albumen, chalazae, vitelline membrane, yolk and germinal disc.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.b] An egg packs protein, fat, vitamins and minerals into a small package, but it is missing carbohydrate, fibre and vitamin C. For each nutrient, learn what the egg supplies and what the body uses it for.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.0] Protein is about 13% of the egg. It is high biological value (HBV) with a biological value of 100%, because it contains all the essential amino acids in the proportions the body needs. It is used for growth and repair and for making hormones and enzymes.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.1] White protein is albumin (ovalbumin) and globulin. Yolk protein is vitellin and livetin.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.2] Fat is about 12%, all in the yolk. It is mostly saturated and is in a very fine emulsion because of lecithin (a natural emulsifier in the yolk), so it is easily digested. It gives heat and energy.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.3] The white is fat free, and cholesterol is found only in the yolk. The yolk supplies most of the energy of the egg.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.4] Eggs contain no carbohydrate (0%) and no fibre, so they are served with carbohydrate foods such as toast or potatoes, and with fibre.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.5] Fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K are found in the yolk. A keeps membranes and eyes healthy, D builds bones and teeth, E is an antioxidant and K helps blood clot.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.6] B group vitamins are found in both the white and the yolk. B1 and B2 help release energy, and niacin and B12 are needed for metabolism. Free-range eggs can contain higher levels of some vitamins, such as niacin and B12, than eggs from caged hens. Eggs lack vitamin C.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.2.7] Minerals make up about 1% of the egg: calcium and phosphorus for bones and teeth, iron in the yolk for making haemoglobin, and zinc and sulphur. The egg is about 75% water, and the white is mostly water.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.b] Eggs earn their place in the diet because they are cheap, nutritious, easy to digest and easy to cook. Their weak points, saturated fat and cholesterol and the missing nutrients, are dealt with by moderation and by what they are served with.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.0] Eggs are cheap and nutritious, so they suit people on a low budget. They are a protein alternative to meat and fish.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.1] Eggs supply HBV protein for ovo-lacto vegetarians, who eat dairy products and eggs but not meat or fish.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.2] Eggs are easily digested, because the fat is already emulsified and the protein is soft when lightly cooked. This makes them suitable for children, older people, invalids and convalescents.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.3] Their protein supports growth and repair in children, adolescents and pregnant women.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.4] Eggs are quick and easy to cook, which saves fuel, and are versatile in sweet and savoury dishes. They can be eaten alone, or as a meal with carbohydrate such as egg on toast or an omelette with potatoes.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.5] Eggs contain saturated fat and cholesterol, so people with raised cholesterol or heart disease, or anyone limiting cholesterol, should eat eggs in moderation.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.6] Eggs lack vitamin C and fibre, so serve them with complex carbohydrate, vegetables, fruit and wholegrain foods to balance the diet.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s5.3.7] Eggs can carry Salmonella (a food poisoning bacterium), so cook them thoroughly for babies, older people, pregnant women and the ill, and wash hands and utensils after handling raw egg.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s5: 5. Properties
[hom-proteins-s5.0.b] Cooking and food preparation change protein in two linked steps. First the folded protein chain unfolds (denaturation), then the unfolded chains join up and set (coagulation). Both are permanent, and together they explain most of what happens to eggs, meat, fish and milk.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.0] Denaturation is the irreversible unfolding of the secondary and tertiary structure of a protein (the folded shape of its chain), without breaking its peptide bonds (the links between amino acids), so the chain keeps its amino acids but changes shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.1] Denatured chains lose the original shape and functional properties of the protein, because the function of a protein depends on its shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.2] Denatured chains can then link to one another, and this linking is coagulation.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.3] Coagulation is the setting of denatured protein into a solid or semi-solid mass, which is why a runny raw egg becomes firm when cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.4] Denaturation changes the texture and appearance of a food, and denatured protein is more digestible, so cooked protein foods are easier to digest than raw ones.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.5] Denaturation can be caused by heat, mechanical action (such as whisking), chemicals (such as acid and salt) and enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.6] Egg white coagulates from about 60 degrees C, turning from transparent to opaque and firm; the yolk sets at about 68 degrees C. These are approximate temperatures because eggs set over a range.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.7] Fish flesh changes from translucent to opaque as its protein coagulates during cooking, which is how you can tell fish is cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.8] Examples of coagulation in cookery: omelette, quiche, boiled egg, meringue, soufflé and lemon meringue pie.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.b] A foam is a mass of air bubbles held in a thin film. Egg white makes a good foam because its protein, albumen, can be stretched into a film around each bubble and then set by heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.0] Whisking or beating stretches and partly denatures the albumen chains, which unfold and arrange themselves around the air bubbles. This is mechanical action, not heat, that creates the foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.1] The protein film around each bubble holds the trapped air, forming a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.2] Gentle heat in cooking then coagulates the albumen around the bubbles, setting the foam permanently, which is why a baked meringue stays firm.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.3] Over-whisking denatures the protein too far and breaks the film, so the foam becomes grainy and collapses.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.4] Examples: meringue, pavlova, soufflé and whisked sponge cake.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.b] A gel is a liquid held in a soft solid. Some proteins, especially gelatine, can do this: they dissolve in hot liquid and set into a network as it cools.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.0] A gel forms when protein chains (gelatine) dissolved in a hot liquid cool and link into a network that traps the liquid, such as water.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.1] Gelatine is the main protein used to make gels in cookery.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.2] A firmer gel results from a higher concentration of gelatine, because more chains form a denser network.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.3] Examples: jelly, aspic, mousses and cheesecake.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.b] Dry heat cooks food without added liquid. It acts mainly on the surface protein, which is why roasted and grilled foods firm up and brown.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.0] Dry heat (roasting, baking, grilling and frying) denatures and coagulates the surface protein, causing shrinkage and firmness.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.1] Prolonged dry heat dries the muscle protein and makes meat tough and dry, so overcooked meat is chewy.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.2] Dry heat at high temperatures produces the Maillard reaction, which browns the surface of meat, bread and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.b] The golden-brown crust on bread and roast meat is not caused by sugar alone or by enzymes. It comes from protein and sugar reacting together under dry heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.0] The Maillard reaction is non-enzymic browning, which happens when the amino acids of protein react with a reducing sugar (a sugar such as glucose) on heating.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.1] It needs high heat and a dry surface with little moisture; it does not need enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.2] It produces brown colour and roasted, savoury flavour, as in a toasted bread crust, roast meat, grilled or toasted cheese and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.b] Moist heat cooks food in liquid or steam at lower temperatures. For meat it does something dry heat cannot: it changes the tough connective tissue into a soft jelly.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.0] Moist heat (boiling, stewing, poaching and braising) converts the collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) to gelatine, which tenderises meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.1] Collagen conversion needs long, slow cooking in moisture, so cheaper cuts of meat, which contain a lot of connective tissue, become tender.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.2] Prolonged high heat toughens muscle protein, so gentle moist heat is used for tender results.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.3] Heat breaks the weak bonds that hold the folded structure, which is why heat denatures protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.b] Whisking, beating and mincing physically stretch and break protein, so they change texture without any heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.0] Whisking, beating and mincing unfold protein chains; whisking egg white is the example that forms a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.1] Kneading bread dough develops the gluten (the protein of wheat flour) into an elastic network that traps the carbon dioxide from yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.2] Beating meat with a mallet breaks up the muscle fibres and tenderises it.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.3] Excessive agitation denatures protein so far that it becomes tough or curdled.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.b] Acids and salt change protein without any heating. That is why a splash of lemon juice curdles milk and a marinade softens meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.0] Acids such as lemon juice and vinegar denature protein; lemon juice added to milk curdles it, which is also how milk sours.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.1] A marinade of acid, such as vinegar, wine or lemon, tenderises meat by denaturing its surface protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.2] Alkalis also denature protein and change its texture.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.3] Cheese-making uses acid to help set the milk protein, and salt to increase the firmness of the curd (the solid part of the milk), which controls the texture of the cheese.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.4] The acid in the stomach denatures food protein before enzymes hydrolyse (break) it, so the chains are easier for digestive enzymes to reach.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.b] Enzymes are biological catalysts, and some of them work on protein. Cooks use them to set milk for cheese and to soften tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.0] Rennin, traditionally from the stomach of young calves, coagulates casein (the main protein of milk) for cheese-making.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.1] Papain, from papaya, is a proteolytic enzyme (one that breaks down protein) that tenderises tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.2] Proteolytic enzymes hydrolyse peptide bonds, so too much enzyme action makes protein soft and mushy.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.3] Enzymes in the digestive tract hydrolyse protein to amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.4] Heat inactivates enzymes, which is why cooking stops enzymic tenderising.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.b] This investigation shows denaturation in practice by comparing egg white that is left alone, heated and whisked.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.0] Aim: to show how heat and mechanical action denature egg white protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.1] Independent variable: the treatment (heating in a water bath, or whisking); dependent variable: the change in appearance and texture of the white.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.2] Controlled variables: the same volume of egg white, the same starting temperature and the same type of dish.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.3] Control: place an untreated sample of egg white in a labelled dish for comparison.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.4] Method: heat a second sample gently in a water bath until it sets, then whisk a third sample until stiff.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.5] Safety: use fresh eggs, wash hands after handling raw egg, and do not taste raw egg white, because raw egg can carry bacteria.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.6] Expected result: the heated white turns opaque and firm, and the whisked white forms a stable foam; the untreated white stays clear and runny.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.7] Conclusion: heat and whisking both denature the protein of egg white and change its appearance and texture.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-lipids-s4.0.b] As a fat is heated it passes through three different stages, each at a higher temperature than the last: it melts, then it smokes, then it can burst into flame. Knowing these temperatures explains how fats are chosen and used in cooking.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.0] Melting point is the temperature at which a solid fat turns to liquid. Fats are mixtures of triglycerides, so they melt over a range rather than at one sharp temperature, and solid fats melt at about 30–40 °C. Oils are already liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.1] Fats with a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points, because their kinked chains do not pack tightly. Fats with more saturated fatty acids, whose chains are straight and pack closely, have higher melting points.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.2] Smoke point is the temperature at which an overheated lipid begins to decompose and gives off a continuous blue haze. Smoke points are about 200 °C for solid fats and about 250 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.3] On decomposition the fat splits into glycerol and fatty acids, and the glycerol breaks down further to release acrolein, which has an acrid, irritating smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.4] Free fatty acids, impurities and food particles left in the oil lower its smoke point, and repeated use builds these up, so used or dirty fat smokes at a lower temperature. This is why frying fat is strained and replaced.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.5] A fat with a high smoke point suits frying, while butter, with a lower smoke point, burns.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.6] Flash point is the temperature at which the vapour from an overheated lipid ignites spontaneously and bursts into flame. Flash points are about 310 °C for solid fats and about 325 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.7] A fat that reaches its flash point is a serious fire hazard. Never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended, never heat fat until it smokes, and smother a fat fire with a fire blanket or lid, never water.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.b] This practical compares how common fats and oils behave on heating, and links the results to their fatty acids and to impurities. Care is needed because the fats become very hot.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.0] Aim: to compare the melting, smoke and flash points of common fats and oils, for example butter, lard, olive oil and sunflower oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.1] 1. Use a small amount of each fat or oil, so any smoke or fire is limited.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.2] 2. Heat each gently, preferably on a hot plate rather than a flame, which is safer for a class demonstration, and read the temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.3] 3. Record the order in which the fats melt, and which fats smoke first. Do not heat beyond the smoke point.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.4] Conclusion: link the order of melting points to how saturated the fatty acids are, and the point at which smoke appears to the fat's impurities.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.b] Oil and water do not mix normally, but they can be forced to, and in some foods they stay mixed. This is the basis of mayonnaise, butter, milk and many sauces.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.0] An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix (immiscible), such as oil and water, with one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.1] Vigorous shaking, whisking or blending forces the liquids together by breaking one liquid into droplets that spread through the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.2] A temporary emulsion, such as French dressing (oil and vinegar), separates again on standing because nothing keeps the droplets apart.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.3] A permanent (stable) emulsion, such as mayonnaise, hollandaise sauce, ice cream, cream, milk or butter, stays mixed because an emulsifying agent coats the droplets.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.4] An emulsifier has a hydrophilic (water-attracting) end and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) end, which sit at the boundary between the oil and the water.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.5] In an oil-in-water emulsion, oil droplets are dispersed in water, as in mayonnaise, milk and cream.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.6] In a water-in-oil emulsion, water droplets are dispersed in fat, as in butter, margarine and some spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.b] Making mayonnaise and a French dressing side by side shows the difference between a temporary and a permanent emulsion.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.0] Aim: to show emulsion formation and the effect of an emulsifier, using mayonnaise (egg yolk, oil, and vinegar or lemon juice) and a French dressing (oil and vinegar).
[hom-lipids-s4.3.1] 1. Make the mayonnaise by adding the oil to the egg yolk slowly while whisking, so the droplets are small and evenly spread.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.2] 2. Shake the oil and vinegar together for the French dressing, and leave both to stand.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.3] Result: the French dressing separates, but the mayonnaise stays stable because the egg yolk is the emulsifier. Comparing with and without an emulsifier shows this.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.b] Two kinds of additive help keep an emulsion together, and they do different jobs. An emulsifier makes the emulsion, while a stabiliser helps it stay that way.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.0] An emulsifying agent is a molecule with a hydrophilic (water-loving) end and a hydrophobic (water-hating, fat-loving) end. The hydrophilic end attaches to the water and the hydrophobic end to the oil, so the droplets are coated and cannot join together.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.1] Emulsifying agents include lecithin (found in egg yolk and soya; the food additive E322), mustard, and mono- and diglycerides such as glycerol monostearate (GMS; mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids are E471).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.2] The function of an emulsifier is to reduce the tension between oil and water so they mix, and to make and keep the emulsion stable.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.3] Stabilisers do not form the emulsion. They thicken the water phase and stop droplets joining together (coalescing).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.4] Examples of stabilisers are starch, pectin, gelatine and gums such as xanthan gum and guar gum. They are used in dressings, sauces, ice cream and low-fat spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.b] Some fats can be spread or shaped without being runny or rock hard. This property, called plasticity, is why butter and margarine behave so differently from oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.0] Plasticity is the ability of a fat to be shaped, spread or worked (creamed, rubbed in) at a given temperature without being fully liquid or fully hard.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.1] It is due to a mixture of solid and liquid fat within the fat: the solid fat forms a network that holds the liquid fat. The proportion of saturated fatty acids only decides how much of the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.2] Butter, lard, margarine and shortening are plastic over a range of temperatures. Oils are not plastic, a very unsaturated fat is too liquid to hold its shape, and a very saturated fat is hard and brittle when cold.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.3] Plastic fats are valued in pastry and cake making because they can be creamed, which traps air, or rubbed into flour and layered. Plasticity also gives butter and margarine their spreadability.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.b] Liquid oils can be turned into solid fats by changing their fatty acids. This process, hydrogenation, is how margarine is made from vegetable oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.0] Hydrogenation is the addition of hydrogen to the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, using a nickel catalyst under heat and pressure.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.1] It converts unsaturated fatty acids to saturated ones, so a liquid oil becomes a semi-solid or solid fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.2] It is used to make margarine and spreads, hard margarines, shortenings and baking fats from liquid vegetable oils. The fats are solid, plastic and more stable against oxidative rancidity.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.3] Partial hydrogenation forms trans fatty acids, so the process is now controlled. Full hydrogenation gives a hard saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.b] Rancidity is the spoilage of fats and oils, which gives them an unpleasant taste and smell. It happens in two different ways, with different causes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.0] Oxidative rancidity: oxygen from the air reacts at the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, which also breaks the chain. This produces aldehydes and ketones, which cause the rancid smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.1] Light, heat and traces of metal ions speed up oxidative rancidity. It affects polyunsaturated oils most because they have the most double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.2] Hydrolytic rancidity: water, together with the enzyme lipase from the food itself or from micro-organisms such as moulds and bacteria, breaks fats down into fatty acids and glycerol. The free fatty acids, for example butyric acid in butter, give the smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.3] Hydrolytic rancidity can occur in a freezer, because lipase is not destroyed by freezing. Blanching vegetables before freezing destroys these enzymes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.4] Fats are protected from rancidity when stored in airtight, opaque containers in a cool, dark place, kept dry, bought in small amounts and used before the date. Refrigerating or freezing, using less-unsaturated fats, adding antioxidants and not reusing frying oil repeatedly also help.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.b] Antioxidants are the main chemical defence against oxidative rancidity. They are added to fats and oils so that they keep their flavour for longer.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.0] An antioxidant is a substance that slows oxidation. It delays oxidative rancidity by reacting with oxygen or free radicals before they attack the double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin E (tocopherols, E306), present in some vegetable oils, and vitamin C (ascorbic acid, E300).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.2] Synthetic antioxidants added to fats include butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, E320) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, E321).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.3] Antioxidants are added to oils, margarines, spreads, snack foods and fried foods to keep their flavour and extend their shelf life. They appear in the ingredients list by name or E number under the additives rules (Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008).

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s6: 6. Safe food preparation
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.b] Almost every rule for handling food comes from two facts about food poisoning bacteria: they multiply fastest in warm food, and they travel easily from one food to another. Knowing these makes the individual rules easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.0] Food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest in the danger zone, 5°C to 63°C. This is why safe preparation keeps food cold, hot, or moving quickly through that range.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.1] Cross-contamination is the transfer of bacteria from raw food to cooked or ready-to-eat food, directly or by hands, chopping boards, cloths, utensils or meat juices. This is why raw and cooked foods are kept apart.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.2] Freezing does not kill bacteria, it only stops them multiplying, and thawing lets them start multiplying again. So frozen food must be handled with care once it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.b] Storage aims to keep food cold or frozen so bacteria cannot multiply, and to stop bacteria passing from one food to another. Short questions usually ask for three to six storage guidelines.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.0] Cover all food and store it in sealed containers, which prevents drying out, odour transfer and cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.1] Keep perishable food (food that spoils quickly) in the fridge at 5°C or below, which slows the growth of bacteria. Check the fridge and freezer temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.2] Store raw meat, poultry and fish on the lowest shelf, on a plate and loosely covered with the shop wrapping removed, so that juices cannot drip onto cooked or ready-to-eat food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.3] Keep raw and cooked foods apart, covered or in sealed containers, so bacteria cannot pass between them.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.4] Cool cooked food quickly before refrigerating or freezing it, and do not overpack the fridge, so that cold air can circulate around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.5] Check the use-by or best-before date of each food and follow the storage instructions on its label. Use-by is a safety date for highly perishable food, while best-before is a quality date.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.6] Remove fruit and vegetables from plastic wrapping, which encourages mould, and check them regularly for mould.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.7] Store dry foods in a cool, dry, well-ventilated cupboard, in vermin-proof containers, and keep storage areas clean.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.8] Keep perishable food out of the fridge for the shortest possible time while it is being prepared, because food left in warm air soon enters the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.b] Freezing keeps food safe for a long time, but only if it is frozen and thawed properly. Bacteria are not killed by freezing, so the care taken when thawing matters.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.0] Freeze food quickly at −25°C, then store it at −18°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.1] Defrost frozen food completely before cooking, preferably in the fridge on the bottom shelf, so that it thaws slowly and any drips cannot reach other food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.2] Never refreeze food that has thawed, because bacteria multiply while it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.b] Cooking and reheating must heat food right through to kill bacteria, and hot food must then stay hot or be eaten promptly. Otherwise bacteria multiply again in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.0] Cook food thoroughly, using the right time and temperature, and turn or stir it so that heat reaches the centre. Cook meat, poultry and minced meat right through, to a core temperature of at least 72°C for poultry.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.1] Reheat food quickly until it is above 100°C throughout, and reheat it only once.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.2] High-risk foods such as stews and gravies should be brought to boiling when reheated, because their moist, protein-rich nature suits bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.3] Cover food when reheating. In a microwave, stir or turn the food and allow standing time, so that heat reaches all parts evenly, and follow the appliance's instructions.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.4] Serve cooked food immediately. If holding it hot, keep it above 63°C, because bacteria multiply rapidly between 5°C and 63°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.5] Eat food that has been held hot within about 90 minutes (1.5 hours).
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.6] Do not keep food in the danger zone. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate them and use them soon.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.b] People carry food poisoning bacteria on their hands, hair, skin and clothes, so the person preparing food is a major source of contamination. Examiners usually ask for four to six personal hygiene points.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.0] Wash hands in hot water with liquid soap, and a nailbrush for the nails, before handling food, because hands carry food poisoning bacteria. Dry them on a clean towel or paper towel.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.1] Wash hands after the toilet, sneezing or coughing, changing nappies, handling pets, handling rubbish and handling raw meat.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.2] Keep nails short and clean and remove jewellery before preparing food, because dirt and bacteria collect under nails and around jewellery.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.3] Tie hair back or cover it. Wear a clean apron or clothing, and change it if it becomes soiled.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.4] Cover cuts, boils and sores with a brightly coloured waterproof plaster, so that infection cannot pass to food and the plaster can be seen if it falls into the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.5] Do not cough or sneeze over food, and do not taste food with your fingers or lick your fingers.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.6] Do not prepare or serve food for others while suffering from diarrhoea or vomiting, because the illness can pass to the people eating it.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.b] A clean kitchen with sensible habits stops bacteria building up on surfaces and equipment and passing to food. Four kitchen hygiene practices is a common exam question.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.0] Clean and disinfect work surfaces often, and wash equipment in hot water and detergent. Clean as you go.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.1] Use colour-coded chopping boards and separate utensils for raw and cooked foods, to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.2] Never prepare raw and cooked foods on the same surface.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.3] Handle food as little as possible during preparation, and keep it covered, because every touch can pass on bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.4] Floors, walls and work surfaces should be easy to clean, and the kitchen should have good lighting.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.5] Effective ventilation reduces condensation, which encourages the growth of micro-organisms.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.6] The kitchen should have a clean water supply and no access for pets or pests.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.7] Keep a covered waste bin and empty it regularly, and change clean cloths and dishtowels often.
