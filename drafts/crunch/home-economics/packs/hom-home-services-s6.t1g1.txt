TARGET hom-home-services-s6: 6. Insulation
[hom-home-services-s6.0.b] A warm house loses heat to the cold outside all the time. Insulation does not make heat; it slows the escape of heat, so less fuel is needed to keep the house warm.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.0] Heat leaves a house in four ways: by conduction through solid parts such as walls and glass, by convection (warm air rising and leaking out), by radiation (heat given off as rays), and by air leakage through gaps. Insulation reduces these losses, so less fuel is needed.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.1] Insulating materials are poor conductors of heat. Still air is a very poor conductor, so poor conductors such as still air, polystyrene, fibreglass, mineral wool, sheep's wool, cellulose and foam trap air in small pockets, which reduces heat transfer by conduction.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.2] Fibre and foam insulation holds air in small cells that are too small for convection currents to form. Because the air cannot circulate, it cannot carry heat by convection, which is why a big open gap is not insulation.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.3] Reflective foil and low-emissivity (low-E) coatings (thin coatings that give off little heat) reflect radiant heat back, so they reduce heat loss by radiation.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.4] Draught proofing stops heat leaving in moving air, because it seals the gaps around doors, windows and floorboards where air leaks.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.5] Insulation also keeps a house cool in hot weather, because it slows heat moving in as well as out.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.b] Warm air rises, so the roof is a main route of heat loss. Insulating the attic is the cheapest and most effective place to start.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.0] Fibre blanket or quilt: strips of mineral or glass fibre, or wool, are laid between and over the ceiling joists (the beams that carry the ceiling).
[hom-home-services-s6.1.1] Loose fill: pellets of polystyrene or vermiculite are poured between the joists.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.2] Sprayed or foam insulation: foam is applied between the roof rafters (the sloping beams that hold the roof), where it expands and sets into a solid layer.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.3] Blown fibre: cellulose or mineral fibre is blown into the roof space to cover it evenly.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.4] Rigid boards are fitted between the rafters in a converted attic, where the roof space is used as a room.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.5] Once the attic floor is insulated, the attic becomes colder, so lag the cold water tank and attic pipes to stop them freezing. Insulate and draught-proof the hatch, and keep the eaves ventilated.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.6] Diagram: cross-section of a roof showing the joists, the ceiling below and an insulation layer laid between and over the joists, with the roof space above.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.b] Walls are a large surface through which heat escapes. The method depends on whether the wall has a cavity (a gap between two layers of wall) or is solid.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.0] Cavity wall insulation: a cavity wall has an outer and an inner leaf (layer) with a gap between them. The cavity is filled with polystyrene beads, foam or mineral wool blown in through small holes, and even an unfilled cavity holds some still air.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.1] Internal dry lining: insulated plasterboard is fixed to the inside face of the wall, which adds insulation on the room side. It reduces room size and takes time to fit, but needs no scaffolding.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.2] External insulation: boards of polystyrene or mineral wool are fixed to the outside of the wall and finished with reinforced, waterproof render. This keeps the wall itself warm and avoids thermal bridges (cold spots where heat escapes), but needs planning checks.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.3] Solid walls have no cavity, so they can only be insulated from the inside (dry lining) or the outside (external insulation).
[hom-home-services-s6.2.4] Diagram: cross-section of a cavity wall showing the outer leaf, the insulation in the cavity, the inner leaf and an internal dry lining.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.b] Heat also escapes through floors, glass, gaps around doors and bare pipes. Each has its own simple method.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.0] Floors: seal the gaps between floorboards, add insulation between the joists on suspended floors, and lay a carpet with underlay. Rigid insulation goes under a new concrete floor. All of these reduce heat loss through the floor.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.1] Double glazing: two panes are separated by a sealed gap of air or argon (a gas used instead of air in some units). The trapped gas is a poor conductor and cannot circulate, so conduction and convection across the gap are reduced.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.2] Triple glazing adds a third pane and a second sealed gap for further insulation.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.3] Low-E glazing: a thin coating on the pane reflects radiant heat back into the room.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.4] Secondary glazing (an extra window fitted inside the existing one), heavy lined curtains and blinds close to the glass, and shutters trap a layer of still air against cold glass.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.5] Doors and gaps: draught excluders, brush strips on doors, a letterbox flap, keyhole covers and sealing strips around windows and floorboards stop air leaking in and out. A porch adds a buffer of still air.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.6] Lagging: an insulating jacket or factory-fitted foam on the hot-water cylinder and sleeves on exposed pipes save heat and help prevent pipes freezing.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.7] Insulation reduces condensation, because warm inner surfaces stay above the temperature at which moisture condenses. However, an airtight, well-insulated house needs controlled ventilation.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.b] Examiners often ask for the benefits of insulation. Each one follows from less heat escaping.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.0] Lower heating bills, because less heat escapes and the heating runs for shorter periods.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.1] Comfort: a steadier indoor temperature with no cold surfaces and fewer draughts.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.2] Less noise, because insulation absorbs sound from outside and between rooms.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.3] Less fuel burned, so lower carbon dioxide emissions and less use of fossil fuel, which makes a more sustainable house.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.4] Less condensation and damp, because inner wall and window surfaces stay warm. This means healthier air and less mould.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.5] A better Building Energy Rating (BER), which rates a dwelling's energy performance on a scale from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient) and is run by SEAI. A better BER can also raise the value of the property.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.6] SEAI grants may help with the cost of insulating.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.b] You may be asked to assess the insulation in your own home and suggest improvements. The method is to inspect, rank the problems and justify each recommendation.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.0] Inspect the attic for the depth and coverage of insulation. Check whether joists are left uncovered and whether the tank and pipes are lagged.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.1] Check doors and windows for draughts by feeling for cold air around the frames on a windy day, and note the type of glazing.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.2] Find out the wall type (cavity or solid) and check whether the hot-water cylinder has a jacket.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.3] Note cold rooms, cold surfaces and any signs of condensation or damp.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.4] Recommend improvements in order of cost and effect: draught proofing and attic insulation first, then a cylinder jacket and lined curtains, then wall insulation and glazing.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.5] Justify each recommendation by the heat it saves and its cost, and mention SEAI grants and the BER.

CANDIDATES hom-home-services-s5: 5. Heating
[hom-home-services-s5.0.b] Heat is a form of energy that always moves from warmer places to cooler ones. It does so in three ways, and every heating system and control in this topic relies on one or more of them.
[hom-home-services-s5.0.0] Conduction: heat passes through a solid because vibrating particles pass energy to their neighbours. Metals conduct well, so they feel cold and carry heat away quickly; air, wool and polystyrene conduct poorly, so they are used to keep heat in.
[hom-home-services-s5.0.1] Convection: in a liquid or gas, the warmed part becomes less dense and rises, and cooler fluid moves in to replace it. This sets up a circulating current, which is how warmth spreads through room air or water.
[hom-home-services-s5.0.2] Radiation: heat travels as infrared waves, which need no material to carry them. The waves pass through air without heating it and warm the objects they hit, so a bar fire or a sunlit wall heats whatever it faces.
[hom-home-services-s5.0.3] A radiator heats a room mainly by convection, because the air beside it warms and rises. Some heat is also given off by radiation from its surface.
[hom-home-services-s5.0.4] Solids, liquids and gases expand when heated, and different metals expand by different amounts. This is the basis of the thermostat and of the expansion tank in a central heating system.
[hom-home-services-s5.1.b] Thermal comfort is the feeling that the temperature of a room suits the people in it, their clothing and what they are doing. Heating a home well means controlling more than the air temperature.
[hom-home-services-s5.1.0] Six factors determine thermal comfort: air temperature, the temperature of surrounding surfaces (walls, windows, floor), humidity, air movement (draughts), clothing and activity level.
[hom-home-services-s5.1.1] A cold wall or single-glazed window makes a person feel cold even when the air temperature is correct, because the body loses heat by radiation to cold surfaces.
[hom-home-services-s5.1.2] Each room needs a different temperature because people do different things in it: living spaces need more warmth than bedrooms, where people are under bedclothes.
[hom-home-services-s5.1.3] Recommended temperatures: sitting room 19 to 23 °C, living room 17 to 21 °C, kitchen 15 to 19 °C, bathroom 16 to 19 °C and bedroom 10 to 16 °C.
[hom-home-services-s5.1.4] Babies, older people and people who are ill need warm rooms with a steady temperature, and older people need at least 20 °C.
[hom-home-services-s5.2.b] Thermal comfort is kept steady by heating that suits the house, by controls that switch it on and off, and by reducing heat loss.
[hom-home-services-s5.2.0] A heating system sized to the house gives enough heat without wasting fuel, because too small a system never warms the rooms and too large a one overheats them.
[hom-home-services-s5.2.1] Zoning gives each area its own control, so rooms are heated only when and to the level they are needed.
[hom-home-services-s5.2.2] Timers, thermostats and thermostatic radiator valves control when heat is supplied and how much.
[hom-home-services-s5.2.3] Insulation, draught proofing and controlled ventilation reduce heat loss and cold surfaces without creating draughts.
[hom-home-services-s5.2.4] Clothing is also a control, since adding or removing layers changes how warm a person feels without changing the heating.
[hom-home-services-s5.3.b] A thermostat is a temperature-sensing switch. It turns a heat source off at a set temperature and back on as it cools, so the temperature stays steady without anyone watching it.
[hom-home-services-s5.3.0] Its working part is a bimetal strip, made of two metals, brass and invar, bonded together so they cannot slide apart.
[hom-home-services-s5.3.1] Brass expands much more than invar when heated, so the strip bends away from the contact and breaks the electric circuit, switching the appliance off.
[hom-home-services-s5.3.2] As the strip cools it straightens and reconnects the circuit, switching the appliance on again.
[hom-home-services-s5.3.3] Diagram: a bimetal strip with brass on one side and invar on the other, shown curved when hot and straight when cool, with the contacts that make and break the circuit.
[hom-home-services-s5.4.b] The same bimetal principle is used wherever a temperature must be held steady, and in heating there are different thermostats for different jobs.
[hom-home-services-s5.4.0] Bimetal thermostats operate kettles (each with its own control), irons, ovens, immersion heaters, electric blankets, refrigerators and freezers, and electric showers.
[hom-home-services-s5.4.1] A boiler thermostat stops the boiler when the water reaches its target temperature and restarts it when the water falls.
[hom-home-services-s5.4.2] A room thermostat senses air temperature and switches the boiler or heating circuit on or off to keep the room at its set level. It is placed in a main living area away from direct heat, so a nearby fire does not give a false reading.
[hom-home-services-s5.4.3] A cylinder thermostat keeps the stored hot water at its set temperature.
[hom-home-services-s5.4.4] A thermostatic radiator valve (TRV) is fitted to one radiator and opens or closes to control the flow of hot water into it, so each room can have its own setting.
[hom-home-services-s5.5.b] Controls make sure heat is supplied only when and where it is needed. This saves fuel and keeps a home comfortable.
[hom-home-services-s5.5.0] A programmer (timer) switches heating and hot water on and off at preset times, such as before the family wakes and before they return home, so rooms are not heated when empty. It may be mechanical, electronic or smart.
[hom-home-services-s5.5.1] Timers and thermostats used together prevent heating empty rooms and overheating, so less fuel is burned and bills are lower.
[hom-home-services-s5.5.2] Zoning gives each area its own timer and thermostat, so bedrooms, living spaces and unused rooms can run at different settings.
[hom-home-services-s5.5.3] Smart controls allow a household to change heating schedules from a phone when its plans change.
[hom-home-services-s5.6.b] Heating options are first described by how much of the dwelling they heat. The four types are easily confused, so learn what makes each one different.
[hom-home-services-s5.6.0] Full central heating: one boiler heats water that is pumped to radiators throughout the whole dwelling, and it can also supply hot water.
[hom-home-services-s5.6.1] Partial central heating: radiators heat only some rooms, usually the main living areas.
[hom-home-services-s5.6.2] Background heating: a low, steady level of heat is supplied to the whole house, with local heaters used for extra warmth.
[hom-home-services-s5.6.3] Local heating: individual room heaters, such as electric fires, gas heaters or stoves, heat only the room they are in.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.b] Systems also differ in how the heat is made and delivered. Some heat water, some use electricity directly, and some draw on renewable sources.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.0] Storage heating (a dry system, with no water): electric elements inside thermal blocks of fireclay or concrete are charged on off-peak night electricity and release heat through the day, often with an afternoon boost.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.1] Underfloor heating: pipes (water) or electric cables beneath the floor warm the floor, which then heats the room by radiation and convection. It leaves walls clear and works well with low-temperature heat sources.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.2] Ground-source heating: pipes buried in the ground collect heat, and a heat pump raises it to a temperature suitable for heating the house.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.3] A heat pump (ground-source or air-source) uses a refrigerant to take heat from the ground, air or water, and a compressor raises its temperature before the heat passes to the heating water. It runs on electricity but delivers more heat than the electrical energy it uses.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.4] Heat pumps work best in a well-insulated house with underfloor heating or large radiators, and they qualify for SEAI grants.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.5] Passive solar heating: large south-facing windows admit winter sunlight, which the walls and floors absorb and release as heat.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.6] Active solar heating: solar collectors absorb sunlight and heat a fluid, which transfers the heat to water stored in a cylinder.
[hom-home-services-s5.7.7] Biomass boilers burning wood pellets or wood chips are another low-carbon option.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.b] No single system suits every home, so a choice weighs cost, the house and the people who live in it. Examiners ask for these factors as a list, each with a reason.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.0] Installation, running and maintenance costs must all be considered, not only the purchase price.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.1] Fuel price and availability differ between oil, gas, solid fuel and electricity.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.2] Energy efficiency: an efficient system uses less fuel for the same heat output, and a condensing boiler recovers heat from its flue gases.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.3] Environmental impact: fossil fuels release carbon dioxide, while wood and solar energy are renewable sources.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.4] Heat output must match the house: floor area, insulation, number of occupants and hot-water demand decide the size of system needed.
[hom-home-services-s5.8.5] Family needs: households with babies, older people or illness need reliable and precise control of warmth.
[hom-home-services-s5.9.b] Beyond cost and efficiency, a system has to fit the home, be safe to live with and be easy to run.
[hom-home-services-s5.9.0] Convenience and control: timers, thermostats and smart controls make a system easier to run economically.
[hom-home-services-s5.9.1] Safety: radiator surfaces and any open flame need consideration where young children are present.
[hom-home-services-s5.9.2] Space and appearance: boilers, tanks, fuel storage and radiators take up wall or floor space, while underfloor systems leave walls clear.
[hom-home-services-s5.9.3] Hot water needs and available grants also affect the choice.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.b] A wet system heats water in a boiler and circulates it round a closed loop of pipes to radiators. It is the usual system to describe in an exam, because it shows all three kinds of heat transfer.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.0] Parts: boiler, circulating pump, small-bore pipes (flow and return), radiators, a feed and expansion tank with an overflow pipe, and a hot-water cylinder with a heat exchanger coil.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.1] The boiler burns oil, gas or solid fuel, or uses electricity. Solar collectors feed a cylinder rather than the boiler.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.2] Water heated in the boiler expands, becomes less dense and rises up the flow pipe to the radiators by convection.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.3] A pump drives the water round the closed loop, so the system works in long pipe runs where natural convection would be too weak.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.4] Each radiator warms the air beside it by convection and gives off heat from its surface by radiation.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.5] The cooler water returns through the return pipe to the boiler to be reheated.
[hom-home-services-s5.10.6] Diagram: plan of the closed loop showing the boiler, pump, flow and return pipes, radiators, expansion tank in the roof space and hot-water cylinder, with arrows showing the direction of water flow.
[hom-home-services-s5.11.b] Water that is heated must have room to expand, and the same boiler can also heat the water used at the taps.
[hom-home-services-s5.11.0] As water heats it expands. The expansion tank, usually in the roof space, takes up the extra water, tops up the system, and acts as an overflow for steam and excess water through its overflow pipe.
[hom-home-services-s5.11.1] The hot-water cylinder transfers heat from the circulating water to the stored domestic hot water through the heat exchanger coil. The cylinder is heated indirectly, so the water in the loop and the domestic hot water do not mix.
[hom-home-services-s5.11.2] The thermostat and timer control the boiler, and a room thermostat, TRVs and a cylinder thermostat control the heat further.
[hom-home-services-s5.11.3] A condensing boiler recovers heat from its flue gases, and an annual service keeps the boiler running efficiently.
[hom-home-services-s5.11.4] One boiler supplies heat for the whole house and hot water.
[hom-home-services-s5.12.b] The impact of a heating system depends mainly on its fuel, and this is a part of any exam answer on central heating.
[hom-home-services-s5.12.0] Burning fuel gives off carbon dioxide, so fossil fuels add to emissions. A flue removes the gases from the house, and a boiler needs ventilation so it has air to burn the fuel.
[hom-home-services-s5.12.1] Renewable options, such as heat pumps, solar heating and biomass, lower emissions.
[hom-home-services-s5.12.2] New dwellings are built to a near-zero-energy standard under Part L of the Building Regulations. Its performance requirements have effectively phased out fossil-fuel boilers as the main heating in new homes.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s2: 2. Heat transfer and the principles of cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.b] Cooking is more than making food hot. Heat changes the food in several useful ways, and each change is a reason examiners expect you to give.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.0] Cooking makes food safe by destroying harmful micro-organisms, so food must be heated right through to the centre, because bacteria deep inside a thick piece can survive if the heat never reaches them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.1] Heat denatures protein (changes its structure), so that digestive enzymes can act on it more easily.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.2] Cooking gelatinises starch (the starch grains swell and burst in water and heat), which makes rice, potatoes and pasta easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.3] Cooking softens the cellulose in plant cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to chew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.4] Heat develops flavour and changes colour and texture, which makes food more appetising and gives variety in the diet.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.5] Cooking destroys enzymes and most spoilage micro-organisms, which extends the time food stays fit to eat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.b] Whatever the method, cooking is heat moving from a source into the food. The cook controls how much heat reaches the food, for how long, and whether moisture or fat is present.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.0] Too low a temperature or too short a time leaves food undercooked and unsafe, because the centre never gets hot enough; too high a temperature or too long a time dries, toughens and burns the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.1] Thickness governs cooking time: thin or small pieces cook quickly and evenly, while a large joint needs lower heat for longer so that the centre cooks before the outside burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.2] Moist methods prevent food drying out; dry methods need added fat, basting (spooning hot fat or juices over the food) or a covered dish to keep food juicy.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.3] Food type governs the method: cuts rich in connective tissue need long, slow cooking, while delicate foods need short cooking at gentle heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.4] Cooked food must be served promptly or cooled quickly, because the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C is where bacteria multiply fastest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.b] Conduction is heat transfer through a solid from one molecule to the next, without the material itself moving. It is the way heat passes from a hot pan into the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.0] As molecules at the hot end are heated they vibrate faster and pass energy to their neighbours, until the whole piece is hot.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.1] Metals such as aluminium, copper and steel conduct heat quickly, which is why saucepans have metal bases; glass, ceramic, wood, plastic and air conduct heat slowly (they are insulators), which is why handles and oven gloves are made from them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.2] Pan-based methods begin with conduction: the cooker heats the pan base, which heats the liquid or fat in the pan, and that then heats the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.3] Boiling, poaching, stewing, shallow frying and stir-frying all depend on conduction through the pan base.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.4] Searing a steak in a hot pan: heat conducts from the pan surface into the outer layers of the meat and then gradually towards the centre.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.5] Baking trays and cake tins conduct heat into the base of a cake, so the base sets before the top.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.6] A large solid piece of food takes longer to cook through, because the heat has to travel inwards from the surface molecule by molecule.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.7] Diagram: arrows from a gas flame through a pan base into the food, labelled as conduction, with the direction of heat flow marked.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.b] Convection is heat transfer through a liquid or gas by currents. The heated fluid expands and becomes less dense so it rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place and is heated in turn.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.0] Natural convection occurs in a pot of boiling water, in a stew and in the hot air of a conventional oven, because the heated fluid rises on its own and the cooler fluid sinks.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.1] Forced convection: the fan in a fan oven circulates the hot air, so food cooks faster and more evenly than in a conventional oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.2] Deep-fat frying: convection currents in the hot oil carry heat to every surface of the immersed food at once.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.3] Steaming: hot steam rises from boiling water and carries heat to the food, and it also condenses on cooler food, releasing its latent heat (the heat stored in steam) to cook it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.4] Examples: boiling, stewing, steaming, deep-fat frying, and baking or roasting in an oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.5] Diagram: a pot of water with arrows showing hot water rising from the base, cooling at the surface and sinking at the sides.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.b] Radiation is heat transfer by infrared rays that travel in straight lines from a hot source and heat the first solid surface they strike. No solid, liquid or gas is needed to carry it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.0] The surface struck by radiant heat absorbs it, so browning and crisping happen on that surface while the inside of the food cooks by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.1] Grilling uses an element or flame above or below the food to heat the surface, sealing and browning it; thin pieces cook quickly because the heat is intense.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.2] Barbecuing and toasting use radiant heat from glowing coals, gas flames or electric elements.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.3] Dark, dull (matt) surfaces absorb radiant heat more readily than shiny surfaces, which reflect it; this is why foil reflects heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.4] Radiant heat weakens with distance from the source, so the height of the grill rack is adjusted to control how much heat the food receives.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.5] Diagram: straight arrows from a grill element to the top surface of a piece of food, labelled as radiation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.b] In practice most cooking uses more than one type of heat transfer at once. Microwave cooking is a special case, because the heat is made inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.0] Roasting uses radiation from the oven walls and element, convection by the hot air, and conduction inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.1] Boiling uses conduction through the pan base, then convection in the water, which is at 100 °C when boiling.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.2] In microwave cooking, a magnetron produces microwaves (electromagnetic waves) that make the water molecules in the food vibrate, and this makes heat inside the outer layer of the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.3] Once the heat is made in the outer layer, it spreads inwards by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.b] Each type of food reacts to heat in its own way, so the principles above are applied differently to each one. The right choice gives food that is safe, tender and keeps its colour and nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.0] Meat: tough cuts, rich in collagen, need long, slow, moist cooking so that collagen converts to gelatine; tender cuts can be grilled or fried quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.1] Fish: its flesh has little connective tissue and sets quickly, so it needs short cooking at gentle heat; poaching, steaming, grilling, baking and frying all suit it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.2] Eggs: egg proteins set (coagulate) at moderate temperatures, so eggs and custards need gentle heat; high heat makes scrambled egg rubbery and custard curdle.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.3] Vegetables and fruit: short cooking in a little boiling water, or steaming, keeps colour, texture and water-soluble vitamins, which would otherwise dissolve into the cooking water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.4] Cereals, rice and pasta: starch needs water and heat to gelatinise, so they are cooked in plenty of boiling water or steam.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.5] Milk and cheese: milk scorches on direct heat and cheese turns stringy at high temperatures, so sauces containing them are cooked gently.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.6] Fats and oils: fat overheats, smokes and breaks down if heated too far, so frying oil must be watched, an oil with a high smoke point chosen for deep-fat frying, and it should not be reused too often.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.7] Pastry and cakes: the fat is kept cold when rubbed in, the dry heat of the oven sets the flaky structure, and the oven should not be opened early when baking a cake.

CANDIDATES hom-home-services-s2: 2. Emissions, energy inefficiency and energy efficiency
[hom-home-services-s2.0.b] Heat is the largest energy use in a home, so wasted heat costs money and adds to emissions. Heat moves from warm to cool by conduction, convection and radiation, and in a house most is lost through the fabric (roof, walls, windows, doors, floors) and through air leakage.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.0] Conduction: heat passes through solid walls, roof, floor and glazing from the warm inside to the cold outside, so any uninsulated surface is a route for loss.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.1] Convection: warm air rises and escapes through gaps, while cold air enters at lower levels, creating draughts.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.2] Air leakage: gaps around doors, windows, floorboards, letterboxes and chimneys let warm air out and draw cold air in.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.3] Heat rises, so an uninsulated roof or attic is one of the largest routes for heat loss in a typical house.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.4] Single glazing loses heat much faster than an insulated wall; double glazing traps a layer of still air between two panes, which slows heat transfer.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.5] Thermal bridges are parts of the structure, such as lintels and junctions between walls and floors, where heat escapes faster than through the surrounding fabric.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.6] Uninsulated hot water cylinders and pipes lose heat continuously to the surrounding air, even when no water is drawn off.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.7] Heating that runs with no timer, room thermostat or radiator valves heats rooms that are not in use, so energy is paid for but wasted.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.b] Beyond the building fabric, energy is wasted by how heating, hot water, electrical appliances and cooking are used. Each habit below is a fault you would record in a home survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.0] Heating: an old or unserviced boiler, no timer, room thermostat or thermostatic radiator valves, rooms heated when not in use, a thermostat set too high, an open fire losing heat up the chimney, and radiators blocked by furniture or curtains.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.1] Hot water: an uninsulated cylinder and pipes, an immersion (electric heating element in the cylinder) left on, long hot showers or baths, water heated at the wrong time, and dripping hot taps.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.2] Electricity and appliances: old filament bulbs, lights left on, appliances left on standby, half loads in washing machines and dishwashers, old or low-rated appliances, a tumble dryer used instead of line-drying, and a fridge door left open.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.3] Cooking: using more water or heat than needed, no lids on pans, a large pan on a small ring (or the reverse), opening the oven door, preheating too long, and cooking one dish in a large oven.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.4] Cooking: using an oven where a microwave, toaster or slow cooker would do, and boiling too much water in the kettle.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.b] Burning a carbon fuel (gas, oil, coal, turf or wood) in boilers, stoves and fires gives off gases and particles. What comes out depends on how completely the fuel burns.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.0] Complete combustion happens with plenty of air and a well-adjusted burner, and gives carbon dioxide and water vapour. For methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.1] Incomplete combustion, caused by too little air, a blocked flue or a dirty or faulty burner, also produces carbon monoxide (CO) and soot or smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.2] Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless and poisonous gas that binds to haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells), so less oxygen is carried around the body. Effects include headache, dizziness, nausea and death.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.3] Carbon monoxide is prevented by servicing appliances, ensuring ventilation, keeping flues clear and fitting a CO alarm, because you cannot smell or see the gas.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.4] Water vapour is also given off when fuels burn, and it can cause condensation in the home.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.5] Diagram: a flow chart from fuel burned in the boiler or stove to the pollutants released (CO₂, CO, SO₂, nitrogen oxides, particulates) and their environmental effects.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.b] Each gas given off by burning fuels does its own kind of damage. Match each gas to its effect and do not swap them.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.0] Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that builds up in the atmosphere and traps heat, contributing to global warming and climate change: rising temperatures and sea levels, extreme weather, and changes to crops and species.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) comes from the sulphur in coal, oil and turf. It dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain, which damages trees and crops, acidifies lakes and kills fish, corrodes metal and wears limestone buildings and statues. It also irritates the lungs.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.2] Nitrogen oxides form when the air is heated in a burner or engine at high temperatures. They contribute to acid rain and smog and irritate the lungs.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.3] Smoke and particles come from coal, turf, wood and badly adjusted oil burners. They cause smog, dirty buildings and breathing problems such as asthma and bronchitis; smokeless fuel and efficient stoves reduce them.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.4] Per unit of heat, coal and turf give the most carbon dioxide and smoke, oil gives less, and natural gas gives the least. Electricity gives no emissions in the house but may do at the power station.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.5] Wood is a renewable fuel, but burning it still releases carbon dioxide and, if the stove is poor, smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.b] Aim: to identify where energy is wasted in a home and to recommend changes that improve efficiency.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.0] Step 1: walk through the house with a checklist and note draughts, uninsulated attic space, single glazing, missing draught seals and any unlagged cylinder or pipes.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.1] Step 2: check for draughts by holding the back of the hand or a smoke pencil near gaps, noting where air movement shows leakage.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.2] Step 3: record the heating controls: whether there is a timer, a room thermostat and radiator valves, and the thermostat setting in each room.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.3] Step 4: record the room temperatures at the same time on two occasions, and the hours the heating runs each day.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.4] Step 5: identify how hot water is heated (boiler, immersion or electric shower), how long the immersion runs, and whether the cylinder is insulated.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.5] Step 6: observe cooking practices, such as opening the oven door often, preheating for longer than needed, using large pans on small rings, leaving pans uncovered and boiling more water than needed in the kettle.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.6] Step 7: compare meter readings or fuel deliveries over a set period, and note appliances left on standby.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.7] Safety: keep naked flames away from gas and oil appliances and do not use a lit taper to check for leaks from fuel pipes.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.b] The cheapest way to cut energy use is to stop heat leaving the house. These measures target the fabric and air leaks found in a survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.0] Insulation: quilt laid between the joists in the attic, cavity wall insulation, internal or external wall insulation, and floor insulation.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.1] Glazing: double or triple glazing, with lined curtains closed at night, and low-emissivity coatings on glass.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.2] Draught-proofing of doors, windows, letterboxes and floorboards with seals and brushes, which stops warm air escaping and cold air entering.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.3] Ventilation balanced with insulation: an airtight fabric needs a controlled ventilation system so that moisture and stale air are removed without wasting heat.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.4] Improvements are ranked by cost and effect: draught-proofing and attic insulation are cheap and effective, while a heat pump or external wall insulation costs much more. SEAI grants help with costs.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.b] Heating and hot water are the biggest energy users, so controlling when and where heat is made saves the most. Cleaner heating also cuts emissions.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.0] Heating controls: a programmer or timer, a room thermostat, thermostatic radiator valves on each radiator, and a cylinder thermostat with a time switch for hot water, so heat is only produced when and where it is needed. Zoning (heating separate areas independently) and closing doors also help.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.1] Efficient boilers: a condensing boiler recovers heat from its flue gases, and an annual service keeps the burner adjusted so combustion stays complete.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.2] Heating systems: a stove in place of an open fire, and background heating where it suits.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.3] Hot water: lagging the cylinder and pipes with jackets, using the immersion only when required on a timer, fixing dripping taps, and setting the cylinder thermostat correctly.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.4] Choosing a shower over a bath saves water only if the shower is short, because a long or power shower can use as much as a bath.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.5] Renewable heat: solar thermal collectors, a heat pump or a well-managed wood-burning stove with a flue and an air supply controlled for complete combustion.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.b] Everyday electricity and cooking habits waste energy too, and changing them costs little. Each fix answers a fault from the home survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.0] Lighting and appliances: LED lamps, switching off unused lights, sensors and timers, and appliances with the highest rating on the energy label.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.1] Laundry and standby: full loads on washing machines and dishwashers, eco programmes, switching off standby, and line-drying clothes instead of using a tumble dryer.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.2] Cooking: using lids, matching pan size to the ring, boiling only the water needed, and filling the oven when it is on.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.3] Cooking: using the microwave, a pressure cooker or a slow cooker for small or long cooking, avoiding opening the oven door, and cooking several items together.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.b] Emissions fall when less fuel is burned and when what is burned burns cleanly. These steps answer the "way to cut it" part of an emissions question.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.0] Burn less fuel overall, through insulation, controls and efficient appliances, because less fuel burned means fewer pollutants.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.1] Change from coal or oil to gas or smokeless fuel, and choose a renewable electricity supplier, because these produce less carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.2] Service boilers so combustion stays complete, keep flues clear, and fit a CO alarm, which warns of carbon monoxide that you cannot smell.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.3] Do not burn rubbish, and use smokeless fuel and efficient stoves to reduce smoke and particles.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.4] Renewable options such as solar thermal and PV (photovoltaic panels that make electricity), and heat pumps, burn no fuel in the home.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.b] It is far cheaper to build efficiency in than to add it later. A new house can be designed to lose little heat and to gain free heat from the sun.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.0] A compact shape with few corners has less outside wall area through which heat escapes.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.1] South-facing living rooms with large windows gain free heat from the sun (passive solar gain), while small north windows lose little heat.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.2] High levels of insulation in the attic, walls and floor, with double or triple glazing, and airtight construction with controlled ventilation.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.3] A porch or lobby cuts draughts when the door is opened, and orientation and shelter from wind cut heat loss.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.4] Zoned heating with controls, and an efficient or renewable heating system.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.5] Rainwater use, and LED and sensor lighting.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.b] A BER is a certificate that rates a dwelling's energy performance, like the energy label on appliances. It tells owners and buyers how efficient a home is.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.0] The BER assessment is carried out by an assessor registered with SEAI, and SEAI runs the system.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.1] It rates a dwelling on a scale from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient). From 24 May 2026 the scale is A0 and A to G, and the sub-bands such as B1 to B3 were removed.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.2] A valid BER is required when a home is sold or rented, it must appear in adverts, and it has been required since 2009. BERs issued before 24 May 2026 stay valid for sale or rent for ten years from issue.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.3] It shows the energy efficiency of a house, including windows, walls, insulation and heating, and where improvements would have most effect.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.4] The EU energy label for appliances and bulbs was rescaled to A to G (appliances in March 2021, lamps in September 2021), so few products now reach A.
