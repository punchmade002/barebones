TARGET hom-food-preservation-s9: 9. Practical application of two methods of preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.b] Home freezing keeps food by cold, and it works best on fresh, good-quality food frozen soon after it is harvested or bought. Freezing does not improve food, so poor food comes out poor.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.0] Vegetables that freeze well include carrots, peas, beans and broccoli, and fruit that freezes well includes apples and strawberries.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.1] Meat and fish, baked foods, soups, sauces and complete meals can all be frozen at home, so a cook can store prepared dishes as well as raw ingredients.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.2] Bananas and lettuce do not freeze well and are not suitable for home freezing, because they turn soft and limp when thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.3] Melons and pears also freeze poorly.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.4] The principle is low temperature: freezing stops enzyme action and microbial growth (the enzymes and micro-organisms are inactivated), and the water in the food turns to ice, which micro-organisms cannot use.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.0.5] Quick freezing makes small ice crystals that protect the texture of the food, whereas slow freezing makes the food softer.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.1.b] Vegetables contain enzymes that keep working in the freezer and spoil colour, flavour and texture. Blanching (a short scald in boiling water) inactivates these enzymes, so it is a key step for vegetables.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.1.0] 1. Choose fresh, young vegetables, then wash them and prepare them by trimming, peeling and cutting them into even pieces so they blanch evenly.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.1.1] 2. Blanch by plunging the pieces into boiling water for the recommended time for that vegetable.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.1.2] 3. Cool quickly by transferring the pieces to ice-cold water for the same time as the blanching, which stops the cooking at once.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.1.3] 4. Drain thoroughly and dry the pieces on absorbent paper or a clean tea towel, so that surface water does not form ice.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.2.b] Fruit such as strawberries is frozen without blanching. The aim is to keep the pieces separate and whole.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.2.0] Select sound, ripe fruit, wash it quickly, hull it (remove the stalk and green top) and dry it.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.2.1] Open-freeze the fruit by spreading it in a single layer on a tray, so the pieces do not stick together, then pack it in portions.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.b] Good packing stops the food drying out and picking up flavours in the freezer, and makes it easy to use later.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.0] 1. Cool cooked food completely before packing, because warm food raises the temperature of the freezer.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.1] 2. Open-freeze fruit such as strawberries by spreading them in a single layer on a tray, so the pieces do not stick together.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.2] 3. Pack the food in usable, family-sized portions in strong, moisture- and vapour-proof freezer bags or rigid containers (rigid plastic containers with tight lids, or foil), so that only what is needed is thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.3] 4. Leave headspace in rigid containers, because liquids expand on freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.4] 5. Remove the air from bags and seal them securely with ties or heat sealing.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.3.5] 6. Label each pack with its contents, quantity and the date.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.b] Food must freeze quickly and then stay cold. Two different temperatures matter: the fast-freeze figure and the storage figure.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.0] Switch the freezer to its fast-freeze setting 3–4 hours before adding food, so the freezer is as cold as possible when the food goes in.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.1] Add no more than one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24-hour period, because a large load of warm food raises the temperature and slows freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.2] Place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze section of the freezer so that they freeze quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.3] Fast freezing takes food down to about −25 °C, so it freezes quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.4] Store the frozen food at −18 °C or colder.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.4.5] Rotate stock so older packs are used first, thaw food safely in the fridge, cook it thoroughly, and do not refreeze thawed raw food.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.5.b] Freezing is one of the best methods for keeping the quality of food close to the fresh product.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.5.0] Colour, flavour, texture and nutrients are well kept when food is frozen quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.5.1] Some vitamin C and B1 are lost in blanching and in drip when the food thaws.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.5.2] Slow freezing softens the food, because large ice crystals damage its structure.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.6.b] Preparing food for the freezer involves sharp tools, boiling water and electrical appliances, so safe habits protect both the cook and the food.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.6.0] Use knives, graters and food processors carefully, cutting away from the body, using the guard on the grater and keeping fingers clear of the blades, so that cuts are avoided.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.6.1] Wash hands before preparation, cover any cuts with a dressing, and tie back long hair, so that bacteria and hair do not get into the food.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.6.2] Use oven gloves when handling the blanching pan, turn pan handles inward, and do not overfill the pan, so that nobody is scalded.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.6.3] Avoid overloaded sockets, trailing flexes and wet hands near electrical appliances, to prevent electric shock and fire.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.6.4] Keep raw and cooked foods separate during preparation and clean surfaces between tasks, to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.7.b] Jam keeps because several things work together: heat, sugar, pectin and acid, and a tight seal.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.7.0] Heat at about 100 °C destroys micro-organisms and enzymes in the fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.7.1] Sugar preserves by osmosis: it draws water out of any micro-organism, so they cannot grow.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.7.2] Pectin (a natural setting substance in fruit) and acid make the jam set.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.7.3] Sealing the jar while hot prevents micro-organisms from getting back in.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.8.b] Jam is made from fruit that is plentiful. Whether it sets depends mainly on how much pectin and acid the fruit contains.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.8.0] Use ripe or just ripe, sound fruit that is free from mould and bruising, such as strawberries, raspberries, blackcurrants, plums, gooseberries, apples and blackberries.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.8.1] Pectin is found mainly in the skin and core of fruit; cooking apples, blackcurrants and citrus fruit are high in pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.8.2] Strawberries are low in pectin, so lemon juice or another acid is added to help the jam set, and if needed pectin or pectin-rich fruit such as apple.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.8.3] Sugar is added in the proportion given in a tested recipe and stirred until fully dissolved before the mixture is boiled, because undissolved sugar makes the jam crystallise.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.b] The steps must be in order, and each one has a purpose: sterile jars, soft fruit, dissolved sugar and a fast boil to setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.0] 1. Wash the jars and lids and sterilise them (in the oven or in boiling water), and keep them hot until filling, so that hot jam does not crack cold glass. Put a saucer in the freezer for the setting test and weigh the fruit and sugar.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.1] 2. Wash the fruit, remove stalks and damaged parts, and cut the fruit as the recipe requires.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.2] 3. Simmer the fruit gently in a heavy-based pan, with a little water, until it softens and releases its pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.3] 4. Add the sugar and stir until it has completely dissolved, adding lemon juice if needed.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.4] 5. Bring to a full rolling boil and boil rapidly until setting point is reached; do not stir after setting point is reached.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.5] 6. Test for setting point with a cold saucer, pushing a drop of jam with a finger to check whether it wrinkles, or with a sugar thermometer at about 105 °C.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.6] 7. Take the pan off the heat and skim off the scum from the surface with a clean, dry spoon, then allow to cool slightly so the fruit does not float.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.9.7] 8. Pour the jam into the hot sterilised jars, leave a small space at the top, and wipe the rim.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.10.b] Packaging for jam has two jobs: keeping micro-organisms out and keeping the jam in good condition.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.10.0] Use clean, undamaged glass jars with new lids (waxed disc and cover, or twist-top lids), and wipe the rim of each jar before sealing.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.10.1] Seal each jar immediately while the jam is hot, so that a vacuum forms as it cools.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.10.2] Label each jar with the name of the contents and the date.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.10.3] Store sealed jars in a cool, dry, dark place, and refrigerate any jar once it has been opened.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.11.b] A jam that goes wrong can be traced back to a step that was missed.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.11.0] Failure to set is caused by too little pectin, acid or sugar, or by not boiling long enough.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.11.1] Crystallised jam is caused by too much sugar or sugar that was not dissolved.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.11.2] Mould or fermentation is caused by too little sugar, unsterile jars or a poor seal.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.11.3] Fruit floating is caused by potting the jam when too hot.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.11.4] Scorching is caused by not stirring or by a pan that is too thin.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.12.b] Boiling jam is very hot and sticky, so most safety guidelines are about preventing burns.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.12.0] Hot sugar syrup can cause severe scalds, so do not touch it, and handle the pan with oven gloves and pour carefully from a jug or jam funnel.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.12.1] Turn pan handles inward and do not overfill the pan, as the mixture foams up and boils over when it is at a rolling boil.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.12.2] Keep children and pets away from the cooker while the jam is boiling.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.12.3] Remove scum with a clean, dry spoon, because a wet or used spoon can introduce micro-organisms into the jam.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.12.4] Use sterilised equipment for food safety.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.13.b] A second method does not have to be jam. Chutney is an alternative, and a student may name other home methods briefly.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.13.0] Chutney is made from foods such as apples, tomatoes, plums, onions and marrow, and heat destroys organisms while vinegar (acid) and sugar inhibit growth.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.13.1] Steps: sterilise jars; chop the fruit and vegetables; cook slowly with vinegar, sugar and spices until thick; pot hot; seal with vinegar-proof lids; label; mature in a cool, dark place; refrigerate after opening. Safety is as for jam.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.13.2] Chutney is boiled with vinegar and sugar and does not need pectin, whereas jam needs pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s9.13.3] Other home methods include drying herbs, apples or tomatoes (oven or dehydrator), bottling fruit and pickling vegetables in vinegar.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s2: 2. Freezing
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.b] Freezing preserves food by lowering its temperature far enough to stop enzyme action and microbial growth, and by turning free water into ice that micro-organisms cannot use. It is popular because no high heat is needed, so food stays close to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.0] Low temperature slows enzyme reactions (enzymes are the natural chemicals in food that make it spoil or change) and stops micro-organisms from reproducing, but it does not kill most of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.1] Frozen food is not sterile: micro-organisms become dormant (inactive) and start growing again when the food thaws, so frozen food is never safe indefinitely.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.2] Turning free water into ice removes the water micro-organisms need, because ice is unavailable to them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.3] Pure water freezes at 0 °C, but food freezes below this because its dissolved sugars and salts lower its freezing point.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.4] Water expands by about 9 per cent when it freezes, which is why liquids need headspace (an empty gap at the top) in their containers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.5] Freezing itself does little harm to nutritive value, and colour, flavour and texture are kept better than with most other methods because no high heat is used, except in blanching; most nutrient losses happen during blanching and thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.b] The speed of freezing decides how big the ice crystals are, and crystal size decides how much damage the food suffers. Quick freezing makes small crystals; slow freezing makes large ones.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.0] Quick freezing takes place at about −25 °C (the 2013 Higher Level scheme gives −25 °C to −40 °C) in the fast-freeze section of a freezer, so the food passes through the freezing range rapidly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.1] Quick freezing forms small ice crystals within the cells, which causes little damage to the cell walls, so there is little drip (juice lost) on thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.2] For this reason quick freezing retains the texture, colour, flavour and nutritive value of food better than slow freezing does.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.3] Slow freezing takes place at a higher temperature than −25 °C, as in an ordinary freezer compartment, where ice forms gradually.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.4] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals, mostly between the cells, that pierce and rupture cell walls, allowing juices to escape.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.5] As a result slow-frozen food is limp and soft when thawed, and it loses colour and flavour and drip containing soluble vitamins B1 and C and minerals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.6] The fast-freeze setting should be switched on about 3 to 4 hours before food is placed in the freezer, so the freezer is cold enough to freeze the new food quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.7] Diagram: draw two cells side by side, one with small crystals inside an intact cell wall (quick) and one with large crystals and a ruptured wall (slow).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.b] Blanching is a brief heat treatment given to vegetables before freezing. Enzymes keep working slowly even at freezer temperatures, so vegetables would spoil in storage without it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.0] 1. The vegetables are plunged into boiling water for a short, set time that depends on the vegetable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.1] 2. They are plunged immediately into ice-cold water for the same length of time, which stops the cooking so the vegetables do not go soft.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.2] 3. They are drained and dried, because surface water left on them would form ice crystals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.3] Blanching inactivates enzymes that would otherwise cause loss of colour, flavour, texture and nutrients during frozen storage.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.4] Blanching also cleans the vegetable, destroys some micro-organisms on its surface, softens it slightly so it packs well, and keeps its bright colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.5] Blanching loses some water-soluble vitamins, particularly vitamin C and vitamin B1, into the water and through heat. Blanch small batches in plenty of fast-boiling water so the water returns to the boil quickly and the timing stays short and accurate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.6] Fruit is not blanched; soft fruit is open-frozen on trays or packed in sugar or sugar syrup. Some foods, such as soups, sauces and casseroles, are frozen cooked after cooling.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.b] Good results at home depend on following a set of steps, and each step has a reason. Most of them aim either to freeze the food quickly or to keep air and moisture away from it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.0] Choose fresh, best-quality food in perfect condition and freeze it soon after harvest or purchase, because freezing keeps quality but cannot improve it. Wash, peel, trim or cut as needed, blanch vegetables, and cool cooked food quickly before it goes into the freezer.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.1] Portion food into usable (family-sized) quantities so that only what is needed is thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.2] Open-freeze (tray-freeze) sticky or delicate items, such as strawberries or raspberries, or items to be kept separate, such as sausages and burgers: spread them in a single layer on a tray, freeze hard, then bag them so that they stay separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.3] Pack food in strong, moisture-proof and vapour-proof freezer bags, foil or rigid plastic containers, and remove as much air as possible so that the food does not dry out. Leave headspace in containers holding liquids to allow for expansion as the food freezes.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.4] Seal tightly and label with contents and date. Place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze compartment, as the freezer instructs, and keep them apart from already frozen food until frozen, so they freeze quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.5] Do not overload the freezer: freeze no more than about one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24 hours, because overloading raises the temperature and slows freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.6] Store frozen food at −18 °C or colder, using the oldest first. The safe storage time depends on the food: fatty foods and oily fish keep for a shorter period, and fatty foods stored too long can turn rancid.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.7] Foods that freeze well include peas, beans, carrots, broccoli, apples, strawberries, meat, fish, bread, cakes, soups, sauces and prepared meals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.8] Foods that freeze poorly include lettuce, whole tomatoes and other high-water salad foods, bananas, melons, pears, whole eggs in the shell, low-fat cream and mayonnaise-based foods, which go limp, watery or separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.b] Problems with frozen food usually come from air reaching it or from careless thawing. Both are preventable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.0] Freezer burn shows as dry, discoloured, tough patches where air has reached the surface of the food, caused by dehydration and oxidation, especially in protein foods such as meat and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.1] Airtight packaging with the air removed prevents freezer burn.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.2] Thaw food in a fridge, or cook it from frozen where the food allows, such as vegetables, so that the food stays below the danger zone while it thaws. Thaw meat and poultry fully before cooking unless the pack says otherwise.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.3] Thawed food should be used promptly and not refrozen as it is, because quality declines and micro-organisms may have multiplied. Raw food thawed in the fridge can be cooked and then frozen as a cooked dish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.4] In a freezer fault or power cut, keep the door closed and do not open it, because frozen food keeps for a time if the cold is held in.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.b] Commercial freezing is faster and colder than home freezing, so ice crystals are smaller and quality is higher. Food is frozen soon after harvesting or catching, often on the farm or on a factory ship. Home freezers are not as cold or as fast, so home freezing does not match these methods.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.0] Air blast freezing: a very cold air stream at about −30 °C is blown over food, often on a conveyor belt or through a tunnel; suitable for most foods, such as fruit, vegetables, meat and bakery products.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.1] Plate (contact) freezing: food is pressed between two hollow metal plates cooled by refrigerant; suitable for thin, flat foods such as fish fillets, flat pieces of meat and burgers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.2] Flow (fluidised bed) freezing: cold air is blown up through a perforated conveyor belt so that small pieces float and freeze individually, staying free-flowing and not sticking together; suitable for peas, sweetcorn and berries.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.3] Cryogenic freezing: food is exposed to liquid nitrogen or carbon dioxide at extremely low temperature, which freezes it almost instantly; suitable for delicate or high-value foods such as strawberries, prawns and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.4] The cold chain keeps frozen food at −18 °C or colder in cold stores, refrigerated lorries and supermarket freezer cabinets until it reaches the home.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.5] When buying frozen food, check that the pack is intact with no ice crystals or frost inside, that it is below the load line on the cabinet, and take it home quickly in a cool bag.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.b] Freezing helps a household plan meals, save money and cut waste. These benefits are the points examiners expect when they ask about frozen foods in meal planning.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.0] Freezing surplus food before it spoils reduces household waste, and leftovers can be frozen for reuse as ready-made portions.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.1] Bulk cooking and freezing portions saves time and fuel, since a large batch is cooked once.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.2] A freezer stock of meals and ingredients provides emergency food and convenience on busy days, including for households with different eating times.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.3] Buying in bulk or when produce is in season is more economical, and freezing keeps it for a longer period (a longer shelf life).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.4] Frozen foods provide a wide variety of vegetables, fish and fruit all year, including items not available fresh locally.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.5] Freezing retains colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value well compared with heat processing; frozen vegetables keep more vitamin C than canned ones, as one past-paper table shows for peas (25 mg fresh, 17 mg frozen, 8 mg canned).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.6] Fruit and salad foods go soft after thawing because of cell damage, so they suit cooking better than eating raw.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.7] Labelling each pack with contents, date and portion size lets older items be used first, which helps planned menus.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s3: 3. Heat processing
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.b] Food spoils because micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) and enzymes (natural proteins in the food that break it down) keep working. Heat processing stops both, and a clean airtight container then keeps new micro-organisms out.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.0] Heat destroys vegetative bacteria (bacteria in their active, growing form), yeasts and moulds, and denatures (permanently changes the shape of) the enzymes that cause spoilage, so the food stops breaking down.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.1] Sealing the food in a clean airtight container while it is hot, or sealing before heating, prevents re-contamination, because otherwise new micro-organisms would simply re-enter after the heating.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.2] Spores (tough resting forms) of some bacteria, such as Clostridium botulinum, are very heat-resistant and survive boiling at normal pressure, so low-acid foods need higher temperatures held for longer, in a pressure cooker or retort (a large industrial pressure cooker).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.3] Acid foods such as fruit, tomatoes and pickles are easier to preserve by heat because acid hinders micro-organisms and their spores. Low-acid foods such as peas, beans, meat and fish need a higher temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.4] Heat also changes the colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value of food, which is why heat-processed fruit and vegetables differ from fresh produce.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.5] Heat-sensitive, water-soluble vitamins (vitamin C and the B group) are reduced by heat processing.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.b] Heat treatments differ in how much they kill. A mild treatment leaves some organisms alive so the food must be chilled, while a severe one kills everything so the sealed food keeps at room temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.0] Pasteurisation is a moderate heat treatment that destroys most food poisoning (pathogenic) bacteria and reduces spoilage organisms, but not all spores, so pasteurised products keep for a limited time and still need refrigeration.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.1] Milk is pasteurised at 72 °C for 15 seconds, then cooled rapidly. Pasteurisation is also used for cream, fruit juice, beer and some canned foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.2] Sterilisation uses a higher temperature for longer, for example milk sterilised in the bottle and canned foods. It destroys all micro-organisms including spores, so the sealed product can be stored at room temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.3] The price of sterilisation is that flavour and colour change and vitamin losses are greater than with pasteurisation.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.4] UHT (ultra-heat treatment) heats milk to about 135 °C for a few seconds, and the milk is then packed aseptically (in a germ-free way) into sterile cartons.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.5] UHT milk keeps for months unopened at room temperature, must be refrigerated after opening, and has a slight cooked flavour.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.6] Three processing methods make milk safe: pasteurisation, sterilisation or UHT, and evaporation or dehydration (drying).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.b] Canning seals food in a container and heats it hard enough to sterilise it, so it keeps for a long time without a fridge. Commercial canning in tin cans is different from home bottling in glass jars.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.0] Commercial canning has stages: food is prepared (washed, peeled, trimmed, blanched where needed), packed into cans, covered with brine, syrup or oil, and air is removed (exhausting).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.1] Then the can is sealed with a double seam, heated in a retort at a high temperature for a set time, and cooled quickly. The heat sterilises the contents and the sealed can prevents re-contamination.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.2] Cans are lined with a lacquer, a protective coating, to prevent the metal reacting with the food.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.3] Canned food is shelf-stable, so it keeps at room temperature for a long time without refrigeration, and it can be eaten straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.4] Foods suited to canning include fruit, tomatoes, vegetables such as peas and beans, fish (tuna, sardines, salmon), meat, soup and baked beans.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.5] Low-acid vegetables such as peas, beans and carrots must be processed in a pressure canner (a sealed pot that heats above 100 °C), because boiling water at normal pressure, which is 100 °C, does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.6] A bulging can end or lid indicates gas produced by micro-organisms. Hissing, leaking, rust, deep dents or an off smell are also warning signs, so the contents must be discarded unopened and never tasted.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.b] The very high temperature and long processing time that make canned food safe also change it. Examiners ask what is lost and what is gained.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.0] Vitamin C is heat-sensitive and water-soluble, so it is lost during high-temperature canning, especially into the canning liquid.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.1] Thiamine (vitamin B1) is unstable at high temperature and is reduced by canning.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.2] Canned vegetables are softer, paler and less bright in colour than fresh or frozen produce, and the flavour changes too.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.3] Canned vegetables gain sodium from added salt (brine), so no-added-salt versions are preferred on a low-salt diet.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.4] Fruit canned in heavy syrup gains sugar, and food packed in oil gains fat, which raises the energy content.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.5] Heat destroys micro-organisms and enzymes in the can, which is the purpose of canning. Minerals and fibre are largely kept, and the liquid can be used in cooking to keep the nutrients it holds.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.6] One past-paper table showed vitamin C in peas falling from 25 mg per 100 g fresh to 8 mg canned, and sodium rising from 1 mg to 230 mg. The reasons were the very high temperature and long time, and the salt used in canning.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.b] Bottling is the home version of heat preservation. Fruit is heated in glass jars so that the contents are sterilised and sealed.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.0] Bottling packs fruit (and tomatoes) into clean sterilised glass jars with sugar syrup or water, seals them, and heats them in a water bath (or oven) until the contents reach a temperature that destroys organisms and enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.1] The seal closes as the jar cools and a vacuum forms, which holds the lid down and keeps new micro-organisms out.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.2] Jars must be filled hot, leaving headspace, and sealed at once; the contents cool and form a vacuum that holds the lid down.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.3] Jars must be sterilised and undamaged with new or sound lids, and filled leaving headspace (a gap at the top). Hot jars must be lifted carefully.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.4] Label bottled food with the date and store it in a cool, dark, dry place. Discard any jar whose lid is not tight or domed.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.5] Low-acid vegetables and meat should not be bottled in a boiling-water bath at home, because boiling at normal pressure does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores. They need pressure processing, so home bottling is restricted to fruit and acid foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.6] Fruit is routinely bottled in a water bath because acid foods are easier to preserve by heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.b] Jam is fruit boiled with sugar until it sets. It keeps because of heat, a high concentration of sugar and a sealed jar, and it sets because pectin, acid and sugar combine.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.0] Heat: boiling at about 100 °C or higher destroys micro-organisms and inactivates enzymes in the fruit. The fruit is first simmered gently to soften its tissues and release pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.1] Sugar preserves by osmosis: at high concentration it draws water out of microbial cells, so they cannot multiply. Sugar also helps the gel to form and gives sweetness.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.2] Pectin is a natural gelling substance, a carbohydrate found in the cell walls of fruit, mostly in the skin and core. It forms a gel with sugar and acid as the jam cools, so the jam sets. Pectin is not a preservative.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.3] Apples, blackcurrants, redcurrants, gooseberries and lemons or other citrus fruit are high in pectin. Strawberries, raspberries, cherries and pears are low, so add pectin-rich fruit or a commercial pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.4] Acid, from lemon juice or the fruit itself, releases pectin from the cell walls and helps the pectin set. It also improves colour and flavour, and lemon juice is added for low-acid or low-pectin fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.5] Choose just ripe, firm, sound fruit. Under-ripe fruit contains more pectin, whereas over-ripe fruit has less pectin and spoils the set.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.6] Sealing: pour the jam hot into clean sterilised jars and cover at once so micro-organisms cannot re-enter. A vacuum forms as the jam cools, and a waxed disc plus cover is the traditional method.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.7] Jam loses some heat-sensitive vitamin C in boiling, its sugar concentration is high, and its flavour and colour change, so it no longer tastes fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.b] A jam method runs in a set order, and each stage protects either the set or the keeping quality. Jam spoils when one of these protections fails.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.0] 1. Wash and sterilise the jars, and prepare the fruit. 2. Simmer the fruit in a heavy pan to soften it and release pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.1] 3. Add the sugar in the correct proportion, with lemon juice if needed, and dissolve it fully before the mixture is boiled rapidly to setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.2] Setting point is confirmed by the wrinkle test: a spoonful of jam dropped onto a cold saucer wrinkles when pushed with a finger. A sugar thermometer reads about 105 °C (220 °F) at setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.3] 4. Skim the scum from the surface with a clean dry spoon. 5. Pot the jam into hot sterilised jars, seal at once, label and store in a cool, dry place.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.4] Jam ferments or grows mould if too little sugar is used, setting point is not reached, jars or lids are not sterilised, the seal fails, or a dirty or wet spoon introduces yeasts and moulds.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.5] Storage in a warm or damp place also spoils jam. Mould on the surface or fermentation (bubbles and an alcoholic smell) is the result.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s5: 5. Chemical preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.b] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) need available water, a suitable acidity and working enzymes to grow. Chemical preservatives attack one or more of these, which is why preserved food keeps for much longer.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.0] Some preservatives draw water out of food and micro-organisms by osmosis (water moving out through a cell wall towards a stronger solution), so the organisms cannot multiply. Sugar and salt work this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.1] Some lower the pH (make the food more acidic) so that most spoilage and food poisoning bacteria cannot grow. Vinegar and other acids work this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.2] Some directly poison or inhibit micro-organisms or their enzymes, such as sulphur dioxide, benzoates, sorbates, nitrite and the chemicals in smoke. Others act as antioxidants (stopping oxygen damaging the food) or inhibit mould.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.3] Sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke and alcohol are the traditional (natural) preservatives. Sugar, salt and vinegar are the traditional home preservatives, and smoke was the traditional preservative for fish and meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.4] The permitted additive preservatives are sorbic acid, benzoic acid, sulphur dioxide and sulphites, and nitrites and nitrates.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.5] Many chemical methods are combined with heat, as in jam and chutney, or with drying, as in smoked and salted fish. Using two methods together makes the food safer than either alone.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.6] Fruit can also be preserved in alcohol, for example fruit kept in brandy or rum.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.b] A strong sugar solution holds on to water so tightly that micro-organisms cannot use it. This is why jam keeps in a sealed jar.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.0] At high concentration, sugar binds water and draws it out of microbial cells by osmosis, so the micro-organisms cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.1] Sugar is the preservative in jam, marmalade, syrups, crystallised and candied fruit and condensed milk.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.2] The concentration must be high enough. Low-sugar jam can ferment or grow mould, so a strong sugar solution is essential.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.3] Yeasts and moulds tolerate sugar better than most bacteria, so sugared products still need sealed, clean containers to keep them out.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.4] Sugar also has other roles in jam: it forms a gel with pectin (the setting substance in fruit), and it adds sweetness and colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.5] The Department of Health healthy eating guidelines advise limiting foods and drinks high in added sugar, since excess sugar is linked to tooth decay and excess energy intake. Preserves contain a lot of added sugar.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.b] Salt works like sugar, pulling water out of food and out of any micro-organisms on it. It has been used on fish and meat, but it has a health cost.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.0] Salt draws water out of food and micro-organisms by osmosis, and it makes the remaining water unavailable to organisms and to enzyme action.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.1] Dry salting: the food is packed in salt, which draws out moisture. This method is used for salt cod, salt pork, bacon and salted herring.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.2] Brining: the food is immersed or soaked in a strong salt solution, as used for olives, ham, pickled meat and brine pickles of vegetables such as cucumbers.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.3] Curing combines salt with sodium nitrite or nitrate, and sometimes sugar, to preserve meat such as bacon, ham and corned beef. The nitrite inhibits Clostridium botulinum (a dangerous food poisoning bacterium) and keeps the meat pink.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.4] Salt-cured foods must be stored in cool, dry conditions, since moisture in the air can cause surface mould.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.5] Salted foods need soaking or rinsing to remove the salt before they are used.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.6] Excess salt (sodium) raises blood pressure, and salted and cured foods are high in sodium. The Department of Health healthy eating guidelines advise reducing salt in the diet.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.b] Vinegar does not dry food out. It preserves by making the food too acidic for most bacteria to survive, and it adds flavour at the same time.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.0] Vinegar is a dilute solution of acetic acid, which lowers the pH of food and inhibits most spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.1] Vinegar pickling: vegetables such as onions, cucumbers, beetroot and red cabbage, and also eggs, are packed in spiced vinegar, which acts as the preservative. It is also used in chutneys, relishes and sauces, sometimes with sugar and spices.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.2] Vinegar also gives sharpness and flavour, and its effectiveness depends on its acetic acid strength. It must be strong enough that the water in the food does not dilute it.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.3] Spiced vinegar, made cold or hot, is poured over vegetables packed into sterilised jars. Malt vinegar is the usual home choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.4] Use non-metallic lids on jars of vinegar foods, because the acid corrodes metal and spoils the seal.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.b] Chutney and pickle both rely on vinegar, but they are different products. Chutney is cooked down to a thick mixture, while pickle keeps its pieces whole in a liquid.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.0] Chutney is made by chopping fruit and/or vegetables (apples, tomatoes, onions, marrow, plums) and cooking them slowly with vinegar, sugar and spices until the mixture is thick. It needs no pectin, unlike jam.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.1] Each ingredient has a job. The cooking destroys micro-organisms and enzymes, the vinegar (an acid) and sugar prevent any survivors from growing, and spices such as mustard seed, chilli and ginger add flavour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.2] Chutney method: 1. Sterilise the jars. 2. Prepare and chop the fruit and vegetables. 3. Cook slowly with vinegar, sugar and spices until thick and no liquid vinegar shows when a spoon is drawn across. 4. Pot into hot jars. 5. Seal with vinegar-proof lids and label.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.3] Chutney is poured hot into sterilised jars, sealed and stored in a cool, dark place, where it matures and improves in flavour after weeks. Once opened it is kept in the fridge.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.4] Pickles are raw, lightly cooked or brined vegetables, eggs or fruit packed whole or in pieces in vinegar or brine. Vinegar pickles are covered with spiced vinegar and sugar and sealed.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.5] Chutney spoils if there is too little vinegar or sugar, if it is not cooked long enough, if jars or lids are not sterile or the seal is faulty, or if a metal lid is corroded by the vinegar.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.b] Smoking preserves food by exposing it to smoke from smouldering wood, and it adds flavour and colour as well. It is used mostly on fish and meat, and also on cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.0] Food is exposed to smoke from smouldering wood (such as oak, beech or hickory) or sawdust. The smoke deposits chemicals (phenols, formaldehyde and organic acids) that inhibit micro-organisms and slow oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.1] Smoke also dries the surface of the food, which slows micro-organism growth and gives flavour and colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.2] Smoking is almost always combined with salting first, by brining or dry salting, because smoking gives limited shelf life on its own. Smoked meat and fish are usually also salted or cured.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.3] Cold smoking is carried out at a low temperature, not exceeding about 27 °C, so the food is flavoured and partly preserved but not cooked and requires further cooking. Smoked salmon is an example.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.4] Hot smoking raises the temperature gradually to about 80 °C, so the food is cooked as it is smoked and is ready to eat, as in smoked mackerel.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.5] Smoking fish, in order: 1. Fillet and trim. 2. Brine or dry-salt. 3. Rinse and dry. 4. Smoke at the chosen temperature. 5. Cool.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.6] Smoked foods have a limited shelf life unless chilled, and they are high in salt. Some smoke compounds (tars) are a health concern if eaten in large amounts.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.b] Besides the traditional preservatives, food manufacturers may add certain chemical preservatives. They are tightly controlled, and each one works best against particular organisms in particular foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.0] They are controlled in the EU by Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives: only listed substances may be used, in listed foods, up to maximum levels. They are given E numbers (E200 to E299 are the preservatives) and are tested for safety by the European Food Safety Authority.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.1] Sorbic acid (E200) and potassium sorbate (E202) inhibit moulds and yeasts in foods such as cheese, baked goods and soft drinks.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.2] Benzoic acid (E210) and sodium benzoate (E211) inhibit yeasts, moulds and bacteria in acidic foods such as soft drinks, pickles and fruit products.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.3] Sulphur dioxide (E220) and sulphites inhibit micro-organisms and prevent browning in products such as dried fruit, wine and fruit juice.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.4] Sodium nitrite (E250) and sodium nitrate (E251) are used in cured meat. Nitrite inhibits Clostridium botulinum and gives cured meat its pink colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.5] Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information to consumers, a preservative in the ingredients list is given with its function ("preservative") followed by its name or E number. Sulphur dioxide and sulphites above the stated level are among the 14 allergens that must be emphasised.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.6] Benefits are longer shelf life, safety, less waste and lower cost. Concerns are that some people react to preservatives (asthmatics may react to sulphites), consumer preference for fewer additives, and high sodium in salt-based preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.7] An E number shows only that an additive is approved, not that it is natural or unsafe.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.b] Sugars are the sweet, water-loving carbohydrates, and these two properties explain why they are used so widely in cooking. Starch is the contrast: it is neither sweet nor soluble in cold water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.0] All sugars are sweet, but to different degrees. Fructose is sweeter than sucrose (table sugar), and lactose (milk sugar) is the least sweet of the common sugars.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.1] On a scale where sucrose scores 100, the 2017 marking scheme gives fructose 170 and lactose 15. Learn the order (fructose, then sucrose, then lactose) and use the figures only as that scale.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.2] Sweetness is why sugar is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.3] Sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve readily in water, and solubility increases with temperature, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.4] A concentrated sugar solution is a syrup. Syrups are used to sweeten drinks and sauces, to make icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.5] Starch is different: it is insoluble in cold water, so it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension (tiny solid particles spread through the liquid). It is also not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.b] Starch behaves differently from sugar: it is insoluble in cold water and must be heated with a liquid before it thickens anything. It also takes in moisture from the air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.0] Gelatinisation: when starch is heated in a liquid, the granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, thickening it into a sol (a colloidal solution, where tiny particles stay spread through the liquid).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.1] On cooling, a concentrated starch mixture may set into a gel (a solid-like network that holds the liquid), which is why a cooled custard or sauce can become firm.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.2] Gelatinised starch thickens white sauces, custard, soups, gravies, risotto and fillings for pies and flans.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.3] To prevent lumps, the granules must be separated before they swell. Mix the starch with a little cold liquid first (a cornflour paste), or coat it in fat as in a roux.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.4] A roux is a mixture of fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added. The fat separates the starch granules, and the starch in the flour gelatinises as the liquid is stirred in, giving a base for white sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.5] Starch is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump if stored in damp air. Store them in airtight containers.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.6] Sugar, especially brown sugar, is also hygroscopic, so it can turn hard or lumpy if left open in damp air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.b] How a carbohydrate food is cooked decides what happens to it. Dry heat browns it, while moist heat softens it and thickens liquids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.0] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch makes the granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs: the starch breaks down into brown dextrins. This gives a brown colour and a crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.1] Dry heat on sugar causes caramelisation and the Maillard reaction, which brown the surface of the food and give it flavour and a crisp texture.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.2] Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch causes gelatinisation: the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid, thickening sauces and puddings. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.3] Moist heat softens the cellulose in vegetable cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest. The vegetable keeps its fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.4] Sugar dissolves in the cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when fruit is stewed, so the syrup carries the flavour of the fruit. Sugar also helps the fruit keep its shape.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.b] Enzymes (proteins that speed up chemical reactions) break complex carbohydrates down in stages until they are small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.0] 1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins breaking starch down into smaller polysaccharides and maltose. This enzyme stops working in the stomach acid, so no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.1] 2. Pancreatic amylase, in pancreatic juice released into the small intestine, completes the breakdown of the remaining starch to maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.2] 3. Maltase, sucrase and lactase, made by the wall of the small intestine and found in the intestinal juice, finish the job. Maltase splits maltose into two glucose units, sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.3] Only monosaccharides (single sugar units: glucose, fructose and galactose) are small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.4] Humans have no enzyme that digests cellulose, so it passes through the intestine as dietary fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.b] Sugar does much more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each property has a use, and exam answers pair the two.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.0] Sweetness: sweetens cakes, desserts and drinks. Solubility: dissolves to make syrups and icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.1] Preservation: sugar draws water out of micro-organisms, so they cannot grow and spoilage is slowed in jam, jelly, chutney and crystallised fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.2] Fermentation: sugar is the food for yeast, which produces carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.3] Aeration (trapping air): creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures such as sponge cakes. Whisking traps air in egg white, and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable, which makes meringue.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.4] Maillard reaction: sugar reacts with amino acids (the units that make up protein) when heated, a non-enzymic browning that gives brown colour and flavour to roast meat, toast, roast potatoes and baked goods. Caramelisation is different because it involves sugar alone.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.5] Gel formation: sugar, acid and pectin together set jam into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.6] Caramelisation gives crème caramel and toffee, crystallisation gives confectionery, and inversion is used in jam and boiled sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.b] Caramelisation, the Maillard reaction and dextrinisation all brown food, so they are easy to confuse. What differs is what is being heated and with what.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.0] Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry until it melts and browns, so it needs no protein, as in caramel sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.1] The Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids together, heated dry, so it browns foods that contain both, such as toast and roast meat.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.2] Dextrinisation is starch, not sugar, broken into brown dextrins by dry heat, which is why toast browns even though bread is mostly starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.3] Gelatinisation is a different change again: starch heated in a liquid (moist heat) swells and thickens it, while inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by acid or enzyme.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.b] Hydrolysis is the reaction that splits big sugars into small ones, and it is the same reaction that happens in digestion. Inversion is the form of it that cooks use on sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: the bond between two units is split and water is added across it. It is the reverse of condensation (joining units with the loss of water).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.1] A disaccharide reacts with water, with enzymes or with heat and acid, and splits into its monosaccharide units. Sucrose gives glucose and fructose, lactose gives glucose and galactose, and maltose gives two glucose units.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.2] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. It produces invert sugar, a mixture of glucose and fructose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.3] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise than sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.4] This is why invert sugar is used in jam and boiled sweets, and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.b] Heating and cooling sugar and starch changes their colour, flavour and texture in three different ways. Each is wanted in some foods and avoided in others.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.0] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a distinctive, pleasant flavour. Overheating makes it dark and bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.1] Caramel is used in caramel sauce, crème caramel, toffee and brûlée.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.2] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution) and the mixture cools, sugar crystals form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.3] Controlled crystallisation gives the texture of fudge, fondant and sweets, while unwanted large crystals make jam grainy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.4] Crystallisation is prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (which causes inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.5] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains of glucose units called dextrins, which are brown. The 2005 scheme names the brown compound pyrodextrin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.6] Dextrinisation gives toast and the crust of baked bread their brown colour and flavour. Browned flour used for a roux thickens less, because its starch has already been broken into shorter chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.7] Dextrinisation is a property of starch, not sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.b] Jam sets because of pectin, a substance in fruit cell walls. Making jam depends on getting the pectin out of the fruit and then giving it the right conditions to set.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.0] Pectin is extracted by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft. Acid, such as lemon juice, helps release the pectin from the cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.1] Fruit high in pectin includes cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit (especially the peel), gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants. Strawberries are low in pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.2] Unripe fruit contains more pectin than ripe fruit, so under-ripe apples are a good source of pectin for jam.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.3] A gel forms only when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions and the mixture is boiled and then cooled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.4] During cooling, the pectin molecules link into a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.5] Too little pectin, as in strawberries, gives a runny jam. Add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit to fix this.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.6] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.b] This simple test shows that starch does not thicken a cold liquid but does thicken it when heated.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.0] Aim: to show the effect of heat on starch mixed with a liquid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.1] Method: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water and observe that it does not dissolve and does not thicken. Then heat the mixture while stirring and observe that it thickens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.2] Variable: temperature is the variable that changes (cold, then heated).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.3] Control and comparison: the unheated cornflour and cold water mixture is compared with the heated one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.4] Result: cold, the starch settles as a cloudy suspension and the mixture stays thin; heated, the granules swell, burst and absorb the water, and the mixture thickens into a sol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.b] Heating sugar on its own, with no water, shows caramelisation happening step by step.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.0] Aim: to observe how granulated sugar changes when it is heated on its own, with no water added.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.1] Method: place a measured quantity of granulated sugar in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat, stirring gently as it melts, and note the colour and smell at regular intervals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.2] Variables: heating time is the independent variable; colour, smell and taste are the dependent variables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.3] Control: a sample of the same sugar left unheated, for comparison.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.4] Safety: molten sugar is extremely hot and causes severe burns, so use heatproof utensils and keep hands and skin away from the pan.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.5] Expected results: the colour darkens gradually from pale to golden, then deep brown, and the caramel smell strengthens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.b] These two tests show a change in starch caused by dry heat and a change in sucrose caused by acid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.0] Dextrinisation, aim: to show that dry heat changes starch. Method: toast white bread, or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.1] Dextrinisation, result: the browned starch is darker and thickens less than unbrowned starch, because its chains are shorter. Safety: the pan is hot.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.2] Inversion, aim: to show that acid changes sucrose. Method: boil a sucrose solution with a little lemon juice and cool it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.3] Inversion, result: the invert sugar does not crystallise as readily and is sweeter.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.4] A Benedict's test shows the invert sugar: reducing sugars give an orange-red colour, while sucrose does not.
