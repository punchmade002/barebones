TARGET hom-clothing-selection-s5: 5. The design process and garment construction
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.b] Making a garment well starts long before the first cut. The design process is a fixed sequence of seven stages that takes you from the brief (the task you are set) to a finished garment that has been judged against that brief.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.0] 1. Define and analyse the brief: identify who the garment is for, its purpose, the occasion, the budget and any constraints, so you know exactly what the garment must achieve.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.1] 2. Investigate and research: gather ideas from magazines, fabric samples, pattern books and observation of current trends, so your design is informed rather than guessed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.2] 3. Generate possible ideas: sketch several designs, varying line, shape, colour and pattern, so you have real choices to compare.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.3] 4. Present the ideas: a labelled fashion sketch and a short written description show the chosen design features and why that design was chosen.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.4] 5. Refine the design: adjust the chosen design, the fabric and the trimmings (the decorative extras) until they meet the brief.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.5] 6. Implement: plan the work, select fabric and pattern, cut, construct and finish the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.6] 7. Evaluate: assess the finished garment against the brief for fit, finish, comfort, function and appearance, and note improvements for next time.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.0.7] The design specification is a list of what the garment must be and do: the wearer, its use, fabric needs, size and fit, care, cost, time and key features. It turns the brief into a checklist you can design and evaluate against.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.1.b] A design has to be communicated before it is made. A fashion sketch shows what the garment will look like, and a written description spells out the details a sketch cannot.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.1.0] A fashion sketch shows the garment's line (the direction of its seams and edges), shape, proportion (how the parts relate in size) and harmony, and it can show colour and fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.1.1] The front view and, where needed, the back view show the design features, and the sketch must label them so the maker can see what is intended.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.1.2] The written description covers colour, design, line, proportion, shape, pockets, seams, neckline, sleeves, fabric and finishes, and accessories.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.1.3] Each design feature named in the description must be visible in the sketch, so the two match and nothing in the description is left unexplained.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.1.4] Diagram: a labelled front-view fashion sketch, with the features (neckline, sleeves, pockets, seams, fabric) labelled.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.b] The fabric decides how a garment looks, hangs and wears, so it must suit both the purpose and the design. Fabric tests help you decide, and the notions (the small items used to make up a garment) must suit the fabric too.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.0] Fabric is chosen for its suitability to the purpose: its absorbency, shrinkage, water repellence and crease recovery. Tests on these properties inform the choice.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.1] A fabric test sample is washed and dried to check shrinkage before cutting, so the finished garment still fits after laundering.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.2] Fabric choice must match the design: a soft drape suits a flowing skirt, while a crisp fabric holds the shape of a tailored jacket. Stretch suits fitted or sportswear, and easy-care fabric suits everyday wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.3] The fabric must also fit the wearer's budget.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.4] Notions such as zips, buttons, interfacing, lining, thread, elastic and trims should be chosen to match the fabric's weight, care needs and colour.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.2.5] Interfacing (a layer sewn or fused inside to stiffen the fabric) gives body and support at collars, cuffs, waistbands and buttonholes, so these areas keep their shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.b] A commercial pattern is a full-scale tissue paper pattern sold in a sealed envelope. Choosing the right one, and reading what is on the envelope, saves money and mistakes. This applies at both levels.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.0] Choose by skill level: a beginner needs a simple pattern with few darts and seams, and a pattern from an easy-sew range (a range of simple patterns) where one is offered.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.1] Also choose by the design and purpose, by the buyer's preference, by the availability of suitable fabric, and by cost and the value it offers for the number of garments it can make.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.2] Choose the size from body measurements: skirts and trousers are sized by hip measurement; dresses, shirts, coats and jackets are sized by bust or chest measurement. Waist is not the sizing measure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.3] The envelope gives the standard body measurements and the finished measurements of the garment, which show how much ease (room to move) the garment has.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.4] The envelope lists the fabric quantity needed for each size and style, which stops you over- or under-buying, and whether the fabric should be plaid or diagonal-matched.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.5] The envelope lists the notions needed, such as buttons, zips, interfacing, lining and trimmings, and the number of pattern pieces, so you can buy everything at once.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.3.6] The envelope shows front and back views and suitable fabrics, which helps you avoid an unsuitable fabric, and it may include advice on the layout and construction order. It also gives the size range.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.4.b] Pattern size is chosen from your measurements, so they must be accurate. Take them over light clothing, with the tape level and comfortably snug, not pulled tight, then compare them with the pattern's measurement chart to choose the size.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.4.0] Bust or chest: measure around the fullest part of the bust or the chest.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.4.1] Waist: measure around the natural waistline, with the tape held comfortably, not pulled tight.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.4.2] Hips: measure around the widest part of the hips, keeping the tape level all the way round, because a tilted tape gives a false reading.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.4.3] Nape to waist: measure from the most prominent bone at the base of the neck down to the waistline.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.4.4] Inside leg: measure from the crotch down the inside of the leg to the required hemline. Shoulder is measured where needed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.b] Where and how the pieces are placed on the fabric decides how the finished garment hangs and looks. Accurate layout and cutting prevent twisted seams and wasted fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.0] Follow the pattern instructions and pin the pattern pieces to the fabric with the fabric folded as directed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.1] Place the straight grain of the pattern (the grain line marked on each piece) parallel to the selvedge (the finished edge of the fabric), so the garment hangs correctly.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.2] Pattern pieces to be placed on a fold must sit exactly on the fold line, so the garment is symmetrical when opened out.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.3] Take account of the nap, or direction of the pile, and of any pattern or print on the fabric, so that all pieces face the same way. Match checks and stripes at the seams.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.4] Keep the fabric flat while pinning and cutting, and cut accurately around the pattern edges, then transfer the markings.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.5.5] Diagram: pattern pieces on fabric showing the grain line parallel to the selvedge and a piece placed exactly on the fold.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.b] A plan of work puts the making in the right order so each step prepares the next. Evaluation follows once the garment is finished, as the last step of the design process.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.0] Stage 1: lay out and cut the pattern pieces.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.1] Stage 2: transfer markings and pin and tack (sew with temporary long stitches) the pieces together to check the fit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.2] Stage 3: machine sew and assemble the garment, joining darts and seams in the order the pattern gives, pressing as you go.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.3] Stage 4: insert fastenings, attach facings and collars, add trimmings and complete the hems and edges.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.4] Stage 5: press the seams and the finished garment, which removes creases and sets the shape, then evaluate the fit and finish and note any changes needed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.5] Pressing removes creases, defines darts, flattens seams and prevents puckering, so the garment looks professional.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.6.6] Careful finishing means neatening seam allowances, clipping curves and trimming corners, so edges lie flat and do not fray.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.b] A commercial pattern is made for a standard figure, so it is often changed to suit a particular person. Check any modification on the pattern before cutting.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.0] A pattern is modified when the buyer's measurements differ from the size chosen, when the design needs a different length or width, or for a different style or preference.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.1] Lengthen a pattern: cut through the alteration lines, spread the pieces, insert tissue paper and redraw the seam and fitting lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.2] Shorten a pattern: fold along one of the alteration lines by half the amount to be removed, and redraw the seam and fitting lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.3] Alteration lines are marked on the pattern, showing where the length can be changed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.4] Similar adjustments can change the width of the waist, hip, bust or sleeve by folding along the marked lines or cutting them.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.7.5] Check the modification on the pattern, matching seams and keeping the grain line, before cutting the fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.b] Elective 2 includes coursework in which you design and make a garment. These are the facts of the 2027 brief and how it is marked.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.0] The coursework is worth 100 marks, or 10 per cent of the total marks: a design folder (25 marks) and a garment (75 marks).
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.1] The 2027 brief: design and sketch an outfit suitable for a young person to wear on holiday, expressing values, interests and personality; the outfit may be knitted, crocheted or machine stitched.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.2] The candidate sets out the design specifications for the outfit, then makes and evaluates one garment from it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.3] The garment must include two of three processes: buttons and buttonholes; darts or shaping; a pocket.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.4] Design folder marks: analysis and research 15, implementation 4, evaluation 6. The folder covers requirements, constraints, theme, sketches and justification, then the work sequence, then evaluation of the garment against the brief.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.5] Garment marks: design aspects 15, skills and processes 50, finish 10.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.8.6] The work must be the candidate's own, done under the teacher's supervision, with every source referenced, including any AI-generated material; the completion date is 6 April 2027.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.b] The garment must include two of three processes: buttons and buttonholes, darts or shaping, and a pocket. Each is made in a set order, and each is reinforced where it takes strain.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.0] Buttonholes: mark the position and length (the button's diameter plus its thickness) on the right front for a woman's garment (left for men), at right angles to the edge for a horizontal hole, then reinforce with interfacing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.1] Buttonholes are worked by machine with a buttonhole foot or by hand with buttonhole stitch, and cut the opening last, without cutting the stitching.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.2] Buttons: sew on to match the buttonholes, marked through the buttonhole position, with a thread shank (a short stem of thread) so the fastened layers lie flat; reinforce on fine or loosely woven fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.3] Darts (folded wedges that shape fabric to the body): transfer the dart markings from the pattern with tailor's chalk or tacking, fold right sides together matching the marks, stitch from the wide end to the point, and tie off or backstitch at the point.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.4] Press bust and waist darts flat, then towards the centre or downward; the direction depends on the dart type and fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.5] Other shaping: gathers, pleats, tucks, princess seams and elastic also shape a garment to the body.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.6] Patch pocket: mark the position, neaten and turn down the top edge as a facing, press under the seam allowances, and topstitch to the garment, reinforcing the top corners so they do not tear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.7] In-seam pocket: the pocket bag is stitched into a side seam so the opening is hidden.
[hom-clothing-selection-s5.9.8] Each process is interfaced or reinforced where it takes strain, and is pressed as it is made.

CANDIDATES hom-clothing-selection-s4: 4. Elements and principles of design in clothing
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.b] The elements are the visual building blocks of a garment, and each can be changed to alter the look of the outfit. There are five: line, shape, colour, texture and pattern.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.0] Line: a visible edge or direction in a garment, created by seams, necklines, hemlines, trims, pleats, fastenings and pattern. Where the eye is led along a line, the garment seems to go in that direction.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.1] Shape: the outline of a garment or of an area within it, such as a neckline, sleeve or pocket. The outline of the whole garment is its silhouette.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.2] Colour: hue (the actual colour), tone (how light or dark it is) and intensity (how bright or dull it is). Warm colours such as red and orange appear to advance, while cool colours such as blue and green appear to recede.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.3] Colour schemes can be monochrome or tonal (one colour in different shades), contrasting or complementary. Colour should also suit the wearer's skin, hair and eye tones.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.4] Texture: the surface quality of a fabric, which can be smooth, rough, shiny or matt, and which can be seen or felt.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.0.5] Pattern: the repetition of line, shape or colour over a surface, such as stripes, checks, herringbone, zig-zag, polka dots, florals or geometric prints.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.b] Because the eye follows lines, the direction of the lines in a garment changes how tall, wide or soft the wearer appears. Designers use this to flatter the figure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.0] Vertical lines give the impression of length, so they make the figure look slimmer and taller.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.1] Horizontal lines draw the eye across the body, which makes the figure look wider and shorter.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.2] Diagonal lines add contrast and movement, and they lead the eye across the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.3] Curved lines soften an outfit and add a feminine feel, such as the curve of a rounded neckline.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.1.4] A princess seam runs from the shoulder or armhole to the hem. It shapes the garment to the body without a waist seam, and the long unbroken line also helps the wearer look taller.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.b] Colour and texture change how much light a garment reflects or absorbs, and that changes how big the wearer appears. Remember that these are effects on appearance only.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.0] Dark colours appear to reduce the size of the figure because they absorb light, while light and bright colours appear to enlarge it. Dark navy and black also define and slim the silhouette.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.1] One colour worn from head to toe, or tonal shades of one colour, creates a longer, unbroken line, so the figure looks taller. Tonal shades also add depth.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.2] Contrasting or complementary colours at the top and bottom divide the outfit and can balance the shape of the figure, for example when the shoulders and hips are different widths.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.3] Shiny fabrics reflect light and increase the apparent size of the figure, while matt fabrics absorb light and reduce it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.2.4] Rough, thick or heavily textured fabrics look bulkier, while smooth fabrics give a neater, more streamlined finish.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.b] Pattern and shape work in the same way as line and colour: they decide where the eye goes and how wide or narrow the figure looks.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.0] Small patterns suit small figures, and a larger figure can carry a large pattern well.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.1] Vertical stripes add height, and horizontal stripes add width and draw attention to the middle of the figure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.2] A patterned area draws the eye to the place where it is worn, for example near the face or neckline for the upper body or at the hem.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.3] Narrow rectangular shapes slim the figure, while triangular or A-line shapes widen the hem or shoulders and so can balance the hips or shoulders.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.3.4] A fitted bodice with a full skirt gives an hourglass shape, and a fitted top with straight trousers gives a streamlined shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.b] The principles describe how the elements are arranged to create a unified garment. There are five: proportion, balance, emphasis, harmony and rhythm.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.0] Proportion: the relationship between the sizes of the parts of a garment, to each other and to the wearer, such as pockets sized to the garment, a collar sized to the neckline, or jacket length to skirt length.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.1] Balance: an even distribution of visual weight. It can be vertical, symmetrical (both sides match) or asymmetrical (the sides differ but still feel even).
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.2] Emphasis: a centre of interest that draws the eye to one area, created by a brooch, contrasting trim, a belt, a bright accessory, a bold hemline or a neckline. There should be only one centre of emphasis in an outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.3] Harmony (unity): the sense that all parts of a design work together and complement one another, for example colours, fabrics and style that suit each other, or one colour repeated.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.4] Rhythm or repetition: the repeated use of a colour, line or shape, such as a repeated button or stripe, which moves the eye through the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.4.5] Diagram: when asked to sketch an outfit, label the elements and principles used, such as line, shape, proportion, harmony and emphasis.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.b] Choosing the elements well lets a wearer look taller, slimmer or better balanced, and draw attention to a feature or away from one. This is the use of the elements that exam questions ask about directly.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.0] To look taller or slimmer, use vertical lines, one colour head to toe or tonal shades, dark colours such as navy and black that absorb light, V-necks, princess seams, a single long line from shoulder to hem and narrow shapes.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.1] To balance or add width, use horizontal stripes, light or bright colours and bulky textures. Contrasting top and bottom colours balance the shoulders and hips.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.2] To draw attention to a feature, put pattern or a bright colour near it, such as near the face for the upper body, or use a belt to define the waist.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.3] To draw attention away from an area, wear dark colour over it, because dark colours appear to reduce size.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.4] Colour also flatters the wearer's hair, eyes and skin; for example, blues can brighten the complexion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.5.5] Shape, fit and proportion matter too: choose lines that suit the figure. A short jacket with slim trousers lengthens the leg line.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.b] The way a garment is made creates its line, shape and emphasis. Here the focus is on the effect of each feature on the design.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.0] Seams join the parts of a garment, and their placement creates line and can add shaping to the body.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.1] Darts remove excess fabric to shape a garment around the bust, waist or hips. Pressing defines the darts.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.2] Princess seams shape the garment without a waist seam.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.3] Pleats, tucks and gathers add fullness and movement, and are used at the waist, sleeve or skirt, where they can add visual interest.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.4] Pockets, collars, buttons, trims and fastenings add line and emphasis, and they must be positioned in proportion to the garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.5] Fabric choice affects the finished design: crisp fabrics hold a shape, as in tailoring, while soft fabrics drape. The fabric's texture and pattern must also suit the design lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.6.6] Design and colour can be applied to fabric by dyeing, screen, roller, stencil or transfer printing, embroidery and appliqué (fabric shapes stitched on).
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.b] A garment is evaluated against three criteria: how well its design serves its function, its comfort and its aesthetic appeal. Examiners often set these as headings for an outfit shown in a picture.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.0] Function: the garment must suit the purpose, activity, climate, weather and occasion it is intended for, such as freedom of movement for sport. Durability, easy care, useful pockets and fastenings that work also count.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.1] Comfort: the fabric (soft, absorbent, breathable or stretch), fit and ease of movement, weight, versatility in different weather and footwear determine whether the wearer is comfortable.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.2] Aesthetic appeal: the colour scheme, line, shape, texture, proportion, balance and emphasis, and any accessories, must work together to produce an attractive and pleasing overall look.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.3] A well-designed garment shows a clear relationship between its design features and the function they serve, for example a drawcord hood, reflective trim or a zip opening.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.4] Using colour and design to flatter the body: choose colours and lines that emphasise the wearer's best features and balance any areas they wish to de-emphasise.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.5] Other headings that appear are shape, proportion, design features, current fashion trend, lifestyle, cost, care and suitability for the occasion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s4.7.6] Method: for each heading make a point from the picture, then give the reason, such as the fabric, cut or colour.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s4: 4. Fabric profiles
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.b] A profile follows one named fabric from its fibre to its end use, with the evidence used to identify it. Examiners ask for it under set headings, so knowing the structure lets you collect marks heading by heading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.0] Name the fabric and its fibres first, for example woven cotton or knitted polyester, because older marking schemes gave a mark for the name alone.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.1] Fibre production: how the fibre is obtained from its raw material, or made from a polymer (a long chain of repeating units).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.2] Yarn production: how the fibres become a yarn, either by spinning staple fibres (short fibres) or by twisting filaments (continuous fibres).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.3] A yarn is fibres or filaments twisted together, and a fabric is made by weaving or knitting yarns, so keep these three terms apart.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.4] Properties: give two or three properties, each linked to the fibre structure or the yarn, so the property has a reason behind it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.5] Identification: give the burning test result and the microscopic appearance of the fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.6] Uses: give suitable household or clothing uses, each with a reason based on the properties.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.7] Other headings seen in past questions are fabric construction technique and fibre composition, so answer exactly the headings the question lists.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.b] Cotton is the usual natural fabric to profile. Its production is a sequence of stages, and each stage has its own job, so learn them in order.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.0] 1. Harvest: the bolls (seed pods) on the cotton plant open when the plant matures, and the fibres are picked by hand or machine.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.1] 2. Ginning: machines separate the fibres from the seeds and remove large impurities, so that only clean fibre goes on to be made into yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.2] 3. Baling and grading: the fibres are baled and graded by length, fineness and colour, so that similar fibres are used together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.3] 4. Cleaning and blending: fibres are cleaned and opened, and mixed so that the yarn is uniform.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.4] 5. Carding: fibres are combed into a loose web and drawn into a continuous sliver (a loose rope of fibres).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.5] 6. Combing (optional): combing before spinning removes short fibres, which gives a finer, smoother yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.6] 7. Spinning: the sliver is twisted into yarn, because the twist is what holds the short fibres together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.b] Cotton's properties come from its fibre structure, and the same structure shows up in the burning test and under the microscope.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.0] Properties: cotton is absorbent, strong and stronger when wet, cool and comfortable to wear, washable and dyes well, which is why it suits everyday clothing and household textiles.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.1] Weaknesses: cotton creases easily unless treated, so it often needs ironing; it also shrinks, burns easily, may mildew and has little elasticity.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.2] Burning test: cotton burns quickly with a steady yellow flame, keeps burning when removed from the flame, smells of burning paper and leaves a soft grey ash.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.3] Microscope: each fibre is a flat, twisted ribbon with natural twists (convolutions), and a central canal (lumen) is visible.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.4] Uses: shirts, jeans, towels, bed linen, curtains and T-shirts, where absorbency and washability matter.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.b] A profile of any natural fabric is accepted, so you can write about wool, linen or silk instead of cotton. Each needs only a few points under each heading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.0] Wool production: shearing (clipping the fleece from the sheep), grading, scouring (washing out grease and dirt), carding and spinning.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.1] Wool properties: warm, resilient and absorbent, but it shrinks and felts, so it needs careful washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.2] Wool burning test: burns slowly with a smell of burnt hair or feathers and leaves a black, crushable ash.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.3] Linen production: it comes from the flax stem, which is retted, scutched, combed and spun.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.4] Linen properties and test: strong, absorbent and creases easily, so it often needs ironing; it burns quickly with a paper smell, like cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.5] Silk production: it comes from the cocoon, with the continuous filaments reeled off.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.6] Silk properties and test: smooth, so it drapes well; it burns slowly with a burnt-hair smell and leaves a crushable black bead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.b] Manufactured fibres are made from chemicals rather than harvested. Nylon is the usual example, and its production turns small molecules into a long chain, then into filaments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.0] 1. Polymer: two chemicals (monomers), joined in equal proportions and heated, link up to form a long nylon polymer.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.1] 2. Extrusion: the molten polymer is forced through a spinneret, a plate with many fine holes, to form filaments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.2] 3. Cooling: the filaments solidify and harden as they cool.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.3] 4. Drawing: the filaments are stretched to line up their molecules, which increases their strength.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.4] 5. Winding: the filaments are wound on bobbins, ready to be made into yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.5] Yarn production: the continuous filaments are twisted together into a filament yarn, which can be textured to add bulk.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.b] Nylon's long, aligned molecules and smooth, non-absorbent filaments explain both its properties and what it does in a flame.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.0] Properties: nylon is very strong, elastic, light, abrasion resistant (it resists rubbing and wear), low in absorbency and quick drying, and it resists creasing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.1] Weaknesses: nylon builds up static, may feel hot to wear because it absorbs so little moisture, and is flammable, melting when it burns.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.2] Burning test: nylon melts and shrinks away from the flame, forms a hard bead and gives off a chemical smell.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.3] Microscope: nylon appears as smooth, even, round rod-like filaments with no scales or twists.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.4] Uses: hosiery, swimwear, lingerie, jackets and carpets, such as in tights and swimsuits, where strength, stretch and quick drying matter.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.b] Polyester and viscose are also accepted as the manufactured fabric in a profile, and they are made in different ways.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.0] Polyester production: a viscous liquid is extruded through a spinneret and solidifies in cold air.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.1] Polyester properties: strong, crease resistant and non-absorbent, so it dries quickly.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.2] Polyester burning test: it burns and melts, with a sweetish smell, and leaves a hard bead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.3] Viscose production: cellulose is treated and dissolved, then forced through a spinneret into a bath.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.4] Viscose properties and test: it is weak when wet, so it needs gentle handling, and it burns like cotton because both are cellulose.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.b] A blend combines two or more fibres in one yarn, or different yarns in the warp and weft, to get the best properties of each. This is different from joining two separate fabrics, because the fibres are combined in the yarn or in warp and weft.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.0] Examples of blends are polyester-cotton, wool-nylon, cotton-Lycra and linen-cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.1] Production: each fibre is produced separately, with cotton harvested, ginned and cleaned and polyester extruded and cut into staple (short) lengths.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.2] Yarn production: the staple fibres are blended before or during carding, then drawn and spun into one yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.3] A blend can also be made by using one fibre yarn for the warp and another for the weft.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.4] Properties: cotton gives absorbency and comfort, while polyester gives strength, crease resistance and quick drying.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.5] Compared with pure cotton, the blend dries faster, is easier to iron and is cheaper; in the same way, linen-cotton is cheaper than pure linen.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.6] Burning test: the results are mixed, because the blend burns like cotton and melts like polyester, so the test is indicative only.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.7] Microscope: both fibre types are visible in the same sample, with cotton's twisted ribbon beside polyester's smooth filaments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.8] Uses: shirts, school uniforms, bed linen and curtains, because easy care and comfort both matter there.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.b] Modification changes a yarn or filament to improve the properties of the fabric made from it. Filament modification changes a continuous filament yarn so that it is bulkier, softer or stretchier, without changing the polymer. Examiners ask for two examples, so know several and what each achieves.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.0] Modification examples in marking schemes include blending, crimping, twisting, mercerising, brushing, flame or stain resistance and water repellence.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.1] Crimping and texturising: the filaments are crimped, looped or curled, trapping air so the yarn is bulkier, warmer and softer, as in textured (bulked) polyester.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.2] Air-jet texturing: compressed air loops the filaments, giving bulk without mechanical crimping.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.3] Heat setting: a textured yarn is heated under tension so that it keeps its crimp or loops in the finished fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.4] Twisting: more twist gives a stronger, smoother yarn, and ply yarns are made by twisting yarns together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.5] Mercerising: cotton under tension is treated with caustic soda, which gives extra lustre, strength, absorbency and a better affinity for dye.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.6] Brushing raises the surface of the fabric, trapping air and giving a warmer, softer fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.7] Textured or slub yarns have uneven thick and thin sections, or loops added to the yarn, to create surface interest in a fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.b] Fibre identification is an investigation in which a known control sample is compared with an unknown sample. Two tests are used together because one alone can mislead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.0] Aim: to identify an unknown fabric sample from its burning behaviour and its microscopic appearance.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.1] Burning method: hold a small fibre sample with tongs or forceps, remove any finish where possible, and bring it slowly towards a flame held over a non-flammable surface.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.2] Observations: record how the fibre behaves as it approaches the flame, as it burns and when removed from the flame, whether it melts, the smell and the appearance of the ash or residue.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.3] Control: so the unknown has something to match, test a known sample of cotton, wool or nylon under the same conditions, and compare the results, so the unknown has something to be matched against.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.4] Microscope: mount a few fibres on a slide, in water, examine them under magnification and compare their shape (twist, scales, rod or ribbon) with reference images.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.5] Safety: ventilate the room, keep water and a fire blanket nearby, use small samples, tie back hair and do not breathe in fumes from the burning sample.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.6] Limits: finishes and dyes change the results, and blends give mixed results, so use both tests together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.7] Conclusion: the fibre is identified when both the burning result and the microscopic appearance match a known fibre.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s5: 5. Fabric construction, finishes, colour, design and testing
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.b] Woven fabric is made by interlacing two sets of yarns at right angles on a loom (the frame that holds the yarns). The way the yarns cross each other decides how firm, smooth or hard wearing the fabric is.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.0] Warp yarns run lengthways and are held under tension on the loom; weft yarns are passed across them at right angles, over and under the warp.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.1] Plain weave: each weft yarn passes alternately over and under each warp yarn. It is the simplest and most stable interlacing, so it is firm and hard wearing, as in calico and cotton shirting.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.2] Twill weave: each weft passes over two or more warp yarns before passing under one, and the pattern shifts each row, forming diagonal lines. Twill is more drapable and hard wearing than plain weave, as in denim.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.3] Satin weave: long floats (stretches of yarn lying loose on the surface) cross with few interlacings, giving a smooth, lustrous face. Because the floats are long, satin is less durable and more prone to snagging.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.4] Plain, twill and satin are the three basic weaves.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.5] Selvedges: the tightly woven edges formed where the weft turns back. They resist fraying, so the edges of the fabric do not unravel.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.0.6] Diagram: interlacing grids for plain, twill and satin weaves, marking the warp and weft yarns that pass over or under at each crossing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.b] Knitted fabric is formed from a series of interlocking loops of yarn, made by hand with needles or by machine. Loops can open and close, so the fabric stretches and recovers its shape.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.0] Weft knitting: loops are formed across each row, so usually one yarn can make the whole fabric. Warp knitting is machine-made, with the yarn zigzagging along the length of the fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.1] Plain (jersey) knit: loops are formed in one direction on each row, giving a smooth face and a looped reverse. It stretches across the width and is used for T-shirts.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.2] Rib knit: knit and purl columns alternate, typically 1 by 1, giving elasticity in both directions, so it suits cuffs and waistbands.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.3] Purl knit: loops are pulled to the reverse side, producing the purl face of a fabric, the reverse of a plain knit. Purl is a looping method, not a different kind of fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.4] Double knit: two sets of needles make a double-thickness fabric that is warm and reversible.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.5] Knitted fabric is stretchy and flexible, with the amount of stretch depending on the yarn used and the looping structure. It is also comfortable and drapes well.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.6] Knitted fabric is warm because its loops trap air, and it can be knitted into shaped pieces or garments with few seams, or none. It may snag, sag or shrink.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.1.7] Uses include jumpers, hats, scarves, socks, T-shirts, blankets and throws.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.2.b] Fabric does not always need yarn. Fibres can be locked together directly, which gives a third construction technique beyond weaving and knitting.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.2.0] Felting: wool fibres are matted together by heat, moisture and agitation (pressure). The scales on each fibre interlock, so no yarn is needed.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.2.1] Non-woven bonding: fibres are fixed into a web using adhesive, heat or needle punching, forming a fabric without weaving or knitting.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.2.2] Needle punching: barbed needles push fibres through a web, interlocking them mechanically.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.2.3] Felt is used for hats, interlinings and insulation; bonded non-woven fabrics are used for interlinings and disposable items.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.2.4] Crochet, lace and macramé are other constructions that appear in marking schemes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.b] A finish is applied after weaving or knitting to change the fabric's appearance, handle (how it feels) or performance. Finishes are used to prevent creasing and shrinkage, add water repellence or stain resistance, increase durability and safety, improve wear and prevent loss of colour.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.0] Crease-resistant finish: a resin cross-links the cellulose in cotton and linen so the fabric resists wrinkling, which means less ironing, for example on a linen skirt. It can slightly reduce strength and absorbency.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.1] Water-repellent finish: a silicone coating makes water bead on the surface and run off, as on raincoats and tablecloths.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.2] Stain-resistant finish: spills sit on the surface so they can be wiped away before they soak in, as on jackets, upholstery and tablecloths.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.3] Anti-static finish: applied to synthetic fibres, or added at home through a fabric conditioner, to reduce static build-up and cling, for example in dresses and linings.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.4] Flame-retardant finish: reduces how easily the fabric burns, so it is used on household textiles and nightwear.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.3.5] Mercerising: cotton is treated with caustic soda, which makes it stronger, lustrous and more absorbent, as in bed sheets and jeans.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.4.b] At Higher Level you must know a third finish. Either of these two will do.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.4.0] Calendering: fabric is passed between heated rollers under pressure, flattening the surface to give a smooth, glossy or embossed finish.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.4.1] Shrink resistance: a finish or controlled pre-treatment reduces the shrinkage of cotton and wool during washing, so the garment keeps its size.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.5.b] Colour is applied either by dyeing or by printing a design onto the surface. Dyeing colours the fibre, yarn or fabric, and the dye must be matched to the fibre type.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.5.0] Reactive dyes form strong chemical bonds with cotton and linen, giving bright, washfast colour. Cotton takes reactive dyes, not acid or disperse dyes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.5.1] Acid dyes are used on protein fibres such as wool and silk, and on nylon, in an acidic dye bath.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.5.2] Disperse dyes are used on polyester and acetate, applied as fine particles dispersed in water.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.5.3] Yarn dyeing: yarn is dyed before weaving or knitting, so it produces stripes, checks and tweeds.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.5.4] Piece dyeing: the finished fabric is dyed in one plain colour after it has been constructed.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.b] Printing places colour on the surface of a made fabric in a pattern, while resist methods block dye from parts of the fabric so those parts stay undyed.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.0] Screen printing: a stencil on a fine mesh screen blocks off the areas that should not print, and ink is pushed through the open mesh with a squeegee. Each colour needs its own screen.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.1] Roller printing: engraved rollers, one for each colour, print the fabric continuously and quickly, so it suits long runs.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.2] Stencilling: a cut stencil and sponge are used, which is simple and suits children's clothing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.3] Transfer printing: the design is printed on paper, and heat transfers it to polyester.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.4] Resist dyeing: wax (batik) or tying (tie-dye) stops dye reaching parts of the fabric, leaving a pattern.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.6.5] Diagram: cross-section of a screen-printing stage showing the screen, stencil, squeegee, ink and the fabric beneath on a padded table.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.b] Design is applied to a fabric by adding decoration once the fabric has been made. Each technique gives a different look and suits different garments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.0] Appliqué: a shaped piece of fabric is attached to a background with stitching, with raw edges turned under or satin stitched, as for logos and jackets.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.1] Embroidery: decorative threads are stitched onto the fabric surface by hand or machine, for example cross stitch or chain stitch.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.2] Quilting: a top fabric, wadding and backing are stitched together through all layers, trapping the wadding in a pattern.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.3] Patchwork: pieces of fabric are sewn together to form a design, often combined with quilting.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.4] Smocking: fabric is gathered and held in pleats by stitching in a pattern, giving stretch and decoration, often on children's clothing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.5] Embossing: a heated, patterned roller presses a raised design into the fabric surface.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.6] Beading and sequins: small pieces are sewn on by hand or machine to add sparkle and texture.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.7.7] Printing (screen, roller, stencil, transfer) also counts as design application in marking schemes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.b] Performance tests compare fabrics under identical conditions to judge their suitability for a particular use, such as a school jacket or uniform.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.0] Fair test: samples are cut to the same size with the grain in the same direction, conditioned in the same room, and tested under the same load and time. This way only the fabric differs.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.1] Crease-recovery test: a sample is folded in half, placed under a set weight for a set time, then released and left to recover. The angle between the two halves is measured with a protractor, and the angle closest to 180 degrees shows the best recovery.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.2] Another crease method is to crumple equal samples in a closed fist for one minute, release them and compare on a five-point scale.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.3] Absorbency test: a set volume of water is dropped onto the fabric and the time it takes to soak in is recorded. A fabric that absorbs quickly is suited to towels.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.4] Colourfastness test: a sample is sewn between white fabrics and washed in a detergent solution. The change in colour of the sample and the staining of the white fabrics are compared with a grey scale.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.5] Shrinkage test: a sample is measured, washed at a set temperature, dried and measured again, and the percentage change is calculated.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.6] Repeat each test and calculate an average so that a single result does not mislead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s5.8.7] Other tests named in schemes: abrasion, insulation, stretch, pilling or snagging, tearing, water resistance and flame resistance.
