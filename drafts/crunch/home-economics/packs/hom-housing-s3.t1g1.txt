TARGET hom-housing-s3: 3. Housing styles
[hom-housing-s3.0.b] Early nineteenth-century housing in Ireland reflected the materials available locally and the wealth of each household. In the countryside the size and roof of a home told you whether the family were labourers, farmers or landowners.
[hom-housing-s3.0.0] Labourers and small farmers lived in single-storey thatched cottages, usually of two rooms (a kitchen and a bedroom), with solid stone or mud walls, which were cheap and came from the local area.
[hom-housing-s3.0.1] Cottages had a half-door and a small number of small windows. The half-door opened into the kitchen and could be closed at the bottom to keep animals out and let in light and air.
[hom-housing-s3.0.2] Thatch was made of reeds, straw or hay, and an open hearth in the main room was used for both cooking and heating, so the kitchen was the centre of family life.
[hom-housing-s3.0.3] Some cottages had a loft directly under the thatch, reached by a ladder, where children slept. This used the roof space as extra sleeping room without adding to the building.
[hom-housing-s3.0.4] Prosperous farmers lived in two-storey, slate-roofed houses with several bedrooms and a parlour (a sitting room kept for visitors), showing their greater wealth.
[hom-housing-s3.0.5] Wealthy landowners lived in large estate houses, with servants' quarters generally in the basement.
[hom-housing-s3.0.6] Diagram: a thatched cottage in cross-section, showing the thatched roof, the loft reached by ladder, the open hearth and the half-door.
[hom-housing-s3.1.b] Towns developed their own styles of housing, and the gap between rich and poor was very visible in the streets. Keep these urban types separate from the rural ones, because examiners do.
[hom-housing-s3.1.0] Georgian terraces in towns (rows of joined houses, for example in Georgian Dublin such as Merrion Square) were two, three or four storeys high, with basements, high ceilings and prominent doors, and were built for the better-off.
[hom-housing-s3.1.1] The middle classes lived in small terraced houses in the suburbs, away from the crowded town centre.
[hom-housing-s3.1.2] Shopkeepers often lived above their shops, which kept them close to their business.
[hom-housing-s3.1.3] Workers lived in small single-storey cottages or, in the towns, in crowded tenements (large buildings divided into rooms let to many families), where space and sanitation were poor.
[hom-housing-s3.2.b] Better transport in the later nineteenth century changed what houses were built from and how they looked, and it changed who lived where in towns.
[hom-housing-s3.2.0] Improved transport brought a wider variety of building materials, because heavy goods such as slate could now be moved cheaply over long distances.
[hom-housing-s3.2.1] Houses became more ornate, with Gothic and Tudor styles.
[hom-housing-s3.2.2] Two-storey, slate-roofed stone buildings became the most common rural dwelling by the end of the century.
[hom-housing-s3.2.3] As wealthier families moved to suburban terraced houses, many large Georgian houses in towns were divided and let to poorer families, which added to overcrowding in town centres.
[hom-housing-s3.3.b] The twentieth century brought new house types and new materials, and for the first time governments played a part in providing and improving homes.
[hom-housing-s3.3.0] Detached houses (standing alone) and semi-detached houses (joined to one neighbour) appeared, along with council housing (homes built by the local authority) and new housing estates.
[hom-housing-s3.3.1] Brick with plaster walls replaced stone, and tiles replaced slates; two-storey houses were built where land was expensive, because building up used less land.
[hom-housing-s3.3.2] Rural electrification in the mid-twentieth century changed rural homes, bringing electric light and power to the countryside.
[hom-housing-s3.3.3] Government housing grants funded improvements to rural houses.
[hom-housing-s3.3.4] The slate-roofed bungalow gradually replaced the thatched cottage in rural Ireland.
[hom-housing-s3.4.b] Later in the twentieth century, housing was built in larger numbers and to higher standards, with new materials that made homes warmer and easier to maintain.
[hom-housing-s3.4.0] Private estates were built to a common design, while individual private houses varied between classical and modern designs.
[hom-housing-s3.4.1] High-rise blocks were built in some cities, for example the Ballymun flats in Dublin in the 1960s.
[hom-housing-s3.4.2] The Building Regulations (legal minimum standards for construction) greatly improved housing standards.
[hom-housing-s3.4.3] New materials and methods such as double glazing, PVC and fibreglass came into use, which cut heat loss and reduced maintenance.
[hom-housing-s3.4.4] Indoor sanitation improved, so that homes had proper indoor toilets and bathrooms.
[hom-housing-s3.5.b] Today's housing is shaped by the cost of land, changing households and concern for the environment, so there is a much wider mix of types than in earlier centuries.
[hom-housing-s3.5.0] Twenty-first-century estates usually consist of one style but include single-storey detached, dormer and two-storey semi-detached houses, which gives a mix of homes for different households.
[hom-housing-s3.5.1] Apartments, townhouses and mixed-density schemes (a mix of house and apartment types on one site) are built, including gated complexes.
[hom-housing-s3.5.2] Old dilapidated buildings and thatched cottages are restored, and custom-designed houses are built on private sites.
[hom-housing-s3.5.3] Attic space is converted into rooms with Velux roof windows, and extensions, conservatories and sunroom extensions are added to many houses to give extra living space.
[hom-housing-s3.5.4] Modern homes include home-office space and open-plan living (a kitchen, dining and living area in one space).
[hom-housing-s3.5.5] Attention is paid to insulation, glazing, security, accessibility and renewable energy, reflecting greater environmental awareness.
[hom-housing-s3.6.b] Each change in style across the centuries had causes, and naming them turns a list of periods into a developed answer.
[hom-housing-s3.6.0] Materials and transport: better transport brought slate, brick and tiles to places that once used only local materials.
[hom-housing-s3.6.1] Rising incomes let more families afford larger and better-built homes.
[hom-housing-s3.6.2] Population moved from the country to towns, which led to terraces, tenements, suburbs and later apartments.
[hom-housing-s3.6.3] Government housing policy and grants, and building regulations, led to council housing, rural improvements and higher standards.
[hom-housing-s3.6.4] Family size and land values: smaller families and expensive land led to semi-detached houses, townhouses and apartments.
[hom-housing-s3.6.5] Environmental awareness led to a focus on insulation, glazing and renewable energy.
[hom-housing-s3.7.b] Different styles suit different households, so each is popular for a reason. Tying each style to the type of household it suits is what examiners reward.
[hom-housing-s3.7.0] Bungalows are single-storey, so they suit older people, people with mobility needs and rural sites, but they need a large site. The dormer bungalow adds bedrooms in the roof space.
[hom-housing-s3.7.1] Two-storey detached houses suit large families and sites with space for a garden, and give privacy, but they cost more to buy and to heat.
[hom-housing-s3.7.2] Semi-detached and terraced houses make efficient use of land and cost less; a terraced house loses less heat than a detached house because it shares walls with its neighbours. The drawbacks are less privacy, noise from neighbours and a small garden.
[hom-housing-s3.7.3] Apartments and duplexes save space and maintenance and are usually in secure complexes with communal space, so they suit first-time buyers, singles, downsizers and city living. The drawbacks are limited space, no private garden and management fees.
[hom-housing-s3.7.4] Townhouses are multi-storey homes built on small urban sites, with limited outdoor space.
[hom-housing-s3.7.5] Traditional, neo-Georgian and restored period houses offer proportion and character, while modern minimalist homes are bright and easy to care for.
[hom-housing-s3.7.6] Custom-designed one-off houses, timber-frame and energy-efficient or passive houses are other styles, and old cottages are kept for holiday and local use in tourist areas.
[hom-housing-s3.8.b] A home has to work for the people living in it, so the make-up and needs of the household strongly affect the style chosen.
[hom-housing-s3.8.0] Family size and stage, and the ages of children, decide how many bedrooms and how much space are needed, so a growing family may choose a two-storey house.
[hom-housing-s3.8.1] Special needs or disability may call for a single-storey home, such as a bungalow, with no stairs.
[hom-housing-s3.8.2] Some households want a garden or a home office, which rules out styles with no private outdoor space.
[hom-housing-s3.8.3] Wanting to be near family and community, and the sense of belonging this gives, can affect where and what a household chooses.
[hom-housing-s3.9.b] Culture shapes what people like and what a place looks like, so it affects both what is built and what people choose to buy.
[hom-housing-s3.9.0] Personal taste, aesthetics and fashion influence choice, for example a preference for a traditional or a modern minimalist look.
[hom-housing-s3.9.1] Regional building traditions and local materials, such as stone or slate, shape the look of rural houses in each area.
[hom-housing-s3.9.2] Respect for heritage and the character of the area leads many people to restore older houses or build in keeping with their surroundings.
[hom-housing-s3.10.b] Money is often the deciding factor, because it limits what a household can buy or build and what it can afford to run.
[hom-housing-s3.10.0] Income and the money available decide what a household can afford, so a first-time buyer may choose an apartment or a terraced house.
[hom-housing-s3.10.1] The price of land affects the cost of any style, which is why cheaper land allows larger, detached houses.
[hom-housing-s3.10.2] The cost of building and the running costs, such as heating, matter because a large detached house costs more to heat than a terraced one.
[hom-housing-s3.10.3] Resale value and investment potential matter because a home is usually a household's largest purchase.
[hom-housing-s3.10.4] A household also decides whether to build, buy new or buy second-hand.
[hom-housing-s3.11.b] The site and its surroundings affect how comfortable a home is and what may be built on it.
[hom-housing-s3.11.0] Aspect (the direction a house faces) matters because south-facing rooms gain daylight and solar heat.
[hom-housing-s3.11.1] Shelter from wind and rain, and exposure to them, affect comfort and heating costs.
[hom-housing-s3.11.2] The slope of the site affects what style can be built on it.
[hom-housing-s3.11.3] Energy efficiency through insulation and glazing lowers heating costs and heat loss.
[hom-housing-s3.11.4] The style must fit the surrounding landscape and neighbouring buildings, and planning rules control scale and materials in many areas.
[hom-housing-s3.11.5] Setting: a school visit to houses from different periods, or photographs of house styles, can be used to discuss their suitability to their surroundings.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s1: 1. Housing choices
[hom-housing-s1.0.b] A household chooses a home by weighing what it needs and can afford against what is available in the place it wants to live. No single factor decides alone, so a good answer shows how several of them pull against each other.
[hom-housing-s1.0.0] Socio-economic factors include income (which decides whether a household can afford a deposit or rent), employment, household size and the stage of the family life cycle, such as a young couple, young children, teenagers, an empty nest or an older couple whose children have left home.
[hom-housing-s1.0.1] Each stage of the family life cycle needs a different amount of space, which is why a growing family and an older couple choose differently.
[hom-housing-s1.0.2] Cost decides the size, type and location of home a household can afford. The deposit and running costs matter as much as the purchase price or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.0.3] Legal and lender fees, stamp duty, repayments, insurance, maintenance, heating and travel to work must all be counted, not just the headline price. Parental help with a deposit is also an economic factor.
[hom-housing-s1.0.4] Special needs, such as those of older people or people with a disability, may call for single-storey living, step-free access or a stairlift, because stairs and steps can make a home unusable.
[hom-housing-s1.1.b] Once a household knows what it needs and can afford, it looks at where homes are, what kinds exist and whether any are on offer.
[hom-housing-s1.1.0] Location is shaped by proximity to work, schools, shops, childcare, public transport, health services and family or friends. Urban areas offer services and jobs, while rural areas offer space and quiet. Safety, noise and risks such as flooding or pollution also count.
[hom-housing-s1.1.1] Personal preference covers layout, aspect (the direction the home faces), view, garden size and whether the household wants to own or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.1.2] Type and size of home: a household chooses between a bungalow, two-storey, detached, semi-detached, terraced, townhouse, duplex, apartment or bedsit. It also weighs room for the family, a possible attic conversion or extension, parking, space to work from home, and energy efficiency, shown by the BER.
[hom-housing-s1.1.3] Availability, meaning the supply of suitable homes at a given time and place, limits choice. In some urban and commuter areas private supply is short, which raises prices and rents, and social housing waiting lists are long because demand exceeds supply.
[hom-housing-s1.1.4] Setting: a case study of a student, a young couple or a retired couple, asking which of these factors matters most to them. For example, a student may rank low cost and closeness to college first, while a retired couple may rank step-free access and nearby health services first.
[hom-housing-s1.2.b] The way new homes are planned and built changes over time. Examiners want you to recognise the current trends and say what each means for households.
[hom-housing-s1.2.0] Mixed estates plan detached, semi-detached, terraced and apartment or duplex units together, so that different household types live in the same community.
[hom-housing-s1.2.1] New developments include shared open space and play areas, and in larger estates a crèche or shops, so families have facilities on their doorstep. Many estates and complexes have a gated or controlled entrance.
[hom-housing-s1.2.2] Refurbishment and inner-city redevelopment bring vacant, derelict and older buildings back into residential use. This includes the restoration of period homes, attic conversions and extensions, which add space without using new land.
[hom-housing-s1.2.3] Apartment living and higher-density development grow in cities and towns, using less land per household. Townhouses are built on small urban sites for the same reason.
[hom-housing-s1.2.4] An eco-house (an energy-efficient, environmentally friendly or passive home) is an emerging trend. They have high insulation and renewable heating, and may use timber frame or other modern methods of construction, so they cost less to heat.
[hom-housing-s1.2.5] Housing development follows employment. Demand rises where jobs grow, which pulls people towards commuter towns and suburbs around cities, and commuting distance becomes a cost to the household. Older marking schemes also name satellite towns and one-off rural houses.
[hom-housing-s1.2.6] Working from home creates demand for a study or home-office space, which affects the size and layout of home a household looks for.
[hom-housing-s1.3.b] Housing policy is the government's plan for how enough suitable homes are provided and who is helped. The current national plan is Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025 to 2030, published by the Government in November 2025. It rests on activating supply and supporting people.
[hom-housing-s1.3.0] Increasing the supply of new homes, with investment in the infrastructure (water, wastewater and roads) that housing needs, because homes cannot be built where these services are missing.
[hom-housing-s1.3.1] Social housing is built by local authorities and approved housing bodies, and social and affordable housing is delivered by these together with voluntary and co-operative housing organisations.
[hom-housing-s1.3.2] Affordable homeownership supports include the Starter Homes programme, and cost rental homes are let at rents based on the cost of providing them.
[hom-housing-s1.3.3] Measures to reduce and prevent homelessness include the Housing First programme, and rented accommodation must be registered, so that rented homes are on a register.
[hom-housing-s1.3.4] National rent controls protect tenants, and modern methods of construction (factory-built components) speed up building.
[hom-housing-s1.3.5] Sustainable communities are a policy aim: new housing should be supported by shops, schools, transport, parks and other infrastructure and services. Higher-density living and increased housing supply in urban areas aim to make more homes available at affordable cost.
[hom-housing-s1.3.6] Energy-efficient homes are promoted through the Building Regulations, BER ratings and SEAI grants for upgrading existing homes.
[hom-housing-s1.3.7] Accommodation for Travellers is planned by each local authority under a Traveller Accommodation Programme, required by the Housing (Traveller Accommodation) Act 1998.
[hom-housing-s1.3.8] Policy affects a household's choice because it changes what is built and where, what supports and grants are open to a buyer or tenant, and how much a tenant is protected.
[hom-housing-s1.4.b] Buying makes the household an owner-occupier. It gives control and a possible asset, but it demands money up front and a long commitment.
[hom-housing-s1.4.0] Advantage: a home may rise in value, so it can act as an investment, and ownership builds equity (the part of the home's value the owner owns).
[hom-housing-s1.4.1] Advantage: security of tenure, meaning the right to stay in the home, because an owner cannot be asked to leave by a landlord.
[hom-housing-s1.4.2] Advantage: freedom to choose the design and type of home, to alter and decorate it, and to decide whether to share it with others.
[hom-housing-s1.4.3] Advantage: mortgage repayments (the regular payments on a loan taken to buy the home) may cost less than rent over the long term, and grants or other supports may apply to some purchases.
[hom-housing-s1.4.4] Disadvantage: a large deposit and a long financial commitment are needed before the purchase can be completed.
[hom-housing-s1.4.5] Disadvantage: legal, lending, valuation and survey fees and stamp duty add to the initial cost on top of the price.
[hom-housing-s1.4.6] Disadvantage: the owner pays for all maintenance, repairs and insurance, and property tax where it applies, because there is no landlord to carry them.
[hom-housing-s1.4.7] Disadvantage: the value may fall, leaving the owner owing more than the home is worth, and buying ties the household to one location, so moving is slow and expensive.
[hom-housing-s1.5.b] A tenant pays a landlord (the owner who lets the home) for the right to live in a property, under a tenancy. Renting asks for little money at the start but gives no ownership.
[hom-housing-s1.5.0] Advantage: little capital is needed, usually only a deposit and rent paid in advance.
[hom-housing-s1.5.1] Advantage: mobility, so a household can move when work or family circumstances change.
[hom-housing-s1.5.2] Advantage: the landlord is normally responsible for structural repairs and maintenance, so the tenant has no repair bills.
[hom-housing-s1.5.3] Advantage: a wider choice of location and accommodation, from apartments and houses to shared houses and digs.
[hom-housing-s1.5.4] Advantage: tenants have legal protections, including notice rules before a tenancy can be ended.
[hom-housing-s1.5.5] Disadvantage: rent paid gives no ownership or return, often described as dead money. The rent can also be as high as a mortgage repayment on a similar home, and there is no long-term security if the landlord sells.
[hom-housing-s1.5.6] Disadvantage: an agency fee, a deposit and rent in advance add to the initial cost.
[hom-housing-s1.5.7] Disadvantage: the tenant must insure the contents of the home.
[hom-housing-s1.5.8] Disadvantage: facilities may be poor or shared, and the tenant has no say over changes to the property.
[hom-housing-s1.6.b] The private rented sector is made up of homes let by private landlords rather than by the State. Its standards and registration are regulated to protect tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.0] Private rented housing (houses, apartments, flats and rooms) is provided by individual landlords and businesses, mainly in cities and larger towns. Rent depends on demand and supply, location, size, quality and amenities, and quality varies.
[hom-housing-s1.6.1] Landlords must register tenancies with the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), which helps the state track rented housing and its standards. The RTB also deals with disputes between landlords and tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.2] Rented homes must meet the minimum standards in the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019, enforced by local authorities, who inspect rented homes. The property must be structurally sound and in good repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.3] Every room must have adequate ventilation and lighting, hot and cold water must be provided, and there must be heating and fire safety. Gas and electricity supplies must be safe.
[hom-housing-s1.6.4] Bathrooms, showers and other sanitary facilities must be in good working order and kept in a proper state of repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.5] Private renting is popular with students, young workers and people who are saving for a deposit to buy a home. It gives flexibility to people in temporary work or who want to move, and the State helps some tenants with rent through social housing supports.
