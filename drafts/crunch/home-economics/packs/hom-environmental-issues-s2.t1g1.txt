TARGET hom-environmental-issues-s2: 2. Recycling and waste management
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.b] Sustainable waste management means reducing waste first, then reusing, recycling and recovering value, and disposing of only what is left. The waste hierarchy ranks the options from most to least preferred, so that as little waste as possible reaches disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.0] The order of preference is prevention, minimisation, reuse, recycling, recovery (including energy recovery) and disposal. Marking schemes phrase it as reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.1] Prevention and minimisation come first: buying only what is needed and avoiding excess packaging stops waste being created in the first place, so there is nothing to collect or treat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.2] Reuse means using an item again for its original or another purpose, such as refilling a container, reusing a jar for storage, repairing, donating or buying second-hand. The item stays whole, which is why reuse is not recycling.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.3] Recycling processes used materials into new products, such as paper, glass, metals, some plastics and textiles. It saves raw materials and usually energy, and keeps waste out of landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.4] Recovery means burning residual waste to generate heat or electricity (energy from waste); gas collected from landfill can also be used for energy. Energy recovery is not recycling, because the material is not made into a new product.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.5] Disposal, mainly landfill, is the least preferred option because it uses up land, and food and other organic waste decomposes without oxygen (anaerobically), releasing methane, a potent greenhouse gas.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.6] In Irish law the hierarchy is applied through the Waste Management Act 1996 (as amended). It sets up controls on waste collection and movement, and gives local authorities and the EPA roles in promoting waste reduction, recycling and reuse.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.b] Much household waste is biodegradable, meaning it can be rotted down by living things. Composting turns it into something useful instead of sending it to landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.0] Biodegradable material such as food scraps, tea leaves and garden waste is broken down by micro-organisms into a nutrient-rich soil conditioner called compost. Composting differs from recycling because organic matter rots down rather than being reprocessed into a new product.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.1] Composting keeps biodegradable waste out of landfill, so less methane is produced as it would otherwise rot without oxygen.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.2] Compost returns nutrients and organic matter to the soil, so gardeners need less peat-based compost, and it is cheap for households to make.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.3] In Irish households food and garden waste is collected in the brown bin where a collection is provided. The three bins are green (dry recyclables), brown (food and garden waste) and black (general waste).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.b] Most waste can be cut at home by what a household buys and how it sorts what is left. Each action below moves waste up the hierarchy, away from disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.0] Plan meals and write a shopping list, so food is not bought and then thrown away.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.1] Buy concentrated products, such as concentrated detergents, which need less packaging per wash. Buy in bulk where it cuts packaging per unit, and use refill stations and refillable containers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.2] Use reusable bags, bottles and containers instead of single-use ones, so fewer items are made and thrown away. Reuse jars, tubs and shopping bags.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.3] Separate waste at home into recyclables, food and garden waste, and general waste, so that each stream can be treated in the right way.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.4] Choose products in recyclable, recycled or biodegradable packaging, and products with the EU Ecolabel. Repair items rather than replacing them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.5] Take paint and other household chemicals to a civic amenity site (the local authority's recycling and waste centre), not the bin, because they can harm the environment and the people handling waste. Do not put paint, oil, chemicals or batteries in the bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.b] Packaging carries symbols that tell the consumer how it can be treated after use. The recycling symbol is the most common, and it says less than many people think.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.0] The recycling symbol (three arrows forming a loop) shows that the packaging is made of material that can be recycled. It does not guarantee that a local collection accepts it, so do not say it is certainly accepted in the household recycling bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.1] A percentage inside the arrows shows the proportion of recycled content in the packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.2] A number inside a triangle on plastic is a plastic-type code: 1 is PET, used for drinks bottles, and 2 is HDPE, used for milk bottles and detergent containers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.b] Two other symbols are often confused with the recycling loop. They tell the consumer about who paid for recovery and about a product's whole environmental effect, not whether the pack can be recycled.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.0] The Green Dot shows that the producer of the packaging has contributed financially to the recovery and recycling of packaging waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.1] In Ireland the Green Dot is shown by members of Repak, the packaging recovery scheme, so a producer who pays into Repak can put it on their packs.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.2] The Green Dot does not mean the item itself is recyclable or recycled, which is why it must not be treated as a recycling symbol.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.3] The EU Ecolabel, a flower symbol, is an audited, voluntary label certifying that a product or service causes minimum damage to the environment across its life cycle.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.4] The Ecolabel assessment considers the energy and materials used in making the product, the pollution produced, and how far it can be reused or recycled.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.b] Packaging makes up a large share of household waste, so government, producers, local authorities and consumers all act to reduce it. A strong answer names one initiative and gives two details about it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.0] The levy on single-use plastic shopping bags, charged at the point of sale since 2002, discouraged their use and increased the use of reusable bags.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.1] The landfill levy is a charge per tonne on waste sent to landfill, which makes landfill more expensive and so encourages recycling and recovery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.2] Packaging producers must meet national recovery and recycling targets under Irish packaging regulations, and most meet them by funding the Repak scheme (the Green Dot).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.3] Local authority recycling schemes gather glass, cans, paper and plastics through kerbside collections and bring banks or civic amenity centres.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.4] The Re-turn deposit return scheme began on 1 February 2024. A refundable deposit is paid on plastic drinks bottles and aluminium and steel cans, which are returned in shops or to reverse vending machines. Glass and dairy containers are not included.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.5] Consumers can refuse over-packaged goods, choose loose produce and bring their own containers to refill counters.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.6] Manufacturers reduce excess packaging by using less material per product and lighter packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.b] Each packaging material has strengths for the product and a different fate as waste. Knowing both lets you judge how sustainable a material is.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.0] Flexible films are thin plastic films that are light, low in cost, fit the shape of the food closely, can be printed on and can be heat-sealed (closed by melting the edges together).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.1] Flexible films give a good barrier against moisture and gases, which extends shelf life and reduces spoilage, and they are suitable for frozen food.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.2] Their drawback is that many multi-layer films are hard to recycle, so they are often sent to landfill or energy recovery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.3] Glass can be washed and reused, or recycled repeatedly without loss of quality, but it is heavy and costly to transport.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.4] Steel and aluminium cans are highly recyclable, and aluminium can be recycled repeatedly without loss of quality.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.5] Paper and card are recyclable, though their fibres shorten with each recycling cycle. They also come from a renewable raw material, are biodegradable, are light and take printing well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.6] Biodegradable packaging breaks down naturally through the action of micro-organisms. Examples are paper or card, and compostable starch-based packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.b] WEEE is waste electrical and electronic equipment, and it is governed by its own collection and take-back rules. It must be kept out of the general bin because of what it contains.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.0] Electrical goods contain hazardous substances such as lead, mercury, cadmium and brominated flame retardants, and also valuable materials that can be recovered, so they must not go into general household waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.1] The crossed-out wheeled bin symbol means the item must be collected separately from household waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.2] Route 1: repair, sell or donate a working appliance first, because keeping it in use is the top of the waste hierarchy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.3] Route 2: when a consumer buys a new equivalent item, the retailer must take back the old one free of charge, including on home delivery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.4] Route 3: a civic amenity site or recycling centre accepts small appliances, batteries and other electrical items. Larger electrical shops must accept very small electrical items without a purchase, and batteries can be returned to shops without buying anything.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.5] Take batteries out of appliances and dispose of them separately before the appliance is handed in. Erase personal data from smart appliances.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.6] Old fridges and freezers contain refrigerant and insulating foam, which must be removed by an authorised treatment facility. Make the appliance safe before disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.7] The legal basis is the WEEE Directive, applied in Ireland by the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014.

CANDIDATES hom-food-additives-s2: 2. Packaging and labelling
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.b] Food packaging is made from six main materials, and each one suits certain foods because of its properties. Knowing a material's strengths tells you what it is used for.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.0] Metals (aluminium and tin) are strong, airtight and can be heat-processed, so they are used for canned fish and fruit, takeaway containers and aerosol whipped cream. Tins are lacquered inside so the metal does not react with the food.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.1] Glass (jars and bottles) is hygienic, transparent, impervious, resealable and reusable, so it is used for beverages, mayonnaise, sauces, preserves and cooking oil. Its drawbacks are that it is heavy and breakable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.2] Plastics (polythene, PET and similar polymers) are lightweight, flexible, moisture-proof, transparent and can be heat-sealed. They are used for polythene bags, PET soft drink bottles, yoghurt pots, dairy spreads and dried pasta, but are mostly non-biodegradable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.3] Polystyrene is lightweight, insulating, shock-absorbing, easily moulded and stackable, which suits egg trays and takeaway trays and containers. It is not readily biodegradable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.4] Paper and cardboard are light, inexpensive, easy to print on and renewable. They are used for cereal boxes, egg cartons and paper bags for bread, cakes, flour and sugar, but they are not moisture-proof unless coated.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.5] Waxed cartons use a wax coating to make the board waterproof, so they are cheap, lightweight, printable and heat-sealable. They are recyclable or biodegradable according to the scheme.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.b] A pack has to do a job for the food inside it, so examiners expect you to list the qualities a good pack needs. Which qualities matter most depends on the food.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.0] Packaging must be strong and durable enough to survive handling and transport, so the food arrives undamaged.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.1] It must be airtight or moisture- and vapour-proof, depending on the food, so the food does not dry out, go soft or spoil.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.2] It must be hygienic and safe, and must not react with or taint the food, so it should be odourless.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.3] It should be easy to open and, where needed, easy to reseal, so the consumer can use it a little at a time.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.4] It should be lightweight, stackable and easy to store, which makes it cheaper to transport and easier to shelve.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.5] It should be heat resistant where the food is heated in the pack, and printable so that labels can be applied.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.6] It should be attractive to the consumer and economical to produce.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.b] Packaging does more than wrap food. It has four jobs that examiners ask for, and a fifth, convenience, is also credited.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.0] Protection: packaging guards food against physical damage and against contamination by micro-organisms and dirt, so the food stays safe and intact.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.1] Containment: it holds the product in the portion size intended for the consumer.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.2] Information: it carries the product name, ingredients, nutrition and storage details, as well as weight, allergens, cooking instructions and branding.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.3] Tamper evidence: a sealed pack shows whether the contents have been interfered with, so the consumer can tell if it has been opened.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.4] Convenience: packs that are easy to open, reseal, store and carry make the food easier to use.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.b] Packaging that protects food can also create waste. How much harm it does depends on whether the material is renewable, biodegradable (breaks down naturally) and recyclable (reprocessed into new products).
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.0] Metal and plastic packaging is not biodegradable and is made from non-renewable resources, but both can be recycled.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.1] Glass can be reused and recycled indefinitely without losing quality.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.2] Paper and cardboard are made from a renewable resource, are biodegradable and are widely recyclable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.3] Polystyrene is bulky and not readily biodegradable, so its environmental impact depends on whether it is collected for recycling.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.4] The impact is reduced by using less packaging, reusing containers and recycling materials.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.5] In Ireland, the Re-turn deposit return scheme supports recycling of plastic drinks bottles and aluminium and steel cans by refunding a deposit: 15c for containers under 500 ml and 25c for 500 ml and over.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.6] The scheme launched nationwide on 1 February 2026 for PET plastic bottles and aluminium or steel cans from 150 ml to 3 litres, under the Separate Collection (Deposit Return Scheme) Regulations 2021 (S.I. 599 of 2021).
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.b] Two kinds of packaging answer particular problems: one makes food last longer, the other makes the pack itself break down after use.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.0] Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP): the air is removed and replaced with a controlled mixture of carbon dioxide, nitrogen and oxygen, and the pack is heat sealed.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.1] The gas mixture slows the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds, so the shelf life of the food is extended. It also helps keep the food's colour.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.2] MAP is used for fresh meat, fish, fruit and vegetables, baked goods and cheese.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.3] Biodegradable packaging breaks down into the raw materials of nature, with micro-organisms doing much of the work.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.4] Examples of biodegradable packaging: paper bags for bread, cakes, sugar and flour, and cardboard for cereals, takeaway foods and eggs.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.5] Diagram: a labelled sealed pack showing the gas mixture inside the tray and the heat-sealed film lid.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.b] Irish food labels follow Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, and the FSAI coordinates enforcement.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.0] The European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014 (S.I. No. 556 of 2014, in force 13 December 2014) give effect in Ireland to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, and official agencies working under FSAI service contracts carry out enforcement.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.1] The name of the food must be stated, along with a list of ingredients in descending order by weight, so the main ingredient comes first.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.2] Allergens such as milk, egg, nuts and gluten cereals must be emphasised within the ingredients list, so people with allergies can spot them. The Regulation lists 14 allergens, including cereals containing gluten, milk, eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, fish, crustaceans, molluscs, soya, celery, mustard, sesame, lupin, sulphur dioxide and sulphites.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.3] The net quantity must be stated, and the name and address of the food business operator must appear on the label.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.4] A use-by date is given for highly perishable food and is a food safety date, so do not eat after it. A best-before date (the date of minimum durability) is a quality date, after which the food may still be safe.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.5] Storage conditions and cooking or usage instructions must be given, for example keep refrigerated or stir before use.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.6] The country of origin or place of provenance is given where the law requires it.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.7] Additives are identified by their class name followed by the specific name or E number, for example preservative: E202.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.8] Labels also carry a lot or batch code, which lets a product be traced and recalled if there is a problem.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.b] The nutrition declaration is a standard table that lets shoppers compare foods on the same terms. Claims on the front of a pack are separate, and need careful reading.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.0] The mandatory nutrition declaration gives seven items: energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.1] Values are given per 100 g or 100 ml, which allows fair comparison between products, and often per portion as well.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.2] Claims such as low fat, high in fibre or contains live cultures must be accurate and not misleading, and nutrition and health claims must meet the EU rules, set by Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.3] Low fat does not necessarily mean low sugar, salt or energy, so the full declaration must be read.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.b] A label is the consumer's main source of information at the point of purchase. It helps shoppers choose, stay safe and avoid waste.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.0] Labels allow consumers to compare products on energy, fat, sugar and salt, rather than being guided by brand, pictures or health claims.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.1] Consumers following a low-salt, low-saturated-fat or high-fibre diet can select products by their declared values.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.2] Ingredients and allergen emphasis protect people with coeliac disease, milk, egg, nut or other allergies.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.3] Use-by and best-before dates, storage and reheating instructions protect safety and reduce food waste.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.4] The origin on the label lets consumers choose to buy Irish.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.5] The manufacturer's details and the lot code support traceability and complaints.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.6] Quality assurance (such as the Bord Bia mark), organic, fair-trade and gluten-free marks guide consumers with particular ethical or dietary choices.

CANDIDATES hom-appliances-equipment-s2: 2. Selecting a large and a small household appliance
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.b] Before buying, compare the cost, size and capacity of each model against what the household needs. A good choice is value for money, which means quality against price, not simply the lowest price.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.0] Initial cost: compare the purchase price, including delivery and installation, across several shops and brands, so you know the true cost of getting the appliance working in your home.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.1] Running cost: this is the energy and water the appliance uses over its life. Compare it with the purchase price, since a cheap appliance can cost more to run, and the energy label shows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.2] Size and space: the appliance must fit the available space and allow room for doors to open, air to circulate (ventilation) and access to plumbing and power points.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.3] Capacity: match it to household size, such as litres of fresh and frozen food for a fridge or kilograms of laundry for a washing machine, and litres for a kettle.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.4] Payment method: compare cash, debit or credit card and credit agreements, and the total amount payable under any credit, because credit adds to the final cost.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.b] Cost and size are not the whole story. A buyer also checks how long the appliance will last, who will fix it, and how easy and safe it is to use every day.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.0] Brand reputation and reliability: established brands with a record of durable products, good reviews and available spare parts are less likely to need early repair.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.1] Guarantee and after-sales service: check the length of the guarantee, what it covers, and how quickly repairs, servicing, spare parts, delivery and installation are available.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.2] Ease of operation and cleaning: clear controls, removable parts and filters, smooth surfaces, dishwasher-safe parts and cord storage reduce the time and effort of use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.3] Safety features: automatic cut-outs, interlocks, child lock, cool-touch surfaces and recognised safety marks reduce the risk of injury or fire.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.4] Family needs and modern features: choose features the household will actually use, such as programmes, timers and smart controls, rather than paying for extras that stay unused.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.b] The same criteria apply to every appliance, but how much each one matters depends on the size and price of what you are buying. Apply them to one large and one small example.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.0] Large appliances (cooker, fridge, washing machine, dishwasher, tumble dryer) are expensive and long-lived and usually delivered and installed, so cost, energy efficiency, size and servicing weigh most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.1] Small appliances (kettle, toaster, iron, food processor, mixer) are cheaper and portable, so storage, cleaning and cord length weigh more.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.2] Examples to use for the individual studies: a washing machine, fridge-freezer or dishwasher as the large appliance, and a kettle, toaster, food processor or iron as the small one.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.b] Design is how an appliance looks and works, and construction is what it is made of and how well it is built. Together they decide how safe, durable and easy to clean it is.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.0] Design affects safety, durability, ease of use and cleaning, and how well the appliance fits the style and colour of the kitchen.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.1] Controls should be clearly labelled, easy to reach and simple to read; large, simple controls suit older users and children.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.2] Weight, handles and grip should suit the person using the appliance, so that lifting and pouring are safe, and non-slip feet give stability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.3] Robust construction and good-quality materials, such as stainless steel for surfaces and blades, increase durability. Other materials used include heat-resistant or cool-touch plastic, glass and enamelled steel.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.4] Smooth, sealed surfaces and removable parts are easier to clean and give fewer places for food and bacteria to collect. Sealed joints and a strong flex and plug add durability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.5] Design for safety and efficiency: insulation, interlocks, automatic cut-off, energy-saving settings and quiet running make the appliance safer and cheaper to use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.6] Examples of safe design: an interlock stops a machine working while its door is open, so fingers cannot reach moving parts.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.b] The energy label lets buyers compare the energy efficiency of appliances at a glance, on a common scale. An efficient appliance costs less to run and does less harm to the environment.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.0] The EU energy label rates an appliance from A to G, where A is the most efficient and G the least efficient. This scale applies to fridges, freezers, washing machines, washer-dryers and dishwashers from 1 March 2021, and to tumble dryers from 1 July 2025.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.1] The label is displayed on refrigerating appliances (fridges, freezers and fridge-freezers), washing machines, washer-dryers, dishwashers and tumble dryers. Two named appliances are enough if asked to name two.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.2] A-rated appliances use the least electricity, so they cost least to run and cause the least harm to the environment; G-rated appliances use the most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.3] The label gives annual energy consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which indicates likely running cost and environmental impact. Washing machine and dishwasher labels give energy per 100 cycles.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.4] It also gives, depending on the appliance, the noise level in decibels, capacity and water use. A refrigeration label shows the fresh and frozen capacity in litres.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.5] The current label carries a QR code that links to the product's entry in the EU product database (EPREL) for more detail.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.6] Energy in kWh equals power in kW multiplied by hours used. Higher Level answers can use this to compare the running costs of two appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.7] Efficiency in use is also the consumer's job: use full loads, eco programmes and lower temperatures, switch off standby, and choose the correct size.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.b] A star rating on a fridge-freezer or freezer tells you how cold the frozen-food compartment is and how long frozen food keeps in it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.0] The star rating applies to the frozen-food compartment of fridges and fridge-freezers and to freezers, not to the fresh-food section.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.1] One star: the compartment holds frozen food at −6°C for up to one week.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.2] Two stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −12°C for up to one month.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.3] Three stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C for up to three months.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.4] Four stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C to −25°C for up to one year, and fresh food can be frozen in it. Only four stars allows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.5] The star rating measures storage performance, not energy efficiency, so the energy label is used to compare running costs.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.b] Marks on an appliance or its packaging tell the buyer that a standard has been met. Each mark means something different, so know what each one does and does not show.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.0] The CE mark is the manufacturer's declaration that the product meets the health, safety and environmental requirements of the European Union. It is not independent testing.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.1] The double insulation symbol, a square inside a square, shows that the appliance has two layers of insulation (a Class II appliance), so it needs no earth wire and its plug has no earth connection. It is common on plastic-bodied small appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.2] The Irish Standard mark of the National Standards Authority of Ireland (NSAI) shows that the product has been certified to a standard.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.3] An ISO 9000 quality mark shows that the maker's quality-management system has been assessed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.4] Marks should appear on the appliance or its packaging, and a mark that cannot be found on the product does not show certification.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.5] Safety marks show that a product has been tested to a standard, but they do not guarantee its performance or reliability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.6] A manufacturer's guarantee or a retailer's own-brand label is not an independent mark.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.b] Before buying, a consumer gathers information from several sources. Some are independent, some come from sellers and may be biased, and some are personal experience.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.0] Independent sources: consumer magazines and websites that test and compare products publish independent results on appliance models.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.1] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC), the body for consumer protection and information, publishes guidance for consumers on their rights when buying appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.2] Citizens Information offers free, independent information on consumer rights and on buying goods and services. The European Consumer Centre Ireland helps with cross-border purchases, and libraries also hold information.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.3] Manufacturers' leaflets and brochures give specifications, guidelines for use and guarantee terms, but they are promotional documents.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.4] Product labels and packaging show the energy rating, safety marks, capacity and guarantee details, so you can compare models for example in the shop.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.5] Sales staff can explain features, but their advice may be influenced by the stock and margins of the shop. Advertisements highlight strengths, so check their claims against independent sources.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.6] Word of mouth, online user reviews and in-shop demonstrations reflect other people's experience; read them critically because they can be unreliable.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.7] Benefits of getting information before buying: compare price, installation and running cost; find the most energy-efficient model; compare features, design, size and ease of operation; reduce the influence of advertising; avoid a costly mistake.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.b] An appliance affects the environment at three points: when it is chosen, while it is used and when it is thrown out. A responsible consumer thinks about all three.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.0] Choosing: pick the best energy class you can afford and the correct size, and prefer repairable, durable appliances with eco and low-water programmes and little packaging.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.1] Using: run full loads, use eco and short cycles and lower temperatures, switch off standby, defrost freezers, and do not leave fridge doors open, because each wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.2] Maintaining: descale and maintain appliances so that they keep working efficiently.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.3] Disposing: repair, sell or donate an appliance while it still works, so it is not wasted.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.4] Maintaining also helps because limescale and ice build-up make an appliance work harder and use more energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.5] Under the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014, a retailer must take back an old appliance of similar type free of charge, one for one, when a new one is bought, so old appliances do not end up in landfill.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.6] Collection is on delivery if notice was given, and larger shops must accept very small items without a purchase.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.7] Electrical waste can also go to a civic amenity site. Never put it in the household bin, which the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol warns.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.b] A washing machine shows how the selection criteria apply to a large appliance, where cost, efficiency, size and installation all matter.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.0] Capacity: the drum size, stated in kilograms of dry laundry, should match the number of people in the household.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.1] Spin speed, measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), affects how dry clothes are after washing and so how much drying is needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.2] Eco and low-temperature programmes reduce water and energy use, and the energy label shows the rating for the model.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.3] Installation needs: a water supply and drain, a suitable power point, and a level floor must all be available where the machine is placed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.b] A kettle shows how the criteria apply to a small appliance, where capacity, cleaning and safety features matter most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.0] Capacity is stated in litres; buy a kettle that holds the amount the household needs, not more, because heating extra water wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.1] A concealed element in the base is easier to clean than an exposed element, where limescale collects.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.2] Automatic cut-off switches the kettle off when the water boils, and boil-dry protection switches it off if the water level is too low.
