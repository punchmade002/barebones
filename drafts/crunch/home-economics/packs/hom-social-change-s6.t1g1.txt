TARGET hom-social-change-s6: 6. Unemployment
[hom-social-change-s6.0.b] Being without a job is not the same as being unemployed. The word has a precise meaning, and the effects that follow depend on how long a person is out of work and why.
[hom-social-change-s6.0.0] Unemployment means being available for and willing to work, and actively seeking work, but having no paid job. Students, full-time carers and retired people are not counted as unemployed because they are not seeking work.
[hom-social-change-s6.0.1] Long-term unemployment means being out of work for 12 months or more. The longer it lasts, the more damaging it becomes, because savings run out and skills fade.
[hom-social-change-s6.0.2] People lose work for different reasons, for example when a business closes, an industry declines or work is only available at certain times of year.
[hom-social-change-s6.0.3] Unemployment black spots are areas where many people are out of work for a long time, often after a major employer closes, so whole communities feel the loss together.
[hom-social-change-s6.1.b] Unemployment is not all of one kind. Economists group it by what causes it, and the type matters because each one needs a different response.
[hom-social-change-s6.1.0] Frictional unemployment is short-term and occurs while people move between jobs, for example while they apply and attend interviews.
[hom-social-change-s6.1.1] Structural unemployment results from lasting changes in the economy, such as technology replacing workers or the decline of a whole industry, so the old skills are no longer wanted.
[hom-social-change-s6.1.2] Cyclical unemployment rises during recessions, when demand for goods and services falls, so firms produce less and recruit fewer workers; recovery usually reverses some of it.
[hom-social-change-s6.1.3] Seasonal unemployment occurs in industries that depend on the time of year, such as tourism or construction, so the same workers may be out of work every off-season.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.b] A job gives a person income, status, routine and company. Losing it can damage all four, and the harm usually grows the longer the person is out of work.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.0] Financially, loss of income means a lower standard of living and difficulty paying for food, clothing, heating, and rent or mortgage, which can lead to debt.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.1] Psychologically, losing a job can mean losing status, identity and daily routine, so a person may suffer low self-esteem and confidence, boredom and a sense of failure.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.2] Unemployment is linked with stress, anxiety and depression (mental health problems), especially when money worries and rejected job applications build up.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.3] Health and behaviour can suffer: physical health may be poorer, and some people turn to alcohol, drugs or gambling to cope, which adds to financial and health problems.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.4] Socially, a person loses workplace contacts and friends, and has less social life because going out costs money, so isolation and loss of motivation can follow.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.5] Skills become out of date, and a gap in the CV makes employers less likely to hire, so long-term unemployment feeds on itself and makes finding work harder.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.6] Young people lose the chance to gain work experience, which delays independence: they may stay living with their parents and find it hard to move out.
[hom-social-change-s6.2.7] Young people may be tempted to emigrate to find work, and some are at risk of antisocial behaviour when they have no job or routine.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.b] When one person loses their job, the whole household feels it. Money, relationships, roles and children are all affected, though how badly depends on how long the unemployment lasts and how much support the family has.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.0] Lower income means a reduced standard of living, cuts to savings, holidays, clothes and activities, and debt, with mortgage or rent arrears that can put the home at risk.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.1] Families may rely on credit or moneylenders, social welfare payments and help from MABS to manage, but borrowing can make the money problem worse.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.2] Financial strain causes arguments between partners and can lead to relationship breakdown, because stress and tension build up, and in some families to violence or addiction.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.3] Roles can reverse: a parent who loses the job may become the main carer at home, which can reduce their status and authority in the family.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.4] The other partner may have to work longer hours or take work they would not choose, to make up the lost income.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.5] Children may have a poorer diet, miss school trips, school costs, activities and leisure, and show stress and behaviour problems because of the tension at home.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.6] Older children may leave education early or choose a college by cost rather than by what they want to study.
[hom-social-change-s6.3.7] Children in long-term unemployed households may lack working adults as role models, which makes it harder for them to see work as normal.
[hom-social-change-s6.4.b] Unemployment can pull a family apart physically, as people leave to find work, though it can also give some families more time with one another.
[hom-social-change-s6.4.0] Family members may emigrate or move to find work. This splits families, leaves older relatives without support and reduces the population of the community they leave.
[hom-social-change-s6.4.1] More time together can be a positive effect, since a parent at home can spend more time with the children and do household tasks, but this is usually outweighed by stress.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.b] Unemployment is not only a private problem. When many people are out of work, the State, the local economy and the community all pay a price.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.0] The State spends more on social welfare while it collects less income tax and PRSI, because fewer people are earning, so there is less money for public services.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.1] Households with less income spend less, so local shops and businesses suffer and more jobs can be put at risk.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.2] Loss of skills and productive effort reduces output and economic growth, and emigration of skilled people (brain drain) takes talent away from the country.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.3] Concentrations of unemployment cause social exclusion and unemployment black spots, where people are cut off from the opportunities most others have.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.4] Poverty, crime, vandalism and antisocial behaviour can increase in areas of high unemployment.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.5] High unemployment puts strain on health, mental health and other services, and contributes to family breakdown and homelessness.
[hom-social-change-s6.5.6] Emigration can cause depopulation of areas, and a weaker community spirit follows when people, clubs and services leave.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.b] Statutory schemes are run by the State and paid for by taxes. They replace some lost income and help people get back to work.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.0] The Department of Social Protection runs the income supports and operates its public employment service as Intreo.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.1] Jobseeker's Benefit is a social insurance payment based on PRSI contributions, paid for a limited period. Jobseeker's Allowance is means-tested, meaning it depends on the person's income and resources.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.2] Supplementary Welfare Allowance meets urgent needs, and the Working Family Payment tops up the income of low-paid workers with children.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.3] A statutory redundancy payment is paid to eligible employees who are made redundant, under the Redundancy Payments Acts.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.4] The Back to Education Allowance gives income support to people on certain social welfare payments while they take approved second or third level courses.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.5] SOLAS (which took over from the earlier training body in 2013) and the Education and Training Boards provide further education and training, and Springboard+ offers courses to help people return to work.
[hom-social-change-s6.6.6] Community Employment and Tús are temporary work placements in communities that give work experience, and Youthreach helps young early school leavers.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.b] Not all help comes from the State. Free advice services and charities support unemployed families, often with the practical problems that the payments do not cover.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.0] Citizens Information is a free, impartial information service that tells people what payments and services they are entitled to.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.1] MABS, the Money Advice and Budgeting Service, gives free, confidential help with debt and household budgeting, so families can manage on a reduced income.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.2] The Society of St Vincent de Paul (SVP) is a voluntary charity with local conferences and charity shops. It helps with bills, food, school costs and housing, and visits families.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.3] The Irish National Organisation of the Unemployed (INOU) is a national network that gives advice, advocacy and information to unemployed people.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.4] Local community and family resource centres also offer support close to home.
[hom-social-change-s6.7.5] Voluntary work benefits the unemployed person by giving structure, skills, contacts and confidence, and it benefits the community through the help given.
[hom-social-change-s6.8.b] Exam questions often describe a family and ask you to apply what you know, so practise working through each member in turn.
[hom-social-change-s6.8.0] Start with the parent who lost the job: loss of income, status and routine, stress, and the risk of skills becoming out of date.
[hom-social-change-s6.8.1] Then the partner, who may work longer or take work they would not choose, and the couple, whose relationship is under financial strain.
[hom-social-change-s6.8.2] Then the children, who may miss trips and activities, have a poorer diet and feel stress at home.
[hom-social-change-s6.8.3] Finish with the supports the family could use: a Jobseeker's payment through Intreo, MABS for money problems, Citizens Information for entitlements, the SVP for practical help, and the Back to Education Allowance or a training course for a route back to work.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s1: 1. Defining unemployment
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.b] Being out of work is not the same as being unemployed. Unemployment is being without paid work while being willing and able to take up work, and statisticians apply a strict three-part test.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.0] Three conditions must all be met: the person has no paid employment, is available to start work, and is actively seeking work. Missing any one of them means the person is not counted as unemployed.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.1] Paid work is the test, so unpaid work such as volunteering or caring at home does not make a person employed.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.2] Available for work: the person could start work within a short period, normally within two weeks, so someone who could not take a job for months is not counted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.3] Actively seeking work: the person has taken specific steps to find work, such as applying for posts, answering advertisements, contacting employers or registering with Intreo, the Department of Social Protection's public employment service. Registering is one such step, not proof on its own.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.4] Students, people in full-time home duties, retired people and those unable to work through illness or disability are not counted as unemployed, because they are not available for work or are not seeking it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.5] A person in part-time work who wants more hours is counted as employed in the standard measure (such a person is underemployed).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.b] To judge how serious unemployment is, a count of unemployed people has to be set against the number of people who could be working. That is what the unemployment rate does.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.0] The labour force is the number of people in employment plus the number of unemployed people.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.1] The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labour force, multiplied by 100.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.2] A rate is more useful than a raw total when comparing periods or areas, because the size of the labour force changes over time.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.3] The Central Statistics Office (CSO) measures unemployment in Ireland using the definition set by the International Labour Organization (ILO). Its Labour Force Survey is a household survey that gives the standardised unemployment rate, the official measure, published monthly and quoted seasonally adjusted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.4] The Live Register is the Department of Social Protection's count of people claiming jobseeker payments (signing on), mainly Jobseeker's Benefit and Jobseeker's Allowance. It includes some part-time, casual and seasonal workers, so it is not the official unemployment figure.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.b] Unemployment is also described by how long it lasts, because a short gap between jobs is a very different problem from years without work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.0] Short-term unemployment is a brief spell out of work, usually between jobs or after leaving education.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.1] Frictional unemployment is short-term unemployment caused by the normal time workers take to move from one job to another, for example while applications are made and interviews arranged.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.2] Long-term unemployment is being out of work for one year or more.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.3] Long-term unemployment is harder to escape because skills, habits and confidence erode with time, which makes re-entry to work harder.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.4] Young people have tended to have higher rates of unemployment than older workers, especially in downturns.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.b] The extent of unemployment means how many people are unemployed, as a rate and as a number, and how that changes over time. It follows the economic cycle: it rises in recession and falls in growth.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.0] The Labour Force Survey, a household survey by the Central Statistics Office, produces the standardised unemployment rate. The Live Register, kept by the Department of Social Protection, counts people claiming jobseeker payments, including some part-time workers, so it is not the standard measure.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.1] Unemployment was low in the boom years of the 2000s, then rose sharply after the economic downturn that began in 2008, with construction and related professions worst affected.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.2] Retail and service industries also shed jobs during the downturn as consumer spending fell.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.3] Unemployment peaked at roughly 15% in the early 2010s and then fell steadily as the economy recovered and jobs returned in construction, retail, services and multinational firms.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.4] Emigration rose during the downturn. It held the measured rate down, because people who left are no longer in the labour force, so the rate alone understates the damage.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.5] Unemployment is not spread evenly. It is higher among young people and among people with few or no qualifications, and rates differ between regions.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.6] Unemployment black spots, areas that lost their main employer, tend to keep higher rates for longer than areas with plenty of work. Long-term unemployment forms a hard core that remains when the overall rate falls.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.7] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused a sudden temporary rise in joblessness, and the State paid a temporary pandemic unemployment payment.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s3: 3. Effects of unemployment
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.b] Work gives a person income, purpose and a place in society, so losing it hits a person's money, status and wellbeing at the same time. These are individual effects, felt by the person who lost the job.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.0] Financial insecurity: lost income forces people to cut spending and lower their living standard, and to rely on savings, credit or welfare payments, which are lower than a wage.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.1] Debt: unpaid bills and loans build up, which can lead to mortgage or rent arrears (payments missed) and, in serious cases, repossession of the home or homelessness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.2] Loss of identity and status: work gives identity, purpose and social standing, so unemployed people may feel undervalued, worthless, a failure or guilty, and lose self-esteem.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.3] Stress, anxiety and depression: long-term unemployment can create feelings of hopelessness, and stress and depression can in turn affect a person's health.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.4] Social isolation: losing work removes daily contact with colleagues, and having less money for leisure reduces participation in social activities.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.5] Loss of daily routine can lead to boredom.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.6] Addiction: some people turn to alcohol, drugs or gambling to cope, which adds to their difficulties.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.0.7] Loss of skills over time and falling confidence make it harder to get a job, so a person can slip into long-term unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.b] Young people who cannot find work are at the start of adult life, so unemployment delays the steps that normally follow a first job.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.0] Delayed independence: without a wage, a young adult may have to remain at the parental home instead of moving out.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.1] Delay in starting a family or buying a home, because there is no steady income to save or borrow against.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.1.2] Risk of emigrating in search of work, which takes the young person away from family and friends.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.b] When an adult loses a job, the whole household feels it. These are family effects, felt by the people who live together and share the household income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.0] Reduced income lowers the family's standard of living, so it may struggle to afford food, clothing, heating and other household bills, and it relies on welfare payments.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.1] Debt and housing risk: arrears on rent, mortgage or utilities build up, and families that fall behind on rent or mortgage repayments face a risk of losing the home and of homelessness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.2] Financial pressure, changed roles within the household and the stress of one or both adults being at home cause arguments and strain relationships.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.3] Relationship strain can lead, in some cases, to separation or family breakdown.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.4] Children may have to give up activities such as sports, music lessons or school trips, and may feel different from classmates or be teased.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.5] Family members may emigrate for work, which breaks up the family's support network.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.2.6] Reliance on welfare payments can shape family finances for long periods.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.b] Children grow up in the household, so the effects of unemployment can follow them into school and into their own working lives.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.0] Children's educational opportunities suffer when a family cannot pay for books, technology or a quiet place to study.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.1] Children of unemployed parents have fewer working role models (people whose working life they can copy), which can lower their expectations of work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.2] Intergenerational unemployment: children raised in a household with no one in work may come to expect unemployment, so the disadvantage is repeated when they reach working age.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.3.3] Financial pressure at home can lead to early school leaving, which reduces the qualifications children take into the labour market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.b] Unemployment is also a cost to the whole community and the State, because jobless people stop paying into the system and start drawing on it. These are society effects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.0] Cost to the State: higher spending is needed on social welfare payments and related supports such as medical cards, fuel and housing supports, and taxes may have to rise to pay for it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.1] Loss of income tax and social insurance (PRSI) income means less money for public services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.2] Loss of production and skills: the economy's output falls and unused skills are wasted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.3] Crime, antisocial behaviour and vandalism can follow in areas with high long-term unemployment. This is an association with those areas, not a rule about unemployed people.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.4] Emigration of working-age and skilled workers reduces the population, the skills base and future tax income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.5] The black economy grows, as unpaid or undeclared work avoids tax and PRSI, so the State loses that income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.6] Social exclusion and inequality: people cut off from work, income and social networks become harder to reintegrate, and the gap widens between those in work and those without.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.4.7] Pressure on health and community services: more people use mental health, addiction and counselling services, and local authorities, voluntary bodies and community groups take on more work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.b] A black spot is an area of very high unemployment. When many people in one place lose their jobs, the effects feed on each other and the whole area declines.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.0] Falling local spending closes shops and services, which causes further job losses in the area.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.1] Population loss reduces the number of users of local schools and transport, making services harder to keep open, because people move away in search of work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.2] Black spots acquire a poor reputation, which deters investment and new businesses.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.5.3] People still in work feel it too: they face job insecurity and fear of redundancy, and where an area empties there is less local demand for their products and services.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.6.b] The levels are connected, and an answer is stronger when it shows how one effect leads to the next.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.6.0] Effects chain from one level to the next: lost income (individual) leads to arrears and strain (family) and, multiplied across a town, to closed shops and falling tax income (society).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.6.1] The same effect can sit at more than one level, but give it once, at the level the question asks about. For example, depression is an individual effect, while the cost of treating it falls on society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.7.b] Marks go to answers that cover every level the question names, with each effect named and explained.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.7.0] Cover each level asked for: at least one effect for the individual, the family and society, then further effects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s3.7.1] Give each point a name and a short explanation of why it follows, for example lost income, so debt.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s6: 6. Statutory responses
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.b] The State pays income support to unemployed people through two routes, and the route decides what a claimant has to prove. All social welfare payments come from the Department of Social Protection.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.0] Social insurance payments (also called benefits) depend on a record of PRSI contributions paid while in work, and they are not means-tested. They are funded from those PRSI contributions.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.1] Social assistance payments are means-tested, which means the household's income and savings are assessed, and no contribution record is required, because they are paid on the basis of need. They are funded from general taxation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.2] Jobseeker's Benefit is the main social insurance payment for an unemployed person who has enough paid PRSI contributions, so the claimant's own contributions earn the right to it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.3] Jobseeker's Allowance is the main social assistance payment for an unemployed person who does not qualify for Jobseeker's Benefit or whose benefit has ended, so it acts as a safety net.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.4] A Jobseeker's Allowance claimant must be unemployed, capable of work, available for full-time work and genuinely seeking employment. This shows the payment supports people who want work, not people who have chosen not to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.5] Supplementary Welfare Allowance is a means-tested payment for people with no or very low income whose basic needs are not met. It also pays exceptional needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.b] Families with children face extra costs, so several payments are aimed at them. Some reach every family, and others are limited to low-income households.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.0] Child Benefit is paid for each qualifying child regardless of the parents' income. It is universal, not means-tested, so it reaches families on every income level.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.1] Payments for lone parents are means-tested, because they are aimed at those in need, and recognise that one adult is carrying the full cost of the household.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.2] The Working Family Payment (formerly Family Income Supplement) is a tax-free, means-tested weekly payment for low-paid workers who have a dependent child. It is an in-work payment, so it makes work pay and eases the poverty trap.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.3] Means-tested payments are reduced as income rises, so the household gains less from extra earnings than the wage itself.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.4] The Fuel Allowance is paid weekly to qualifying low-income households during the winter season to help with heating costs, which rise in cold weather.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.b] Besides cash payments, the State lowers the cost of essentials such as health, school, housing and childcare for families on low incomes. Each scheme either pays for something or removes a cost.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.0] Medical card: gives free GP visits and access to many health services, run by the HSE. Eligibility depends on income and, in some cases, health needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.1] Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance: a once-yearly payment, because the cost falls at the start of the school year, that helps qualifying families meet the cost of school uniforms and shoes.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.2] Household Benefits Package: help with electricity or gas and a free television licence for qualifying older people and people with certain disabilities or long-term illnesses.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.3] Housing supports: local authority (social) housing is let at rents related to income. The Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) is paid through the local authority to help households pay rent in the private rented sector, and the tenant's rent contribution is based on income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.4] Education supports: the school meals programme and free school book schemes ease the cost of school, and the free pre-school place (ECCE), an early-learning place before primary school, reduces childcare costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.5] Money Advice and Budgeting Service (MABS): a free, confidential, State-funded service, working under the Citizens Information Board, that helps people in debt, mainly low-income households, to budget and deal with creditors.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.b] These initiatives work through training, business supports and job creation rather than income support alone. A statutory body is one set up by law or run by the State.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.0] SOLAS is the State further education and training authority. It funds, plans and coordinates further education and training through the Education and Training Boards (ETBs).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.1] SOLAS supports apprenticeships, vocational courses and literacy, numeracy and digital training, and it provides skills training and upskilling, matching the skills of unemployed people to vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.2] Enterprise Ireland is the State agency that helps Irish-owned companies increase sales, exports and employment through grants, market advice and training. More sales and exports create jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.3] Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs) in each county are the first stop shop for people starting or growing small businesses, giving training, mentoring, advice and grants and encouraging job creation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.4] Intreo is the Department of Social Protection's public employment service. It gives job-seekers advice, job-matching and referral to training, so unemployed people can be linked to vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.5] Work-based learning and CV and job-search preparation give unemployed people the experience that employers ask for, which makes them more employable.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.6] Community Employment (CE) offers temporary part-time work placements on local community projects for long-term unemployed and other eligible people. It is a Department of Social Protection scheme, not a SOLAS one.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.7] Other schemes that support a return to education and training are the Vocational Training Opportunities Scheme (VTOS), the Back to Education Allowance and Youthreach, an ETB programme for early school leavers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by an overseas company that sets up or expands operations in Ireland. The State encourages it because it creates jobs, but it also brings risks.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.0] IDA Ireland is the State agency responsible for attracting overseas companies and for helping foreign companies already here to expand, through grants, site selection and advice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.1] A low corporation tax rate is a key incentive for multinational companies to locate in Ireland: 12.5%, and 15% for the largest multinational groups.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.2] Other attractions are a young, skilled, flexible, educated and English-speaking workforce, grant assistance, good telecommunications and access to the European Union's single market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.3] Foreign investment, for example a new plant, creates direct jobs in the foreign firms and indirect jobs in suppliers and services, so it reduces unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.4] It also brings tax revenue and new skills and technology into local labour markets, so workers can move on to other employers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.5] Dependence on a few large multinationals is a weakness: if a parent company closes or moves a plant, a local area can lose many jobs at once.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.6] Multinational employment tends to be concentrated in larger towns and cities, so rural areas benefit less from it. IDA Ireland aims to spread investment to regions outside the main cities.
