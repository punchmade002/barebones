TARGET hom-meal-planning-s3: 3. Diet-related problems
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.b] Obesity develops when the energy taken in as food regularly exceeds the energy the body uses, and the surplus is stored as fat (adipose tissue).
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.0] Body mass index (BMI) is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. A BMI of 30 or more in adults is classed as obese, and 25 to under 30 as overweight.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.1] Fatty and sugary foods are energy-dense, which means a small portion supplies a large amount of energy, so it is easy to take in more than the body needs.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.2] Sugary drinks, cream sauces, fried foods, large portions, snacking, late-night eating, too much processed food and a low-fibre diet all increase energy intake.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.3] Not all causes are dietary. Inactivity, heredity, stress and emotional eating also contribute, and a sedentary lifestyle means less energy is used up.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.4] Obesity increases the risk of coronary heart disease, Type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and gallstones, as well as joint strain and arthritis, breathing problems and low self-esteem.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.5] Weight-loss guidance is a balanced low-energy diet: lower fat (especially saturated fat), reduced sugar and salt, extra fibre, fruit, vegetables and water, lean protein and less processed food.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.6] Low-fat cooking methods, removing visible fat from meat, avoiding cream sauces, smaller portions, avoiding alcohol and late-night eating, and regular exercise all help, because they cut energy in and raise energy used.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.b] Coronary heart disease (CHD) happens when the coronary arteries, which supply the heart muscle with blood, become narrowed or blocked by fatty deposits (atherosclerosis), which can cause angina or a heart attack.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.0] Saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol, which is deposited in artery walls and narrows them, so less blood reaches the heart muscle.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.1] Trans fats, found in some hard margarines and in fried and processed foods, raise LDL and lower HDL cholesterol, so they are doubly harmful.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.2] Refined carbohydrates and sugar contribute to raised blood triglycerides (blood fats), reduced HDL cholesterol and weight gain.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.3] Excess salt raises blood pressure, which damages artery walls and makes the heart work harder.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.4] A diet low in fibre, fruit and vegetables offers little protection against CHD, because these foods help lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.5] Other causes are smoking, lack of exercise, obesity, excess alcohol, stress, high blood pressure, heredity, age and gender.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.6] Cholesterol is a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell. The liver makes most of it, with some coming from animal foods. It forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed to make some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.7] LDL cholesterol (associated with saturated fat) deposits cholesterol in artery walls, hardening and narrowing them. HDL cholesterol (associated with unsaturated fat) carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.b] Diabetes means the level of glucose in the blood is raised. It happens because of a problem with insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas that lets glucose move from the blood into body cells.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.0] Type 1 diabetes (insulin-dependent): the pancreas produces little or no insulin. It often starts in childhood, adolescence or young adulthood, may be hereditary, is controlled with insulin, diet and exercise, and cannot be reversed.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.1] Type 2 diabetes (non-insulin-dependent): insulin is produced but cells respond poorly to it, or too little is made. It usually develops later in life, although some young people now develop it, and it accounts for about 90 per cent of cases.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.2] Excess weight and inactivity are the main risk factors for Type 2 diabetes. Diets high in sugar and refined carbohydrate, combined with excess weight, raise the risk. Sugar alone is not the cause.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.3] Type 2 can often be controlled with diet, weight loss, exercise and tablets, so losing weight can improve it.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.4] Gestational diabetes develops during pregnancy and usually resolves after the birth.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.5] Symptoms include frequent urination, thirst, hunger, weight loss and tiredness. Long-term complications can affect the eyes, kidneys, nerves and heart.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.b] A diet low in fibre and fluid makes stools hard and dry, and the effort of passing them puts the large intestine under strain. Several bowel problems follow from this in a chain.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.0] Constipation: hard, dry stools are passed with difficulty, usually caused by too little fibre and fluid and too little activity.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.1] Diverticular disease: small pouches called diverticula form in the wall of the large intestine when the pressure from straining pushes the lining outwards.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.2] Diverticulitis: inflamed or infected diverticula cause pain and need medical treatment.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.3] Haemorrhoids: swollen veins around the anus, caused by straining during constipation.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.4] A low-fibre diet also raises the risk of bowel disorders, including bowel cancer.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.5] Prevention: eat wholegrain cereals, brown bread, wholegrain rice, pulses, nuts and seeds, and fruit and vegetables with their skins on, eaten raw where possible, because skins and raw plant foods keep most of their fibre.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.6] Whole fruit and vegetables supply more fibre than juice, and fresh or dried fruit is a better snack than processed alternatives. Plenty of fluid, exercise and regular meals also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.7] A high-fibre diet reduces the risk of bowel disease.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.b] Bones are living tissue that is built up in childhood and lost slowly in later life. Osteoporosis is a thinning and weakening of the bone, which becomes porous, brittle and prone to fracture.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.0] Bone mass peaks in early adulthood, after which bone is gradually lost, so building strong bones in childhood and adolescence gives a bigger store to draw on later.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.1] Too little calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C or phosphorus over life raises the risk. Vitamin D helps the gut absorb calcium, and vitamin C is needed for the collagen framework of bone.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.2] Phytates, oxalates, excess fibre and tannins in some foods can reduce calcium absorption, because they bind calcium in the gut.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.3] Excess caffeine and alcohol increase calcium loss, and excess saturated fat can also reduce calcium availability.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.4] Risk factors include older age, the fall in oestrogen after menopause (which puts women at higher risk), smoking and inactivity.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.5] Weight-bearing exercise helps to maintain bone strength.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.6] Prevention: increase calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus in the diet, build bone in childhood and adolescence, take weight-bearing exercise, and limit the inhibitors of calcium absorption listed above.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.b] Teeth are damaged by acid made from sugar in the mouth. Dental caries (tooth decay) and gum disease are separate problems, although both start with plaque.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.0] Bacteria in plaque ferment sugars in the mouth to produce acid, which dissolves enamel and causes dental caries.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.1] Frequent snacking, sticky sweets and sugary drinks keep the mouth acidic for longer, which increases the risk of decay.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.2] Fluoride strengthens enamel and makes it more resistant to acid; fluoride toothpaste is used twice a day.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.3] Calcium, phosphorus and vitamin D support the formation of enamel and dentine, the teeth's hard tissues.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.4] Periodontal (gum) disease is inflammation of the gums and the tissues around the teeth, caused by a build-up of plaque.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.5] Protection: limit sugary food to mealtimes, brush, have dental check-ups, and eat cheese and crunchy fruit and vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.b] Diet problems rarely come singly. A surplus of one nutrient or too little of another can lead to several conditions, and some conditions lead on to others.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.0] Excess energy leads to obesity, which increases the risk of Type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease and high blood pressure.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.1] Excess salt raises blood pressure, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.2] A diet high in sugar contributes to both obesity and dental caries.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.3] A low-fibre, low-fluid diet can cause constipation, which in turn can lead to diverticular disease.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.4] Too little calcium and vitamin D leads to osteoporosis, and too little iron leads to anaemia.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.5] A poor diet in a teenager can cause several problems at once: overweight, CHD risk, tooth decay, constipation, anaemia, diabetes risk and osteoporosis later.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s2: 2. Energy balance
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.b] Your body takes energy in from food and drink and uses it up all day long. Comparing the two tells you whether your weight will stay steady, rise or fall.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.0] Energy input is the energy in the food and drink consumed. Energy output (also called energy expenditure) is the energy the body uses for BMR (the energy needed just to keep the body alive at rest), physical activity, growth and digestion.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.1] Energy balance is achieved when energy input equals energy output. Nothing is left over to store and nothing extra is needed from the body's stores, so body weight stays steady.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.2] Positive energy balance means input exceeds output. The surplus is converted to fat and stored as adipose (fat) tissue, so weight is gained. Note that 'positive' here does not mean good.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.3] Negative energy balance means output exceeds input. The body makes up the shortfall by using its stored glycogen first and then fat, so weight is lost.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.4] Too little energy for a long time (underweight or prolonged negative balance) leads to weight loss, tiredness and poor resistance to infection.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.5] Maintaining energy balance keeps a person at a healthy weight, helps prevent overweight and obesity and the diseases that go with them, and supplies energy for work, study, growth and activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.b] If a person is out of balance, there are only two levers: eat and drink less energy, or use more. Most advice combines both.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.0] To reduce energy intake, cut back on sugary drinks, sweets, fried and fatty foods, and check portion sizes. Grilling or baking instead of frying, and avoiding snacking on high-energy foods, also lower intake.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.1] Eating more fruit, vegetables and fibre helps because fibre fills you up, so you feel full on less energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.2] To increase energy output, add daily walking, cycling, stairs instead of lifts, active leisure or a regular sport or gym, because each extra activity uses energy that would otherwise be stored.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.3] To reduce time spent sedentary (sitting still), limit television, computer and gaming time and break up long periods of sitting.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.4] A gradual change, sustained over time, is more effective than crash dieting, because it is a habit a person can keep up.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.5] If weight problems continue, a person can return to balance with these changes plus support from a doctor, a dietitian or a weight management programme.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.b] Case studies give a person's lifestyle and daily menu. The answer should analyse the energy balance first and then recommend changes, so work through it in order.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.0] 1. Identify the person's energy output: occupation, activity level, time spent sitting, age and any growth or pregnancy needs, because these decide how much energy they use.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.1] 2. Estimate the energy input from the menu: note the high-energy items, such as fried foods, cakes, sugary drinks and large portions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.2] 3. State whether the person is in positive or negative energy balance, and what this may do to body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.3] 4. Recommend a change to intake: replace the high-energy items with fruit, vegetables, wholegrain foods or lean protein.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.4] 5. Recommend a change to output: name a specific activity, how often and for how long.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.5] 6. Write a one-day menu that meets the person's energy needs and matches the healthy eating guidelines: breakfast, lunch and dinner, each with two or three items.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.6] Menus may be asked for a person who is obese, a teenager who plays sport, a person with a high energy requirement or a family with young children. A weight-reducing menu uses low-fat milk, grilled or poached foods, wholegrain cereals, vegetables and salad, fruit and water.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.7] Diagram: none is needed. A simple balance diagram, with input on one side, output on the other and a tilt for positive or negative balance, is optional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.b] Not all energy comes with goodness attached. Some foods give plenty of energy but almost nothing else the body needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.0] Empty kilocalories (empty calories) are calories from foods that supply energy but little or no nutritional value, such as sweets, sugary soft drinks, crisps, alcohol, cakes and biscuits, and fried snack foods.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.1] They add to energy intake without supplying protein, vitamins, minerals or fibre, so they can cause positive energy balance and weight gain while the diet is still poor in nutrients. Over-indulgence in sugary food can lead to obesity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.2] Replacing empty kilocalories with fruit, vegetables, wholegrain foods, lean protein or low-fat dairy gives the same or less energy with more nutrients.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.b] Obesity is a condition of excess body fat that harms health, usually caused by a long-term positive energy balance. Overweight is the milder stage. The causes below are mostly about lifestyle.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.0] Energy imbalance: intake consistently exceeds output, so the surplus is stored as fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.1] Sedentary lifestyle: a job or leisure time spent sitting lowers energy output, so more of the food eaten is left over to be stored.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.2] Lack of exercise: regular physical activity is not part of the daily routine, so output stays low.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.3] Psychological factors: depression, boredom and stress can lead to comfort eating, overeating and snacking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.4] Hormonal imbalance: an underactive thyroid gland slows metabolism and lowers BMR, so the same food intake produces weight gain. This applies to some people only, not as the usual cause.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.5] Unhealthy eating patterns: frequent snacking, skipped meals followed by overeating, and frequent takeaways.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.6] Poor food choices: a high intake of processed foods, sugary drinks and fatty foods. Large portion sizes of energy-dense foods also increase intake without adding to fullness.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.7] In children, causes include sweets and fast food, sugary drinks, too much television and computer time, less outdoor play, advertising aimed at children, and parents using food as a reward. Genetic and family factors may also influence weight, but lifestyle causes are stressed.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.b] Carrying excess fat for years strains almost every system in the body. Exams want each risk with a reason how it happens.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.0] Type 2 diabetes: excess fat reduces the body's response to insulin, so blood glucose rises. It can be caused by a sedentary lifestyle and unhealthy diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.1] Coronary heart disease and stroke: obesity raises blood pressure and blood cholesterol levels.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.2] High blood pressure and high cholesterol, both linked to excess weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.3] Gallstones: excess body fat increases the risk of gallstone formation.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.4] Arthritis, joint pain and back pain: extra weight puts stress on the weight-bearing joints and the spine.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.5] Breathing difficulties and sleep apnoea, and some cancers, are also linked to obesity. Varicose veins arise because excess weight increases pressure on the veins in the legs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.6] Depression and poor self-esteem, which can both result from obesity and make it harder to change habits. Difficulties in pregnancy and childbirth, and infertility, are further risks.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.7] Reduced life expectancy, as the combined risks shorten life. Corrective measures are to return to energy balance and get support from a doctor, dietitian or weight management programme.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.b] BMI is a simple measure that relates body weight to height and is used to classify body weight in adults. It is a screening measure, not a diagnosis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.0] BMI = weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m²).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.1] Below 18.5: underweight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.2] 18.5 to 24.9: healthy weight range.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.3] 25 to 29.9: overweight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.4] 30 and above: obese.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.5] BMI does not distinguish between muscle and fat, so a muscular athlete may score as overweight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.6] Waist measurement is used alongside BMI, because fat stored around the abdomen carries greater health risk.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.7] Children use age and sex charts, not the adult categories above.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.b] Exams give you weight and height, or a table of results, and ask for a category. Follow the steps in order, and keep height in metres.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.0] Step 1: convert height to metres (for example, 175 cm = 1.75 m).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.1] Step 2: square the height (1.75 x 1.75 = 3.06).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.2] Step 3: divide weight by the squared height (sample data: 70 kg / 3.06 = 22.9, in the healthy range). Dividing, not multiplying, is what gives the right answer.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.3] Read a BMI table by finding the row for the height and the column for the weight, and matching the result to the categories above.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.4] When comparing groups in data, note which group has the higher share in the overweight or obese categories and link this to diet and activity.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s5: 5. Functions and energy value
[hom-lipids-s5.0.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do far more in the body than provide energy. Each function below follows from where fat sits in the body or what it does as it passes through the digestive system.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.0] Lipids are a concentrated source of energy, supplying the body with more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein, so a small amount of fat gives a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.1] Excess energy from any food is stored as adipose tissue (the body's stored fat), which is a reserve of energy for when food intake is low.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.2] Adipose tissue lies under the skin and insulates the body against heat loss, which helps to keep body temperature constant.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.3] Adipose tissue around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against knocks and pressure, and holds them in place.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.4] Lipids slow the emptying of the stomach, which helps to delay hunger, so meals containing fat are more satisfying.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.b] Beyond energy and body protection, lipids carry nutrients, build cell structures and affect how food tastes.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.0] Lipids carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K and help the body absorb them, because these vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water. Dairy fat, oily fish and egg yolk supply them.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.1] Lipids are a source of essential fatty acids, the fatty acids the body cannot make for itself and must get from food.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.2] Phospholipids (fat-like lipids containing phosphorus) and cholesterol form part of the structure of every cell membrane, the thin layer around each cell.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.3] Fat also adds flavour and a smooth texture to food, although this is a cooking benefit and not a biological function.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.4] Digestion link: the enzyme lipase digests triglycerides (the main fats in food) to fatty acids and glycerol, and any excess is stored as adipose tissue.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.b] Cholesterol is a lipid that is often blamed for heart disease, yet the body needs it. It is different from the saturated fat in food, and students often confuse the two.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.0] Cholesterol is a fat-like substance called a sterol, found only in animal foods such as egg yolk, offal (liver and kidney), meat, butter, cream, cheese, prawns and other shellfish. Plant foods contain none.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.1] The liver makes cholesterol itself, in fact most of what the body has, so the amount eaten has less effect on blood cholesterol than the saturated fat in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.2] Saturated fat in the diet raises blood LDL cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol does. A plant oil such as coconut oil is cholesterol-free but can still be high in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.3] The body uses cholesterol to make cell membranes, bile salts that emulsify fat (break it into tiny droplets) for digestion, and steroid hormones such as oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone and cortisol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.4] Vitamin D is made in the skin from a derivative of cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.5] Too much cholesterol in the blood, especially LDL, builds up on artery walls and narrows them, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke. It is also a main constituent of most gallstones.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.6] To lower blood cholesterol, eat less saturated and trans fat and more unsaturated fat, oily fish, oats, pulses, fruit and vegetables (their fibre helps). Keep to a healthy weight, be active and do not smoke.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.7] Functional spreads containing plant stanols or sterols also help to lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.b] Foods high in saturated fat are energy-dense and raise blood LDL cholesterol. A diet high in them over years raises the risk of several diseases, and each has its own mechanism.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.0] Obesity: saturated-fat foods are energy-dense, and surplus energy from them is stored as adipose tissue, which can lead to obesity.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.1] Coronary heart disease and high blood pressure: raised LDL cholesterol narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis), increasing the risk of coronary heart disease, high blood pressure (hypertension) and stroke.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.2] Type 2 diabetes: excess body fat reduces the effectiveness of insulin (the hormone that controls blood sugar), which is called insulin resistance, so the risk of Type 2 diabetes increases.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.3] Gallstones: cholesterol is a main component of gallstones, which can form in the gall bladder.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.4] Varicose veins and infertility are also linked to a high-fat diet; the first four effects carry the most weight.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.5] Lifestyle changes recommended for someone at risk of CHD: a low-fat, low-cholesterol, low-salt diet; losing weight; not smoking; limiting alcohol; exercise; and managing stress.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.6] Irish healthy eating guidelines from the Department of Health advise replacing saturated fats with unsaturated (mono- and polyunsaturated) fats and using fats and oils in small amounts, which is the fats, spreads and oils shelf of the Food Pyramid.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.7] Other advice: choose lean meat and low-fat dairy, and grill, steam or bake instead of frying. Saturated fat should supply less than 10% of daily energy (HSE and Department of Health guidelines).
[hom-lipids-s5.4.b] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal). Fat has the highest energy value per gram of the three nutrients, which is why fatty foods are so energy-dense.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.0] Fat provides 37 kJ or 9 kcal of energy per gram, which is more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein. The true/false statement that 1 gram of fat provides 20 kilocalories is false.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.1] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ or 3.75 kcal per gram; protein provides 17 kJ or 4 kcal per gram; alcohol provides 29 kJ or 7 kcal per gram.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.2] Energy from fat is calculated by multiplying the grams of fat by 37 kJ, or by 9 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.3] Worked example (sample data): 20 g of fat gives 20 × 37 = 740 kJ, or 20 × 9 = 180 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.4] A large part of the energy in the average Irish diet comes from fat, much of it hidden in processed and convenience foods, meat products, dairy, cakes, pastries, biscuits, chocolate, fried foods and takeaways, and much of that is saturated.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.5] Guidance is to reduce total fat, especially saturated and trans fat, replacing them with unsaturated fat and complex carbohydrate. Over-consumption of fat leads to obesity because fat is energy-dense and stores readily.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s5: 5. Functions
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.b] Carbohydrate is the body's main and quickest source of energy. Sugars and starch both end up as glucose in the body, and what the body does with that glucose explains each of the functions below.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.0] Heat and energy: glucose is oxidised in the cells (respiration, the process by which cells release energy from glucose using oxygen). This releases energy for movement and growth and heat to keep body temperature at about 37 °C.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.1] The brain and nervous system rely on glucose as their main fuel, and red blood cells depend on it too, so the body needs a steady supply.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.2] Protein sparing: when enough carbohydrate is eaten to supply energy, protein is kept for growth and repair rather than being used as fuel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.3] Glycogen store: glucose not needed at once is converted to glycogen and stored in the liver and muscles as a short-term energy reserve, which can be turned back into glucose when blood glucose falls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.4] Fat store: when the glycogen stores are full, surplus glucose is converted to fat and stored as adipose tissue (body fat) under the skin and around organs, where it also insulates the body and protects organs. Excess energy of any kind is stored this way.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.5] Starch is digested to glucose in the body, so it provides the same energy as sugars, but more slowly.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.6] Sugars give a quick supply of energy. Starch gives a slower, steadier release, along with other nutrients, in foods such as cereals and potatoes.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.b] Dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharide, mainly cellulose) cannot be digested by humans, so it passes through the digestive system. Its value lies in what it does on the way through the bowel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.0] Fibre absorbs water and adds bulk and softness to the contents of the intestine and to the stool, so waste is easier to pass.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.1] The bulk stimulates peristalsis, the wave-like muscular contraction that moves food and waste through the intestine. Waste leaves faster, so there is less time for toxins to form in the bowel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.2] Regular movement of the bowel prevents constipation and reduces the straining that causes haemorrhoids (piles, swollen veins at the anus).
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.3] Low fibre intake is a risk factor for constipation, haemorrhoids, diverticular disease (small pouches forming in the bowel wall, which can become inflamed, called diverticulitis) and colon (bowel) cancer.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.4] Fibre absorbs water and gives a feeling of fullness, which helps control appetite and body weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.5] Soluble fibre, such as pectin and the soluble fibre in oats, beans and lentils, slows the release of glucose into the blood, which helps keep blood sugar steady.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.6] Soluble fibre also helps to lower LDL cholesterol (the type of blood cholesterol that raises the risk of heart disease), so it reduces the risk of heart disease.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.7] There are two kinds. Insoluble fibre (cellulose, wheat bran) speeds the movement of waste. Soluble fibre (pectin) slows sugar absorption and lowers LDL cholesterol. Fibre provides little or no usable energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.b] A diet low in fibre and fluid slows the movement of waste through the bowel. Examiners ask for one bowel disease with an account and its symptoms, and constipation is the model answer.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.0] Constipation: stools pass too slowly through the large intestine, so they become hard and difficult to pass, causing discomfort and straining.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.1] Cause: a diet low in fibre and too little water, because without bulk and moisture the stool stays hard and peristalsis is weak.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.2] Effects: hard stools, straining, bloating and discomfort, and a risk of haemorrhoids (piles) from the straining.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.3] Prevention: eat more fibre, drink plenty of fluids and take regular exercise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.4] Haemorrhoids (piles) and diverticular disease are the other bowel conditions to name if asked, and both are linked to low fibre intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.5] Bowel-protecting guidelines: choose wholegrain cereals, leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, snack on fresh or dried fruit, nuts and seeds, eat whole fruit rather than juice, and drink water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.b] Sugar does far more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each use below is something sugar does for the food, and exams ask for three or more.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.0] Sweetening and flavouring cakes, desserts, drinks, sauces and sweets, which improves palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat).
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.1] Preserving jam, chutney, syrup and crystallised fruit: sugar in high concentration draws water out of micro-organisms, so spoilage is slowed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.2] Feeding yeast: fermentation of sugar by yeast produces carbon dioxide, which makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.3] Browning and caramel flavour: caramelisation and the Maillard reaction brown the surface of baked goods, glazes and crème caramel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.4] Giving the texture of fudge, fondant and boiled sweets through controlled crystallisation, since sugar crystals form their structure.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.5] Giving volume: creamed sugar and fat trap air in sponge cakes, and sugar helps egg white hold a stable foam in meringues.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.6] Coating: syrups and icing (a solution of sugar) are used for coating, and sugar syrup stops cut fruit in a fruit salad discolouring.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.7] Sugar holds moisture (it is hygroscopic), so cakes stay moist and keep for longer.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.b] Starch and pectin are carbohydrates that change how food holds together. Starch mainly thickens, while pectin sets fruit into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.0] Starch is a thickening agent: it gelatinises (swells and thickens) in a hot liquid, thickening sauces, soups, gravies, custards and pie fillings.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.1] In a roux the fat binds with the flour and separates the starch granules, so the sauce is lump-free.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.2] Starch binds and coats: it forms the thickening in batters and coatings and gives body to a sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.3] Starch helps structure in baking: starch in flour gelatinises as a cake, pastry or bread bakes and helps to set the crumb. The elastic structure of bread comes from gluten, a protein in wheat flour, not from starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.4] Starch browns on dry heat (dextrinisation), giving crust colour and flavour to toast and baked goods.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.5] Pectin is the gelling agent in jam, jelly and marmalade: with sugar and acid it sets the fruit mixture into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.6] Pectin is also used in food manufacture as a gelling agent and stabiliser (additive E440) in confectionery, desserts and yoghurts, and it supports shape in cooked fruit and thickens fruit fillings.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s4: 4. Mineral elements
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.b] Minerals are the part of food that is not made by living things at all. The body needs them in small amounts to build structures, run processes and make enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.0] Minerals are inorganic elements (not made by living things) that the body cannot make, so they must be obtained from food. They build body structures, regulate body processes and form parts of enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.1] Major (macro) minerals are needed in larger amounts, above 100 mg a day. They are calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium and sulphur.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.2] Minerals are not destroyed by cooking, because they are elements. They can, however, be lost into cooking water and in the trimming of food. For example, boiling vegetables in a lot of water washes minerals out.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily amount) is the daily intake that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, so some groups need more than others.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.4] Phosphorus works with calcium to harden bones and teeth and forms part of ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and cell membranes. Sources are milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs, wholegrains and nuts, and deficiency is rare.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.b] Calcium is the most important mineral for the skeleton, but it also keeps blood, muscles and nerves working. Because bone is built in childhood and lost in later life, some groups need more of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.0] Milk, cheese and yoghurt are the richest dietary sources of calcium. This is why dairy foods matter for bone health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.1] Canned sardines and canned salmon eaten with their bones, green leafy vegetables such as kale, broccoli and cabbage, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks and fortified breakfast cereals are further sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.2] Calcium works with phosphorus to form the mineral salts that give bones and teeth their hardness. This is why a diet low in calcium leaves bones weak.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.3] Calcium is needed for blood clotting, normal muscle contraction (including the heartbeat) and nerve impulse transmission. It also activates some enzymes and helps regulate blood pressure.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.4] Rickets in children (soft, bent bones and poor teeth) and osteomalacia in adults result from too little calcium, phosphorus or vitamin D.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.5] Osteoporosis, which develops over years, makes bones thin, porous, brittle and prone to fracture, with loss of height and a stooped back. Other effects of low calcium are tooth decay, slow clotting, and muscle cramps (tetany) and poor nerve function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.6] Osteoporosis risk is raised by low calcium and vitamin D intake, lack of weight-bearing exercise, smoking, alcohol, the fall in oestrogen after menopause and family history.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.7] Need is highest when bone is being built (childhood, teenage years, pregnancy, breastfeeding) and when it is lost (women after menopause). Bone density peaks in the late teens and twenties, so teenage intake matters.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.b] Iron is needed to carry oxygen, so a shortage leaves the whole body short of energy. It comes in two forms, and the form decides how easily the body absorbs it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.0] Haem iron comes from animal foods and is absorbed easily. Sources are red meat, liver and offal, kidney, black pudding, poultry and oily fish.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.1] Non-haem iron comes from plant foods and eggs and is absorbed less easily. Sources are eggs, wholegrain and fortified cereals, wholemeal bread, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried apricots and other dried fruit, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.2] Iron is an essential part of haemoglobin, the red protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs around the body to all cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.3] Iron is part of myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle cells, and part of enzymes that release energy from food. It also supports immunity and concentration. This is why a shortage leaves people tired.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.4] Iron deficiency causes anaemia (too few red blood cells or too little haemoglobin). Effects are tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, dizziness, irritability, poor concentration, feeling cold and reduced resistance to infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.5] Causes of iron deficiency are a diet low in iron or poor in vitamin C, blood loss (menstruation, ulcers, operations), high needs in growth spurts, pregnancy and infancy, and poor absorption.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.6] Corrective measures are eating more iron-rich foods together with vitamin C, avoiding tea with meals, and taking an iron supplement on medical advice. This is because vitamin C improves absorption and tea blocks it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.7] Groups at risk are teenage girls, menstruating and pregnant women, infants and toddlers, people with heavy blood loss, and vegetarians and vegans, who eat only non-haem iron. Children are at risk because of fast growth and low-iron foods. For example, vegetarians miss out on the more easily absorbed haem form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.b] Trace minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, yet the body cannot do without them. Each has a job that no other mineral can do.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.0] Trace (micro) minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms a day, and the diet must supply them. They are tiny in amount yet essential. For example, zinc and iodine are needed in milligrams or micrograms.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.1] The trace minerals are iron, zinc, iodine, fluoride, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium and cobalt. Iron is a trace mineral by the amount needed, but it is studied in detail at Ordinary Level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.2] Iron, zinc and iodine each play a specific role in the body that the other minerals cannot replace. Zinc and iodine are set out in the blocks that follow.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.b] Zinc is a trace mineral that works inside enzymes, so it affects growth, healing and defence against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.0] Zinc sources include oysters and other shellfish, red meat, poultry, liver, dairy products, eggs, wholegrain cereals, pulses, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.1] Zinc is a component of many enzymes and supports growth and cell division, which is why a shortage slows growth in children.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.2] Zinc supports wound healing and a healthy immune system. This is because new cells must be made to repair the wound.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.3] Zinc contributes to a normal sense of taste and smell, and to healthy skin and hair and reproductive health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.4] Deficiency causes poor growth, delayed wound healing, dry flaky skin and rashes, loss of taste and appetite, reduced immunity and hair loss.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.b] Iodine is needed to make a hormone that sets the body's pace. Without it the thyroid gland cannot do its job.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.0] Iodine sources include sea fish and shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy products and eggs. Iodised salt also exists as a source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.1] Iodine is part of thyroxine, the hormone from the thyroid gland that controls the rate of metabolism (and growth and brain development).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.2] Deficiency causes goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck as it works harder to make more thyroxine. This is because the gland enlarges as it works harder.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.3] Iodine deficiency causes tiredness and sluggishness (lethargy) and weight gain in adults. It causes delayed mental and physical development in children, and in babies of deficient mothers.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.b] Potassium and sodium work as a pair, one inside cells and one outside, to keep fluid, nerves and muscles balanced.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.0] Potassium sources include bananas, potatoes eaten in their skins, tomatoes, dried fruit, orange juice, pulses, green and leafy vegetables, milk, meat, fish and nuts.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.1] Potassium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) inside cells and, with sodium, helps regulate fluid balance in and around cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.2] Potassium is needed to transmit nerve impulses and for muscle contraction, including the heartbeat. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.3] Potassium works with sodium to regulate blood pressure, and a high potassium intake helps lower it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.4] Deficiency (hypokalaemia) causes muscle weakness, cramps, an irregular heartbeat and tiredness. It is usually caused by severe vomiting, diarrhoea or some medicines rather than by normal diets.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.b] Sodium is essential, and the problem in Ireland is eating too much of it, not too little. Most of it comes from salt, which is sodium chloride.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.0] Sodium is mainly consumed as salt. Table salt, cured and smoked meats and fish such as bacon and ham, cheese, bread, crisps and snack foods, processed and ready meals, sauces, stock cubes and canned foods supply most of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.1] Fresh, unprocessed foods contain only small amounts of naturally occurring sodium. This is why processed foods, such as bacon and ready meals, are the main source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.2] Sodium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) outside cells and, with potassium, regulates fluid balance and blood volume.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.3] Sodium is needed to transmit nerve impulses, for muscle contraction and for enzyme function. It helps the small intestine absorb glucose and some amino acids. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.4] Excess sodium raises blood pressure (hypertension), which increases the risk of stroke, heart disease and kidney problems. Most people in Ireland eat more salt than advised, mostly from processed foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.5] To cut salt, cook from fresh food, do not add salt at the table, read labels (salt is on the nutrition declaration) and choose low-salt products.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.6] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) began a voluntary Salt Reduction Programme in 2003 to cut salt in processed food. Later reformulation work continues under the FSAI-Healthy Ireland Food Reformulation Task Force.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.7] Deficiency is uncommon but causes muscle cramps, headache, nausea and confusion. It can follow heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s6: 6. Energy value and dietary fibre targets
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.b] Every nutrient releases a set amount of energy per gram, so we can work out how much energy a food supplies from its nutrient content. Carbohydrate is the main energy nutrient in most diets, so you need its figure and how to use it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.0] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram. Sugars and starch have the same energy value per gram, so a gram of sugar gives no more energy than a gram of starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.1] For comparison, protein provides 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, fat 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram and alcohol 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. Fat gives more than twice the energy of carbohydrate for the same weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.2] Fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) is not digested by human enzymes, so it contributes little usable energy. Say 'little', not 'none'.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.3] To calculate the energy from a food, multiply the grams of carbohydrate by 16 to get kilojoules (kJ), or by 3.75 to get kilocalories (kcal).
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.4] Worked example: 30 g of sugar provides 30 × 16 = 480 kJ, or 30 × 3.75 = 112.5 kcal. One kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ), which is why the kcal figure is smaller.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.5] Label point: the nutrition declaration on a food label states carbohydrate and, of which, sugars; fibre is an optional item. The energy figure is calculated by the manufacturer, so it may not match your own calculation exactly.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.6] Settings, comparative study: compare two breakfast cereals, one with added sugar and one without, using the label's carbohydrate, sugar and energy per 100 g. Discuss which supplies more energy and which supplies more fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.7] Settings, activity comparison: work out the energy in a food such as a bar of chocolate and compare it with the time an activity such as walking or running takes to use it up. Use the energy values from the source given with the question.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.b] Carbohydrate, mostly as starch, is the main source of energy in most diets, and it should supply the largest share of the energy in the diet, with fat and protein supplying the rest. The kind of carbohydrate matters, because some carries other nutrients and some does not.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.0] Healthy eating guidelines use the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid (Department of Health and HSE, 2016). Its base shelf is vegetables, salad and fruit, and the second shelf up is wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.1] The top shelf holds foods high in fat, sugar and salt. These are eaten in small amounts and not every day, so they should not be the main source of energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.2] Choose wholemeal and wholegrain starchy foods, because they supply energy together with fibre, vitamins and minerals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.3] Foods with a lot of added sugar supply energy but few other nutrients, so they are described as energy-dense and nutrient-poor ('empty calories').
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.4] Replacing sugary foods and refined starchy foods with wholegrain starchy foods, fruit and vegetables raises the intake of fibre and nutrients without raising the energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.b] Fibre is the part of plant foods that humans cannot digest, and adults are advised to eat a set amount each day. Many Irish adults do not reach it, so you should know the target and the everyday ways to meet it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.0] The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommends a daily intake of dietary fibre for adults of 25 g a day. This is its adequate intake, set in 2010 for normal bowel function.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.1] Irish healthy eating guidance from the Department of Health and the HSE (the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid) also advises eating wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetables every day.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.2] Breakfast: choose a high-fibre cereal (look for at least 3 g of fibre per 100 g on the label), porridge, bran or wholegrain bread or toast. Check the fibre per 100 g when choosing cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.3] Choose wholegrain or wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta and brown flour in baking in place of white versions, because the fibre is in the outer part of the grain that white versions have lost.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.4] Add pulses such as beans, lentils, peas and chickpeas to soups, stews and salads. Half a tin of beans gives about 7.5 g of fibre, and chickpeas, kidney beans and butter beans give about 10 g.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.5] Add two tablespoons of seeds a day, such as sunflower, sesame, pumpkin and linseed, or bran or porridge oats, to cereal, yoghurt, bread and salads.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.6] Eat vegetables at lunch and dinner, about a third of each meal; frozen vegetables are as good as fresh. Leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, and bake or boil potatoes in their jackets, since most of the fibre in a potato is in the skin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.7] Eat 2-3 pieces of fruit a day (a piece gives about 2 g of fibre), and snack on fresh fruit, dried fruit or a handful of nuts instead of biscuits or crisps. Increase fibre gradually and drink plenty of water, because fibre absorbs water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.b] Added sugar supplies energy with few other nutrients, so cutting it down is a main healthy eating target. Strategies fall into two groups: what you buy, and how you prepare and eat food.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.0] Purchasing: read labels and ingredient lists, because added sugars appear under names such as sucrose, glucose syrup, fructose, honey, cane sugar, brown sugar, corn syrup and fruit juice concentrate.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.1] Choose reduced-sugar, lower-sugar or no-added-sugar versions of drinks, jams, cereals and yoghurts, choose fruit canned in juice rather than syrup, and avoid sugar-coated cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.2] Front-of-pack colour (traffic-light) labelling, where used, shows at a glance whether a food is high, medium or low in sugars. It is voluntary, so not every product carries it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.3] Preparing: reduce the sugar in recipes and use spices, vanilla or ripe fruit for flavour. Pureed fruit, banana, dates or apple can sweeten recipes instead of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.4] Do not add sugar to tea, coffee or breakfast cereals. Replace fizzy drinks with water, or with fruit juice diluted with sparkling water, which cuts the sugar per glass.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.5] Eat whole fruit rather than fruit juice or smoothies. Juice contains little fibre, and the sugar in juices and smoothies is released from the fruit's cells so it counts as free sugar, whereas whole fruit keeps its fibre and fills you up.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.6] Snacks and treats: limit sweets, biscuits, cakes, sweet sauces and sugary drinks, and keep fresh fruit, fruit or vegetable sticks, nuts and natural yoghurt as snacks instead.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.b] A diet high in sugar causes harm in several ways, some directly on the teeth and others through the energy and nutrients the diet supplies. Each effect needs its reason to earn marks.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.0] Tooth decay: bacteria in plaque (the sticky film on teeth) ferment sugar to produce acid, which dissolves the enamel of the teeth.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.1] Weight gain and obesity: sugary foods are energy-dense and nutrient-poor, so regular intake adds to excess energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.2] Type 2 diabetes: a diet high in sugar and energy is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes, which is linked to excess body weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.3] Heart disease and high blood pressure: high sugar intake is associated with a higher risk of heart disease and raised blood pressure. High fructose intake has been linked with higher blood pressure, and refined carbohydrate with excess insulin that can raise LDL cholesterol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.4] Nutritional deficiency: foods high in sugar displace fruit, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods from the diet, so vitamins and minerals fall short.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.5] Sugary foods are also low in fibre, so they do little for bowel health.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.b] Artificial sweeteners give a sweet taste without the energy of sugar, or with less of it. They fall into two types, intense and bulk, and each has its uses and limits.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.0] Intense sweeteners, such as aspartame and saccharin, are many times sweeter than sugar, so only tiny amounts are needed and they add almost no energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.1] Bulk sweeteners, such as sorbitol, mannitol and xylitol, have a similar bulk to sugar and provide some energy, though less than sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.2] Uses in food manufacture: they sweeten and improve palatability, and they are used in low-calorie, sugar-free, slimming and reduced-sugar foods, soft drinks and confectionery.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.3] Sorbitol and other bulk sweeteners act as humectants: they retain moisture and stop products drying out.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.4] Bulk sweeteners are used where sugar's bulk is needed, as in confectionery and fudge. Xylitol and sorbitol do not cause tooth decay, so they are used to avoid it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.5] Sweeteners cannot replace all of sugar's functions: they do not provide the bulk, browning, preserving or bulk-texture roles of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.6] Sweeteners are food additives with E numbers. Labels must say 'contains sweetener', and aspartame carries a warning for people with phenylketonuria.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.7] Excess bulk sweeteners can have a laxative effect, so their labels carry a warning.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s1: 1. Fat-soluble vitamins
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.b] Vitamins are organic compounds that the body needs in very small amounts. They are grouped by what they dissolve in, and that one difference decides how they are absorbed, stored and lost.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.0] Vitamins are needed for normal growth, health and chemical reactions in the body. The body cannot make enough of most of them, so they must come from food.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.1] There are two classes: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) and water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C). For example, vitamin A is fat-soluble and vitamin C is water-soluble.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.2] RDA (recommended dietary allowance) is the daily amount that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, because needs change at different life stages.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.b] Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat, so they are absorbed together with dietary fat. This also explains why they are stored in the body and why too much can be harmful.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.0] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed in the small intestine along with dietary fats and are carried in the lymphatic system (the body's drainage network) before entering the blood.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.1] Because they need fat to be absorbed, a diet very low in fat, or a condition that stops fat being absorbed, can reduce vitamin intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.2] The liver and fatty (adipose) tissue store fat-soluble vitamins, so the body keeps a reserve between meals and deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.3] Because they are stored, excess intake from supplements or large amounts of liver can build up to toxic levels (toxicity). Water-soluble vitamins are mostly excreted in urine instead.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.4] Excess vitamin A (retinol) is harmful in pregnancy because it can harm the developing baby, so high-dose vitamin A supplements and large amounts of liver are avoided during pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.b] Vitamin A comes in two forms: one ready-made in animal foods and one made from a plant pigment. Its main jobs are vision, healthy surface tissues and protection of cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.0] Retinol is the active, ready-made (preformed) form of vitamin A, found only in animal foods: liver, fish liver oils, oily fish, dairy products (butter, full-fat milk and cheese), eggs and fortified margarine (margarine with vitamin A added).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.1] Beta-carotene is a plant pigment and a provitamin (a substance the body converts into a vitamin). The body converts it into retinol in the intestine, so beta-carotene is not itself vitamin A.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.2] Good beta-carotene sources include carrots, sweet potatoes, apricots, mango, red and orange peppers, tomatoes and dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach and kale, where the green hides the orange pigment.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.3] Retinol combines with the protein opsin to form rhodopsin (visual purple), the light-sensitive pigment in the rod cells of the retina. This allows vision in dim light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.4] Vitamin A keeps the cells lining the eyes, mouth, respiratory tract, urinary tract and skin healthy and moist, which protects them against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.5] Vitamin A supports growth, cell repair and a healthy immune system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.6] Beta-carotene acts as an antioxidant, neutralising free radicals (unstable molecules that damage cells).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.b] Without enough vitamin A, the eye pigment cannot be rebuilt and the body's moist surface linings break down. The eyes show the first signs.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.0] Night blindness is the earliest sign: poor vision in dim light because rhodopsin is not regenerated.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.1] Xerophthalmia: the eyes become dry and damaged, and in severe cases the cornea (the clear front of the eye) is destroyed and blindness follows.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.2] Dry, rough skin and dry, brittle hair, because the skin and other surface tissues are no longer kept healthy and moist.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.3] Poor growth, because vitamin A supports growth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.4] Lowered resistance to infection, because the mucous membranes (moist linings) dry out, crack and lose their protective role.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.5] In severe cases deficiency causes blindness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.b] Vitamin D is unusual because the body can make it in the skin when sunlight falls on it, which is why it is called the sunshine vitamin. It is the vitamin that lets the body use calcium to build bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.0] Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) forms in the skin when ultraviolet B from sunlight acts on a cholesterol derivative in the skin. It is also found in animal foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.1] Ergocalciferol (vitamin D2) is the plant and fungal form, made when mushrooms and yeast are exposed to ultraviolet light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.2] Dietary sources include oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, sardines and herring, egg yolk, liver, butter, fortified margarine and spreads, fortified milk and some fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.3] Vitamin D increases the absorption of calcium and phosphorus from the small intestine, which is why it is the vitamin needed to absorb calcium.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.4] Vitamin D raises blood calcium levels, working with parathormone (the parathyroid hormone) when blood calcium falls, so blood calcium stays at the right level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.5] Vitamin D supports the mineralisation of bones (the deposit of calcium and phosphorus that hardens them) and the formation of teeth, so it builds and maintains strong bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.6] In Ireland, weak winter sunlight means that dietary sources of vitamin D matter most in the winter months. People who cover up, stay indoors or have dark skin also make less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.b] Low vitamin D means too little calcium reaches the bones, so they stay soft. The condition has a different name in children and in adults.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.0] Rickets in children: bones soften and bend, causing bow legs, delayed growth and poor tooth formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.1] Osteomalacia in adults: bones become soft, weak and painful, and fractures can occur, because they are not properly mineralised.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.2] Low vitamin D lowers calcium absorption, which is a contributing factor in osteoporosis, where bone density falls, and in dental decay.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.b] Vitamin E is the fat-soluble vitamin whose main job is as an antioxidant: it stops oxidation damaging fatty parts of the body's cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.0] Tocopherols are the active forms of vitamin E. Alpha-tocopherol is the form the human body uses most, and the main form in the body.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.1] Rich sources are vegetable oils such as sunflower, wheatgerm and olive oil, nuts and seeds such as almonds and sunflower seeds, wholegrain cereals, avocado, green leafy vegetables and egg yolk.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.2] Vitamin E is an antioxidant that protects the membranes of cells and polyunsaturated fatty acids from oxidation (damage by free radicals).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.3] Vitamin E also helps protect the vitamin A and vitamin C in the body, and supports healthy skin and immune function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.4] Deficiency is rare in healthy people. In people who cannot absorb fat, and in premature babies, it can cause nerve and muscle damage and the early breakdown of red blood cells (a form of anaemia).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.b] Vitamin K is the clotting vitamin: the K comes from the German word for coagulation. It has two forms, one from plants and one made by bacteria in the gut.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.0] Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) comes from plants: green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, cabbage, kale and sprouts, and vegetable oils and liver.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.1] Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) is made by bacteria in the large intestine and is also found in dairy products, eggs and meat. The gut bacteria are therefore a second source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.2] The liver needs vitamin K to make the clotting factors, including prothrombin, that allow blood to clot. This is its primary function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.3] Vitamin K activates osteocalcin, a protein that binds calcium into bone, so it supports bone formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.4] Deficiency impairs blood clotting, causing bleeding and easy bruising. Long courses of antibiotics or poor fat absorption can also reduce vitamin K.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.5] The HSE offers every newborn baby a vitamin K injection within hours of birth, because their gut bacteria are not yet established and they have low stores, so they risk bleeding. It is the parents' choice.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.b] All four fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water, but they differ in what destroys them: heat, oxygen, light or alkalis. Knowing this explains how storage and cooking affect each one.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.0] Vitamin A is insoluble in water but soluble in fats and organic solvents.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.1] Vitamin A is heat-stable in normal cooking but is destroyed by prolonged high temperatures.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.2] Vitamin A is destroyed by oxygen (it is oxidised), so foods kept exposed to air lose it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.3] Vitamin D is insoluble in water and is relatively heat-stable in normal cooking and storage, so cooking oily fish loses little.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.4] Vitamin E is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking, but it is destroyed by oxidation, including by rancid fats, by light and air, and by frying at high temperature for long periods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.5] Vitamin K is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.6] Vitamin K is destroyed by light (ultraviolet) and by alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.b] To judge how good a food is as a source of vitamins, use food composition tables and food labels, and think about whether the food is fresh, processed or fortified.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.0] Food composition tables give the vitamin content of foods, usually per 100 g, so fresh and processed foods can be compared on the same basis (like for like).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.1] The mandatory nutrition declaration on a food label lists energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. Vitamins appear only when added or when a claim is made, so not listing vitamins does not mean the food has none.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.2] Where vitamins are listed, the label states the amount per 100 g (or per serving) and as a percentage of the reference intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.3] In Ireland, the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014 give effect to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) advises on food labelling and nutrition in Ireland.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.4] Fortified foods have vitamins added that the natural food does not contain, or that were lost in processing. Examples are margarine with added vitamins A and D and fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.5] Fresh produce loses vitamin C in storage, while frozen vegetables, blanched before freezing, usually retain more vitamin C than long-stored fresh vegetables.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.6] Canning and prolonged cooking destroy heat-sensitive vitamins, so a processed food may contain less of a vitamin than its fresh form.
