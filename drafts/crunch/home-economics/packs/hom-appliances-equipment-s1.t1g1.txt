CANDIDATES hom-resource-management-s1: 1. Family resource management
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.b] Every family has more things it wants to do than it has time, money or energy for. Resource management is how a family decides what to do with what it has.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.0] Family resource management is using the resources a family has wisely and efficiently to achieve its goals. Resource management is the planned use of the resources available.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.1] A resource is anything a family can draw on to meet its needs and wants, such as a parent's time, a cooker or a weekly wage.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.2] Resources are limited, so choices have to be made about how they are used. This scarcity is why management is needed.
[hom-resource-management-s1.0.3] Goals are what the family is trying to achieve, and they give management its direction. Short-term goals include this week's meals or a family outing; long-term goals include buying a home, education or retirement.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.b] Managing resources is not only about saving money. The purpose has three parts, and a full answer names all three.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.0] The first purpose is to use available resources efficiently in order to achieve goals, so that the family gets the most from what it has.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.1] The second purpose is to improve the quality of family life, so that home is more comfortable and less stressful for everyone.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.2] The third purpose is to enable each family member to reach their full potential, because well-managed time, money and support leave room for learning, work and interests.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.3] Good management reduces waste of time, money, food and energy, and gets the best value from limited resources.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.4] It also avoids conflict and stress by agreeing who does what, so members know what is expected of them.
[hom-resource-management-s1.1.5] Example of efficient management: a weekly meal plan with a shopping list saves money and time and reduces food waste, because the family buys only what it will use. Other examples are budgeting, planned childcare, sharing household tasks, or resolving a conflict by agreeing a timetable.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.b] Families draw on four kinds of resource, and only one of them is money. Naming each type with an example shows you understand what the family has to work with.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.0] Human resources: the time, energy, skills and knowledge of family members, such as one parent's cooking skills or a teenager's free afternoon.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.1] Material resources: equipment, food, ingredients, clothing, furniture and other household goods, such as a washing machine or a freezer.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.2] Economic resources: income, savings and credit available to the family.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.3] Environmental resources: the physical surroundings and community facilities that support family life, such as space, daylight and local services like schools, transport and health care.
[hom-resource-management-s1.2.4] Resources other than money matter as much as money. A family with little money can still manage well by using time, skills, knowledge and good planning.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.b] A management system is a planned, step-by-step way of turning a need or goal into a result using the resources available. In other words, it turns what you have into what you wanted.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.0] A management system lets a family decide what needs doing, who does it, when it is done and which resources it needs.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.1] Everyday tasks that use a management system include meal planning, budgeting, housework, childcare and shopping. Money and home management, problem solving and decision-making are also uses of the management process.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.2] One-off events such as a birthday party, a holiday or a house move also use a management system, as does a problem such as choosing an appliance.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.3] Systems work best when the goals, standards and responsibilities are agreed in advance and tasks are shared, so that members know what is expected of them.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.4] Systems are not fixed: they are adjusted when circumstances change, such as a new job, a job loss, a move or the arrival of a baby.
[hom-resource-management-s1.3.5] A system is a cycle, not a one-off. The result is checked against the goal, and what was learned improves the next attempt. This checking is called feedback.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.b] When a family uses a management system well, the benefits show up in everyday life: in time, money, workload, relationships and the ability to cope with change.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.0] Time is used well. Planned routines make sure meals, laundry and school preparation are done on time, with less last-minute stress.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.1] Money is used well. Meal plans and shopping lists reduce impulse buying and the amount of food that is wasted, and budgeting helps the family avoid debt.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.2] Work is shared fairly. Shared tasks spread the workload so no single member carries the whole household, and each member learns skills.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.3] Goals and standards agreed by all members give the family a shared direction, support communication and reduce conflict.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.4] Energy, water and food are used carefully, so the cost of running the home is lower and there is less waste.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.5] Efficient use of time and equipment frees time for leisure, rest and relationships, so quality of life improves.
[hom-resource-management-s1.4.6] The family copes better with change and emergencies because it has a plan and some resources in reserve.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.b] The family is a small group that pools, allocates and controls its own resources. Because it sets goals and takes responsibility for results, it acts as a managerial unit.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.0] Each member contributes resources such as earnings, time or skills, and each member benefits from the shared pool of the household.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.1] The family sets goals, makes decisions, allocates resources and takes responsibility for the results.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.2] Adults usually plan, earn and coordinate; children and teenagers take on tasks and share in decisions as they grow older. This means management duties are shared and change over time.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.3] Family goals may be short term, such as a weekly shop or a family outing, or long term, such as buying a home or saving for education.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.4] Management requires balancing individual wants against the needs and goals of the household as a whole, so the unit works best when decisions take everyone's views into account.
[hom-resource-management-s1.5.5] Article 41 of the Irish Constitution recognises the family as the natural, primary and fundamental unit group of society.

CANDIDATES hom-personal-finance-s2: 2. Household expenditure and budgeting
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.b] Expenditure is the money a household spends, and income sets the limit on it. Spending that goes beyond income leads to debt, so spending must be matched to income, and many factors shape how a household divides its money.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.0] Low-income households spend most of their income on essentials such as housing, food and heat, and a larger proportion on food than richer households do, so they have little or no discretionary spending (spending on non-essentials) or saving.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.1] Higher-income households spend more in total and a bigger share on discretionary items such as leisure, holidays and eating out, so they have more discretionary income (money left after essentials) and can also save and pay for credit.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.2] Family size and composition matter, for example the number of children and whether it is a one-parent or two-parent household, because each extra person adds to food, clothing and housing needs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.3] The stage of the family life cycle changes priorities: young children add costs for food, clothing, childcare and schooling, while teenagers cost more for food, clothing and education.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.4] Once children are independent and at retirement, spending falls and shifts towards health, leisure and heating; older children can also help decide and manage spending.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.5] Employment affects money, travel time and cost, and meal and sleep patterns. Working outside the home adds travel, convenience food and childcare costs, and shift work changes meal and sleep patterns and transport costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.6] Urban and rural location change patterns, for example through transport costs and housing costs: rural households depend more on cars, while urban rents and mortgages are higher. Heating costs also differ.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.0.7] Other influences include socio-economic class and the gender of the earner, personal values and priorities, health or special needs, advertising and peer pressure (the influence of friends and others the same age).
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.b] Not all spending is equally urgent. Households divide it into what they must pay to manage family life and what they can choose to spend once the necessities are covered.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.0] Essential expenditure is spending on necessities needed to manage family life, such as housing (rent or mortgage), utilities (electricity, heating), food, clothing, transport, insurance, medical bills, loan repayments and household bills.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.1] Discretionary expenditure is spending on non-essentials, made only after essential expenditure and savings have been provided for.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.2] Examples of discretionary expenditure: holidays, entertainment, alcohol, cosmetics, eating out, leisure and gym membership, and streaming services.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.3] Fixed essential costs stay the same amount at regular times each week or month, such as rent or a mortgage repayment, insurance and telephone bills, so they are easy to plan for.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.4] Irregular essential costs vary in amount or timing, such as clothing, doctor's bills, school expenses and car repairs, so a budget must set money aside for them. Some are also seasonal, such as heating, Christmas and back to school.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.5] Whether an item is essential depends on the household. The same item can be essential for one household and discretionary for another, for example a car for a rural family compared with a city family.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.1.6] Credit repayments are fixed commitments in the budget, and credit used for essentials adds to future spending because it must be paid back.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.b] A budget is a plan that sets expected net income against planned spending for a period such as a week, month or year. Following a series of steps means essentials are paid first and money is left for savings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.0] 1. List all income, using net (take-home) income, never gross, because net income is the money actually available to spend. Include wages, welfare, maintenance and any other income.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.1] 2. List planned fixed, irregular and discretionary expenditure for each week or month, including credit repayments, and choose the time period the budget will cover.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.2] 3. Plan essential needs first, such as rent or mortgage and household bills, before any discretionary spending, so the necessities are always covered.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.3] 4. Allocate money for short-term and long-term savings, and for emergencies, so unexpected costs do not have to be borrowed.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.4] 5. Allow for personal spending and special occasions such as Christmas and holidays, and for changes in circumstances, so these annual costs do not cause a shortfall.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.5] 6. Keep bills and receipts, and record one month of actual spending to find where overspending occurs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.6] 7. Compare income with expenditure and adjust. A surplus (income greater than spending) can be saved; a deficit (spending greater than income) means cutting discretionary spending, raising income or seeking advice, rather than borrowing.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.2.7] 8. Review the budget regularly, and change it when family circumstances or income change, so it stays realistic.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.b] When a budget shows overspending or a deficit, a household can cut costs without going without essentials. The savings come from shopping smarter and using less.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.0] Use a shopping list and stick to it, because this avoids impulse buying of items you did not plan to purchase.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.1] Choose own-brand goods and compare prices between shops, since similar goods can cost much less.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.2] Buy second-hand or in charity shops, use sales, and buy food in season, when it is cheaper and plentiful.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.3] Cook from scratch and choose cheaper cuts of meat, because convenience foods and prime cuts cost more.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.4] Cut energy use and switch supplier to reduce utility bills.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.3.5] Cancel unused subscriptions, such as gym membership or streaming services, so you are not paying for things you do not use.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.b] A budget does more than record numbers. It shows a household where its money goes and helps it avoid problems before they happen.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.0] Budgeting shows where money goes, and identifies overspending and impulse buying before they lead to unplanned debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.1] Budgeting reduces reliance on credit and supports a good credit record, because bills and repayments are planned for and paid on time.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.2] A budget gives financial security and reduces the worry and stress caused by unpaid bills, because it ensures bills are paid.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.3] A budget that allows some saving can provide for emergencies and cover unexpected events, such as job loss or an urgent repair.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.4] Budgeting helps a household reach its goals, because money is set aside for them.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.4.5] Budgeting builds money-management skills that transfer to independent living.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.b] A sample budget shows how a net income can be divided between headings so that every euro is planned. The first sample is a family of two adults and two children with a combined net income of 900 euro a week.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.0] Housing, the largest fixed essential cost, is allocated about a quarter of income (25 per cent): 225 euro.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.1] Food is allocated about a quarter of income (25 per cent): 225 euro, because food needs rise with growing children.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.2] Household bills are allocated about 15 per cent of income: 135 euro. Education is allocated about 10 per cent: 90 euro, to cover school costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.3] Clothing, leisure, savings, health and discretionary spending are each allocated about 5 per cent: 45 euro each, with savings kept for emergencies.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.4] A second sample, 800 euro a week for the same type of family, gives 200 euro for housing, 200 for food, 120 for household bills, 80 for education and 40 for each of the five 5 per cent headings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.5] The totals must add up to the net income, so the plan allocates the full net income, leaves no money unplanned, and is reviewed each month.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.6] The percentages are a planning guide for this sample, not a rule, and should be adjusted to the family's actual fixed costs.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.5.7] A lower income cannot cover the same percentages, so essentials come first while discretionary spending and savings shrink; a higher income allows more savings.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.b] Households that are in debt, or at risk of it, can get free help from MABS. It advises and negotiates for them but does not lend money or pay their debts.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.0] MABS is a free, confidential and independent service for people in debt or at risk of debt, funded by the State through the Citizens Information Board.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.1] MABS helps a household work out its income and spending, and draws up a realistic budget and repayment plan (schedule) with the family.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.2] MABS negotiates with lenders, including moneylenders and mortgage lenders, on behalf of households in debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.3] MABS offers a mortgage arrears service, and advises on personal insolvency (when a person cannot pay their debts) and income maximisation, which means checking and claiming entitlements to State supports.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.4] MABS advises on financial products and interest rates, and points to cheaper sources of credit such as credit unions.
[hom-personal-finance-s2.6.5] MABS also teaches money-management skills, so households can manage their budgets themselves in future.

CANDIDATES hom-personal-finance-s3: 3. Methods of payment and credit
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.b] A household can pay for what it buys in several ways. Each method moves money differently, and knowing how each works lets you match the method to the purchase.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.0] Cash: immediate payment in notes and coins. It is fast, costs nothing and shows exactly what has been spent, but carrying large amounts is unsafe, it leaves no record, and it cannot be used for online or distance purchases.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.1] Cheque: a written order to the bank to pay a sum from the account to a named person. It requires a current account, takes time to clear, and can be refused if there are not enough funds. Cheques are used less now but are still examined.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.2] Debit card: the amount is taken from the bank account straight away, so spending stays within the balance. It works by contactless or chip and PIN, and can be used online, for cashback and for ATM withdrawals.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.3] Credit card: goods are bought now and paid for later, up to a credit limit. A minimum monthly payment is required, there is an interest-free period if the balance is cleared by the due date, and interest applies to any balance not cleared.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.4] The card issuer may also protect some credit card purchases.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.5] Direct debit: the account holder authorises a company or utility to take money from the account on a due date. The amount can vary, which suits bills such as utilities and insurance.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.6] Standing order: the account holder instructs the bank to make a fixed payment at regular intervals to a named payee, such as rent, savings or loan repayments. The difference from direct debit is that the amount is fixed and the account holder controls the payment.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.7] Online or mobile banking and payment apps: payments, bill payments and transfers made from a bank account through a computer or phone. They are convenient and keep a record, but need internet access and care with security.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.0.8] Prepaid methods, gift vouchers and store credit are other ways to pay, where the money is paid in advance or held by one retailer.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.b] No single method is best for every purchase. A household compares methods against a set of criteria, then picks the one that suits the item and its own situation.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.0] Cost: whether the method charges fees or interest, since this adds to the price of the goods.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.1] Security: the risk of loss, theft or fraud, and whether the method leaves a record of the payment, which a cash payment does not.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.2] Convenience: speed of payment, the ability to pay at a distance or online, and ease of budgeting.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.3] Availability: whether the retailer or service provider accepts the method, and whether the buyer holds the card or account needed.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.4] Control: whether the buyer can see and limit spending, which is easier with cash or a debit card than with credit, because credit lets you spend money you do not yet have.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.1.5] Size of purchase: cash suits small everyday purchases, while credit or instalment methods suit large, expensive items the household cannot pay for at once, so the cost is spread.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.b] Credit means obtaining goods, services or money now and paying later, usually with interest or charges. Households can use several different forms, each suited to a different need.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.0] Credit card: a revolving credit facility (borrowing that can be reused as you repay) with a credit limit. It can offer an interest-free period if the full balance is paid on time.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.1] Charge card: the full balance is due each month, and no interest is charged on it because it is repaid in full.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.2] Store card: credit offered by a retailer, usable for purchases in that shop. It is usually costly.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.3] Overdraft: the bank allows the current account balance to go below zero up to an agreed limit, with interest charged on the amount overdrawn. It is short-term and flexible but can be costly.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.4] Personal or term loan from a bank, credit union or other lender: a fixed sum borrowed and repaid in fixed instalments over a set period at an agreed interest rate. It suits one-off large purchases such as a car.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.5] Credit union loan: members can borrow from the credit union, usually after building up a savings record. Credit unions often have lower rates.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.2.6] Other forms named in marking schemes: a budget account (spreads household bills), a credit sale, a moneylender (a licensed lender, usually very expensive) and a mortgage (a loan for house purchase).
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.b] Hire purchase lets a household use an expensive item straight away while paying for it over time. It is different from buying on a credit card, because ownership does not pass to the buyer at once.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.0] Hire purchase combines hiring and buying: the buyer pays a deposit and then instalments over an agreed period, and uses the goods straight away.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.1] The goods belong to the finance company until the final payment is made, so the buyer is only the hirer until then. Ownership passes to the buyer only at the last payment.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.2] Once one-third or more of the total hire-purchase price has been paid, counting the deposit, instalments and the value of any trade-in, the owner cannot repossess the goods without a court order.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.3] Before that point the owner must follow a procedure, including a default notice and time for the buyer to put things right. The buyer may also end the agreement.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.4] Advantages: the goods can be used immediately, repayments are fixed and the cost is spread across instalments, so no large lump sum is needed.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.5] Disadvantages: interest can make the total cost much higher than the cash price, and the buyer does not own the goods until the end.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.3.6] Missed payments can lead to repossession. The goods may also lose value faster than the debt falls.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.4.b] Credit nearly always costs more than paying cash. To decide whether it is worth it, a household has to work out exactly how much extra it will pay.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.4.0] Total cash price: the price of the goods if they are paid for in full at the time of purchase.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.4.1] Total amount repayable: the deposit plus all the instalments paid under the credit agreement.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.4.2] Cost of credit: the total amount repayable minus the cash price. It is the extra paid for the convenience of credit, so do not say the cost of credit is the total repayable.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.4.3] Annual percentage rate (APR): the yearly cost of borrowing, as a percentage, including charges. Because every lender must express cost the same way, it allows different credit offers to be compared.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.4.4] To compare cash and credit, the household adds all the payments, subtracts the cash price to find the cost of credit, and decides whether the immediate use of the goods is worth that extra cost.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.b] Credit can solve a real problem for a household, but it also carries costs and risks. Exam answers need both sides, each linked to the household.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.0] Advantages: goods are available immediately, and large purchases become affordable because the cost is spread.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.1] Advantages: credit can cover urgent or emergency costs, such as an emergency repair, and avoids the need to carry large amounts of cash, which is safer.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.2] Advantages: loan repayments are fixed over a set period, so the cost is predictable, and a household can take advantage of sales. A good repayment record also helps later borrowing.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.3] Disadvantages: interest makes goods cost more than the cash price, and high interest is charged if the credit balance is not repaid in full.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.4] Disadvantages: credit makes overspending and impulse buying easier, and several credit agreements at once increase the risk of unmanageable debt.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.5.5] Disadvantages: missed repayments can lead to penalties, repossession or a poor credit record, which makes future borrowing harder.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.b] Before signing a credit agreement, a household should check that the borrowing is sensible and that it can afford it. These are the questions to ask.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.0] Whether the purchase is needed, because credit makes it easy to buy things that are only wanted.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.1] The total cost, the interest rate and the APR, so that the offer can be compared with others and with the cash price.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.2] The repayment amount and the length of the agreement, because a longer period usually means more interest in total.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.3] Fees and penalties for missed payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.4] Ownership, and what happens if repayments are missed, for example repossession under hire purchase.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.6.5] Whether the repayments fit the household budget.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.b] The Consumer Credit Act 1995 (as amended) is the law that protects people who borrow or buy on credit. It sets rules for lenders, so consumers know what they are agreeing to.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.0] The Act protects consumers who buy on credit, including through hire purchase and other credit agreements. It regulates lenders, moneylenders and credit advertising, and the Central Bank of Ireland regulates the sector.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.1] A credit agreement must be in writing and signed, and must set out the APR and the terms of repayment. The consumer must be given a copy.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.2] The consumer has a short cooling-off period of 10 days after receiving a copy of the agreement to withdraw from it in writing.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.3] Credit advertisements must be accurate and must show the APR, so offers can be compared.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.4] For hire purchase, once one-third or more of the total price has been paid, the goods cannot be repossessed without a court order.
[hom-personal-finance-s3.7.5] Where to get help: MABS (the Money Advice and Budgeting Service) and the Central Bank's consumer hub, the CCPC, and the Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman for complaints about a bank or lender.

CANDIDATES hom-environmental-issues-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.b] A natural resource is a material or energy source taken from the environment, such as fuels, water, minerals, timber, wind and sunlight. Because some resources run out and others do not, what we use and how we use it matters for the future.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.0] Sustainable use means meeting present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs, so resources are still there for the people who come after us.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.1] Resources are classed by how quickly they are replaced: renewable resources are replenished naturally, while non-renewable resources are finite (limited in supply).
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.2] Consumers influence resource use through what they buy, how long they keep products in use and how they dispose of them, so everyday choices add up.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.3] Consumers conserve resources by buying only what is needed, choosing durable and repairable goods, and using items fully before replacing them, which means fewer new materials and less energy are used.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.b] A renewable resource is naturally replenished and is not used up by constant use, provided it is used no faster than it is replaced. Each renewable energy source works by turning a natural movement or heat into electricity or heat for buildings.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.0] Solar energy: photovoltaic (PV) panels convert sunlight directly into electricity, and solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water. Passive solar design, which sites the house and its glazing towards the sun, uses sunlight for heat and light.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.1] Wind energy: turbines convert the kinetic energy (movement energy) of moving air into electricity; wind farms are usually sited on exposed upland and coastal land, or at sea, where the wind is strongest.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.2] Hydroelectric energy: falling or flowing water turns turbines, so the source depends on a steady flow of water and a difference in height. The Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha, opened in 1929, is an Irish example.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.3] Biomass: wood, straw, energy crops and plant or animal waste are burned, or converted to biogas, to release heat or electricity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.4] Geothermal energy: heat stored in the earth's crust is brought to the surface as hot water or steam to heat buildings or generate electricity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.5] Tidal energy uses the rise and fall of the tides to drive turbines; wave energy uses the motion of waves to drive devices.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.6] Renewable heat in the home comes from heat pumps (which take low-grade heat from the air, ground or water), solar thermal panels, and stoves burning wood from managed forests. Heat pumps count as renewable heat because most of their heat comes from air, ground or water.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.7] Hydrogen can also carry energy, but it is renewable only if it is made using renewable electricity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.b] Renewable energy is attractive because it does not run out and is cleaner to produce, but it is not free of drawbacks. Exam answers need both sides.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.0] Renewable sources are sustainable: their supply does not run out on human timescales, so a well sited and maintained system can last indefinitely.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.1] Most produce little or no air pollution or carbon dioxide while generating energy, which reduces damage to air and water.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.2] Using renewables reduces dependence on imported fuel, which improves energy security (a dependable supply that does not rely on other countries).
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.3] Some renewables can be installed in a home, such as solar panels on a roof or a small wind turbine, so energy is produced where it is used.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.4] Building, maintaining and manufacturing renewable systems creates employment, and running costs are low once the system is installed.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.5] Limitation: wind and solar output varies with weather and daylight, so storage or a backup supply is needed.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.6] Limitation: installation costs can be high, and hydro and tidal systems need suitable sites.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.7] Limitation: wind farms and dams can affect the landscape and habitats.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.b] A non-renewable resource is limited in supply and cannot be replaced within a human lifetime once it is used up. Fuels and minerals dug out of the ground fall into this group.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.0] Fossil fuels are coal, oil, natural gas and peat, formed from the remains of plants and animals over very long periods: peat over thousands of years, coal, oil and gas over millions. Coke and anthracite (a type of coal) are other fuels in this group.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.1] Peat (turf) is cut from bogs for home heating and electricity generation. Bogs regrow far too slowly to count as renewable, so peat is a non-renewable fossil fuel, and bog habitats do not regenerate quickly.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.2] Nuclear energy uses uranium, a mined, finite fuel, so it is non-renewable. Ireland has no nuclear power station.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.3] Iron ore, copper ore and bauxite (the ore of aluminium) are non-renewable mineral resources because they are mined and finite; recycling extends the life of the metal they yield.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.4] For one non-renewable source used in the home, give the source and an advantage: fossil fuels are concentrated, give a lot of heat, and have an established supply and appliances.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.5] The disadvantages are that the fuel is finite, its price rises as reserves fall, burning it causes emissions and extracting it damages the land. For these reasons it is not sustainable.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.b] Using non-renewable resources harms the environment twice: when they are extracted and when they are burned. As they become scarce, the supply itself also becomes a problem.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.0] Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, the main greenhouse gas (a gas that traps heat in the atmosphere), which contributes to global warming and climate change.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.1] Burning coal, oil and peat releases sulphur dioxide, which forms acid rain.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.2] Mining, quarrying, drilling and peat extraction destroy habitats and landscapes, so the wildlife that lived there loses its home.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.3] Cutting bogs releases stored carbon, which adds to the carbon dioxide in the air.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.4] As reserves fall, fuel becomes harder and more costly to extract, so supplies become less secure.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.b] Consumers are responsible for how they use resources because their daily choices decide how much fuel is burned and how much material is thrown away. Practical steps cut demand for non-renewable resources and support renewable ones.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.0] Reduce energy demand, for example by insulating the home, using appliances efficiently and switching off standby, so less fossil fuel is burned.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.1] Choose a renewable electricity supplier, or install a renewable system such as solar panels or a heat pump, where practical. Smokeless or renewable fuel is also a better choice.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.2] Buy wood and paper products certified by a recognised scheme such as FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) or PEFC, which confirms that the timber came from managed forests.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.3] Reuse and recycle metals, glass and paper so that finite materials are used again.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.4] Choose products made with recycled content, which means less new material has to be mined or felled.

CANDIDATES hom-environmental-issues-s2: 2. Recycling and waste management
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.b] Sustainable waste management means reducing waste first, then reusing, recycling and recovering value, and disposing of only what is left. The waste hierarchy ranks the options from most to least preferred, so that as little waste as possible reaches disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.0] The order of preference is prevention, minimisation, reuse, recycling, recovery (including energy recovery) and disposal. Marking schemes phrase it as reduce, reuse, recycle, recover, dispose.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.1] Prevention and minimisation come first: buying only what is needed and avoiding excess packaging stops waste being created in the first place, so there is nothing to collect or treat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.2] Reuse means using an item again for its original or another purpose, such as refilling a container, reusing a jar for storage, repairing, donating or buying second-hand. The item stays whole, which is why reuse is not recycling.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.3] Recycling processes used materials into new products, such as paper, glass, metals, some plastics and textiles. It saves raw materials and usually energy, and keeps waste out of landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.4] Recovery means burning residual waste to generate heat or electricity (energy from waste); gas collected from landfill can also be used for energy. Energy recovery is not recycling, because the material is not made into a new product.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.5] Disposal, mainly landfill, is the least preferred option because it uses up land, and food and other organic waste decomposes without oxygen (anaerobically), releasing methane, a potent greenhouse gas.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.0.6] In Irish law the hierarchy is applied through the Waste Management Act 1996 (as amended). It sets up controls on waste collection and movement, and gives local authorities and the EPA roles in promoting waste reduction, recycling and reuse.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.b] Much household waste is biodegradable, meaning it can be rotted down by living things. Composting turns it into something useful instead of sending it to landfill.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.0] Biodegradable material such as food scraps, tea leaves and garden waste is broken down by micro-organisms into a nutrient-rich soil conditioner called compost. Composting differs from recycling because organic matter rots down rather than being reprocessed into a new product.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.1] Composting keeps biodegradable waste out of landfill, so less methane is produced as it would otherwise rot without oxygen.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.2] Compost returns nutrients and organic matter to the soil, so gardeners need less peat-based compost, and it is cheap for households to make.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.1.3] In Irish households food and garden waste is collected in the brown bin where a collection is provided. The three bins are green (dry recyclables), brown (food and garden waste) and black (general waste).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.b] Most waste can be cut at home by what a household buys and how it sorts what is left. Each action below moves waste up the hierarchy, away from disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.0] Plan meals and write a shopping list, so food is not bought and then thrown away.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.1] Buy concentrated products, such as concentrated detergents, which need less packaging per wash. Buy in bulk where it cuts packaging per unit, and use refill stations and refillable containers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.2] Use reusable bags, bottles and containers instead of single-use ones, so fewer items are made and thrown away. Reuse jars, tubs and shopping bags.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.3] Separate waste at home into recyclables, food and garden waste, and general waste, so that each stream can be treated in the right way.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.4] Choose products in recyclable, recycled or biodegradable packaging, and products with the EU Ecolabel. Repair items rather than replacing them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.2.5] Take paint and other household chemicals to a civic amenity site (the local authority's recycling and waste centre), not the bin, because they can harm the environment and the people handling waste. Do not put paint, oil, chemicals or batteries in the bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.b] Packaging carries symbols that tell the consumer how it can be treated after use. The recycling symbol is the most common, and it says less than many people think.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.0] The recycling symbol (three arrows forming a loop) shows that the packaging is made of material that can be recycled. It does not guarantee that a local collection accepts it, so do not say it is certainly accepted in the household recycling bin.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.1] A percentage inside the arrows shows the proportion of recycled content in the packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.3.2] A number inside a triangle on plastic is a plastic-type code: 1 is PET, used for drinks bottles, and 2 is HDPE, used for milk bottles and detergent containers.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.b] Two other symbols are often confused with the recycling loop. They tell the consumer about who paid for recovery and about a product's whole environmental effect, not whether the pack can be recycled.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.0] The Green Dot shows that the producer of the packaging has contributed financially to the recovery and recycling of packaging waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.1] In Ireland the Green Dot is shown by members of Repak, the packaging recovery scheme, so a producer who pays into Repak can put it on their packs.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.2] The Green Dot does not mean the item itself is recyclable or recycled, which is why it must not be treated as a recycling symbol.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.3] The EU Ecolabel, a flower symbol, is an audited, voluntary label certifying that a product or service causes minimum damage to the environment across its life cycle.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.4.4] The Ecolabel assessment considers the energy and materials used in making the product, the pollution produced, and how far it can be reused or recycled.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.b] Packaging makes up a large share of household waste, so government, producers, local authorities and consumers all act to reduce it. A strong answer names one initiative and gives two details about it.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.0] The levy on single-use plastic shopping bags, charged at the point of sale since 2002, discouraged their use and increased the use of reusable bags.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.1] The landfill levy is a charge per tonne on waste sent to landfill, which makes landfill more expensive and so encourages recycling and recovery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.2] Packaging producers must meet national recovery and recycling targets under Irish packaging regulations, and most meet them by funding the Repak scheme (the Green Dot).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.3] Local authority recycling schemes gather glass, cans, paper and plastics through kerbside collections and bring banks or civic amenity centres.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.4] The Re-turn deposit return scheme began on 1 February 2024. A refundable deposit is paid on plastic drinks bottles and aluminium and steel cans, which are returned in shops or to reverse vending machines. Glass and dairy containers are not included.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.5] Consumers can refuse over-packaged goods, choose loose produce and bring their own containers to refill counters.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.5.6] Manufacturers reduce excess packaging by using less material per product and lighter packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.b] Each packaging material has strengths for the product and a different fate as waste. Knowing both lets you judge how sustainable a material is.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.0] Flexible films are thin plastic films that are light, low in cost, fit the shape of the food closely, can be printed on and can be heat-sealed (closed by melting the edges together).
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.1] Flexible films give a good barrier against moisture and gases, which extends shelf life and reduces spoilage, and they are suitable for frozen food.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.2] Their drawback is that many multi-layer films are hard to recycle, so they are often sent to landfill or energy recovery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.3] Glass can be washed and reused, or recycled repeatedly without loss of quality, but it is heavy and costly to transport.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.4] Steel and aluminium cans are highly recyclable, and aluminium can be recycled repeatedly without loss of quality.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.5] Paper and card are recyclable, though their fibres shorten with each recycling cycle. They also come from a renewable raw material, are biodegradable, are light and take printing well.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.6.6] Biodegradable packaging breaks down naturally through the action of micro-organisms. Examples are paper or card, and compostable starch-based packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.b] WEEE is waste electrical and electronic equipment, and it is governed by its own collection and take-back rules. It must be kept out of the general bin because of what it contains.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.0] Electrical goods contain hazardous substances such as lead, mercury, cadmium and brominated flame retardants, and also valuable materials that can be recovered, so they must not go into general household waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.1] The crossed-out wheeled bin symbol means the item must be collected separately from household waste.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.2] Route 1: repair, sell or donate a working appliance first, because keeping it in use is the top of the waste hierarchy.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.3] Route 2: when a consumer buys a new equivalent item, the retailer must take back the old one free of charge, including on home delivery.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.4] Route 3: a civic amenity site or recycling centre accepts small appliances, batteries and other electrical items. Larger electrical shops must accept very small electrical items without a purchase, and batteries can be returned to shops without buying anything.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.5] Take batteries out of appliances and dispose of them separately before the appliance is handed in. Erase personal data from smart appliances.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.6] Old fridges and freezers contain refrigerant and insulating foam, which must be removed by an authorised treatment facility. Make the appliance safe before disposal.
[hom-environmental-issues-s2.7.7] The legal basis is the WEEE Directive, applied in Ireland by the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014.
