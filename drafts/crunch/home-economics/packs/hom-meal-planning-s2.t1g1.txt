TARGET hom-meal-planning-s2: 2. Dietary requirements through the life cycle
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.b] No two people need exactly the same diet. Energy and nutrient needs change with the stage of life and with circumstances, so every meal plan starts from the requirements of the person it is for.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.0] A balanced diet contains all the nutrients in the correct proportions for the individual's needs and good health, so what counts as balanced differs from person to person.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.1] Age: needs change at each life stage, with growth in childhood and adolescence, maintenance in adults and falling energy needs in older age.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.2] Sex: men generally need more energy than women because they have greater muscle mass and a larger body, while girls and women need extra iron once menstruation begins.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.3] Health status: illness, allergy, diabetes or a digestive disorder can change the foods a person is allowed to eat or how well their body absorbs nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.4] Pregnancy and breastfeeding increase the need for energy, protein, folate, iron and calcium, because the body is building a baby or making milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.5] Activity: energy needs rise with physical activity, and active people also need more fluid and carbohydrate. A sedentary adult needs less energy.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.0.6] Energy balance: energy taken in as food should match energy used by the body; any surplus is stored as body fat.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.b] A baby's first food and a young child's meals are planned around fast growth, a small stomach and teeth that are still forming. Pre-school guidelines are examined as short lists of practical rules.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.0] Breast milk supplies all the nutrients a baby needs for about the first six months.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.1] Breastfed babies, and babies taking little formula, are advised to take a vitamin D supplement from birth to 12 months, because breast milk contains little vitamin D.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.2] Weaning (moving from milk to solid food) begins at about six months, when the iron stored at birth starts to run low. Puréed meat and iron-fortified cereals supply iron.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.3] Salt should be kept low and sugar limited in foods for babies and young children, with none added to baby foods. Sugar harms developing teeth.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.4] Pre-school children have small, variable appetites, so regular balanced meals in small, manageable portions with nutritious snacks between suit them better than large meals. Attractive presentation helps.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.5] Young children need protein for growth, calcium and vitamin D for bones and teeth, iron for blood, and fruit and vegetables, following the Food Pyramid.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.6] Fussy eating is common. Offering new flavours one at a time in small portions, without forcing a child to eat, and involving the child in food preparation, helps them accept new foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.1.7] Sugary foods and drinks should be limited to protect developing teeth.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.b] School-age children need every nutrient in balance, and adolescents need even more during the growth spurt. Teenage eating habits, though, often work against meeting those needs.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.0] Children need every nutrient in balanced proportions, following the Irish Food Pyramid with portions suited to their age and activity. Protein is for growth, carbohydrate and fat for energy, calcium for bones and teeth, iron for blood, plus fibre and water, with nutritious snacks and less sugar and salt.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.1] Ways to include vegetables for children: stir-fries, cut shapes, grated or blended into sauces, smoothies, soups, and vegetables in lunchboxes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.2] Adolescents go through a growth spurt, roughly between the ages of 12 and 19, which increases the need for every nutrient.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.3] Girls need extra iron once they begin menstruating, because menstrual blood loss depletes iron stores. Fruit and vegetables supply fibre and vitamin C, and vitamin C improves the absorption of iron from plant foods, helping avoid anaemia.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.4] Calcium and vitamin D build bone mass during adolescence, so good intake now builds peak bone mass and reduces the risk of osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones) later.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.5] Active teenagers need high-energy starchy foods such as pasta, potatoes, rice and wholegrain cereals. Protein from meat, fish, eggs, dairy and pulses (animal and plant sources) supports growth and repair.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.6] High-salt, high-sugar and high-fat snacks and fizzy drinks add energy but few nutrients, so they should be limited. Irregular meals and skipped breakfasts are common in adolescence and make it harder to meet nutritional needs, so teenagers should eat three varied meals daily and avoid skipping meals, especially breakfast.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.2.7] Factors making a healthy diet difficult for teenagers: likes and dislikes, early exposure to fatty, salty and sugary foods, convenience and eating on the go, availability, cost, advertising and peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.b] Adult bodies are no longer growing, so the aim shifts to maintaining health and weight. The main guideline is to match what you eat to how active you are.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.0] Adults need to balance energy intake with activity to maintain a healthy weight, and to eat a balanced diet with all the nutrients in proportion.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.1] Daily starchy foods, fruit and vegetables supply the fibre, vitamins and minerals that support bowel health.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.2] Protein maintains muscle and repairs tissue, so a protein food is included at each main meal.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.3] Unsaturated oils should replace saturated fats, and sugar and salt should be kept low.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.4] Women of childbearing age need adequate folate and iron, because menstrual losses continue.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.3.5] A sedentary worker (one who sits most of the day) needs less energy, so should reduce fat and sugar, eat fibre-rich foods, get enough calcium, iron and vitamins, and limit salt.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.b] Older bodies burn less energy but still need nearly as many nutrients, so food must be nutrient-dense. Practical problems with teeth, appetite and money also shape what older people can eat.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.0] Energy needs fall with age because muscle mass and activity decline, so portions should be smaller but nutrient-dense (packed with nutrients per mouthful). Small, frequent, appetising meals suit reduced appetite, and fortified foods help.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.1] Protein remains important for maintaining muscle and repairing tissue, so older people should include a protein food at main meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.2] Calcium and vitamin D protect the bones, and calcium absorption decreases with age.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.3] Vitamin B12 absorption can fall in later life, so older people may need B12 from fortified foods or supplements.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.4] Fibre and fluid prevent constipation, which is common among older people who are less active. Iron and vitamin C are also needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.5] Salt should be reduced to help control blood pressure, and saturated fat reduced.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.6] Chewing and swallowing difficulties, or dentures, call for soft, moist and easy-to-chew foods. A limited budget and reduced mobility also affect shopping and cooking.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.4.7] Diet-related conditions common in older people include osteoporosis, diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure and high cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.b] A pregnant woman's diet must feed her own body and build a baby, so the need for particular nutrients rises while food safety matters more. This is the group examined most often, usually as nutrient, reason and food source.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.0] Folic acid (also called folate) taken before conception and in early pregnancy helps prevent neural-tube defects in the developing baby. Sources include green vegetables and fortified cereals, plus a supplement as advised.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.1] Iron supports the extra blood volume of pregnancy and the baby's iron stores; vitamin C eaten at the same meal improves iron absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.2] Calcium and vitamin D are needed for the baby's bones and teeth and to protect the mother's bones.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.3] Protein from lean meat, fish, fully cooked eggs and pasteurised dairy supports the growth of fetal tissue, the placenta and maternal tissues.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.4] Omega-3 fatty acids from cooked oily fish, such as salmon, support the development of the baby's brain and nervous system. Iodine and vitamins A, C, D, K and folate are also needed.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.5] Water is needed for blood plasma and fetal tissues, to carry nutrients and remove waste, and to prevent dehydration and constipation. Fibre also helps prevent constipation, which is common in pregnancy.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.6] Energy needs rise only modestly, mainly in later pregnancy, so a pregnant woman should not double her food intake. She should balance food with activity, follow the Food Pyramid and choose low-GI starchy foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.5.7] Breastfeeding women need extra energy, protein, calcium and fluid to produce milk.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.b] Some foods carry bacteria that can harm an unborn baby, so pregnancy guidelines restrict them. Any menu for a pregnant woman must follow these rules.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.0] Pâté, unpasteurised milk, soft mould-ripened cheese, and raw or undercooked meat, fish and eggs carry a risk of listeria or salmonella (food-poisoning bacteria) and should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.1] Alcohol should be avoided in pregnancy, and caffeine and salt should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.2] Taste changes and morning sickness can be managed with dry toast before rising and small, frequent meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.3] A sample one-day menu uses pasteurised dairy and fully cooked foods. Breakfast: fruit juice, porridge made with pasteurised milk and berries, wholemeal toast.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.6.4] Lunch: vegetable soup, wholemeal bread, yoghurt and fruit. Dinner: well-cooked lean chicken or fish with vegetables and potatoes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.b] A convalescent is someone recovering from illness. Their appetite is low but their body needs nutrients to repair itself, so food must be light, appealing and nourishing.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.0] Convalescents need small, appetising, easy-to-digest portions, because appetite is often reduced during recovery. Regular balanced meals are important.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.1] Protein from lean meat, fish, eggs and dairy supports tissue repair.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.2] Iron and vitamin C support recovery of blood levels, and vitamin C from fruit and vegetables aids iron absorption. Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and fluids keep the bowel working.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.7.3] Lightly seasoned meals are better tolerated; spicy, fried, oily and greasy foods should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.b] Active people burn more energy and lose fluid in sweat, so their diet is built around extra fuel and water.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.0] Active people need extra energy, mainly from complex carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, pasta, rice and potatoes.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.1] Sweat losses mean active people need extra fluid, drunk before, during and after exercise.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.8.2] Protein supports muscle repair and growth after exercise, and fruit, yoghurt or wholegrain sandwiches supply energy between meals.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.b] Shift work and long or varied days disrupt normal mealtimes. Good planning keeps meals balanced even when no one is home at the usual time.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.0] Irregular shifts disrupt mealtimes, so people often skip meals or rely on fast food and vending machines, which should be avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.1] Planning ahead with a list, and keeping staples and some frozen or prepared ingredients, makes balanced meals easier.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.2] Batch cooking and freezing portions allows balanced meals to be eaten at staggered times without much last-minute preparation.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.3] Soups, casseroles and other reheatable portions suit people eating at different times.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.4] Packed lunches with fruit, wholegrain bread and yoghurt keep nutrition on track during long working days.
[hom-meal-planning-s2.9.5] Slow cookers and timers allow a meal to cook while the household is out. Portions should stay balanced, following the guidelines.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s1: 1. Energy requirements and the role of energy
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.b] Every activity the body carries out, from breathing to running, needs energy, and that energy comes from the food and drink we take in. Knowing what energy is for, and how it is measured, is the starting point for working out how much a person needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.0] Energy is what the body needs to carry out every function: breathing, circulating blood, nerve and brain activity, growth and repair, movement and keeping warm.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.1] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal, often called "calories"). 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, and food labels give both units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.2] The body releases energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein, and from alcohol, through chemical reactions, and uses it for every body function.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.3] Energy not needed straight away is stored, because the body cannot waste it: a small store as glycogen (a stored form of carbohydrate) in the liver and muscles, and the large store as fat in adipose tissue (fatty tissue under the skin and around organs).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.4] Three main functions of energy are basal metabolism (keeping the body working at rest), growth and physical activity. Marking schemes also credit the working of the internal organs and the production of heat to maintain body temperature.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.b] Even when you are lying still, your body is working hard to keep you alive. The energy for this hidden work is called the basal metabolic rate, and it is the biggest part of what most people use each day.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.0] BMR is the minimum amount of energy the body needs to stay alive when completely at rest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.1] It pays for the functions that continue at rest, such as breathing, the heartbeat and circulation of blood, and the work of the brain, liver, kidneys and other internal organs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.2] It also pays for maintaining body temperature, and for the continuing renewal of cells.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.3] BMR is measured with the person lying at complete rest and awake, in a warm room, about 12 hours after eating (fasting). These conditions mean no movement, cold or digestion adds extra energy use to the result.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.4] For most people BMR is the largest single part of the energy used in a day, more than the energy used for activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.b] Two people lying side by side will not burn exactly the same amount of energy. BMR varies with the amount and type of body tissue a person has and with the hormones controlling the speed of their metabolism.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.0] Age: BMR per kilogram is highest in infancy and childhood because of rapid growth, and it falls with age as lean muscle tissue is lost.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.1] Gender: men generally have a higher BMR than women of the same weight, because men have a higher proportion of lean muscle tissue to fat. Do not say it is simply because they are men.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.2] Body size and weight: the larger the body, the more tissue there is to maintain, so the higher the BMR.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.3] Body composition: lean muscle tissue is more active than fat, so it uses more energy at rest. A muscular person has a higher BMR than a person of the same weight with more fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.4] Hormones: the thyroid gland controls the speed of metabolism (the chemical reactions in the body). An overactive thyroid, producing too much thyroid hormone, raises BMR, and an underactive thyroid lowers it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.5] Health: fever, infection and recovery from injury raise BMR, because the body is working harder to fight illness or repair itself.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.6] Pregnancy and lactation (breastfeeding) raise the requirement, and in a cold climate the body works to stay warm, which raises it too; a hot climate has a smaller effect.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.7] Activity does not change BMR itself. It is a separate part of the total energy the body uses in a day.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.b] Building a new body takes energy as well as nutrients. This is why people who are growing quickly need a lot of energy for their size.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.0] Energy is needed to build new body tissue, especially muscle, bone, blood and organs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.1] Infants and young children need a high amount of energy for their size because they are growing quickly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.2] Teenagers have adolescent growth spurts (short periods of very rapid growth) which raise the requirement sharply.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.3] Teenage boys generally need more energy than girls at this stage because of their greater body size and muscle.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.4] After growth stops, adult energy is needed for the maintenance and repair of tissue, not for new growth.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.b] Physical activity is the most variable part of daily energy use, so it is the factor an individual can change most easily.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.0] The energy used in activity depends on the type, intensity, duration and frequency of the activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.1] Occupational activity: a manual worker, such as a builder or farm worker, or an athlete uses far more energy than someone in a desk job such as an office worker.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.2] Leisure activity: walking, cycling, gardening and playing sport add to daily energy use.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.3] Sedentary lifestyle (little activity and much sitting) means low energy output, so less food energy is needed to meet the body's needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.b] A person's daily energy requirement is more than their BMR. It adds the energy for activity and for dealing with food, and then rises or falls with circumstances such as climate, pregnancy and health.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.0] Total daily energy use is made up of BMR, plus the energy for physical activity, plus the small amount used to digest, absorb and process food (the thermic effect of food). BMR on its own is not the total requirement.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.1] Occupation: manual and athletic work increases the requirement, while sedentary work lowers it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.2] Climate: in cold conditions the body burns more energy to keep its temperature stable, partly through the muscular work of shivering, so energy needs are higher than in a warm climate.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.3] Pregnancy: extra energy is needed for the growth of the baby and the placenta and for the mother's added tissue, especially in the later months.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.4] Lactation: extra energy is needed to make breast milk.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.5] Health: fever, illness, burns and recovery from injury or surgery raise energy needs. Prolonged bed rest lowers the activity part of the requirement.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.6] The factors examiners list are age, gender, body size or weight, occupation, level of physical activity, climate, pregnancy, lactation, health or illness, growth spurts, sedentary lifestyle and BMR.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.7] When a person is described, such as a teenager who plays sport, a builder, an office worker, a pregnant woman or an older retired person, name the factor, say whether it raises or lowers the requirement, and give the reason.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s4: 4. Dietary guidelines
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.b] Dietary guidelines are official advice, based on scientific evidence, on the types and amounts of food that keep a population healthy. They turn nutrition science into practical advice for the general healthy population, and are revised as evidence changes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.0] Evidence comes from nutrition research, national food consumption surveys (which record what people actually eat) and health data on diet-related diseases.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.1] Expert committees review this evidence and turn it into practical advice on foods rather than nutrients, setting the types of food to eat and the amounts to limit. The guidelines are reviewed as new evidence appears.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.2] The Department of Health publishes the healthy eating guidelines for Ireland and the Food Pyramid, which is one of the tools used to explain them (the current pyramid is called 'Healthy Food for Life', 2016).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.3] The HSE supports the guidelines, and the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) and safefood are other Irish bodies in this field.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.4] The guidelines are written for the general healthy population, so they are not a substitute for advice for individuals with special medical needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.5] The aims are to make sure people get enough nutrients, to prevent deficiency, and to reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, some cancers and osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.6] One consistent message also helps consumers, schools and food planners, because everyone is working from the same advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.b] The Irish guidelines can be given as about eight principles. Together they say which foods to eat most, which to eat in moderation and which to keep small.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.0] Eat plenty of vegetables, salad and fruit: about five to seven portions a day, spread across meals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.1] Base meals on starchy foods, choosing wholegrain bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.2] Include protein from lean meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans, lentils and nuts, with fish eaten regularly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.3] Choose milk, yoghurt and cheese in lower-fat forms, as they supply calcium and HBV protein (high biological value protein, which contains all the essential amino acids).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.4] Use fats and oils sparingly, and prefer unsaturated oils such as rapeseed and olive oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.5] Limit foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, which are not needed for health and should be occasional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.6] Drink water as the main drink, and keep alcohol within recommended limits.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.7] Eat regular meals and prepare food with methods that add little fat, such as steaming, grilling and baking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.b] Each guideline is linked to a health benefit or a health risk. Examiners want the guideline and its reason together.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.0] Fruit and vegetables supply fibre, vitamins, minerals and antioxidants (substances that protect cells from damage), which protect against chronic disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.1] Wholegrain starchy foods give more fibre and nutrients than refined white flour products, and promote bowel health because fibre helps prevent constipation and bowel disorders.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.2] Saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.3] Excess salt is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of stroke and heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.4] Sugar adds energy without nutrients, contributes to obesity, and causes tooth decay.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.b] An RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) is a planning value: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of nearly all healthy people in a group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.0] An RDA is set for each nutrient and differs by age, sex and life stage, such as growth, pregnancy, lactation (breastfeeding) or adolescence.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.1] RDAs are used to plan menus and to check that a diet supplies enough of each nutrient over the day, by comparing the day's nutrient total with the RDA. They also guide food-labelling and supplement standards and institutions such as schools and hospitals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.2] An RDA is a guide for a group; it is not a precise target for an individual.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.3] Intake far above the RDA does not give extra benefit and can be harmful for some nutrients, so supplements should be used with care.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.4] Nutrients that are often low in Irish diets, such as iron and calcium, are the ones to check against the RDA when planning a menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.b] A food composition table is a reference book of food values. It lets you turn a menu into numbers, so you can check it against the RDAs and the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.0] A food composition table gives the nutrient and energy content of a food, usually per 100 g of edible food. Irish tables are produced from national food analysis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.1] Tables state whether values are for raw or cooked food, so the matching version must be used because cooking changes the nutrient content.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.2] Method: for each food, work out the grams eaten, divide by 100, then multiply by the value per 100 g.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.3] Example (sample data): 150 g of a food with 3 g fibre per 100 g supplies 150 / 100 x 3 = 4.5 g fibre.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.4] Add the results for all foods in the menu to give the day's total for each nutrient and for energy, then compare the total with the RDA. Where a total is low or high, adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.b] A computer nutrition program does the same job as a food composition table, but automatically. It is quick to use, though it is only as good as the information put in.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.0] Nutrition programs contain a built-in food composition database; the user enters each food and the quantity eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.1] The program totals the energy and nutrients for the day and compares them with the guidelines or RDAs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.2] Check that the portion size entered matches the food as eaten, and that the correct cooking method is selected, because the results depend on correct portions and the right cooking state.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.3] A program shows which nutrients are low or high, which helps the planner adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.4] Advantages are that it is fast and accurate and holds many foods and recipes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.b] The Food Pyramid turns the healthy eating guidelines into the types and relative amounts of food needed each day. Foods with similar nutrients are grouped on shelves, with the base shelf eaten most and the top shelf least.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.0] The current Irish pyramid has six shelves. At the base are vegetables, salad and fruit, with wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice above them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.1] Next come milk, yogurt and cheese, then meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (including lean choices), then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, to be used sparingly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.2] The top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt. These are not needed for health, have no recommended servings, and should be occasional. Water is the main drink.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.3] Servings: five to seven servings of vegetables, salad and fruit a day, three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese (five for ages 9 to 18), and three to five servings of wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.4] The number and size of servings from the other shelves are adjusted for age, sex, health, activity level and life stage, for example children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.5] To plan meals, take most foods from the large lower shelves, some from the middle and very little from the top, and vary the foods within each shelf. Include vegetables or fruit at every meal, and choose wholemeal and lower-fat versions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.6] Spread the choices across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks so the day covers every shelf and matches the person's portions. This supports energy balance and prevents both deficiency and excess.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.7] The pyramid guides what to eat and roughly how much, not an exact diet, and it does not replace advice for medical conditions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.8] Diagram: draw the Food Pyramid, label each shelf with the main foods on it, and mark the base shelf and the top shelf.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.b] A serving (portion) is a standard measure of food used to plan and count intake against the pyramid. It makes diets easy to compare, because everyone counts in the same units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.0] A portion is a standard amount of food used for planning, counting and comparing diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.1] One piece of fruit, such as an apple, or one serving of vegetables counts as one portion of fruit or vegetables.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.2] One glass of milk counts as one portion of milk, yoghurt or cheese in the dairy group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.3] Portion size should be adjusted to the person's age and activity: teenagers and active adults need larger portions from the starchy foods shelf than older, less active adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.4] Serving sizes and numbers rise for growing teenagers and active people and fall for older, less active adults. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra servings of some groups.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.b] Food supplements are concentrates of important nutrients. They can fill a gap in a diet, but they cannot do everything that food does.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.0] Food supplements are concentrates of nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, omega-3 fatty acids (polyunsaturated fatty acids) and amino acids, sold in tablet, capsule or powder form.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.1] They may be used to fill gaps where diet is restricted, where allergies or intolerances exclude foods, or where a person has special needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.2] Folic acid prevents neural tube defects (defects of the baby's brain and spine) in a developing baby when taken before conception and in early pregnancy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.3] Iron supplements can treat anaemia caused by low iron intake, under medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.4] Older people may absorb nutrients less efficiently, so they may need supplements, particularly vitamin D.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.5] Their popularity is growing for reasons such as busy lifestyles, poor diets, media and advertising, a wish to prevent illness, special needs (vegans, restricted diets, illness), sports performance and convenience.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.6] Supplements cannot replace a varied diet, because food supplies fibre and other nutrients that supplements do not contain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.7] High doses of some vitamins and minerals can be harmful, especially fat-soluble vitamins, so supplements should not be taken above the recommended levels. They can also cost a lot, so take medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.b] Healthy eating is only part of staying healthy. The lifestyle guidelines cover activity, mental wellbeing and habits that harm the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.0] Regular physical activity: build activity into daily life, such as brisk walking, cycling, sport or active housework, and include muscle-strengthening and weight-bearing exercise (which loads the bones, such as walking or running).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.1] Physical activity improves cardiovascular fitness, maintains muscle and bone, supports weight control and mental wellbeing, and may raise protective HDL cholesterol (the protective form of cholesterol).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.2] Mental health: get adequate sleep and rest, keep a realistic workload, and use stress management, relaxation and social contact.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.3] Avoid tobacco and second-hand smoke, which damage the lungs and blood vessels and greatly raise the risk of cancer and coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.4] Limit alcohol: it supplies empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrients), harms the liver and judgement, and must never be used to manage stress.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.5] Drink plenty of water and keep a healthy body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.6] Regular health checks and good personal hygiene complete the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.7] Together these habits reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension (high blood pressure) and heart disease.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s4: 4. Mineral elements
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.b] Minerals are the part of food that is not made by living things at all. The body needs them in small amounts to build structures, run processes and make enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.0] Minerals are inorganic elements (not made by living things) that the body cannot make, so they must be obtained from food. They build body structures, regulate body processes and form parts of enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.1] Major (macro) minerals are needed in larger amounts, above 100 mg a day. They are calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium and sulphur.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.2] Minerals are not destroyed by cooking, because they are elements. They can, however, be lost into cooking water and in the trimming of food. For example, boiling vegetables in a lot of water washes minerals out.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily amount) is the daily intake that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, so some groups need more than others.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.4] Phosphorus works with calcium to harden bones and teeth and forms part of ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and cell membranes. Sources are milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs, wholegrains and nuts, and deficiency is rare.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.b] Calcium is the most important mineral for the skeleton, but it also keeps blood, muscles and nerves working. Because bone is built in childhood and lost in later life, some groups need more of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.0] Milk, cheese and yoghurt are the richest dietary sources of calcium. This is why dairy foods matter for bone health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.1] Canned sardines and canned salmon eaten with their bones, green leafy vegetables such as kale, broccoli and cabbage, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks and fortified breakfast cereals are further sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.2] Calcium works with phosphorus to form the mineral salts that give bones and teeth their hardness. This is why a diet low in calcium leaves bones weak.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.3] Calcium is needed for blood clotting, normal muscle contraction (including the heartbeat) and nerve impulse transmission. It also activates some enzymes and helps regulate blood pressure.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.4] Rickets in children (soft, bent bones and poor teeth) and osteomalacia in adults result from too little calcium, phosphorus or vitamin D.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.5] Osteoporosis, which develops over years, makes bones thin, porous, brittle and prone to fracture, with loss of height and a stooped back. Other effects of low calcium are tooth decay, slow clotting, and muscle cramps (tetany) and poor nerve function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.6] Osteoporosis risk is raised by low calcium and vitamin D intake, lack of weight-bearing exercise, smoking, alcohol, the fall in oestrogen after menopause and family history.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.7] Need is highest when bone is being built (childhood, teenage years, pregnancy, breastfeeding) and when it is lost (women after menopause). Bone density peaks in the late teens and twenties, so teenage intake matters.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.b] Iron is needed to carry oxygen, so a shortage leaves the whole body short of energy. It comes in two forms, and the form decides how easily the body absorbs it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.0] Haem iron comes from animal foods and is absorbed easily. Sources are red meat, liver and offal, kidney, black pudding, poultry and oily fish.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.1] Non-haem iron comes from plant foods and eggs and is absorbed less easily. Sources are eggs, wholegrain and fortified cereals, wholemeal bread, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried apricots and other dried fruit, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.2] Iron is an essential part of haemoglobin, the red protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs around the body to all cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.3] Iron is part of myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle cells, and part of enzymes that release energy from food. It also supports immunity and concentration. This is why a shortage leaves people tired.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.4] Iron deficiency causes anaemia (too few red blood cells or too little haemoglobin). Effects are tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, dizziness, irritability, poor concentration, feeling cold and reduced resistance to infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.5] Causes of iron deficiency are a diet low in iron or poor in vitamin C, blood loss (menstruation, ulcers, operations), high needs in growth spurts, pregnancy and infancy, and poor absorption.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.6] Corrective measures are eating more iron-rich foods together with vitamin C, avoiding tea with meals, and taking an iron supplement on medical advice. This is because vitamin C improves absorption and tea blocks it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.7] Groups at risk are teenage girls, menstruating and pregnant women, infants and toddlers, people with heavy blood loss, and vegetarians and vegans, who eat only non-haem iron. Children are at risk because of fast growth and low-iron foods. For example, vegetarians miss out on the more easily absorbed haem form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.b] Trace minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, yet the body cannot do without them. Each has a job that no other mineral can do.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.0] Trace (micro) minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms a day, and the diet must supply them. They are tiny in amount yet essential. For example, zinc and iodine are needed in milligrams or micrograms.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.1] The trace minerals are iron, zinc, iodine, fluoride, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium and cobalt. Iron is a trace mineral by the amount needed, but it is studied in detail at Ordinary Level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.2] Iron, zinc and iodine each play a specific role in the body that the other minerals cannot replace. Zinc and iodine are set out in the blocks that follow.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.b] Zinc is a trace mineral that works inside enzymes, so it affects growth, healing and defence against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.0] Zinc sources include oysters and other shellfish, red meat, poultry, liver, dairy products, eggs, wholegrain cereals, pulses, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.1] Zinc is a component of many enzymes and supports growth and cell division, which is why a shortage slows growth in children.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.2] Zinc supports wound healing and a healthy immune system. This is because new cells must be made to repair the wound.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.3] Zinc contributes to a normal sense of taste and smell, and to healthy skin and hair and reproductive health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.4] Deficiency causes poor growth, delayed wound healing, dry flaky skin and rashes, loss of taste and appetite, reduced immunity and hair loss.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.b] Iodine is needed to make a hormone that sets the body's pace. Without it the thyroid gland cannot do its job.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.0] Iodine sources include sea fish and shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy products and eggs. Iodised salt also exists as a source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.1] Iodine is part of thyroxine, the hormone from the thyroid gland that controls the rate of metabolism (and growth and brain development).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.2] Deficiency causes goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck as it works harder to make more thyroxine. This is because the gland enlarges as it works harder.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.3] Iodine deficiency causes tiredness and sluggishness (lethargy) and weight gain in adults. It causes delayed mental and physical development in children, and in babies of deficient mothers.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.b] Potassium and sodium work as a pair, one inside cells and one outside, to keep fluid, nerves and muscles balanced.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.0] Potassium sources include bananas, potatoes eaten in their skins, tomatoes, dried fruit, orange juice, pulses, green and leafy vegetables, milk, meat, fish and nuts.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.1] Potassium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) inside cells and, with sodium, helps regulate fluid balance in and around cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.2] Potassium is needed to transmit nerve impulses and for muscle contraction, including the heartbeat. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.3] Potassium works with sodium to regulate blood pressure, and a high potassium intake helps lower it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.4] Deficiency (hypokalaemia) causes muscle weakness, cramps, an irregular heartbeat and tiredness. It is usually caused by severe vomiting, diarrhoea or some medicines rather than by normal diets.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.b] Sodium is essential, and the problem in Ireland is eating too much of it, not too little. Most of it comes from salt, which is sodium chloride.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.0] Sodium is mainly consumed as salt. Table salt, cured and smoked meats and fish such as bacon and ham, cheese, bread, crisps and snack foods, processed and ready meals, sauces, stock cubes and canned foods supply most of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.1] Fresh, unprocessed foods contain only small amounts of naturally occurring sodium. This is why processed foods, such as bacon and ready meals, are the main source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.2] Sodium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) outside cells and, with potassium, regulates fluid balance and blood volume.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.3] Sodium is needed to transmit nerve impulses, for muscle contraction and for enzyme function. It helps the small intestine absorb glucose and some amino acids. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.4] Excess sodium raises blood pressure (hypertension), which increases the risk of stroke, heart disease and kidney problems. Most people in Ireland eat more salt than advised, mostly from processed foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.5] To cut salt, cook from fresh food, do not add salt at the table, read labels (salt is on the nutrition declaration) and choose low-salt products.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.6] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) began a voluntary Salt Reduction Programme in 2003 to cut salt in processed food. Later reformulation work continues under the FSAI-Healthy Ireland Food Reformulation Task Force.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.7] Deficiency is uncommon but causes muscle cramps, headache, nausea and confusion. It can follow heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s2: 2. Water-soluble vitamins C, B12 and folate
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.b] Vitamin C, vitamin B12 and folate are water-soluble vitamins, so they behave differently from vitamins that dissolve in fat. Knowing this explains why they are needed every day and why cooking can ruin them.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.0] Water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C) dissolve in water and are not stored in useful amounts in the body. Any excess is lost in urine, so they are needed regularly in the diet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.1] The exception is vitamin B12, which the liver stores, so a store builds up that can last for several years.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.2] Because they dissolve in water, these vitamins are lost into cooking water and are easily destroyed during storage, preparation and cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily allowance) is the daily amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It varies with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and smokers are advised more vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.b] Vitamin C is the most-asked vitamin in the exam, and it comes mainly from fruit and vegetables. Fresh, raw and in-season produce has the most.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.0] Citrus fruit (oranges, lemons, grapefruit), blackcurrants, rosehips, kiwi fruit and strawberries are rich sources of vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.1] Peppers, broccoli, sprouts, cabbage, tomatoes, new potatoes and peas are good vegetable sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.2] Fresh fruit juices and smoothies, and fortified drinks, also supply vitamin C, and a supplement can too. Any fruit or vegetable is accepted as a source in an answer.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.b] Vitamin C does several different jobs in the body, and exam questions usually ask for two or three of them. The key one is collagen, because so many other effects follow from it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.0] Vitamin C is needed to make collagen, the protein of connective tissue that gives strength to skin, blood vessels, gums, cartilage, bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.1] By supporting collagen formation, vitamin C speeds up the healing of wounds, because new tissue can be built.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.2] Vitamin C aids iron absorption. It converts non-haem iron (the iron in plant foods) from the ferric form (Fe³⁺) to the ferrous form (Fe²⁺), which is absorbed more readily.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.3] Vitamin C also aids calcium absorption, which marking schemes accept.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.4] Vitamin C is an antioxidant: it neutralises free radicals (damaging particles) and so protects cells from damage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.5] Vitamin C supports the immune system, helping the body resist infection, and it prevents scurvy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.b] If the diet lacks vitamin C for a long time, collagen cannot be made properly and the body's tissues start to fail. The deficiency disease is scurvy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.0] Scurvy develops after a severe and prolonged lack of vitamin C in the diet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.1] Weak collagen causes bleeding, sore and swollen gums, loose teeth, easy bruising and painful joints.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.2] Wounds heal slowly because new tissue cannot be formed properly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.3] Reduced iron absorption can lead to anaemia, with tiredness and pale skin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.4] The body becomes more susceptible to infection because its resistance is lowered.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.b] Vitamin C is easily lost, but the loss depends on method and time. Cooking does not destroy all of it: steaming, microwaving and short cooking keep most.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.0] Buy fresh produce and use it quickly, and eat it raw where possible.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.1] Wash vegetables before cutting rather than soaking them, prepare them just before cooking and cut them in large pieces, so less of the surface is exposed.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.2] Cook in a little boiling water for a short time with the lid on, or steam, microwave or stir-fry, so less vitamin C dissolves or is destroyed.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.3] Do not use bicarbonate of soda or copper pans, which destroy the vitamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.4] Serve at once rather than keeping the food hot, and use the cooking water in soups and sauces to recover the vitamin that dissolved into it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.b] Ascorbic acid is a very unstable vitamin, and its properties explain every rule for keeping it in food. Higher Level answers on vitamin C add these properties to sources, functions and deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.0] Vitamin C is a white, crystalline, acidic substance with a sweet-sour taste, and it is very soluble in water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.1] Because it is water-soluble, vitamin C is lost into cooking water when vegetables are boiled or soaked.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.2] Vitamin C is destroyed by prolonged heat, by light, by alkalis such as bicarbonate of soda, and by oxidation in air.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.3] The enzyme oxidase is released when plant cells are cut or bruised and destroys vitamin C, so cut vegetables should be cooked promptly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.4] Copper and brass pans destroy vitamin C, and it is also lost during long storage and dehydration.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.5] Vitamin C is an antioxidant, which means it acts as a reducing agent (it donates electrons), and this is how it changes ferric iron to ferrous iron.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.b] Vitamin B12 is unusual because only animal foods contain it naturally. Its absorption also needs a helper made in the stomach.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.0] Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods: liver and kidney, meat, fish and oily fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.1] Plant foods contain no natural vitamin B12, so vegans rely on fortified foods such as some breakfast cereals, plant milks and yeast extract, or on supplements.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.2] The lining of the stomach makes intrinsic factor, a protein that binds vitamin B12 so that it can be absorbed in the ileum (the end of the small intestine).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.3] The liver stores vitamin B12, and the store can last for several years, so deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.b] B12 is needed wherever cells are being made or nerves are working, which is why its deficiency affects both the blood and the nervous system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.0] Vitamin B12 works with folate to form healthy red blood cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.1] Vitamin B12 is needed for the synthesis of DNA, so it is needed for cell division, especially in cells that divide rapidly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.2] Vitamin B12 maintains the myelin sheath, the fatty layer that insulates nerve fibres and keeps nerve impulses working normally, so it keeps nerve cells healthy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.3] Vitamin B12 is also involved in the body's use of fatty acids and amino acids.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.b] A lack of B12 harms both the blood and the nerves. The cause can be the diet or a failure to absorb the vitamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.0] B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia, in which red blood cells are too large and immature and cannot carry oxygen effectively.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.1] Symptoms of this anaemia include tiredness, weakness, pale skin and breathlessness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.2] Nerve damage causes numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, poor balance and memory problems.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.3] A strict vegan diet without fortified foods or supplements can cause deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.4] Pernicious anaemia is the form of B12 deficiency caused by a lack of intrinsic factor, often through an autoimmune condition, which prevents B12 absorption even when the diet is adequate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.5] Failure to absorb B12 can also come from stomach or gut disease, and older people absorb less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.b] B12 is more stable than vitamin C, but it is not indestructible.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.0] Vitamin B12 is water-soluble, so some is lost into cooking water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.1] Vitamin B12 is a red crystalline compound that contains the mineral cobalt, which gives it the name cobalamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.2] Vitamin B12 is stable to heat in ordinary cooking but is destroyed by light and by strong acids and alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.b] Folate is the natural form of vitamin B9, and folic acid is the synthetic form. Folate is needed for making new cells, which matters most in early pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.0] Natural folate is found in dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, sprouts and cabbage, in pulses such as chickpeas, lentils and beans, and in asparagus, oranges, liver and yeast extract.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.1] Wholegrain and fortified breakfast cereals also supply folate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.2] Folic acid is the synthetic form used in supplements and in fortified foods such as breakfast cereals. It is not a different vitamin from folate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.3] Folate is needed to synthesise DNA and RNA, so it is essential for cell division and growth, including the growth of the foetus.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.4] Folate works with vitamin B12 to form red blood cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.5] In the first weeks of pregnancy, folate is essential for the formation of the neural tube, the early brain and spinal cord, and it protects against neural tube defects such as spina bifida and anencephaly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.b] The neural tube forms in the first weeks of pregnancy, before many women know they are pregnant, so folate advice is aimed at women before they conceive.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.0] A lack of folate causes megaloblastic anaemia, with the same large, immature red blood cells as B12 deficiency, and tiredness, weakness, pale skin, breathlessness and a sore tongue.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.1] Neural tube defects, such as spina bifida and anencephaly, can develop in the unborn baby if the mother lacks folate in early pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.2] Poor growth is also an effect of folate deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.3] Women who may become pregnant are advised, in HSE advice, to take a folic acid supplement before conception and during early pregnancy to reduce the risk of neural tube defects, as well as eating folate-rich foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.b] Folate is fragile in the kitchen, which is why fortified foods use the more stable synthetic form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.0] Folate is water-soluble and is sensitive to heat, light and oxygen, so it is destroyed by prolonged cooking and storage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.1] Folate is lost into cooking water during prolonged boiling.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.2] Synthetic folic acid is more stable than natural folate, which is why it is used in fortification.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s6: 6. Water
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.b] Water looks plain, but a handful of its physical and chemical properties explain why the body depends on it. Each property below links to a job water does in the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.0] Pure water is colourless, odourless and tasteless.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.1] Water boils at 100 °C and freezes at 0 °C at normal atmospheric pressure. Above 100 °C it changes to steam.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.2] Water is a good solvent (a liquid that other substances dissolve in): it dissolves many minerals, sugars, water-soluble vitamins and gases, which is why it carries nutrients and wastes around the body. It is also why water-soluble vitamins and minerals can be lost into cooking water.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.3] Water absorbs and retains heat well: it takes a lot of heat to warm it and it cools slowly, so it helps keep body temperature steady.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.4] Water is neutral, with a pH of 7 for pure water, meaning it is neither acid nor alkaline.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.5] Ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats; water expands when it freezes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.6] Hard water contains dissolved calcium and magnesium salts, while soft water has little. Hard water is therefore a useful source of calcium in the diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.0.7] Water has no energy value and contains no protein, fat or vitamins of its own, so it is not a source of energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.b] Water is a major component of the body and is needed in every body cell and tissue fluid. Because it dissolves so many substances, it is also the body's main transport medium.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.0] Water is an essential component of all body cells.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.1] Water is the main part of blood plasma, lymph and the other body tissue fluids, as well as urine and sweat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.2] Water is a part of saliva, digestive juices and mucus.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.3] Water lubricates joints and membranes, such as those around the lungs and digestive tract, and the fluid it forms cushions organs, for example around the brain and spinal cord.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.4] Water carries nutrients, oxygen, carbon dioxide, hormones and enzymes around the body in the blood and tissue fluids.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.5] Because water dissolves these substances, they can move from the blood into the cells that need them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.1.6] Water is the medium in which the chemical reactions of the cells (metabolism) take place.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.2.b] The body makes heat all the time and must keep its core temperature steady. Water does this in two ways: it spreads heat around and it carries heat away as sweat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.2.0] Water absorbs heat from the body when it evaporates, so sweating cools the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.2.1] Water in the blood and tissues helps spread heat evenly, keeping the internal body temperature near 37 °C.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.2.2] When the body is hot, more sweat is produced; when cold, less water is lost from the skin, so less heat is carried away.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.3.b] Cells produce waste that would poison the body if it built up. Water carries these wastes out, mainly through the kidneys.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.3.0] The kidneys filter the blood, and water carries waste products such as urea out of the body in urine.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.3.1] Water is also lost in faeces, sweat and breath, and this loss must be replaced by drinks and food.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.3.2] Adequate water intake keeps urine dilute, so the kidneys can remove waste efficiently.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.4.b] Digestion breaks food into molecules small enough to be absorbed, and water takes part in the process as well as helping food move along.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.4.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of large food molecules into smaller ones by adding water, and it is needed for the digestion of carbohydrate, protein and fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.4.1] Water in saliva and digestive juices softens food, so it is easier to swallow and digest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.4.2] Water keeps faeces soft, so it prevents constipation, especially when fibre intake is high.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.5.b] Tap and bottled water carry dissolved minerals, so water makes a small contribution to the diet beyond hydration.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.5.0] Water supplies small amounts of calcium, fluoride and magnesium.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.5.1] Hard water contains more calcium than soft water, so it is a useful source of calcium in the diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.5.2] Fluoride from water helps to strengthen tooth enamel and protect teeth from decay.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.5.3] Public water supplies in Ireland are fluoridated under the Health (Fluoridation of Water Supplies) Act 1960.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.b] The body loses water every day and must replace it. Thirst is the first warning that it is falling behind, and dehydration is what happens when it does.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.0] Thirst is the body's signal that its water content has fallen; quenching thirst is one function of water.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.1] Dehydration occurs when water losses are greater than intake. Causes include heat, exercise, fever, vomiting, diarrhoea and not drinking enough.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.2] Signs of dehydration are thirst, dark and small amounts of urine, headache, tiredness, dizziness and a dry mouth.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.3] Severe dehydration can cause confusion, low blood pressure and collapse (shock), so fluids must be replaced promptly and urgent medical help is needed.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.4] Water is the main drink in the diet, and foods such as fruit and vegetables, and soups, also supply water. Water is the main drink advised in the healthy eating guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.6.5] Factors that increase water needs include hot weather, exercise, fever, vomiting, diarrhoea, pregnancy, breastfeeding and high-fibre or high-protein diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.7.b] Many drinks add energy to the diet but water adds none, which is why it is the best choice for staying hydrated.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.7.0] Water supplies no energy, so it can be drunk freely without adding to energy intake.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.7.1] Sugary drinks supply energy as empty kilocalories (energy with no useful nutrients), so plain water is the best choice for hydration.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.8.b] Pregnancy raises the body's demand for water, because the mother must supply the growing baby as well as herself.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.8.0] The blood volume of a pregnant woman increases, and water is needed to carry nutrients and oxygen to the foetus through the placenta, and to carry waste away.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.8.1] Amniotic fluid, which surrounds the baby in the uterus and is mostly water, cushions and protects the developing baby and keeps its temperature steady.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.8.2] Water helps to prevent constipation, which is common in pregnancy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.8.3] Breastfeeding women need extra water, because breast milk is mostly water and is produced in large amounts.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.9.b] Exercise makes the body hot and the body cools itself by sweating, so active people lose water quickly and must replace it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.9.0] Exercise raises heat production; sweating cools the body, but sweat contains water and salts, including sodium and potassium, so these are lost too.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.9.1] Water losses are replaced by drinking before, during and after activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.9.2] Dehydration during sport reduces stamina, strength, concentration and coordination, and raises the risk of cramps, heat exhaustion and heat stroke.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.9.3] Drinking regularly in small amounts during prolonged or intense exercise is more effective than drinking a large amount at once.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s6.9.4] Water is enough for most activity. For prolonged or very intense exercise, isotonic sports drinks containing sodium, potassium and sugar replace the salts and energy lost, but they are not needed for ordinary activity and add empty kilocalories if used unnecessarily.
