TARGET hom-work-leisure-s6: 6. Child care
[hom-work-leisure-s6.0.b] Choosing child care starts with the family, not the service. A family's working pattern, support network, budget and school calendar decide which options are even possible.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.0.0] Working pattern: whether both parents, or a lone parent, are in paid work, and how many hours each works. Shift work or irregular hours need a more flexible carer than a regular nine-to-five job.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.0.1] Support network: whether grandparents, other relatives or friends are available and willing to mind the child. If they are, the family may need little or no paid care.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.0.2] Cost: what the family can afford to pay, which may limit the options available. Some families qualify for a State subsidy towards the cost of registered child care.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.0.3] After-school and holiday cover: school closing times and school holidays create gaps that the family must fill, because school usually finishes before work does and the holidays are long.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.b] Once the family's situation is clear, it must be matched to what a service can actually offer. These requirements test whether an option is practical, reliable and right for the child.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.0] Availability and convenience: the option must have a place free and be open at the times needed, and be reachable without a long journey.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.1] Hours and flexibility: the option must cover the parents' hours, including early starts, late finishes and changing shifts.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.2] Proximity: a service near home, school or work reduces travel time for the child and the parent.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.3] Child's age or special needs: the option must suit the child's stage of development and any additional care needs, including health needs.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.4] Reliability: the option must stay open during staff illness and closures, so that the parent is not left without cover if the carer is ill or the service closes.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.1.5] Quality: safety and hygiene, staff qualifications, the activities provided and the ratio of staff to children all matter, as do the parents' trust in the carer and whether the child is happy there.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.b] Some options care for children for the whole working day. They differ in who the carer is, where the care happens and who employs them.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.0] Crèche or day nursery: a centre with trained staff that cares for children in groups during the working day, usually open all day, in premises used for that purpose, with structured routines and play.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.1] Childminder: an adult who minds one or a small number of children in the minder's own home. The setting is a home, the attention is personal and the hours are often flexible. A childminder is self-employed.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.2] Nanny: a carer employed by the family, who usually looks after the children of that one family in the family home. The family is the employer.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.3] Au pair: a young person, often from abroad, who lives with the host family and provides childcare and does light household duties in exchange for board and pocket money. An au pair is not a trained carer or nanny, works limited hours and suits older children or extra support.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.4] Relative or friend: a grandparent, aunt, uncle or friend who minds the child informally. This is usually low cost or free and familiar and trusted, but it depends on the helper's health and availability.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.2.5] Employer or workplace crèche: care provided at or near the workplace by the employer, which saves the parent a separate journey.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.3.b] Other options fill part of the day. After-school groups cover the gap when school ends, while pre-school services give younger children a few hours of play and learning.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.3.0] After-school group: a service that collects children from school and cares for them until a parent finishes work. Some also run holiday or summer camps and may offer homework help and activities.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.3.1] Playschool or playgroup: a sessional service (one that runs in set sessions) for pre-school children, mainly play-based, usually for a few hours in the morning or afternoon.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.3.2] Montessori: a pre-school method, following the educational ideas of Maria Montessori, that encourages self-directed learning through structured materials and activities.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.3.3] Naíonra: an Irish-medium pre-school (naíonraí is the plural), where the children learn through the Irish language by play. Naíonraí are supported by Forbairt Naíonraí.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.3.4] Pre-school under the national free pre-school scheme (ECCE): State-funded pre-school hours in the year or years before primary school, in registered services, so the family does not pay for the funded sessions.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.4.b] Parents hand their children to carers they do not supervise, so the State regulates child care. Knowing who registers and inspects a service tells parents what to check before they choose.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.4.0] Early years services (crèches, playschools, Montessori schools, naíonraí and after-school services) must be registered with Tusla, the Child and Family Agency. Tusla's early years inspectorate inspects them under the Child Care Act 1991 (Early Years Services) Regulations 2016.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.4.1] This is why parents should check that a service is registered and read its inspection reports. Staff-to-child ratios are set by regulation.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.4.2] Childminders: the Child Care (Amendment) Act 2024 and the Child Care Act 1991 (Early Years Services) (Childminding Services) Regulations 2024 bring childminders into Tusla registration. Registration opened on 30 September 2024 and is currently voluntary, with mandatory registration due after a transition period.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.4.3] Registered childminders can take part in the National Childcare Scheme. Parents should ask whether a childminder is registered, has training, Garda vetting (a police check), references, insurance and emergency cover.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.4.4] The National Childcare Scheme (NCS) is the State's scheme that subsidises the cost of registered child care according to the family's circumstances.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.b] Pre-school is more than minding children while parents work. It is the first place many children learn to mix with others and begin to learn, which prepares them for primary school.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.0] Children become more socially aware by playing and interacting with other children.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.1] Children form relationships outside the home and learn to share and take turns.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.2] Children become more independent and confident when they are cared for away from their parents.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.3] Intellectual development is supported through activities that build language, number, listening, problem-solving and creativity skills, which also prepares children for primary school.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.4] Physical development is supported through games and active play, which build coordination and fine motor skills (the small, precise movements of hands and fingers).
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.5] Emotional development: children learn to separate from their parents, which builds self-esteem.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.5.6] Pre-school also helps working parents, because the child is cared for in a safe, structured setting.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.b] A crèche suits parents with regular hours who want trained staff and structure. Its strengths come from being a larger, organised centre, and its weaknesses come from the same thing.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.0] Advantages: fixed daily opening hours, which suit parents with regular working patterns and make the service reliable.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.1] Advantages: several staff care for a group of children, so cover is available when one staff member is absent.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.2] Advantages: structured activities and peer play (play with other children) support the child's social and intellectual development.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.3] Advantages: staff often hold early-childhood qualifications and work to a written programme, and the service is registered and inspected.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.4] Disadvantages: fixed hours give little flexibility when a parent's shift changes at short notice, and travel must happen at set times.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.5] Disadvantages: group care exposes young children to infections, so they may be ill more often.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.6] Disadvantages: fees are often charged by the session or by the week, and can be a significant cost for the family.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.6.7] Disadvantages: the child gets less individual attention and may be unsettled by a large group.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.b] A childminder offers a small, home-like setting, which many parents prefer for babies and young children. The trade-off is that everything depends on one person.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.0] Advantages: care is given in a home setting with a small group, which suits very young children and babies.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.1] Advantages: childminders are often able to offer flexible or extended hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.2] Advantages: the child receives individual attention and the minder may collect children from school.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.3] Advantages: a childminder is often cheaper for a family with several children.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.4] Disadvantages: the service depends on one adult, so illness, holidays or an emergency can leave the family without cover.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.5] Disadvantages: the range of planned educational activities may be narrower than in a larger centre, and there are fewer facilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.7.6] Disadvantages: quality depends on the minder, so the family must check registration with Tusla, experience, training, vetting, safety and insurance before making a decision.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.b] To judge any option fairly, a family uses the same set of criteria each time. These criteria come straight from the family's requirements.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.0] Cost: the total weekly cost, including fees, extras such as meals or outings, and the cost of travel.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.1] Hours and location: whether the opening hours and the journey time fit the parents' working day.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.2] Hygiene and safety: cleanliness of premises, safe equipment, secure entry and a safety policy.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.3] Staff qualifications and training: the level of early-childhood training held by the staff, and the ratio of staff to children.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.4] Staffing cover: what happens when a staff member is ill or on leave.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.5] Activities and learning: the play, learning and outdoor activities provided for the child's age.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.6] Suitability for the child: whether the option meets any additional needs and whether the child is happy there.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.8.7] Investigating the options in your area: research each option's type, cost, hours, registration and activities, then compare them in a table.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.b] An evaluation compares each option with the family's requirements, then weighs its advantages against its disadvantages. Any two options can be chosen, but a crèche and a childminder is the usual pair.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.0] Step 1: identify the two options and the family requirements they must meet, such as hours, cost, reliability and the child's age.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.1] Step 2: test each option against each requirement, rating how well it meets it.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.2] Step 3: compare the options point by point; a crèche offers fixed hours and cover for absent staff, while a childminder offers flexibility and a home setting.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.3] Step 4: identify which requirement decides the choice; for a family whose hours change often, flexibility may outweigh a crèche's structure.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.4] Step 5: state the conclusion, showing which option suits which family and what the family must check before choosing.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.5] Other pairs work too. An au pair is low cost and flexible in the home but untrained and short-term, while an after-school group is structured and registered but only covers after school.
[hom-work-leisure-s6.9.6] A playschool, Montessori school or naíonra is educational and sociable, but runs only a few hours each day, so another arrangement is needed to cover the working day.

CANDIDATES hom-work-leisure-s5: 5. Reconciling employment with family responsibilities
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.b] Reconciling paid work with family life depends on how a household divides its roles and tasks between its members. Over time, the roles people expect of men and women have changed, and that change shapes how dual-earner families run.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.0] A dual-earner family (also called a dual-career family) is one in which both partners are in paid employment, so the household has two incomes and two sets of work demands.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.1] A role is the set of behaviours and responsibilities expected of a person in a position such as parent, employee, partner or homemaker (the person who runs the home). Sex roles, also called gender roles, are the tasks and behaviour expected of men and women.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.2] Traditional sex roles gave the man the main earning role, as breadwinner, and the woman the main responsibility for childcare and home care, as homemaker.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.3] Sex roles have loosened as more women take up paid work and more men take an active part in childcare and housework, so tasks are increasingly shared rather than fixed by sex.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.4] Fathers can now take paternity leave and parent's leave, and some families have a stay-at-home father, which shows that caring is no longer seen as only a woman's job.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.5] The change is real but not complete: attitudes have changed, yet women on average still do more of the household and caring work.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.6] Children learn sex roles from the way tasks are shared at home, as well as from school, peers and the media, so what they see their parents do shapes what they expect for themselves.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.b] When both partners work, someone still has to do the childcare and the housework. Role overload is what happens when one person is asked to do too many of these jobs at once.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.0] Role overload occurs when one person tries to perform several roles at the same time, for example employee, parent, homemaker and carer, and cannot meet the demands of all of them. It is about too many roles to cope with at once.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.1] Effects on the individual: less leisure and rest time, tiredness, stress and poorer health, with a risk of burnout (complete exhaustion from long-term stress) if the load continues.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.2] Effects on work: difficulty finding time for training, extra hours or promotion, and less energy for them, because the person has little left after the demands of home. There may also be pressure to take work home.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.3] Effects on the family: tension between partners and other family members, less time with children, and neglect of family and household tasks that no one has time to do.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.4] Vulnerable members, such as young children, an elderly relative or a family member who is ill, may have no one available to care for them.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.5] Women often carry a larger share of the strain because they usually remain the main carers at home, so they end up doing a full job and then the home tasks as well.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.6] Overload can be reduced by sharing tasks, using labour-saving appliances, buying in outside help such as a cleaner or childcare, keeping routines simple, planning meals, and using leave and flexible work arrangements.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.b] Role conflict is a different problem from overload. Here a person may be able to manage each role, but two roles pull in opposite directions at the same moment and a choice has to be made.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.0] Role conflict arises when the expectations of one role clash with the expectations of another, so that both cannot be met and one has to give way. It is two roles pulling in different directions.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.1] Example: a child becomes sick and needs a parent at home while a work deadline or meeting requires the same parent to be at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.2] Priority choice: a parent must decide which role to put first at a given moment, and that decision often causes guilt about the role that lost out.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.3] Conflict between partners: one partner may not support the other's employment, or the couple may compete over career priorities, so each feels the other is getting in the way.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.4] Conflict with the employer: an employer may expect availability, such as late meetings or travel, that clashes with school hours and childcare arrangements.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.5] Effects include stress, guilt, and tension and arguments between partners. Employment may suffer through absence or missed promotion.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.6] Outcome for the family: children may feel lonely or neglected, and the family may need outside help to resolve the conflict.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.b] Every household has two sets of jobs: caring for the children and caring for the home. How a dual-earner couple divides them decides whether the family copes or falls into overload and conflict.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.0] Parental responsibilities include feeding, dressing, supervising, school runs and collections, helping with homework, health care, emotional support, discipline and play, and transporting and supporting children.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.1] Home-care responsibilities include cooking, cleaning, laundry, shopping, budgeting, bills, repairs, the garden and maintaining the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.2] Fair sharing: in the modern pattern, partners agree which tasks each one owns, so that tasks are not done only when one partner asks for help. Fathers are more involved in childcare.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.3] Labour-saving appliances, such as washing machines, dishwashers, microwave ovens and slow cookers, reduce the time needed for home-care tasks, which frees time for the family.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.4] Outside help, such as paid childcare, a childminder, cleaning services, or grandparents and other relatives, takes over some parental and home-care duties.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.5] Older children can take on age-appropriate tasks, such as tidying their rooms or setting the table, which builds independence.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.6] Benefits of sharing: less overload, stronger parent-child bonds including father-child bonds, and fairer family relationships. When both parents take an active part in care, children see shared parenting as normal, which models gender equality and shows that men and women have equal roles.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.7] Drawbacks if tasks are not shared or are poorly managed: resentment and arguments, one partner carrying a double shift (a full day of paid work followed by the home tasks), and children given too much responsibility.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.b] Having two earners changes family life in both good and bad ways. The balance depends on how tasks are shared, how childcare is arranged and how flexible the employers are.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.0] Positive: a second income raises household living standards and reduces financial worry, and it gives security if one job is lost.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.1] Positive: both partners gain independence and self-esteem, and parents gain wider social contact through work.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.2] Positive: children may become more independent when they take on responsibilities, and they see their parents working, which gives them a role model for work and equal roles.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.3] Positive: when parents share tasks, children learn that care and housework are shared responsibilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.4] Negative: less time for shared family activities, conversation and leisure, and tiredness and stress, which can weaken family relationships. The couple may also have less time for each other.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.5] Negative: children may feel lonely, neglected or left out, especially at times such as after school or when they are ill.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.6] Negative: extra responsibility falls on older children, who may become carers for younger siblings. Less time for home tasks can also mean meals eaten out or convenience foods.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.7] Negative: childcare costs can absorb a large part of the second income, and the family may face role overload and role conflict.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.b] Employers and employees use working arrangements that give more control over when and where work is done, and families add supports at home. Together they make it easier to hold a job and care for a family.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.0] Flexitime: the employee chooses start and finish times within agreed limits, so that the working day can fit around school hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.1] Job-sharing: two employees share one full-time post, dividing the hours and responsibilities between them.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.2] Parental leave: unpaid leave taken to care for a child, which may be taken in blocks or spread over an agreed period. Under the Parental Leave Act 1998 (as amended) it is 26 weeks unpaid leave per child under 12.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.3] Other leave arrangements are provided under the Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004, the Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016, the Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019 and the Carer's Leave Act 2001, and force majeure leave covers family emergencies.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.4] Career break: a temporary period of unpaid leave that allows the employee to return to the same job later.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.5] Teleworking: working from home reduces commuting time and lets a parent be available around the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.6] Part-time and term-time work: reduced hours allow the employee to be at home during school hours or school holidays. Compressed working week: longer working days over fewer days free up time for family commitments.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.7] At home and at work: help from childcare, after-school care and relatives, sharing housework, labour-saving appliances, planning such as meal planning, shopping lists and online shopping, and family meetings to share the load. Employers can help with flexible hours, crèche facilities and understanding about family emergencies.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.b] A class survey shows how real households divide tasks and whether traditional sex roles still hold. This investigation uses a questionnaire about twenty home tasks.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.0] Aim: to find out how male and female family members share twenty named home tasks in a sample of households.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.1] Method step 1: list twenty home tasks, such as cooking, ironing, grocery shopping, car maintenance, paying bills, childminding, gardening, cleaning the bathroom and DIY.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.2] Method step 2: design a questionnaire that asks who usually does each task: mainly male, mainly female, shared or someone else.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.3] Method step 3: survey a sample of students, or their parents, and ask respondents to answer about their own household.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.4] Method step 4: keep responses anonymous, so that no family or individual can be identified and people can answer honestly.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.5] Analysis: count the answers in each category for each task and express each category as a percentage of the total answers, so you can find which tasks are mostly male, mostly female or shared.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.6] Conclusion: the results show whether the sample's home tasks are still divided on traditional sex-role lines or are shared. Compare the results with traditional roles and link them to dual-earner households.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.7] Limits: a small sample, bias in who answers and how honest the answers are can all weaken the conclusions.

CANDIDATES hom-education-s3: 3. The provision of education in Ireland
[hom-education-s3.0.b] Pre-school (early childhood) education is provided by private, community and voluntary services before a child starts primary school. It is not compulsory, but it gives children a head start, so the State funds some of it.
[hom-education-s3.0.0] The Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) scheme provides a free pre-school year to children before they enter primary school, delivered through approved providers. It is a government scheme, so it costs families nothing.
[hom-education-s3.0.1] Early Start is a one-year preventative pre-school programme for three- and four-year-olds, run in selected primary schools in disadvantaged areas. It aims to give children a positive start and prevent later school failure.
[hom-education-s3.0.2] Early Start is staffed by primary teachers working with a child care assistant, so the programme is led by trained teachers.
[hom-education-s3.0.3] The Access and Inclusion Model (AIM) supports children with disabilities to take part in the free pre-school programme, so they are not left out.
[hom-education-s3.0.4] Naíonraí are pre-schools where children learn through the Irish language.
[hom-education-s3.0.5] Montessori schools, which are generally privately run, use trained teachers and encourage children to learn through play at their own pace.
[hom-education-s3.0.6] Playschools give children of a similar age the opportunity to play together and learn to share, take turns and follow group rules.
[hom-education-s3.0.7] Pre-school builds language, social skills and the routines a child needs for the transition to primary school, so children settle in more easily when they start.
[hom-education-s3.1.b] Primary school is the first stage of compulsory education. Exams ask for the ages involved, the types of school to choose from and the alternatives open to parents.
[hom-education-s3.1.0] Children may enrol in primary school from age four and must begin by age six, because school is compulsory from six.
[hom-education-s3.1.1] Primary education is an eight-year cycle, from junior infants to sixth class, and most pupils finish between the ages of 12 and 14.
[hom-education-s3.1.2] Primary schools are state-funded or private. A state-funded school is paid for by the State but run by its patron (the religious body, Educate Together or other group that runs it), so it is not state-run and not private.
[hom-education-s3.1.3] State-funded schools may be religious, multidenominational or non-denominational. Educate Together is an example of a non-denominational school, and it is not a private school.
[hom-education-s3.1.4] Specialist types include Educate Together schools, Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools, where subjects are taught through Irish), Montessori schools and single-sex schools.
[hom-education-s3.1.5] Parents may educate a child at home instead of enrolling them in a school. Home-educated children are registered with Tusla.
[hom-education-s3.2.b] Second-level education follows primary school and is made up of several programmes. Each has a set length, a particular kind of curriculum and its own form of assessment, which is how exams ask you to describe them.
[hom-education-s3.2.0] Junior Cycle is a three-year programme that uses classroom-based assessments and ends with a state examination at the end of third year, marked by the State Examinations Commission. Its award is the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement, which replaced the old Junior Certificate.
[hom-education-s3.2.1] Transition Year is an optional year between Junior Cycle and Leaving Certificate with no state examination. It focuses on personal development, work experience and self-directed learning (students taking charge of their own learning).
[hom-education-s3.2.2] The Leaving Certificate Programme is a two-year programme that ends with state examinations at the end of sixth year.
[hom-education-s3.2.3] Leaving Certificate subjects are offered at higher and ordinary level, and foundation level exists only in Irish and mathematics. Grades are converted to points for third-level entry.
[hom-education-s3.2.4] Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA) is a two-year modular programme (made up of separate units) with practical, written and oral assessment. It suits students who prefer practical learning and can lead to Post Leaving Certificate courses.
[hom-education-s3.2.5] The Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP) has two link modules: Preparation for the World of Work and Enterprise Education. Work experience is part of Preparation for the World of Work.
[hom-education-s3.3.b] Work experience is a placement in a workplace. It lets a student try out working life while still at school, and the benefits are a common exam question.
[hom-education-s3.3.0] Work experience is part of the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (inside the Preparation for the World of Work link module) and is a standard element of Transition Year.
[hom-education-s3.3.1] A placement lets students test their career preferences and see what a job actually involves, so they can make a better choice about their future.
[hom-education-s3.3.2] Students learn workplace co-operation and how working life differs from school.
[hom-education-s3.3.3] Work experience develops communication, teamwork, planning, organisation, punctuality and problem solving.
[hom-education-s3.3.4] Students learn the expectations of a workplace, including timekeeping, appearance and health and safety.
[hom-education-s3.3.5] Completing a placement builds confidence and responsibility, and gives the student a work record for a curriculum vitae.
[hom-education-s3.4.b] Third level is education after second level. It includes degree courses in colleges and further education courses, and students need a way in and a way to pay.
[hom-education-s3.4.0] Third-level institutions include universities, technological universities and institutes of technology, which offer full-time degree and diploma courses. TU Dublin, created under the Technological Universities Act 2018, is an example of a technological university.
[hom-education-s3.4.1] Most full-time undergraduate places are allocated through the Central Applications Office (CAO) using Leaving Certificate points, which are based on the grades a student earns.
[hom-education-s3.4.2] Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses are full-time further education courses, usually delivered by Education and Training Boards in second-level schools and colleges. They provide training with work experience and can give access to third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.3] The Student Universal Support Ireland (SUSI) scheme provides means-tested grants (based on family income) towards the costs of approved third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.4] The Free Fees Initiative pays tuition for eligible undergraduates, so students pay only a student contribution.
[hom-education-s3.4.5] The Higher Education Access Route (HEAR) and the Disability Access Route to Education (DARE) are reduced-points entry routes for students from disadvantaged backgrounds or with disabilities, so these students can enter with fewer points.
[hom-education-s3.5.b] Adult and second-chance education lets people return to learning at any age, whatever their earlier educational level.
[hom-education-s3.5.0] Adult education courses are available full-time, part-time, modular, day, evening and weekend, and by distance learning, so adults can fit study around work and family.
[hom-education-s3.5.1] Second-chance education gives people who left school early a route to qualifications, such as Youthreach.
[hom-education-s3.5.2] Education and Training Boards (ETBs) run adult, community and further education courses; they replaced the Vocational Education Committees in 2013.
[hom-education-s3.5.3] SOLAS, the Further Education and Training Authority, funds and co-ordinates further education and training programmes across the country.
[hom-education-s3.5.4] Adult literacy schemes provide basic reading, writing and numeracy courses for adults.
[hom-education-s3.5.5] Adult education builds skills for work, supports career change, and helps adults keep pace with new workplace technology.
[hom-education-s3.6.b] Students with special educational needs are supported through a combination of planning, specialist staff and school settings. The aim is that each student gets the help that suits their need.
[hom-education-s3.6.0] An Individual Education Plan (IEP) sets out the goals, teaching approach and supports for a student with special needs, drawn up with the school and the parents.
[hom-education-s3.6.1] The National Council for Special Education (NCSE) plans and co-ordinates special educational provision, and Special Educational Needs Organisers allocate supports to schools.
[hom-education-s3.6.2] Special Needs Assistants (SNAs) provide care and support in class for students who have care needs or who need help to take part in lessons.
[hom-education-s3.6.3] Special education teachers (formerly called learning support and resource teachers) give withdrawal teaching, taking students out of class individually or in small groups, to students with learning difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.6.4] Special classes in mainstream schools, such as autism classes, have a reduced number of pupils per teacher, so each pupil gets more attention.
[hom-education-s3.6.5] Special schools provide for students with significant general or specific learning difficulties, physical disabilities, or sensory impairments.
[hom-education-s3.6.6] Visiting teachers advise and support students who are blind or vision impaired, or deaf or hard of hearing, and the teachers who teach them.
[hom-education-s3.6.7] Assistive technology, such as text-to-speech software, helps students with reading or writing difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.7.b] Beyond the school, specialist professionals assess needs and provide therapy, and the examinations themselves are adjusted for students who need it.
[hom-education-s3.7.0] Educational psychologists assess students' needs and recommend supports, which helps the school decide what help to give.
[hom-education-s3.7.1] The Health Service Executive (HSE) provides speech and language therapy and occupational therapy to children with special needs.
[hom-education-s3.7.2] The State Examinations Commission provides reasonable accommodations in state examinations, such as a reader, a scribe or extra time, so that a student's disability does not stop them showing what they know.

CANDIDATES hom-family-caring-s2: 2. Child–parent relationships
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.b] Young children depend entirely on adults to meet their basic needs for growth and survival. If a physical need is missed, the child's health and development suffer, so each one matters.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.0] Food: a balanced diet in suitable amounts supports growth, and new foods are introduced as the child grows so that the body gets all the nutrients it needs.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.1] Shelter: a warm, dry, safe home protects children from weather and hazards, because young children cannot regulate their body temperature or avoid danger for themselves.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.2] Clothing: suitable clothing protects against cold and injury and allows free movement, so the child can play and explore.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.3] Sleep and rest: regular sleep supports growth, concentration and the repair of the body.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.4] Health care: regular check-ups, immunisation (vaccination against disease) and prompt treatment of illness prevent disease and support healthy development.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.5] Hygiene: washing, care of teeth and toilet training prevent infection, because germs spread easily where hygiene is poor.
[hom-family-caring-s2.0.6] Safety and protection: supervision and safe surroundings prevent accidents and abuse, since young children cannot recognise danger.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.b] Children need more than food and shelter. Their emotional and mental needs shape how confident they feel and how well they relate to others, now and as adults.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.0] Love and affection: warm physical and verbal affection helps children feel valued, so they grow up believing they matter.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.1] Security and trust: a dependable, consistent caregiver teaches children that the world is predictable and safe.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.2] Attachment: a strong bond with a consistent caregiver forms the basis for later relationships, because the child learns what a trusting relationship feels like.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.3] Praise and encouragement: recognising effort builds self-esteem (belief in your own worth) and motivation.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.4] Stimulation and learning: play, conversation, reading and exploration develop language and thinking.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.5] Routine and boundaries: consistent limits help children learn self-control and understand expectations.
[hom-family-caring-s2.1.6] Social contact: interaction within the family teaches children to form friendships and relate to others.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.b] How well a child's needs are met in the early years affects the adult they become.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.0] When physical and psychological needs are met, children grow into well-adjusted, confident adults who are able to contribute to society.
[hom-family-caring-s2.2.1] When needs go unmet, children are more likely to grow up insecure, with poor self-esteem and difficulty forming relationships.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.b] Adolescents are changing quickly in body and mind. They still need care, but they need it in a different form from young children, with more freedom and respect.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.0] Growth: rapid growth (the growth spurt) increases the body's need for energy, protein, calcium and iron, and adequate physical activity supports healthy development.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.1] Sleep: adolescents need extended sleep because they are growing, and changes in their body clock can make early starts difficult.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.2] Independence: adolescents need increasing freedom to make choices, with parents gradually reducing direct control while keeping limits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.3] Identity and self-esteem: adolescents build a personal identity through relationships, interests and feedback from others.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.4] Peer acceptance: friendships become central, so a safe place at home to meet friends supports them.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.5] Privacy: adolescents need personal space and private time, which parents should respect, because it helps them develop their own identity.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.6] Emotional support: adolescents need to be listened to without judgement when problems arise, so they feel able to turn to parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.3.7] Guidance on risks: adolescents benefit from advice on the risks they meet, so they can make safe choices as they gain independence.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.b] The family is where children's needs are met day to day, mostly through ordinary routines and the way adults behave.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.0] Daily routines: regular meal, sleep and homework times give structure and security, so the child knows what to expect.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.1] Modelling: children learn behaviour, language and values by watching how adults act.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.2] Teaching: parents teach self-care, manners, safety and social skills during everyday activities.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.3] Health monitoring: parents arrange medical care, watch for illness and promote healthy eating and exercise.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.4] Responsiveness: a prompt response to a child's distress builds attachment and trust.
[hom-family-caring-s2.4.5] The home is where language, values and early skills are first learned, before formal schooling begins. This first learning within the family is called primary socialisation.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.b] Families do not meet every need alone. The State also helps with the cost and care of rearing children.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.0] Education through schools, for example, gives every child the chance to learn and develop.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.1] Child Benefit is a regular payment to parents for each child, which helps with the cost of rearing children.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.2] Medical cards give access to medical care for families who qualify, so health needs are not left untreated because of cost.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.3] Supports for disadvantaged pupils in school help children from poorer backgrounds to take part and do well.
[hom-family-caring-s2.5.4] Child-protection services through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, protect children from harm.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.b] Children are not just dependants. They have rights, which are things every child is entitled to, and these are linked to the needs children have.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.0] Right to life, and to a name and nationality, so the child has an identity and belongs to a country.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.1] Right to be cared for by parents and to live with them, unless this would be contrary to the child's interests.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.2] Right to protection from abuse and neglect, because children cannot defend themselves.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.3] Right to education and to develop physically, mentally, socially, morally and spiritually.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.4] Right to rest, play, leisure, culture and recreation, because play is how children learn and relax.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.5] Right to protection from discrimination, so every child is treated fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.6] Right to maintenance, meaning financial support from parents. The duty of parents to maintain their children is in the Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976.
[hom-family-caring-s2.6.7] Right to express an opinion and be heard in matters affecting the child, in line with age and maturity. Where parents live apart, children also have the right to see both parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.b] Children's rights come from international and Irish law, and they are balanced with the duties of parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.0] The UN Convention on the Rights of the Child was adopted in 1989 and ratified by Ireland in 1992.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.1] The Irish Constitution, since the Children Amendment, states the State's duty to protect children's rights.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.2] Rights come with parents' duties, and the State steps in only where a child's welfare requires it.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.3] The Child Care Act 1991 sets out the State's duty to protect children whose health, development or welfare is at risk.
[hom-family-caring-s2.7.4] The Child Care Act 1991 gives the State, through Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, powers to protect children at risk. These include care orders and emergency care orders, which can place the child outside the home, for example with foster carers.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.b] Conflict is common in adolescence because young people are moving towards adulthood while parents are still responsible for them. Most causes come back to the wish for independence.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.0] Adolescents seek independence and may feel their parents are out of touch with them.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.1] Peer and media influence can bring adolescents' values into conflict with the values of their parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.2] Adolescents accept adult responsibilities but are not yet allowed adult freedoms, which causes frustration.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.3] Relationships with peers and partners can take priority over schoolwork and family commitments, leading to disagreement.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.4] Parents may struggle to let go gradually and trust young people, such as by allowing more freedom, while still needing to set limits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.8.5] Common flashpoints include curfews (a time to be home by), technology and money.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.b] Conflict is easier to resolve when both sides stay calm and work towards a solution together instead of trying to win.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.0] Firm but fair discipline: rules are enforced consistently and, where possible, explained and agreed.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.1] Listening and defining the issue: each side states its view and the problem is named clearly rather than made personal.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.2] Negotiation and compromise: possible solutions are weighed together and reviewed after a trial period.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.3] Home atmosphere: adolescents are more likely to resolve conflict when they feel free to talk to parents.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.4] Parents can draw on their greater life experience while still hearing the young person's view.
[hom-family-caring-s2.9.5] Parents need to give trust gradually, so the young person has the chance to show responsibility.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.b] Role-play lets you practise handling a disagreement, such as a curfew, in a safe way.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.0] 1. Choose a conflict, for example an adolescent who wants a later curfew, and give one person the part of the adolescent and one the parent.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.1] 2. Each person states their view without interrupting, so each side feels heard.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.2] 3. Use "I" statements, such as "I feel left out when I have to leave early", so the other person is not blamed.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.3] 4. Name the problem clearly and suggest a compromise, for example a later curfew at weekends.
[hom-family-caring-s2.10.4] 5. Agree a trial period and a time to review it, then discuss how it went.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.b] Communication is how family members share what they need and feel. Good communication keeps relationships strong and helps problems get solved.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.0] Communication lets members express needs, feelings and opinions, so that each person feels heard, valued and secure.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.1] Problems discussed openly are more likely to be solved before they become serious conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.2] Bottled-up feelings, such as anger kept hidden, can lead to resentment, stress and withdrawal.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.3] Discussion of spending, meals, childcare and the care of vulnerable members allows household work to be shared fairly.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.4] Explaining the reasons for rules, rather than simply imposing them, makes them more likely to be accepted.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.5] Children learn language, empathy and negotiation by taking part in conversation with adults.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.6] Older members who encourage and praise achievement build confidence in younger members.
[hom-family-caring-s2.11.7] Secrecy or aggressive criticism creates mistrust, loneliness and conflict.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.b] Good communication is a skill, and it can be blocked by everyday habits.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.0] Active listening means listening without interrupting, using calm language and checking understanding before replying.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.1] Taking turns to speak gives everyone a chance to be heard, so no one person dominates.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.2] Family meetings give a regular, planned time to discuss plans and problems, so issues are raised before they grow.
[hom-family-caring-s2.12.3] Barriers to communication include lack of time, screens, differences in age and tone of voice.
