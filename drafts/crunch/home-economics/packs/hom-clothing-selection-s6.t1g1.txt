TARGET hom-clothing-selection-s6: 6. The clothing and textile industries in Ireland
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.b] The clothing and textile industries cover the design, manufacture, distribution and sale of clothing, textiles and accessories. Each stage passes the product on towards the consumer, and the whole sector is market-driven, so it depends on skill, imagination and creativity to keep up with what customers want.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.0] The parts of the industry are designers, who create ranges (collections of garments), manufacturers of clothing and of textiles, distributors, and retailers such as shops, department stores and online sellers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.1] Manufacturers produce menswear, womenswear, children's wear, knitwear, sportswear, accessories, lingerie and footwear, so the industry covers almost everything people wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.2] Designers create ranges that are made by manufacturers and sold through their own shops or through retailers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.3] Distributors and retailers move the finished goods from the factory to the consumer, through shops, department stores and online sales.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.4] The industries provide employment in manufacture, distribution and sales, and they earn export income because Irish designers sell their work abroad.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.5] Irish designers sell under their own label and also produce ranges for high street stores, which gives their designs a much wider customer base.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.0.6] Local Enterprise Offices (the county and city enterprise support bodies that replaced the Enterprise Boards in 2014) give small firms and designers advice, training and grants, so they can grow their businesses.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.b] A cottage or home-based industry is a business run in or near a person's home, often by one or a few people making a product by hand and selling to craft shops or directly to customers. Small firms like these are especially important in rural areas.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.0] Start-up costs are low, so a business can be set up without a large investment in premises or machinery.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.1] The work can be fitted around family life, which suits people who cannot work fixed hours.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.2] Cottage industries provide employment in rural areas and so help keep people in their communities.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.3] Some work is subcontracted (given out by a larger business) to local people who do it at home, for example Aran knitting.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.4] They keep traditional skills and crafts alive, such as hand-knitting, weaving, lace-making and crochet.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.5] Small businesses can develop distinctive products that stand out from mass-produced clothing, and a successful one can grow into a larger business.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.6] Hobbies can turn into businesses, especially when the economy is poor and people look for another source of income.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.7] Cottage industries are often promoted through craft shops and design centres, which sell the work of makers directly to the public.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.1.8] Their weaknesses are small scale, limited marketing and finance, and an income that can be low.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.b] Knitting, stitching and similar crafts have become popular again. Several different reasons explain why, and an exam answer needs a few of them.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.0] Socialising: people join knitting and stitching clubs to meet others.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.1] Learning traditional skills, which many people want to pass on or take up for the first time.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.2] Saving money, because making or mending an item yourself can cost less than buying it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.3] More free time, which gives people the chance to take up a craft.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.4] Start-up businesses, because a craft can be turned into a small business with little money.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.5] A desire for original items that are not mass-produced.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.2.6] The social influence of celebrities who knit or wear handmade items.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.b] Irish designers draw on traditional crafts for ideas and materials. This links modern fashion to Irish heritage and gives Irish design a recognisable identity.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.0] Hand-knitting and hand-weaving inspire coats and coatigans (a long knitted jacket) that use Aran stitches, the textured stitches of Aran knitting.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.1] Irish linen, woven from flax, is used for products ranging from light tops and skirts to heavy coats and jackets.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.2] Lace and crochet work feature in wedding dresses and other occasion wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.3] Hand-weaving produces textured fabrics that designers use in contemporary clothing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.4] Tweed, Aran sweaters and hand-weaving are recognised Irish products that link design to Irish heritage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.5] These traditional crafts contribute to the identity of Irish design, which designers use to market Irish fashion abroad.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.3.6] John Rocha used Irish fabrics and Celtic influence to create unique designs.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.b] Irish designers show that fashion can be a successful business in Ireland. Learn one or two designers well enough to name them and give two or three points about their work.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.0] John Rocha and Simone Rocha are Irish designers whose work is sold internationally, and John Rocha uses Irish fabrics and Celtic influence in his designs.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.1] Philip Treacy is a contemporary Irish milliner (hat designer) from Ahascragh, County Galway, known internationally for couture hats.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.2] Philip Treacy studied fashion at NCAD in Dublin, then took a master's degree in millinery at the Royal College of Art in London, and he built an international career creating hats for couture, royalty and celebrities.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.3] Lainey Keogh is known for knitwear, and Louise Kennedy is known for detailed work and designed for a previous president.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.4] Other Irish designers include Paul Costelloe, Carolyn Donnelly, Sybil Connolly and Orla Kiely, who is Irish-born.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.5] Irish designers have created clothing and accessories for royalty, celebrities, world leaders and major events.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.4.6] Designers contribute to the industry by creating employment in manufacture, distribution and sales, exporting menswear, womenswear, children's wear, knitwear and accessories, and promoting Irish design at home and abroad.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.b] Irish fashion needs publicity and support to reach customers at home and abroad. Several routes help a designer or maker become known.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.0] Local Enterprise Offices support small designers and makers with advice, training and grants.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.1] Shops and design centres sell Irish design, for example Kilkenny Design Centre, a craft and design centre in Castle Yard, Kilkenny.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.2] Designers produce ranges for high street stores, so their work reaches a wide public.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.3] Irish designers show their collections at fashion weeks and international fashion shows, which gives them exposure to buyers and the press.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.4] Press coverage and public figures wearing Irish designers' clothing create publicity for the designers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.5.5] Fashion blogs and social media promote Irish designers to a wide audience at home and abroad.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.b] The Irish clothing and textile industries face strong pressures. Most of them come from competition on price and from the small size of the home market.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.0] Imports made in low-wage countries with cheaper and faster labour mean Irish producers find it hard to compete on price.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.1] Discount stores sell clothing at low prices in high volumes, which pressures Irish firms that make smaller runs of higher-quality garments.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.2] The industry is labour-intensive (it needs many workers), so high labour costs for skilled workers push up the price of Irish goods.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.3] A global recession leaves people with less disposable income to spend on clothes.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.4] A small home market limits the number of customers available to Irish designers, so growth depends on export sales.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.5] Firms must adapt to new shopping patterns such as online and mobile shopping, and keep up with trends and technology.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.6] Small firms and cottage industries have limited funds for marketing, equipment and expansion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.6.7] A shortage of skilled workers means that keeping them and training new ones is a continuing challenge for manufacturers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.7.b] The industries offer jobs for creative, technical, presentation and retail talents. Exams ask you to name one career and give details of it, so know what each person does.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.7.0] Creative careers include fashion designer (creates the range), milliner (makes hats), stylist and fashion journalist or writer.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.7.1] Technical careers include tailor, machinist (sews garments in a factory), clothing manufacturer, factory worker and fashion buyer.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.7.2] Presentation careers include model and photographer, and fashion advertising and blogging.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.7.3] Retail careers include retail sales assistant.
[hom-clothing-selection-s6.7.4] To answer a career question, name one career and give two details, such as what the person does, the skills or qualities needed, where they work, and the training route.

CANDIDATES hom-clothing-selection-s3: 3. Current fashion trends
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.b] A trend is recognised by the features it repeats across many garments and outfits. The elements of design (colour, shape, line, proportion, pattern and texture) give you the words to describe those features, and the examples below are only examples, because the trend shown in an exam may be any current one.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.0] Colour: bright, fluorescent or tonal colour schemes, colour panelling and monochrome outfits make a trend easy to spot from a distance.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.1] Shape and silhouette: oversized or boxy shapes, high-waisted trousers, and tailored coats or capes. Cropped tops are another example of a current shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.2] Line: exaggerated vertical lines add height, horizontal lines widen and shorten the look, and diagonal lines give contrast and movement. This is why a trend can be described through its lines.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.3] Proportion: for example a short jacket worn over a long dress, or a deep cropped top with wide trousers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.4] Pattern: geometric prints and bold retro statements. Retro patterns are old designs revived by designers, so name the pattern and say it is a revival.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.5] Texture: shiny finishes reflect light, while matt fabrics absorb it. Embroidery, sequins, beads and distressed or frayed surfaces add surface interest.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.6] Accessories: belts, large bags, statement jewellery and hats, which can carry a trend on their own.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.0.7] Seasonal examples are examples of their year, not fixed lists: summer 2024 gave light leggings, loose T-shirts, crochet knits, denim jackets, cut-outs, cargo trousers and oversized jackets. Teenage trends gave ripped denim, bomber jackets and chunky jewellery. Casual wear showed neon and panelled colour and geometric prints.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.b] Trends do not appear by accident. Several forces decide which colours, garments and styles become popular, and exams ask you to outline or evaluate these influences.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.0] Social media spreads a fashion image internationally within hours, and repeated exposure can turn a colour, garment or vintage look into a trend. This is why trends also increase sales.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.1] Influencers and bloggers display outfits and styling videos, give samples and feedback, and their endorsements can encourage or discourage a purchase.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.2] Celebrities, musicians and athletes are photographed in specific designers' clothes, and fans copy their style. Music and dance styles, sports and world events also shape what people wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.3] Designers and fashion houses define the season's direction through seasonal collections and fashion weeks. Ready-to-wear (mass-produced) clothing then copies the runway looks.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.4] Environmental concern has created demand for sustainable or circular fashion (clothing kept in use for as long as possible). This favours durable natural or recycled materials, repair and upcycling, and fast fashion is criticised for waste.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.5] Trends are also shaped by travel and by culture, including global and ethnic styles from other countries that become popular at home.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.1.6] Past decades return: retro and vintage styles and bold retro patterns are revived by designers and high street brands.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.b] Athleisure (also called leisure wear) is sportswear styling worn for everyday life, not sportswear for playing sport. It has become a major trend because it puts comfort first.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.0] Typical items include leggings or joggers, hoodies, zipped jackets and trainers, worn for work, school and leisure.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.1] Stretch, breathable and easy-care fabrics give comfort and freedom of movement, and they wash easily, which suits busy everyday use.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.2] Its strengths are comfort, versatility, durability, affordability and adaptability to many occasions. It is casual and trendy and is promoted by influencers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.3] Athleisure can support activity and posture and a healthy active lifestyle, since clothing that is comfortable to wear makes it easier to exercise and move.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.4] It works as a wardrobe staple (a basic item worn again and again) when the pieces are neutral in colour and can be combined with smarter items.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.5] Its weaknesses are that it can look too informal for some workplaces and occasions, and that tight leggings and tops do not suit every body shape.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.2.6] Another weakness is that it may encourage a casual dress code to become the default in everyday life.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.b] Buying older or second-hand clothes has moved from a bargain habit to a fashion statement. The two terms are different, and both are examined.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.0] Vintage clothing is older clothing from a past era, often a previous decade. Pre-loved clothing is any second-hand clothing, so all vintage is pre-loved but not all pre-loved is vintage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.1] Both are bought in charity shops, vintage or second-hand shops, online marketplaces and swaps.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.2] Pre-loved clothing extends the life of garments already made, which reduces the demand for new production. This is why it is linked to sustainability and the reaction to fast fashion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.3] Reasons for its popularity: it is cheaper than new, it gives a unique, individual look because fewer people wear the same piece, and influencers and celebrities promote it. The economic climate also encourages it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.4] Distressed looks, such as frayed edges, grated patches and bleached areas, take inspiration from the vintage punk look, along with studs, sequins and embroidery.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.5] Retro statements, such as bold patterns and bright colours from earlier decades, are revived by designers and high street brands. They are eye-catching because they stand out from plain everyday wear.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.6] Upcycling changes an old garment into a new one, for example by adding patches, embroidery or appliqué (fabric shapes sewn on), dyeing, shortening, or customising denim with studs.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.3.7] To restyle a garment to follow a trend, add belts or embellishment, alter the hem or trouser length, add layers or shoulder pads, crop or deconstruct a jacket, distress or recolour jeans, or change the buttons, patches or linings.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.b] Accessories are the items added to an outfit rather than the main garments. They are often the quickest way to show a trend.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.0] Examples include belts, bags, scarves, hats, gloves, jewellery, sunglasses, footwear, ties, cufflinks and pocket squares.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.1] Accessories complete an outfit and create a focal point (the place the eye goes first), drawing attention to a feature of the look. A belt, for example, shows off the waist.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.2] They add colour and contrast, and they tie an outfit together by repeating a colour from another garment. A colourful scarf brightens a plain outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.3] Accessories can change the formality of a basic outfit, for example a scarf or hat that makes a plain outfit look smart or casual. They also help balance colour and proportion.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.4] A vintage look can be created with items such as retro sunglasses, large brooches and patterned scarves.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.5] Footwear contributes to the overall look, complements the outfit and can create a focal point.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.4.6] Accessories are often the cheapest way to follow a trend, as one item can update or modernise an existing outfit.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.b] A critical evaluation weighs the advantages and disadvantages of a trend rather than simply describing it. Describe the trend first, then judge it under the headings the question gives.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.0] Advantages: a trend can express identity, create employment in the fashion sector and bring new ideas to design.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.1] Disadvantages: fast fashion encourages frequent buying, leading to waste and to garments of low quality that wear out quickly.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.2] Cost: following trends can be expensive, and garments may date quickly once a trend passes.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.3] Comfort and function: a trend should be judged on whether the garment is comfortable and suits the wearer's activity.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.4] Suitability: a trend may not flatter every body shape, so a critical evaluation considers who it suits and who it does not, and the occasion it is worn for.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.5.5] Other headings the exam may give include colour, pattern, line, shape, proportion, texture, accessories, aesthetic appeal and influences.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.b] Elective questions ask you to sketch and describe an outfit built around one current trend, so you need to show the trend and explain your choices.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.0] Diagram: draw a front view of the outfit (add a back view if needed) and label the design features, such as line, shape, proportion and harmony. Give the colour and fabric, and say which trend it follows and why.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.1] Fabric reasoning: choose for a reason, such as absorbent, breathable, stretch, easy-care or shape-retaining. Cotton-elastane jersey suits fitted tops because it stretches, and polyester/cotton suits durable jackets and joggers.
[hom-clothing-selection-s3.6.2] Aesthetic appeal: explain how colour, line, balance and emphasis work together, and how the outfit suits the wearer and the occasion.
