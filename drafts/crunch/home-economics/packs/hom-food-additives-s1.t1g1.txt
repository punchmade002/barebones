TARGET hom-food-additives-s1: 1. Processed foods and three processed-food profiles
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.b] A processed food is any food changed from its raw state by one or more processes, such as milling, heating, freezing, drying, canning or adding ingredients. Processing makes a raw food safer, longer-lasting, more convenient or more valuable.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.0] Main reasons for processing: to extend shelf life, make food safe to eat, cut down on waste, provide a wide choice of good-quality food all year round and create new food products, which varies the diet.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.1] The range of processed foods includes frozen, canned, dried, instant or ready-to-eat and cook-chill foods, as well as preserved or pickled foods and fortified foods.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.2] Minimal processing includes washing, cutting, bagging salad leaves or freezing vegetables, with no ingredient added, so the food stays close to its raw state.
[hom-food-additives-s1.0.3] Extensive processing involves many stages and ingredients, so the finished product is far removed from its raw material.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.b] Flour is the standard example of extensive processing: wheat grain is turned into a fine powder that looks and behaves nothing like the raw grain, and it then becomes the base of many other foods.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.0] Flour is made by cleaning and blending wheat grain, then milling it between rollers (break rolls and smooth rolls) and sieving it. This separates the bran and germ from the starchy endosperm (the white inner part of the grain), and white flour is mainly endosperm.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.1] Removing the bran and germ lowers fibre and some vitamins and minerals, so white flour has fewer of these than wholemeal flour. Nutrients lost in milling, for example B vitamins and iron, may be added back, and flour is a food named as fortified.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.2] White flour keeps longer than wholegrain flour because the germ, which contains oils that can turn rancid (go off), has been removed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.3] Improvers (additives that help flour perform when baked) or added nutrients may be incorporated at the end of milling.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.4] Flour is weighed into paper bags. The label gives the brand, product name, ingredients, best-before date, quantity, nutrition information and storage.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.5] Flour is then an ingredient in bread, cakes, pasta and breakfast cereals, each of which passes through further stages such as mixing, baking or extrusion (forcing through a shaped opening), then packaging.
[hom-food-additives-s1.1.6] Setting: a visit to a local mill shows these stages in practice.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.b] Milk spoils quickly because micro-organisms and enzymes act on it. Preservation methods slow or stop the micro-organisms and enzymes that spoil food, so milk goes through several treatments at the creamery.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.0] A creamery receives milk from farms, tests its quality, cools it, separates cream from skimmed milk, standardises the fat content, pasteurises it, cools it again, packs it and keeps it in chill storage.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.1] Pasteurisation heats milk (to about 72 °C for 15 seconds) to destroy pathogens and most spoilage organisms, then cools it rapidly. The milk still needs refrigeration and keeps for days.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.2] UHT (ultra-high temperature) treatment heats milk to about 135 °C for a few seconds. Sealed in aseptic (germ-free) packaging, it keeps for months at room temperature until opened. Sterilised milk is heated even more severely in the sealed bottle.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.3] Homogenisation forces milk under pressure through tiny openings, breaking fat globules so that cream does not rise to the top. This improves consistency but is not a preservation step and does not kill bacteria.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.4] Canning heats food in sealed containers to destroy micro-organisms; freezing and storage at or below -18 °C stops most micro-organisms from growing.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.5] Drying removes moisture so micro-organisms cannot grow, and vacuum packing removes air to slow spoilage.
[hom-food-additives-s1.2.6] Setting: a visit to a local creamery or cheese plant shows milk being pasteurised and separated in practice.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.b] Added value means processing a raw material into a product with greater economic value, not a more nutritious one. The product is worth more to the manufacturer and is better matched to consumer taste, convenience or health.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.0] For example, cook-chill soups and readymade meals, cheese and grated cheese, flavoured yoghurts, sliced cooked ham, herb-flavoured oils and vinegars, jams, pickles and fruit sauces.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.1] Cook-chill foods are cooked, chilled rapidly (to 3 °C within 30 minutes and fully chilled within 90 minutes), stored and transported at 1-3 °C, and reheated thoroughly before serving.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.2] Milk is the raw material for cheese and yoghurt; each involves culturing, coagulating or otherwise changing the milk into a new product with a higher selling price.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.3] Cheese: pasteurised milk is inoculated (given a starter) with lactic acid bacteria, which turn lactose into lactic acid. Rennet coagulates it into curds and whey, the curds are cut and the whey drained, then the curds are heated, cheddared, salted, moulded and pressed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.4] The cheese is then ripened for months, graded, cut and sealed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.3.5] The manufacturer gains a higher selling price, while the consumer gains convenience, taste or a health benefit.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.b] Yoghurt is a worked example of added value: ordinary milk is fermented into a thicker, sour product that sells for more. Follow the stages in order.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.0] 1. Milk is tested on reception and its fat content is standardised (adjusted to a set level); milk solids may be added to give body.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.1] 2. Milk is homogenised under pressure so that fat globules stay evenly dispersed.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.2] 3. Milk is pasteurised to destroy pathogens, then cooled to the temperature the starter culture needs.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.3] 4. Lactic acid bacteria, and any probiotic culture, are added and the milk is incubated; the bacteria ferment lactose to lactic acid.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.4] 5. The falling pH (it becomes more acidic) coagulates the casein (the main milk protein), giving yoghurt its thick texture and sour flavour.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.5] 6. Once the correct acidity is reached, the yoghurt is chilled quickly to slow fermentation.
[hom-food-additives-s1.4.6] 7. Fruit purée, permitted sugar, stabiliser or flavouring may be blended in; the yoghurt is filled, sealed, coded and kept refrigerated in the chill chain (the unbroken cold storage from factory to shop).
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.b] Some foods are chosen for more than their basic nutrients. A functional food has a health benefit on top of ordinary nutrition.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.0] A functional food gives health benefits beyond its basic nutritional value because of an added or naturally present ingredient.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.1] Live yoghurt with probiotic bacteria supports the digestive system, because the added bacteria are the beneficial ingredient.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.2] Plant sterol spreads reduce blood cholesterol, so they are chosen by people wanting to lower it.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.3] Omega-3 enriched foods are promoted for heart health.
[hom-food-additives-s1.5.4] Folic acid enriched foods reduce the risk of neural tube defects (birth defects of the brain and spine) in a developing baby.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.b] Fortification is a way of putting nutrients into foods that people eat every day. It is different from food supplements, which are tablets.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.0] Fortification adds nutrients so that their level in the food is higher than the level naturally present.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.1] Fortification replaces nutrients lost in processing, so the food is not poorer, and addresses public health needs.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.2] Breakfast cereals are often fortified with iron and vitamins, fruit juices with vitamin C, and margarine and dairy spreads with vitamins A and D.
[hom-food-additives-s1.6.3] Other foods named as fortified include milk, flour, bread and biscuits.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.b] Convenience foods are processed foods that are ready to eat or quick to prepare. They are popular because they solve real problems for busy households.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.0] They save preparation, cooking and cleaning time and need little cooking skill, which suits busy households and people with limited mobility.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.1] Long shelf life gives a wide choice of food all year round, including out-of-season produce.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.2] They give consistent quality, taste and portion size, and clear portions aid meal planning.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.3] Processing reduces waste, so less food is thrown out, and frozen or canned foods keep many of their nutrients.
[hom-food-additives-s1.7.4] They are easy to store, so they are useful as an emergency meal.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.b] The same foods have drawbacks for health, cost and the environment, which is why they should not make up the whole diet.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.0] Many are high in salt, sugar and saturated fat and low in fibre.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.1] They are often more expensive per portion than home-prepared food of similar size, because the maker's processing and packaging costs are added to the price.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.2] Many contain additives, a minority of consumers react to particular substances.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.3] Packaging adds waste and environmental impact.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.4] Reliance on ready meals reduces cooking skills, because meals are no longer made from scratch, and can unbalance the diet.
[hom-food-additives-s1.8.5] Taste, colour and texture can be inferior to the fresh equivalent, because heating and storage change them.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.b] Ultra-processed foods are at the far end of processing. They are made in factories from refined ingredients and additives rather than from whole foods.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.0] Ultra-processed foods are industrial formulations made largely from refined ingredients and additives, with little or no whole food. They are classed as group 4 in the NOVA system.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.1] Examples, such as packaged snacks, sugary drinks, instant noodles and many ready meals.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.2] They are designed for convenience, palatability (how pleasant they taste) and long shelf life, and often contain flavourings, colourings, emulsifiers and sweeteners that are not used in home cooking.
[hom-food-additives-s1.9.3] They are less healthy than fresh food.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.b] Two other labels on food describe how it was produced: genetic modification changes the plant or animal itself, while organic farming limits the chemicals used to grow it.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.0] Genetic modification alters the DNA of a plant or animal to change a characteristic such as size, speed of ripening or resistance to disease.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.1] Soya and maize are examples of crops widely grown as genetically modified varieties.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.2] EU law requires foods containing genetically modified ingredients, including foods made from genetically modified soya or maize, to be labelled.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.3] Organic produce is grown without synthetic chemical fertilisers, pesticides and preservatives.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.4] Organic farming is less intensive, so it gives lower yields, and must follow rules on fertiliser use and on pest, weed and disease control.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.5] Organic certification is given by inspection bodies recognised by the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, and certified products carry the symbol of the certifying body.
[hom-food-additives-s1.10.6] Lower yields and more labour make organic food more expensive.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s1: 1. Principles of food preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.b] Food starts to spoil as soon as it is harvested or slaughtered. Preservation means treating food so that spoilage is slowed or stopped and the food can be kept, moved and eaten later, with colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as near as possible to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.0] It reduces waste by keeping surplus produce, such as a glut of home-grown fruit or vegetables, usable for months rather than days, instead of being thrown away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.1] Because less food is thrown away, preservation also reduces the environmental footprint of food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.2] It makes food available out of season and over long distances, so perishable foods (those that spoil quickly) can be transported and stored.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.3] It makes food safer by destroying food poisoning micro-organisms or stopping them from growing, and it lengthens shelf life (the time food stays good to eat).
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.4] It creates foods with distinct textures and flavours, such as jam, chutney, dried fruit, smoked fish and cured meat, which increases variety and choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.b] Spoilage is not caused only by micro-organisms. Several separate processes make food unusable, and each has its own visible effect.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.0] Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions and are naturally present in food. After harvest or slaughter they carry on working, so food ripens, softens, darkens and changes flavour even when no micro-organism is present. Cut apple, banana and potato brown this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.1] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) grow on food, causing decay, rot, souring, off-odours and slime, and in some cases food poisoning. Moulds give fuzzy growth on bread, cheese and jam; yeasts ferment fruit juice and jam, with bubbling and an alcoholic smell; bacteria cause souring, putrefaction and food poisoning.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.2] Putrefaction is the breakdown of proteins by bacteria, producing foul-smelling products.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.3] Oxidation occurs when oxygen reacts with fats, causing rancidity (an unpleasant, stale taste and smell), and with the cut surfaces of fruit and vegetables, causing browning. Vitamin C is also lost by oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.4] Moisture loss through the skins of fruit and vegetables causes shrivelling, wilting, wrinkling and loss of weight. It also dries and hardens bread and cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.5] Incorrect storage temperature allows micro-organisms and enzymes to work quickly, because warmth speeds both of them up.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.6] Poor hygiene and careless handling carry micro-organisms from unwashed hands, equipment, pests or raw food onto prepared food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.7] Chemical contaminants, such as insecticide or pesticide residues, antibiotics, metals and residues from cleaning products, can make food unsafe. Physical damage such as bruising also opens food to spoilage.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.b] Micro-organisms need six conditions to grow and multiply. Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.0] The six conditions are food (nutrients), moisture (available water), warmth (a suitable temperature), time (to multiply), a suitable pH or acidity and, for aerobic ones (those that need air), oxygen. Most bacteria prefer a neutral or slightly acid pH.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.1] Bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply rapidly in the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C, which is why chilled and hot food is kept outside it. Below 5 °C growth is slowed; above 63 °C it is stopped and most are killed by sustained heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.2] Under good conditions one bacterial cell divides about every 20 minutes (binary fission, splitting in two), so numbers rise very fast.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.3] Moulds and yeasts tolerate acid, sugar and low moisture better than most bacteria, so they spoil jam and dried fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.4] Moulds appear as fuzzy colonies or threads; yeasts produce bubbling, gas and a sour or alcoholic smell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.5] Enzymes work best at a moderate temperature and are destroyed (denatured) by heat or strong acid; cold slows them but does not stop them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.6] Micro-organisms cannot multiply in food with little available water, which is why drying and concentrated sugar or salt preserve food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.b] Three groups of micro-organism spoil food, and spores and enzymes also have to be understood. Each acts differently, which is why different methods suit different foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.0] Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause food poisoning and others spoil food by producing acid and gas.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.1] Yeasts are single-celled fungi that ferment sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.2] Moulds are multicellular fungi that grow as visible threads and spread by airborne spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.3] Spores are dormant (resting), resistant forms produced by some bacteria and all moulds; they survive ordinary heat and cold and start growing again when conditions improve.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.4] Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein, and each one acts on a specific substance in food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.b] Osmosis explains why strong sugar or salt solutions preserve food. It is the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane (one that lets water through but not most dissolved substances) from a weaker solution to a stronger one.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.0] The cell membrane of a micro-organism is partially permeable, so water leaves the cell when the surrounding solution is concentrated.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.1] Loss of water makes the cell shrink and stop growing, and at high concentrations it can kill the cell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.2] The same process draws water out of food, which is why salted meat and fish and sugared fruit keep. A strong sugar or salt solution outside a microbial cell draws water out, so the cell shrivels and cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.b] Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of the conditions that micro-organisms and enzymes need, and by preventing re-contamination. There are only a few principles, and each method is an application of one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.0] Destroy micro-organisms and inactivate enzymes with heat, as in canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching. Irradiation also destroys organisms and some enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.1] Slow or stop activity with low temperature: refrigeration slows growth and enzyme action, and freezing makes organisms dormant (inactivated, not killed) and makes water unavailable as ice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.2] Remove available water: dehydration (drying, freeze drying) takes water away, while sugar and salt tie it up and draw it out of organisms by osmosis, as in jam, salted fish and cured meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.3] Alter the pH with acid: vinegar (acetic acid) in pickles and chutney, or acids produced in fermentation, such as lactic acid in yoghurt and sauerkraut and acetic acid in vinegar. This prevents the growth of many spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.4] Use chemical preservatives that inhibit organisms: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives such as sulphur dioxide, sorbates, benzoates and nitrite.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.5] Exclude oxygen and prevent re-entry of organisms with airtight packaging, vacuum sealing, sealed hot jars (a vacuum forms as the contents cool) and moisture-proof wraps for the freezer. Removing oxygen also inactivates enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.6] Prevent re-contamination after treatment, using sterilised jars and lids, clean utensils, good hygiene and sealing straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.7] Most methods combine principles. Jam uses heat, sugar (osmosis), acid and pectin, and a sealed jar; chutney uses heat, vinegar and sugar; smoked fish uses salt, drying and smoke chemicals.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.b] The methods of preservation fall into groups according to the principle they use. Knowing the groups shows the structure of food preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.0] Low temperature: refrigeration and freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.1] Heat: canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.2] Water removal: drying and freeze drying, and sugar or salt that bind water.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.3] Chemicals: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives, with sugar, vinegar, salt, smoking and alcohol being the traditional natural preservatives.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.4] Fermentation and irradiation.

CANDIDATES hom-food-industry-s1: 1. Structure of the Irish food industry
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.b] The food industry takes raw produce from farms and fisheries, processes it into finished goods, and distributes and sells them through a service sector. It is organised in three levels, each depending on the one before it: a processor needs farmers' produce, and a retailer needs the processor's goods.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.0] Primary sector: resource-based production, meaning farming, horticulture (growing fruit, vegetables and similar crops), fishing and fish farming (mariculture). It produces raw materials such as milk, cattle, sheep, pigs, poultry, eggs, grain, fruit, vegetables, fish and shellfish.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.1] Secondary sector: manufacturing and processing, which converts raw materials into finished food and drink, such as milk into cheese, butter and milk powder, wheat into flour and bread, carcasses into cuts and meat products, or barley into beer and whiskey.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.2] Tertiary sector: services that move, sell, support and regulate food, including transport, wholesale and retail, catering and food outlets, tourism and hospitality, research, education, advice, marketing and regulation.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.3] Agri-food is one of Ireland's largest indigenous industries (industries that grew up in Ireland rather than being brought in from abroad), and most of its raw material is grown or reared on Irish farms.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.4] The industry matters to the economy because it provides employment, much of it in rural areas, and earns income from export sales.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.5] The UK and other EU member states are the main markets for Irish food overall, and much dairy and beef output is exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.b] Besides the three levels, the industry is divided into product sectors. A single sector such as dairy can contain primary, secondary and tertiary activity, so keep the two lists apart.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.0] Dairy and ingredients: milk, cheese, butter, yoghurt, cream and milk powder, plus dairy ingredients sold to other food manufacturers.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.1] Meat and livestock: beef, lamb, pork and poultry, slaughtered and processed in licensed meat plants.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.2] Beverages: beer and stout, whiskey and other spirits, fruit juice and bottled water.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.3] Horticulture: fruit, vegetables and mushrooms, sold fresh or processed.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.4] Seafood: fish and shellfish landed at Irish ports and processed for home and export sale.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.5] Cereals and bakery: flour milling, bread, biscuits and breakfast cereals.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.6] Prepared consumer foods: ready meals, snacks, confectionery and sauces sold in retail packaging.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.b] Ireland produces far more of some foods than its small population can eat, so these are sold abroad. A climate and soil suited to grass means livestock and dairy output is much larger than home demand.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.0] Dairy products, especially cheese, butter, milk powder and infant formula ingredients, are among Ireland's largest food exports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.1] Beef and lamb are exported to EU and non-EU markets.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.2] Pork, poultry and processed meat products are also exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.3] Irish whiskey, beer and stout are exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.4] Seafood, including shellfish and farmed salmon, is exported from Irish ports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.5] Prepared foods and bakery products are sold to retailers and food service companies abroad.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.b] Ireland imports foods it cannot grow, cannot grow all year round, or chooses to buy in for variety and price. Reasons include climate (tropical crops), seasonal gaps, consumer demand for variety and ethnic foods, ingredients for manufacturers and feed for livestock.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.0] Tropical and out-of-season fruit, such as bananas, pineapples, oranges and avocados, which Ireland cannot grow commercially.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.1] Coffee, tea and cocoa, which are grown in warmer countries.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.2] Spices and sugar, which Ireland does not produce in quantity.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.3] Soya and other animal feed ingredients used by Irish livestock producers.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.4] Wine, and some processed foods such as chocolate and confectionery.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.5] Rice, dried pasta, some vegetables and herbs, and sauces are also imported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.6] Ireland also imports some foods it exports, such as cheeses and meat products, for variety and price, so trade runs both ways.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.7] Ireland is a net exporter of food overall, so the value of exports is greater than the value of imports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.b] Several State bodies support, develop and regulate the food industry. Each has its own job, so learn each name together with one function.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.0] Bord Bia (the Irish Food Board) promotes Irish food, drink and horticulture at home and abroad, and develops national and international markets for Irish products.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.1] Bord Bia runs quality assurance schemes for food producers, such as the Sustainable Beef and Lamb Assurance Scheme and the Sustainable Dairy Assurance Scheme, shown by a quality mark on pack.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.2] Bord Bia's Origin Green programme records and verifies the sustainability commitments of food and drink companies: companies set measurable targets and their progress is independently verified.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.3] Bord Bia helps Irish food companies with market research, buyer introductions and trade fairs.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.4] Teagasc, the agriculture and food development authority, provides research, advice and training for farmers and food producers, so they can improve what they grow and make.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.5] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the seafood development agency, develops the seafood sector and promotes seafood at home and abroad.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.6] Enterprise Ireland supports export growth and innovation in Irish companies, including food and drink firms.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.7] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) sets and enforces food safety standards across the sector.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.8] The Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine is the government department responsible for the sector, covering policy and schemes.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.b] A local factory, business or home enterprise can be used to show how a food business fits into the industry structure. Work through the same set of questions for whichever business you choose.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.0] Identify the business's sector, such as dairy, meat, beverages or bakery, and whether its main activity is primary, secondary or tertiary.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.1] Record its raw materials, where they come from, and the finished products it makes.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.2] Note its markets: local shops and customers, other Irish retailers, or export markets.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.3] Assess its contribution to the area through jobs, local suppliers and the income it brings to rural communities, which is why the industry matters locally.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.4] List the careers it offers, such as production operative, technologist, quality staff, chef or sales and marketing roles.
