TARGET hom-carbohydrates-s1: 1. Formation of carbohydrates in plants
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.b] Green plants make their own food. They build glucose, a carbohydrate, from carbon dioxide and water, using light energy, and this process is photosynthesis.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.0] Plants are producers because they make their own food. Animals, including people, are consumers: they get their carbohydrate by eating plants, or animals that ate plants.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.1] Word equation: carbon dioxide + water, in the presence of light and chlorophyll, gives glucose + oxygen.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.2] Balanced equation: 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂, with light energy and chlorophyll written above the arrow. It is balanced because there are 6 C, 12 H and 18 O on each side.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.3] Four things are required: light energy, chlorophyll, carbon dioxide and water. There are two products: glucose and oxygen.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.4] Light energy is changed into chemical energy stored in the glucose molecule. This stored energy is what a person later releases from carbohydrate in respiration.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.5] Chlorophyll is the green pigment (colouring substance) in the chloroplasts, the parts of leaf cells where photosynthesis takes place. It absorbs light energy, mainly red and blue light, and uses it to drive the reaction.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.6] Chlorophyll reflects green light rather than absorbing it, which is why leaves look green.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.0.7] Diagram: leaf cross-section labelled with stomata, palisade cells containing chloroplasts, xylem and phloem, with arrows showing carbon dioxide entering, oxygen leaving, water entering through the xylem and sucrose leaving through the phloem.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.1.b] A leaf can only make glucose if carbon dioxide, water and light all reach its cells. Each arrives by its own route, and the oxygen made leaves by one of them.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.1.0] Carbon dioxide enters the leaf from the air by diffusion (spreading from where there is more of it to where there is less) through stomata, small pores mainly on the lower surface of the leaf.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.1.1] Root hair cells absorb water from the soil, and it is carried up the stem to the leaves in the xylem, the tubes that carry water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.1.2] Light comes from the sun.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.1.3] Oxygen, the by-product of photosynthesis, diffuses out of the leaf through the stomata. Some of it is used by the plant in its own respiration.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.1.4] Respiration goes on in plant cells all the time, day and night, whereas photosynthesis needs light. This is why plants do not simply breathe in carbon dioxide by day and oxygen by night.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.b] A plant does not keep its glucose as glucose. It uses it for energy, stores it, moves it and builds with it, and each job uses a different form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.0] Respiration: some glucose is used immediately to release energy for growth and the plant's other processes.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.1] Starch: surplus glucose is joined into starch, an insoluble polysaccharide (a large carbohydrate made of many glucose units) kept as a store in leaves, stems, roots and tubers such as potatoes.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.2] Starch is insoluble, so it can be stored in large amounts without affecting the water balance of the cell.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.3] Sucrose: glucose is converted to sucrose, which is carried in the phloem, the transport tubes for sugars, from the leaves to the other parts of the plant, such as growing tips, roots, fruits and storage organs.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.4] Cellulose: glucose is joined into cellulose, the main structural material of plant cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.2.5] Plants also make fruit sugars (glucose and fructose), pectin in their cell walls, and, with nitrogen from the soil, proteins, and from glucose, oils and other lipids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.3.b] Every carbohydrate in a person's diet starts in a plant. This link is the opening of any answer about where carbohydrates come from.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.3.0] Plants make glucose by photosynthesis and join it into larger carbohydrates, so every sugar, starch and fibre in the diet was first made by a plant.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.3.1] Plant foods supply carbohydrate directly, and animal foods supply it indirectly because animal feed is plant material.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.3.2] Lactose in milk is made by the animal from glucose; it is not made by plants.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.3.3] Fibre in the diet is cellulose, which is plant cell-wall material.
[hom-carbohydrates-s1.3.4] Photosynthesis also releases the oxygen that people use in aerobic respiration (respiration using oxygen).
