TARGET hom-unemployment-poverty-s4: 4. Concepts of poverty
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.b] Poverty is more than having a low income. It is a lack of the income and resources needed for basic needs and for normal participation in society, and it is examined through two concepts.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.0] Poverty means being without enough income and resources (material, cultural and social) for an acceptable standard of living.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.1] It means inadequate food, clothing, shelter and warmth, because there is not enough income or resources to pay for them.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.2] It also means being unable to take part in socially acceptable activities that most people take for granted, which is why poverty brings social exclusion (being shut out from ordinary community life).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.3] Poverty includes the lack of opportunities, comforts and self-respect that other people enjoy, so it cannot be measured by income alone.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.4] Poverty is examined through two concepts: absolute poverty and relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.b] Absolute poverty is the most severe form: a person cannot afford the things needed to stay alive and healthy.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.0] Absolute poverty is income too low to meet basic physical needs for food, clothing, warmth and shelter, so health and survival are threatened.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.1] The standard is a fixed minimum, set regardless of how other people in society live. It does not change with the living standards of the surrounding society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.2] Absolute poverty is rare in a developed country like Ireland, but homeless people, and people living without proper housing, clothing or medical care, may experience it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.3] In Ireland the main concern is relative poverty rather than absolute poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.b] Relative poverty is judged against the living standards of the society in which a person lives, so it exists even in a wealthy country.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.0] A person in relative poverty lives below the standard of living that society regards as basic, so cannot take part in activities that are normal for others.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.1] The poverty line is the level of income below which a household is counted as poor: the estimated minimum income for basic food, clothing and shelter and an acceptable standard of living. A household below it is at risk of poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.2] In Irish official statistics the Central Statistics Office (CSO) sets the line at 60% of median equivalised household income. The median is the middle income, and equivalised means adjusted for household size and composition.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.3] Because the poverty line moves as incomes change, relative poverty can persist even when incomes rise.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.4] Examples of relative poverty: being unable to afford a warm waterproof coat, two pairs of strong shoes, adequate heating for the home, or a meal with meat, chicken or fish (or a vegetarian equivalent) every second day.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.5] Photographs of the same type of home or setting from different times and places show that the accepted standard of living changes, which is why poverty is relative.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.6] A child's clothing or a family's home in a photograph from another time or place may look very different from today's norm, yet be an ordinary standard for its own society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.b] Governments need numbers to track poverty. Two official measures are used, and the second is stricter than the first.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.0] The at-risk-of-poverty rate is the share of people whose income is below the poverty line (60% of median income). It is another way of describing relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.1] Enforced deprivation means being unable to afford basic items that most people regard as normal, such as those in the examples of relative poverty. It is not by choice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.2] The CSO counts a household as deprived if it lacks two or more of 11 listed items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.3] Consistent poverty means being both below the poverty line and in enforced deprivation, lacking at least two of the 11 items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.4] Consistent poverty is narrower and more severe than the at-risk-of-poverty rate, because it counts only people who both have a low income and cannot afford the basics.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.5] The CSO publishes these measures every year from the Survey on Income and Living Conditions (SILC), an EU-wide household survey. Social Justice Ireland also publishes a count of people in poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.b] The level of poverty in Ireland has followed the economy and unemployment. It is also not spread evenly across the population.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.0] Poverty fell from the 1960s and 1970s onwards as living standards rose, but rose again during the recession of the 1980s, when unemployment was high.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.1] Poverty fell during the Celtic Tiger period of economic growth in the 1990s and early 2000s, because employment grew.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.2] Poverty rose again after the 2008 downturn as unemployment soared and living costs rose, and rose further with high housing and living costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.3] The COVID-19 pandemic increased the risk of poverty for people who lost work or income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.4] Today poverty is a real problem for a sizeable minority of people, with children and some adult groups at higher risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.5] Poverty is not spread evenly. Risk differs by household type, by labour-market status, by region (urban black spots and some rural areas) and by age.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.b] Some groups face a higher risk of poverty because of low income, limited skills, or caring responsibilities. Each group below has its own reason for being at risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.0] Lone-parent households, particularly those without paid work, because they have one income and childcare costs make it hard to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.1] Unemployed people and long-term unemployed people, who depend on welfare or low-paid work; welfare payments are lower than wages, so they may be too low for their needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.2] Low-paid workers (the working poor), including those on the minimum wage who may not qualify for state supports, and people in insecure or part-time work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.3] People with disabilities or long-term illness, who may be unable to work or face extra costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.4] Older people, especially those living alone on a State pension in poorly insulated or substandard homes that are costly to heat, which can lead to illness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.5] Large families, because there are more children to feed and clothe on one income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.6] Travellers and other ethnic minorities, who face discrimination, lower education and employment, and poorer accommodation, including in the labour market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.7] Homeless people, who face isolation and poor health; asylum seekers, who have a restricted right to work while awaiting a decision; and refugees, who may have low income and language barriers and lack skills and qualifications.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.b] Education and opportunity also shape who is at risk. Two further groups are linked to low qualifications and low pay.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.0] Early school leavers and people with few qualifications are at risk because they face low pay and poor job prospects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.1] People in households in a cycle of deprivation lack education and opportunity, so low income passes from one generation to the next.
