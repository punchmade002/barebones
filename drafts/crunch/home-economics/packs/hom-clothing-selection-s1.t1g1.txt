TARGET hom-clothing-selection-s1: 1. Textile care
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.b] A garment's care label is not arbitrary: a fabric's properties come from its fibres, its yarn and its construction, and these decide how it reacts to water, heat, friction and detergent. Knowing the fibre family tells you how to wash, dry and iron it safely.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.0] Natural cellulose fibres (cotton, linen) absorb water readily, swell when wet and are strong when wet, so they can be washed hot and often. They also crease easily and tend to shrink on first washing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.1] Protein fibres (wool, silk) absorb moisture but are damaged by alkaline detergents and high temperatures, so they need a mild or wool-specific detergent, a low temperature and gentle action. Wool is washed cool, about 30 °C, as the label states.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.2] Wool can felt or shrink when agitated in hot water, which is why hot, rough washing ruins it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.3] Manufactured fibres such as polyester and nylon absorb very little water, dry quickly, keep their shape and resist creasing. They can attract static, and they melt or glaze under a hot iron or high tumble-dry heat.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.4] Blended fabrics combine the care needs of both fibres, so the more sensitive (most delicate) fibre sets the washing temperature and ironing heat. For example, a wool/polyester mixture is washed and ironed as wool.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.5] Fabric construction matters too: a tightly woven fabric resists water and wind more than a loosely knitted one, because the gaps between yarns are smaller. Knitted fabric stretches and can sag or shrink.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.b] Shrinkage is a permanent reduction in a fabric's length or width after laundering. It happens in three different ways, and the cause decides how to prevent it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.0] Relaxation shrinkage happens because fibres stretched during spinning, weaving and finishing relax back to their natural length in water. It is most marked in cotton, linen and viscose.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.1] Felting shrinkage happens to wool when the scales on its fibres interlock under heat, moisture and agitation. The fabric thickens and shrinks, and this cannot be reversed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.2] Heat shrinkage occurs when a fabric is washed or tumble dried hotter than it can tolerate, especially in knitted and thermoplastic fabrics (those that soften with heat).
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.3] Percentage shrinkage = (original length − new length) ÷ original length × 100.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.4] Prevention: follow the care label, wash at the stated temperature and avoid hot tumble drying, because heat is a common cause.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.5] Pre-shrunk fabric or a shrink-resistant finish reduces shrinkage in garments that will be laundered often. Washing a test sample of fabric before cutting out a garment shows how much it will shrink.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.b] Water repellence is how well a fabric resists wetting. It matters for rainwear and for choosing the right fabric for a garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.0] Three things give a fabric water repellence: a close weave or tight knit, so the gaps between yarns are too small for water to penetrate; a fibre that absorbs little water; and a water-repellent finish.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.1] Fibres that do not absorb water, such as polyester and nylon, shed water more readily than cotton or linen. Cotton and wool absorb water, so they are not naturally repellent.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.2] A water-repellent finish coats the surface of the fabric with a chemical so that water forms beads that roll off.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.3] Washing and wear reduce a finish, so a repellent garment may need re-proofing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.4] Water-repellent (showerproof) fabric resists wetting but will let water through in heavy or prolonged rain.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.5] A waterproof fabric (coated or laminated, with sealed seams) stops water passing through entirely.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.6] Breathable fabrics let perspiration vapour escape while keeping rain out, so the wearer stays dry inside as well as outside.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.b] Crease recovery is how well a fabric springs back to its smooth state after being folded or crushed. It decides how much ironing a garment needs.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.0] A crease forms when fibres are bent and the bonds between them shift, holding the fabric in the folded position.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.1] Cotton, linen and viscose have poor recovery, so they crease easily and need ironing. In cotton and linen, cellulose chains form hydrogen bonds that break and re-form when the fabric is wet and dried.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.2] Wool recovers well because its fibres are elastic, and creases hang out or can be relaxed by steaming.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.3] Polyester and nylon have good crease recovery because their fibres spring back to shape once the folding force is removed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.4] Crease-resistant finishes, such as resin finishes on cotton or linen, improve recovery and cut down ironing. Blending with polyester also helps.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.5] Care link: damp garments can be shaken and hung to reduce creases, and ironing temperature must match the fibre.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.b] Care labels use a fixed set of symbols so that anyone can read how to launder a garment safely, whatever language the label is in. Reading the label before buying and before washing helps you avoid shrinkage, colour loss and damage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.0] The symbols are international (standard ISO 3758; GINETEX is the European association behind them) and appear in a fixed order: washing, bleaching, drying, ironing, then professional care.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.1] Wash: a washtub means the garment may be machine washed, and the number or dots in the tub give the maximum water temperature. Bars under the tub mean gentler action.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.2] A hand in the tub means hand wash only, usually at a low temperature with gentle action. A cross over the tub means do not wash in water.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.3] Bleach: a triangle means bleach may be used; a crossed-out triangle means do not bleach.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.4] Drying: a square containing a circle means tumble drying is allowed, with dots showing the heat setting; a crossed tumble-dry symbol means do not tumble dry.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.5] A square with a horizontal line means dry flat; a square with one vertical line means line dry. Drip dry means hang the wet garment to drip without wringing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.6] Iron: one dot means a cool (low) iron, two dots a warm or medium iron and three dots a hot iron; a crossed iron means do not iron.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.7] Dry cleaning: a circle shows dry cleaning is allowed, and the letters inside tell the cleaner which solvent process to use. A crossed circle means do not dry clean.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.8] Diagram: draw and label the washtub (with 30 or 40 or dots), hand wash, triangle with a cross, tumble dry, dry flat, iron with two dots, and circle (dry clean).
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.b] A modern laundry detergent contains several components, each with a specific job in removing soil from the fabric. Choosing a detergent to match the fabric depends on knowing what each does.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.0] Surfactants reduce the surface tension of water, so the water can wet the fibres thoroughly. One end of a surfactant molecule attracts water and the other attracts oil and grease, so they lift oily and greasy soil away and break it into small particles.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.1] Surfactants hold the loosened soil suspended in the wash water, so it is rinsed away and does not redeposit on the fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.2] Enzymes (in biological detergents) are proteins that act as biological catalysts, speeding up the breakdown of stains: protease breaks down protein stains such as blood and egg, amylase breaks down starch stains and lipase breaks down fat stains.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.3] Enzymes work best in warm, not hot, water and are damaged or destroyed by very high temperatures.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.4] Fluorescent whitening agents (optical brighteners) absorb ultraviolet light and re-emit it as blue-white light, which makes white and light fabrics look brighter and whiter.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.5] Fluorescent agents do not clean or remove stains; they only change how the fabric reflects light.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.6] Match the detergent to the fabric: use a mild detergent for wool and silk. A biological detergent is not for wool or silk, because enzymes can attack their protein fibres.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.b] Simple tests let you compare how different fabrics behave, using their general properties of shrinkage, water repellence and crease recovery. They only give useful results if the test is fair.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.0] Shrinkage test: mark and measure a square on each sample, wash and dry under identical conditions, remeasure and calculate the percentage shrinkage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.1] Water-repellence test: place drops of water on each sample (flat or on a slope) and time or observe whether the drops bead and roll off, or soak in and darken the fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.2] Crease-recovery test: crumple equal-sized samples in a closed fist for one minute, release, and compare how smooth each is after a set time.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.3] Fair test: use equal-sized samples, the same conditions and the same timing, so that the only variable is the fabric. Name the fibre or fabric of each sample.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.4] Repeat the test on several samples of each fabric to give reliable results, note any variation, and record the results in a table.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.5] Draw a conclusion on how suitable each fabric is for a garment, using your results.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s1: 1. Textiles as a household resource
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.b] A textile is a material made from fibres or yarns (the threads spun from fibres) that has been woven, knitted or otherwise made into fabric. Textiles are a household resource, like food or fuel: they are bought, used, cared for and eventually replaced.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.0] Textiles serve household purposes, such as bedding, curtains, carpets, towels and upholstery, and clothing purposes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.1] Because textiles cost money and need care, choosing well saves money and effort over the life of the item.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.2] Most household textiles do several jobs at once. A curtain, for example, gives privacy, decoration and some warmth.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.b] Textiles are used throughout the home for practical and decorative purposes, often several at once. Examiners often ask for a list of functions, so learn each one with its example item.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.0] Warmth and insulation: blankets, duvets, throws and thick curtains trap still air within their fibres and layers, reducing heat loss from the body.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.1] Sound absorption (sound insulation): carpets, rugs, curtains and upholstery absorb sound and reduce echo in a room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.2] Privacy and light control: curtains, net curtains and blinds screen the room from view and control the light entering it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.3] Comfort: cushions, mattress covers, bed linen and upholstery give softness and support.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.4] Protection: oven gloves and aprons protect the user from heat and spills, carpets protect floors from wear, and mattress protectors protect the mattress.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.5] Absorbency: towels, tea towels, dishcloths and bath mats take up water from the body, from dishes or from the floor, which makes washing and drying easier.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.6] Decoration and aesthetics: curtains, cushions, wall hangings, bedspreads and rugs add colour, pattern and texture to a room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.7] Hygiene: washable textiles can be laundered to remove dirt and micro-organisms. Safety is a further function, because fire-resistant finishes make textiles safer.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.b] A textile is chosen by weighing the properties of the fabric against the job it has to do, the money available and the household's preferences. Examiners often ask for three or four of these criteria, each with an explanation.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.0] Fitness for purpose: the fabric's properties must suit its job, for example a towel needs absorbency and a curtain needs to drape well and resist fading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.1] Suitability in use: the fabric must withstand the wear, washing and conditions of the room it is used in, such as a kitchen, a living room or a child's bedroom. Purpose is the job; use is the wear and conditions, and both come under fitness.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.2] Cost: the price must fit the budget and the expected life of the item, so a durable fabric can be better value. Wool and silk cost more than most other fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.3] Personal choice: likes and dislikes, colour and style, comfort, a preference for natural or easy-care synthetic fabrics, and current trends.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.4] Aesthetic appeal: appearance, drape, weight, texture, lustre, colour and pattern, because the textile must look right in the room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.5] Care and cleaning: whether the fabric is washable or dry clean, how much ironing it needs, and its fade resistance, stain resistance and shrinkage, which affect how practical it is for the household.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.6] Safety: a flame-retardant finish or fire-resistance label is wanted, and a loose or pile surface is avoided because it burns more easily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.7] In a longer Higher Level answer, durability (how long the fabric lasts), finishes (treatments added to improve a fabric) and sustainability (the environmental effect of the fabric) are also accepted, with one developed line on each.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.b] Each property below describes how a fabric behaves in use. Define each in plain words and give an example fabric, since examiners ask what a property means and which fabric has it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.0] Absorbent: the fabric takes in and holds moisture. Cotton and linen are absorbent, which makes them suitable for towels, and an absorbent fabric also takes up dye easily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.1] Flammable: the fabric ignites and burns readily when exposed to a flame, creating a fire risk. Cotton burns easily, so flammability is not wanted in household textiles.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.2] Crease-resistant: the fabric resists wrinkling in wear and washing and returns towards its smooth shape, so less ironing is needed. Polyester and wool resist creasing, but cotton and linen crease unless finished.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.3] Colourfast: the colour resists running in water and fading in light, so it stays bright after washing, because the dye is fixed firmly to the fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.4] Durable (hard wearing): the fabric resists abrasion, tearing and wear over a long period of use.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.5] Drape: the way a fabric hangs in folds. A good drape suits curtains and loose clothing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.6] Other properties examiners accept as desirable are warm, strong, washable, easy to dye, shrink resistant, stain resistant, quick drying and resilient (springs back into shape).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.7] Static: synthetic fibres build up static electricity, so the fabric clings, and fabric conditioner reduces this. Wool can shrink in hot washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.b] Choosing a fabric means matching its properties to the job of the item. When you recommend a fabric for a stated item, give the fabric and a reason from its properties.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.0] Cotton towels: absorbent, strong when wet and able to withstand hot washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.1] Linen tea towels: absorbent, strong and quick drying, but they crease easily unless treated.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.2] Polyester curtains: drape well, resist fading, wash easily, dry quickly and resist creasing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.3] Cotton curtains: easy to launder and dye, with a crisp appearance and strong when wet, but they shrink if not pre-shrunk.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.4] Wool blankets: warm, insulating, absorbent and elastic (resilient), but they can shrink and felt (the fibres mat together) if washed at high temperatures or carelessly.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.5] Carpets in nylon or wool: strong, hard wearing and resistant to abrasion from foot traffic.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.6] Bed linen in cotton or polyester-cotton: washable, strong and easy to iron.
