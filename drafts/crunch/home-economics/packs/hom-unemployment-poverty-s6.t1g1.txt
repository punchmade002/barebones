[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.b] A household is a group who share a home and make shared decisions about income, spending and saving. It is the basic financial unit of the economy, because everything the economy produces is ultimately bought, taxed or worked for by households.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.0] Households spend on goods and services, which supports the businesses that supply them and the jobs those businesses provide.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.1] Households pay taxes, which fund public services and State welfare payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.2] Households buy property, and property purchases generate stamp duty, a tax that is income for the State.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.3] Households supply the workforce: children who grow up in families become the future workers, so families that teach children money management also prepare the next generation.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.4] Families usually run their finances independently, but they may need State aid, such as welfare payments or help from MABS, when income falls short of need.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.b] Households receive money in different forms depending on whether, and how, their members work. The main sources are wages, salaries, pensions and State payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.0] Wages: payment for hours worked or output, usually paid weekly or fortnightly, often for manual or hourly work. Because pay follows hours, a week with fewer hours means a smaller wage.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.1] Salaries: a fixed annual amount paid in regular instalments, usually for non-manual or professional work, so the pay is the same each period.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.2] Pensions: income after retirement from an employer's scheme, a personal pension fund or the State. Money paid into a pension while working is a voluntary deduction.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.3] Social welfare allowances and benefits: payments made by the State to people who are unemployed, ill, disabled, caring, retired, widowed or raising children.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.4] Other sources, in one line: profit from self-employment, rent from property, interest on savings, gifts and maintenance payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.b] Two households with similar jobs can still have very different incomes. Age, gender, social class, culture and employment pattern all shape how much a household earns and what it must spend.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.0] Age: income tends to rise with experience and promotion, then falls at retirement unless a pension has been arranged.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.1] Age: teenagers can earn part-time income, which eases the financial strain on the family.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.2] Gender: equal-pay law requires equal pay for the same work, but women are still under-represented in senior management, which men hold more of in many sectors. Women are also more likely to work part-time or take career breaks for caring.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.3] Social class: low qualifications or early school leaving can lead to low pay or unemployment, while higher education widens access to better-paid work.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.4] Culture: cultural attitudes shape whether women take paid work, which occupations are seen as suitable, and which spending priorities a household sets.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.5] Employment pattern: the number of family members in paid work, and whether hours are full-time, part-time, job-sharing, flexible or seasonal, all change household income.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.6] Disability, illness or addiction can reduce earning capacity and raise household costs, so income falls while spending needs rise.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.b] The pay a person earns is not the pay they take home, because deductions are taken off first. Knowing which figure is which matters for both exams and household planning.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.0] Gross income is income earned before any deductions are made.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.1] Net income is take-home pay: gross income after deductions have been made.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.2] Compulsory deductions from gross pay are PAYE, PRSI and USC. The law requires them, so the employee cannot choose to avoid them.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.3] Voluntary deductions are chosen by the employee and include union subscriptions, pension contributions, additional voluntary contributions (AVCs, extra payments into a pension), health insurance, savings, social club fees and holiday fund payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.4] Budgets are planned from net income, because net income is the money a household actually has to spend and save.

[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.0] PAYE (Pay As You Earn) is income tax deducted by the employer from each pay, based on the employee's earnings, and paid to the Revenue Commissioners (the State). It rises with income through a standard rate and a higher rate.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.1] Revenue works out the employee's credits and rate band, and the employer uses these details to calculate the PAYE to deduct.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.2] PAYE and USC go to the State to pay for public services such as health, education, roads and welfare.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.3] PRSI (Pay Related Social Insurance) is a compulsory percentage of pay, so it is related to income. It is paid by the employee, through deduction, and separately by the employer, and it funds social insurance benefits.

[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.5] USC (Universal Social Charge) is a compulsory charge on gross income, calculated at rates that rise in bands as income rises. It is separate from income tax.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.6] Employees with gross income below a set threshold are exempt from paying USC.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.b] A tax credit lowers the tax bill itself, so it is worth the same as an equal amount of tax saved. It is not a reduction in the pay the tax is worked out on.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.0] A tax credit reduces the amount of income tax a person has to pay. Examples are the personal credit and the employee (PAYE) credit.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.1] Income tax is worked out on gross income first, and the person's tax credits are then subtracted from the tax calculated. A credit does not reduce income before tax is worked out.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.2] Revenue works out the employee's credits and standard rate cut-off point (the income up to which the lower rate applies), and the employer uses them when deducting PAYE.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.3] Tax credits do not reduce the USC, which is charged separately on gross income.

[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.0] Social insurance payments are paid to people who have made the minimum PRSI contributions, so the person has earned them, and they are not means tested.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.1] Examples of social insurance payments: Jobseeker's Benefit, Illness Benefit, Maternity Benefit, Carer's Benefit and the contributory State pension.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.2] Social assistance payments are paid from taxes to people whose PRSI contributions are insufficient to qualify for social insurance payments, and they are means tested.

[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.0] Child Benefit: a monthly universal payment for each qualifying child. It is not means tested and does not depend on PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.1] Working Family Payment: a weekly payment to employees on low pay who have children, and means tested against a family-size income limit. It replaced the name Family Income Supplement.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.2] Supplementary Welfare Allowance: means tested, and paid to people whose means cannot meet their basic needs or those of their dependants.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.3] Supplementary Welfare Allowance can also be a temporary payment for people awaiting another benefit while their claim is being processed.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.4] Maternity Benefit: a social insurance payment for employees on maternity leave.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.5] Other payments a student may name: Carer's Allowance, Disability Allowance, Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance and Fuel Allowance.

[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.0] The contributory State pension (formerly called the old age pension) is based on the claimant's record of PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.1] The contributory pension is not means tested, so other income does not normally remove entitlement to it.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.2] The non-contributory State pension is means-tested social assistance for an older person who lacks enough PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.3] Eligibility for the non-contributory pension depends on means and on residence conditions, including habitual residence in the State (a settled, normal home there).

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s4: 4. Concepts of poverty
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.b] Poverty is more than having a low income. It is a lack of the income and resources needed for basic needs and for normal participation in society, and it is examined through two concepts.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.0] Poverty means being without enough income and resources (material, cultural and social) for an acceptable standard of living.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.1] It means inadequate food, clothing, shelter and warmth, because there is not enough income or resources to pay for them.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.2] It also means being unable to take part in socially acceptable activities that most people take for granted, which is why poverty brings social exclusion (being shut out from ordinary community life).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.3] Poverty includes the lack of opportunities, comforts and self-respect that other people enjoy, so it cannot be measured by income alone.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.0.4] Poverty is examined through two concepts: absolute poverty and relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.b] Absolute poverty is the most severe form: a person cannot afford the things needed to stay alive and healthy.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.0] Absolute poverty is income too low to meet basic physical needs for food, clothing, warmth and shelter, so health and survival are threatened.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.1] The standard is a fixed minimum, set regardless of how other people in society live. It does not change with the living standards of the surrounding society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.2] Absolute poverty is rare in a developed country like Ireland, but homeless people, and people living without proper housing, clothing or medical care, may experience it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.1.3] In Ireland the main concern is relative poverty rather than absolute poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.b] Relative poverty is judged against the living standards of the society in which a person lives, so it exists even in a wealthy country.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.0] A person in relative poverty lives below the standard of living that society regards as basic, so cannot take part in activities that are normal for others.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.1] The poverty line is the level of income below which a household is counted as poor: the estimated minimum income for basic food, clothing and shelter and an acceptable standard of living. A household below it is at risk of poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.2] In Irish official statistics the Central Statistics Office (CSO) sets the line at 60% of median equivalised household income. The median is the middle income, and equivalised means adjusted for household size and composition.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.3] Because the poverty line moves as incomes change, relative poverty can persist even when incomes rise.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.4] Examples of relative poverty: being unable to afford a warm waterproof coat, two pairs of strong shoes, adequate heating for the home, or a meal with meat, chicken or fish (or a vegetarian equivalent) every second day.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.5] Photographs of the same type of home or setting from different times and places show that the accepted standard of living changes, which is why poverty is relative.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.2.6] A child's clothing or a family's home in a photograph from another time or place may look very different from today's norm, yet be an ordinary standard for its own society.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.b] Governments need numbers to track poverty. Two official measures are used, and the second is stricter than the first.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.0] The at-risk-of-poverty rate is the share of people whose income is below the poverty line (60% of median income). It is another way of describing relative poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.1] Enforced deprivation means being unable to afford basic items that most people regard as normal, such as those in the examples of relative poverty. It is not by choice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.2] The CSO counts a household as deprived if it lacks two or more of 11 listed items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.3] Consistent poverty means being both below the poverty line and in enforced deprivation, lacking at least two of the 11 items.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.4] Consistent poverty is narrower and more severe than the at-risk-of-poverty rate, because it counts only people who both have a low income and cannot afford the basics.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.3.5] The CSO publishes these measures every year from the Survey on Income and Living Conditions (SILC), an EU-wide household survey. Social Justice Ireland also publishes a count of people in poverty.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.b] The level of poverty in Ireland has followed the economy and unemployment. It is also not spread evenly across the population.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.0] Poverty fell from the 1960s and 1970s onwards as living standards rose, but rose again during the recession of the 1980s, when unemployment was high.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.1] Poverty fell during the Celtic Tiger period of economic growth in the 1990s and early 2000s, because employment grew.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.2] Poverty rose again after the 2008 downturn as unemployment soared and living costs rose, and rose further with high housing and living costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.3] The COVID-19 pandemic increased the risk of poverty for people who lost work or income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.4] Today poverty is a real problem for a sizeable minority of people, with children and some adult groups at higher risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.4.5] Poverty is not spread evenly. Risk differs by household type, by labour-market status, by region (urban black spots and some rural areas) and by age.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.b] Some groups face a higher risk of poverty because of low income, limited skills, or caring responsibilities. Each group below has its own reason for being at risk.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.0] Lone-parent households, particularly those without paid work, because they have one income and childcare costs make it hard to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.1] Unemployed people and long-term unemployed people, who depend on welfare or low-paid work; welfare payments are lower than wages, so they may be too low for their needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.2] Low-paid workers (the working poor), including those on the minimum wage who may not qualify for state supports, and people in insecure or part-time work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.3] People with disabilities or long-term illness, who may be unable to work or face extra costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.4] Older people, especially those living alone on a State pension in poorly insulated or substandard homes that are costly to heat, which can lead to illness.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.5] Large families, because there are more children to feed and clothe on one income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.6] Travellers and other ethnic minorities, who face discrimination, lower education and employment, and poorer accommodation, including in the labour market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.5.7] Homeless people, who face isolation and poor health; asylum seekers, who have a restricted right to work while awaiting a decision; and refugees, who may have low income and language barriers and lack skills and qualifications.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.b] Education and opportunity also shape who is at risk. Two further groups are linked to low qualifications and low pay.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.0] Early school leavers and people with few qualifications are at risk because they face low pay and poor job prospects.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s4.6.1] People in households in a cycle of deprivation lack education and opportunity, so low income passes from one generation to the next.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s1: 1. Defining unemployment
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.b] Being out of work is not the same as being unemployed. Unemployment is being without paid work while being willing and able to take up work, and statisticians apply a strict three-part test.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.0] Three conditions must all be met: the person has no paid employment, is available to start work, and is actively seeking work. Missing any one of them means the person is not counted as unemployed.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.1] Paid work is the test, so unpaid work such as volunteering or caring at home does not make a person employed.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.2] Available for work: the person could start work within a short period, normally within two weeks, so someone who could not take a job for months is not counted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.3] Actively seeking work: the person has taken specific steps to find work, such as applying for posts, answering advertisements, contacting employers or registering with Intreo, the Department of Social Protection's public employment service. Registering is one such step, not proof on its own.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.4] Students, people in full-time home duties, retired people and those unable to work through illness or disability are not counted as unemployed, because they are not available for work or are not seeking it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.5] A person in part-time work who wants more hours is counted as employed in the standard measure (such a person is underemployed).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.b] To judge how serious unemployment is, a count of unemployed people has to be set against the number of people who could be working. That is what the unemployment rate does.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.0] The labour force is the number of people in employment plus the number of unemployed people.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.1] The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labour force, multiplied by 100.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.2] A rate is more useful than a raw total when comparing periods or areas, because the size of the labour force changes over time.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.3] The Central Statistics Office (CSO) measures unemployment in Ireland using the definition set by the International Labour Organization (ILO). Its Labour Force Survey is a household survey that gives the standardised unemployment rate, the official measure, published monthly and quoted seasonally adjusted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.4] The Live Register is the Department of Social Protection's count of people claiming jobseeker payments (signing on), mainly Jobseeker's Benefit and Jobseeker's Allowance. It includes some part-time, casual and seasonal workers, so it is not the official unemployment figure.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.b] Unemployment is also described by how long it lasts, because a short gap between jobs is a very different problem from years without work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.0] Short-term unemployment is a brief spell out of work, usually between jobs or after leaving education.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.1] Frictional unemployment is short-term unemployment caused by the normal time workers take to move from one job to another, for example while applications are made and interviews arranged.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.2] Long-term unemployment is being out of work for one year or more.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.3] Long-term unemployment is harder to escape because skills, habits and confidence erode with time, which makes re-entry to work harder.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.4] Young people have tended to have higher rates of unemployment than older workers, especially in downturns.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.b] The extent of unemployment means how many people are unemployed, as a rate and as a number, and how that changes over time. It follows the economic cycle: it rises in recession and falls in growth.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.0] The Labour Force Survey, a household survey by the Central Statistics Office, produces the standardised unemployment rate. The Live Register, kept by the Department of Social Protection, counts people claiming jobseeker payments, including some part-time workers, so it is not the standard measure.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.1] Unemployment was low in the boom years of the 2000s, then rose sharply after the economic downturn that began in 2008, with construction and related professions worst affected.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.2] Retail and service industries also shed jobs during the downturn as consumer spending fell.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.3] Unemployment peaked at roughly 15% in the early 2010s and then fell steadily as the economy recovered and jobs returned in construction, retail, services and multinational firms.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.4] Emigration rose during the downturn. It held the measured rate down, because people who left are no longer in the labour force, so the rate alone understates the damage.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.5] Unemployment is not spread evenly. It is higher among young people and among people with few or no qualifications, and rates differ between regions.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.6] Unemployment black spots, areas that lost their main employer, tend to keep higher rates for longer than areas with plenty of work. Long-term unemployment forms a hard core that remains when the overall rate falls.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.7] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused a sudden temporary rise in joblessness, and the State paid a temporary pandemic unemployment payment.
