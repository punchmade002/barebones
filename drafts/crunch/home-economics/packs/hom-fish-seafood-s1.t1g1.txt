[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.b] Fish and shellfish fall into three groups. The groups are based mainly on where the fat is found, and shellfish are then split by the kind of shell they have.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.0] White fish have little or no fat in the flesh, because their fat is stored in the liver (this is why cod liver oil exists). Examples are cod, haddock, whiting, plaice and sole.

[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.2] Crustacean shellfish have a hard, jointed outer shell. Examples are lobster, crab and prawns.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.3] Mollusc shellfish have a soft body inside a hard shell with hinged halves that open and close. Examples are oysters, mussels and cockles.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.4] Fish flesh is made of short muscle fibres called myomeres, separated by thin layers of connective tissue (the tough tissue that holds muscle together). With less connective tissue than meat, fish is tender, cooks quickly and is easy to digest.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.5] The flesh of fish is about 65 to 80 per cent water, 0 to 18 per cent fat and 16 to 18 per cent HBV (high biological value) protein.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.0.6] Water and fat are inversely related: the fattier the fish, the less water its flesh contains.

[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.0] Protein: fish is a source of high biological value (HBV) protein, which supplies all the essential amino acids (the ones the body cannot make) needed for growth and repair.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.1] Fat: white fish has almost no fat in the flesh, while oily fish is high in polyunsaturated fat, including the omega-3 fatty acids EPA and DHA. Fat-soluble vitamins are carried in this oil.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.2] Carbohydrate: fish contains none, so it is best served with a starchy food.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.3] B group vitamins are supplied by all fish.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.4] Vitamins A and D are supplied by oily fish and by fish liver oils such as cod liver oil. Vitamin C is supplied by oysters, but fish is otherwise a poor source of it.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.5] Iodine and fluorine come from sea fish, and phosphorus, potassium and zinc from most fish.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.6] Calcium comes from canned fish eaten with the soft, edible bones, such as sardines and canned salmon, and from shellfish. Iron comes from shellfish and sardines.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.1.7] Cholesterol is present in shellfish, which matters for anyone on a low-cholesterol diet. Water makes up 65 to 80 per cent of the flesh.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.b] Oily fish is the main food source of these two nutrients, and both do important jobs in the body.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.0] Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that the body cannot make in the amounts it needs, so they must be obtained from food.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.1] Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, not saturated, and oily fish is their main dietary source. The types found in it are EPA and DHA.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.2] Omega-3 fatty acids make blood platelets (the cell fragments that help blood clot) less sticky, so fewer blood clots form.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.3] Omega-3 fatty acids reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke and circulatory disease, because fewer clots and less cholesterol mean the blood vessels are less likely to block.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.4] Omega-3 fatty acids reduce the build-up of cholesterol in the arteries and can help lower high blood pressure.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.5] DHA also forms part of brain and eye tissue.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.6] Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin found in oily fish and fish liver oils. It helps the body absorb calcium (and phosphorus), giving healthy bones and teeth.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.2.7] A lack of vitamin D causes rickets in children and osteomalacia (soft bones) in adults. Vitamin D (the form is D3, cholecalciferol) is heat-stable: it is not destroyed by normal cooking and is not affected by oxygen, acids or alkalis.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.b] Dietetic value means how well fish suits the needs of different people and diets. A good answer links each feature of fish to a person or diet that benefits from it.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.0] Fish is easy to digest, because its short muscle fibres and low connective tissue make the flesh soft. This suits children, the elderly, convalescents and invalids.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.1] Its HBV protein supports growth in children and adolescents and repair of tissue in all age groups, and fish is a good alternative to meat in the main meal.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.2] White fish is low in fat and energy, so it suits low-calorie, low-fat and weight-reducing diets.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.3] Oily fish is recommended for people at risk of coronary heart disease, or with high cholesterol or high blood pressure. Its polyunsaturated fats, including omega-3, are linked to lower blood cholesterol and a lower risk of heart disease.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.4] Shellfish contains cholesterol, so people on a low-cholesterol diet should take it with care.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.5] Fish contains no carbohydrate, so it should be served with starchy foods such as potatoes, rice, pasta or bread. Fish in breadcrumbs, batter or sauce does contain some carbohydrate.
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.6] Iodine in sea fish helps prevent goitre (a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck caused by too little iodine).
[hom-fish-seafood-s1.3.7] Fish is versatile: it can be prepared by many cooking methods and cooks quickly, which saves time and fuel. Canned and frozen fish products make fish available all year, out of season.

[hom-fish-seafood-s1.4.4] Shellfish and tuna consumption rose because of foreign travel and eating abroad, trendy recipes, falling cost and farming.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s1: 1. Fatty acids
[hom-lipids-s1.0.b] Fatty acids are the building blocks of fats and oils (lipids). They are sorted into three classes by the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain, and that count decides how the fat behaves.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.0] A fatty acid is a long chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. It has a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end and a methyl group (–CH₃) at the other.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.1] Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds between carbon atoms, so every carbon carries its full quota of hydrogen atoms. Saturated means saturated with hydrogen.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.2] Monounsaturated fatty acids have one double bond (C=C) in the carbon chain, so two neighbouring carbons each hold one hydrogen fewer.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.3] Polyunsaturated fatty acids have two or more double bonds in the carbon chain.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.4] Saturated fats are generally solid at room temperature, while fats rich in monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids are generally liquid oils. Coconut oil and palm oil are plant oils that are rich in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.5] Examples: stearic acid (or butyric acid) is saturated, oleic acid is monounsaturated, and linoleic acid (or linolenic acid) is polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.b] The shape of the carbon chain explains why some fats are solid and others are liquid. Higher Level questions ask you to describe and draw each type.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.0] Saturated fatty acids contain only single C–C bonds and are fully saturated with hydrogen. The chain is straight and flexible, so the chains pack tightly together and the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.1] Each cis C=C double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid puts a kink (bend) in the chain, so the molecules pack less tightly and the fat stays liquid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.2] A monounsaturated fatty acid has one double bond and so one kink. A polyunsaturated fatty acid has two or more double bonds, so it has one kink for each cis double bond and is liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.3] Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, with the first double bond between the third and fourth carbon atoms counted from the methyl (omega) end. Omega-6 fatty acids have their first double bond at the sixth carbon from the methyl end.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.4] Diagram: draw a zig-zag or a simple row of carbons for a saturated, a monounsaturated and a polyunsaturated fatty acid. Label the –COOH (carboxyl) end, the –CH₃ (methyl) end and mark each C=C double bond.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.b] A double bond can hold its two hydrogen atoms in two different arrangements. The arrangement changes the shape of the chain and how the fat acts in the body.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.0] Cis and trans describe the hydrogen atoms at one double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid, not how many double bonds there are.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.1] In a cis fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on the same side of the carbon chain (both above or both below), so the chain bends.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.2] In a trans fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight, like a saturated fatty acid.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.3] Most naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids are cis, for example those in plant oils such as cooking oils and in oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.4] Trans fatty acids form when oils are partially hydrogenated in food manufacture, so they are found in some processed foods such as biscuits, pastries and fried takeaway foods. Small natural amounts occur in the fat of ruminant animals, in dairy fat and in beef and lamb fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.b] Most fatty acids can be made by the body, but a few cannot. These essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated and must come from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.0] Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that the body cannot manufacture (or cannot make in enough quantity), so they must be obtained from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.1] Linoleic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) and linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid) are the two essential fatty acids most often named.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.2] Arachidonic acid is also counted as essential by examiners. Strictly the body forms it only from linoleic acid, when enough linoleic acid is eaten.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.3] Essential fatty acids are needed for growth, for the structure of cell membranes, for the nervous system and brain, and for the immune response.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.4] The body uses essential fatty acids to make prostaglandins, hormone-like compounds that regulate blood clotting, blood pressure and inflammation.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.5] Good sources include vegetable oils such as sunflower, corn, soya and rapeseed oil, nuts and seeds, and oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.b] Omega-3 fatty acids are a family of essential polyunsaturated fats with several health benefits. They come from a limited range of foods, so it helps to know the sources by name.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.0] Omega-3 fatty acids help reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, circulatory disease and the formation of blood clots, and they can help raise HDL cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.1] Omega-3 fatty acids are linked with healthy brain activity and reduced inflammation, and they may reduce the risk of certain cancers.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.2] Oily fish are the main animal source: salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.3] Plant sources include walnuts, flaxseed (linseed), soya beans and rapeseed (canola) oil.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.4] Some eggs and milk are enriched with omega-3 by changing the diet of the hens or cows, and fish-oil supplements are also accepted as a source.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.b] The type of fatty acid in the diet affects the amount of cholesterol carried in the blood. This is why the balance of saturated, unsaturated and trans fats matters for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.0] Cholesterol travels in the blood bound to proteins, forming lipoproteins (carriers of cholesterol). LDL and HDL are these carriers, not types of cholesterol itself.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.1] LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol through the blood to the tissues and can deposit it on artery walls, where it narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis) and raises the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.2] HDL (high-density lipoprotein) removes cholesterol from the circulation, carrying it away from the tissues to the liver for removal.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.3] Saturated fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.4] Unsaturated cis fatty acids, both monounsaturated and polyunsaturated, reduce LDL cholesterol and help to raise HDL cholesterol, so they are generally good for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.5] Trans fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of CHD. They are thought to do more harm than saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.6] Monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats can lower total cholesterol when they replace saturated fats in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.7] Essential fatty acids must be eaten because the body cannot make them, and omega-3 fatty acids add the heart and brain benefits.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s1: 1. Fat-soluble vitamins
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.b] Vitamins are organic compounds that the body needs in very small amounts. They are grouped by what they dissolve in, and that one difference decides how they are absorbed, stored and lost.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.0] Vitamins are needed for normal growth, health and chemical reactions in the body. The body cannot make enough of most of them, so they must come from food.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.1] There are two classes: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) and water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C). For example, vitamin A is fat-soluble and vitamin C is water-soluble.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.2] RDA (recommended dietary allowance) is the daily amount that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, because needs change at different life stages.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.b] Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat, so they are absorbed together with dietary fat. This also explains why they are stored in the body and why too much can be harmful.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.0] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed in the small intestine along with dietary fats and are carried in the lymphatic system (the body's drainage network) before entering the blood.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.1] Because they need fat to be absorbed, a diet very low in fat, or a condition that stops fat being absorbed, can reduce vitamin intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.2] The liver and fatty (adipose) tissue store fat-soluble vitamins, so the body keeps a reserve between meals and deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.3] Because they are stored, excess intake from supplements or large amounts of liver can build up to toxic levels (toxicity). Water-soluble vitamins are mostly excreted in urine instead.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.4] Excess vitamin A (retinol) is harmful in pregnancy because it can harm the developing baby, so high-dose vitamin A supplements and large amounts of liver are avoided during pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.b] Vitamin A comes in two forms: one ready-made in animal foods and one made from a plant pigment. Its main jobs are vision, healthy surface tissues and protection of cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.0] Retinol is the active, ready-made (preformed) form of vitamin A, found only in animal foods: liver, fish liver oils, oily fish, dairy products (butter, full-fat milk and cheese), eggs and fortified margarine (margarine with vitamin A added).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.1] Beta-carotene is a plant pigment and a provitamin (a substance the body converts into a vitamin). The body converts it into retinol in the intestine, so beta-carotene is not itself vitamin A.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.2] Good beta-carotene sources include carrots, sweet potatoes, apricots, mango, red and orange peppers, tomatoes and dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach and kale, where the green hides the orange pigment.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.3] Retinol combines with the protein opsin to form rhodopsin (visual purple), the light-sensitive pigment in the rod cells of the retina. This allows vision in dim light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.4] Vitamin A keeps the cells lining the eyes, mouth, respiratory tract, urinary tract and skin healthy and moist, which protects them against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.5] Vitamin A supports growth, cell repair and a healthy immune system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.6] Beta-carotene acts as an antioxidant, neutralising free radicals (unstable molecules that damage cells).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.b] Without enough vitamin A, the eye pigment cannot be rebuilt and the body's moist surface linings break down. The eyes show the first signs.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.0] Night blindness is the earliest sign: poor vision in dim light because rhodopsin is not regenerated.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.1] Xerophthalmia: the eyes become dry and damaged, and in severe cases the cornea (the clear front of the eye) is destroyed and blindness follows.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.2] Dry, rough skin and dry, brittle hair, because the skin and other surface tissues are no longer kept healthy and moist.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.3] Poor growth, because vitamin A supports growth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.4] Lowered resistance to infection, because the mucous membranes (moist linings) dry out, crack and lose their protective role.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.5] In severe cases deficiency causes blindness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.b] Vitamin D is unusual because the body can make it in the skin when sunlight falls on it, which is why it is called the sunshine vitamin. It is the vitamin that lets the body use calcium to build bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.0] Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) forms in the skin when ultraviolet B from sunlight acts on a cholesterol derivative in the skin. It is also found in animal foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.1] Ergocalciferol (vitamin D2) is the plant and fungal form, made when mushrooms and yeast are exposed to ultraviolet light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.2] Dietary sources include oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, sardines and herring, egg yolk, liver, butter, fortified margarine and spreads, fortified milk and some fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.3] Vitamin D increases the absorption of calcium and phosphorus from the small intestine, which is why it is the vitamin needed to absorb calcium.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.4] Vitamin D raises blood calcium levels, working with parathormone (the parathyroid hormone) when blood calcium falls, so blood calcium stays at the right level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.5] Vitamin D supports the mineralisation of bones (the deposit of calcium and phosphorus that hardens them) and the formation of teeth, so it builds and maintains strong bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.6] In Ireland, weak winter sunlight means that dietary sources of vitamin D matter most in the winter months. People who cover up, stay indoors or have dark skin also make less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.b] Low vitamin D means too little calcium reaches the bones, so they stay soft. The condition has a different name in children and in adults.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.0] Rickets in children: bones soften and bend, causing bow legs, delayed growth and poor tooth formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.1] Osteomalacia in adults: bones become soft, weak and painful, and fractures can occur, because they are not properly mineralised.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.2] Low vitamin D lowers calcium absorption, which is a contributing factor in osteoporosis, where bone density falls, and in dental decay.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.b] Vitamin E is the fat-soluble vitamin whose main job is as an antioxidant: it stops oxidation damaging fatty parts of the body's cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.0] Tocopherols are the active forms of vitamin E. Alpha-tocopherol is the form the human body uses most, and the main form in the body.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.1] Rich sources are vegetable oils such as sunflower, wheatgerm and olive oil, nuts and seeds such as almonds and sunflower seeds, wholegrain cereals, avocado, green leafy vegetables and egg yolk.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.2] Vitamin E is an antioxidant that protects the membranes of cells and polyunsaturated fatty acids from oxidation (damage by free radicals).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.3] Vitamin E also helps protect the vitamin A and vitamin C in the body, and supports healthy skin and immune function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.4] Deficiency is rare in healthy people. In people who cannot absorb fat, and in premature babies, it can cause nerve and muscle damage and the early breakdown of red blood cells (a form of anaemia).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.b] Vitamin K is the clotting vitamin: the K comes from the German word for coagulation. It has two forms, one from plants and one made by bacteria in the gut.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.0] Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) comes from plants: green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, cabbage, kale and sprouts, and vegetable oils and liver.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.1] Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) is made by bacteria in the large intestine and is also found in dairy products, eggs and meat. The gut bacteria are therefore a second source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.2] The liver needs vitamin K to make the clotting factors, including prothrombin, that allow blood to clot. This is its primary function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.3] Vitamin K activates osteocalcin, a protein that binds calcium into bone, so it supports bone formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.4] Deficiency impairs blood clotting, causing bleeding and easy bruising. Long courses of antibiotics or poor fat absorption can also reduce vitamin K.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.5] The HSE offers every newborn baby a vitamin K injection within hours of birth, because their gut bacteria are not yet established and they have low stores, so they risk bleeding. It is the parents' choice.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.b] All four fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water, but they differ in what destroys them: heat, oxygen, light or alkalis. Knowing this explains how storage and cooking affect each one.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.0] Vitamin A is insoluble in water but soluble in fats and organic solvents.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.1] Vitamin A is heat-stable in normal cooking but is destroyed by prolonged high temperatures.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.2] Vitamin A is destroyed by oxygen (it is oxidised), so foods kept exposed to air lose it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.3] Vitamin D is insoluble in water and is relatively heat-stable in normal cooking and storage, so cooking oily fish loses little.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.4] Vitamin E is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking, but it is destroyed by oxidation, including by rancid fats, by light and air, and by frying at high temperature for long periods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.5] Vitamin K is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.6] Vitamin K is destroyed by light (ultraviolet) and by alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.b] To judge how good a food is as a source of vitamins, use food composition tables and food labels, and think about whether the food is fresh, processed or fortified.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.0] Food composition tables give the vitamin content of foods, usually per 100 g, so fresh and processed foods can be compared on the same basis (like for like).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.1] The mandatory nutrition declaration on a food label lists energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. Vitamins appear only when added or when a claim is made, so not listing vitamins does not mean the food has none.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.2] Where vitamins are listed, the label states the amount per 100 g (or per serving) and as a percentage of the reference intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.3] In Ireland, the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014 give effect to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) advises on food labelling and nutrition in Ireland.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.4] Fortified foods have vitamins added that the natural food does not contain, or that were lost in processing. Examples are margarine with added vitamins A and D and fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.5] Fresh produce loses vitamin C in storage, while frozen vegetables, blanched before freezing, usually retain more vitamin C than long-stored fresh vegetables.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.6] Canning and prolonged cooking destroy heat-sensitive vitamins, so a processed food may contain less of a vitamin than its fresh form.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s7: 7. Biological value
[hom-proteins-s7.0.b] Not all protein foods are equally useful to the body. Biological value (BV) measures how fully a protein food supplies the essential amino acids (the amino acids the body cannot make for itself) in the right proportions for growth and repair.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.0] High biological value (HBV) protein, also called complete protein, contains all the essential amino acids in adequate amounts, so the body can use it to build and repair tissue.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.1] HBV sources are mainly animal foods: meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.2] Soya beans are the main plant exception: they contain all the essential amino acids, so soya beans and soya products are classed as HBV.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.3] Low biological value (LBV) protein, also called incomplete protein, lacks one or more essential amino acids or contains them in small amounts.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.4] LBV sources are mainly plant foods: cereals such as wheat, rice and maize, and pulses (dried peas, beans and lentils), along with nuts and seeds. Peas, beans and lentils are LBV on their own.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.5] To sort foods quickly: eggs, fish, meat and milk are HBV; peas, rice, wheat and maize are LBV.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.b] A low biological value food is not a dead end. Two LBV foods eaten together can supply all the essential amino acids, because each makes good what the other lacks.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.0] This is called complementation, or supplementary value: one protein food makes up the deficiency of another, so the combined meal provides protein of high biological value. The foods are eaten in the same meal or the same day.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.1] Beans on toast is the classic example: bread supplies methionine, which beans lack, while beans supply lysine, which bread lacks, so together they give a complete set of essential amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.2] Hummus with pitta bread and dhal with chapatti are further complementary pairs, as are rice with lentils, lentil soup with wholemeal bread and bolognese with beans.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.3] Adding a small amount of an HBV food such as milk, cheese or egg to a cereal or pulse also raises its value, for example in macaroni cheese, rice pudding or cereal with milk.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.4] Complementation lets vegetarians and vegans meet their protein needs from plant foods alone, makes meals cheaper, and lets pulses stretch meat as meat extenders.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.b] Complementation is easy to put into practice, because everyday meals can be built around a pair of foods that fill each other's gaps. When you plan a simple meal, say which two foods complement each other and why.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.0] Pair a cereal (rice, pasta, bread or oats) with a pulse (lentils, chickpeas, beans or peas) in the same meal, for example lentil soup with wholemeal bread, because the cereal and pulse lack different essential amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.1] Add a small amount of egg, milk, cheese or yoghurt to a plant-based meal to raise its biological value.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.2] Soya products such as tofu and textured vegetable protein are HBV, so they can provide protein alone in a vegetarian meal.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.3] Use pulses as meat extenders, adding them to minced meat dishes such as bolognese or chilli to stretch the meat, which makes the meal cheaper.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.b] Protein is often a large part of a food bill, so value depends on cost per usable portion rather than the price of the pack.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.0] Compare unit prices, such as price per kilogram or per 100 g, to judge value across different pack sizes and brands.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.1] Allow for waste: bone, gristle, fat and cooking losses reduce the usable portion of meat and fish, so a cheap-looking pack may give less to eat.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.2] Cheaper cuts of meat for stewing and braising are as nutritious as expensive cuts and suit slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.3] Eggs, milk, cheese, dried or tinned pulses, tinned fish such as mackerel, tofu, textured vegetable protein and mycoprotein are low-cost sources of protein.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.4] Fish is usually cheaper when in season, for example mackerel.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.5] Own-brand products and special offers save money only if the extra food can be used before it spoils.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.6] Bulk buying saves money when the food is divided into portions and frozen, so that only the amount needed is thawed.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.7] Plan meals and use a shopping list, and use pulses or TVP to extend mince. Low-cost protein foods also fit the Food Pyramid shelf of meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, where two servings a day are recommended.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.b] Protein foods such as meat, fish and dairy spoil quickly and can carry bacteria, so safe buying and storing matter as much as price.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.0] Buy from a clean, reputable retailer, and check the use-by date, and the quality assurance and traceability information on the label, before buying.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.1] Chilled meat, fish and dairy should be kept at 5 degrees Celsius or below, so they are bought last and carried home in a cool bag.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.2] Frozen protein foods should be solid when bought and stored at −18 °C, and packs that have thawed and refrozen should be avoided.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.3] Packaging should be sealed and undamaged, and cans should not be bulging.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.4] Fresh fish has firm flesh, bright eyes and red gills; fresh meat has a fresh colour and smell.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.5] At home, refrigerate protein foods quickly, keep raw and cooked foods apart, defrost in the fridge and cook thoroughly.
