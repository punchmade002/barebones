TARGET hom-education-s3: 3. The provision of education in Ireland
[hom-education-s3.0.b] Pre-school (early childhood) education is provided by private, community and voluntary services before a child starts primary school. It is not compulsory, but it gives children a head start, so the State funds some of it.
[hom-education-s3.0.0] The Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) scheme provides a free pre-school year to children before they enter primary school, delivered through approved providers. It is a government scheme, so it costs families nothing.
[hom-education-s3.0.1] Early Start is a one-year preventative pre-school programme for three- and four-year-olds, run in selected primary schools in disadvantaged areas. It aims to give children a positive start and prevent later school failure.
[hom-education-s3.0.2] Early Start is staffed by primary teachers working with a child care assistant, so the programme is led by trained teachers.
[hom-education-s3.0.3] The Access and Inclusion Model (AIM) supports children with disabilities to take part in the free pre-school programme, so they are not left out.
[hom-education-s3.0.4] Naíonraí are pre-schools where children learn through the Irish language.
[hom-education-s3.0.5] Montessori schools, which are generally privately run, use trained teachers and encourage children to learn through play at their own pace.
[hom-education-s3.0.6] Playschools give children of a similar age the opportunity to play together and learn to share, take turns and follow group rules.
[hom-education-s3.0.7] Pre-school builds language, social skills and the routines a child needs for the transition to primary school, so children settle in more easily when they start.
[hom-education-s3.1.b] Primary school is the first stage of compulsory education. Exams ask for the ages involved, the types of school to choose from and the alternatives open to parents.
[hom-education-s3.1.0] Children may enrol in primary school from age four and must begin by age six, because school is compulsory from six.
[hom-education-s3.1.1] Primary education is an eight-year cycle, from junior infants to sixth class, and most pupils finish between the ages of 12 and 14.
[hom-education-s3.1.2] Primary schools are state-funded or private. A state-funded school is paid for by the State but run by its patron (the religious body, Educate Together or other group that runs it), so it is not state-run and not private.
[hom-education-s3.1.3] State-funded schools may be religious, multidenominational or non-denominational. Educate Together is an example of a non-denominational school, and it is not a private school.
[hom-education-s3.1.4] Specialist types include Educate Together schools, Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools, where subjects are taught through Irish), Montessori schools and single-sex schools.
[hom-education-s3.1.5] Parents may educate a child at home instead of enrolling them in a school. Home-educated children are registered with Tusla.
[hom-education-s3.2.b] Second-level education follows primary school and is made up of several programmes. Each has a set length, a particular kind of curriculum and its own form of assessment, which is how exams ask you to describe them.
[hom-education-s3.2.0] Junior Cycle is a three-year programme that uses classroom-based assessments and ends with a state examination at the end of third year, marked by the State Examinations Commission. Its award is the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement, which replaced the old Junior Certificate.
[hom-education-s3.2.1] Transition Year is an optional year between Junior Cycle and Leaving Certificate with no state examination. It focuses on personal development, work experience and self-directed learning (students taking charge of their own learning).
[hom-education-s3.2.2] The Leaving Certificate Programme is a two-year programme that ends with state examinations at the end of sixth year.
[hom-education-s3.2.3] Leaving Certificate subjects are offered at higher and ordinary level, and foundation level exists only in Irish and mathematics. Grades are converted to points for third-level entry.
[hom-education-s3.2.4] Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA) is a two-year modular programme (made up of separate units) with practical, written and oral assessment. It suits students who prefer practical learning and can lead to Post Leaving Certificate courses.
[hom-education-s3.2.5] The Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP) has two link modules: Preparation for the World of Work and Enterprise Education. Work experience is part of Preparation for the World of Work.
[hom-education-s3.3.b] Work experience is a placement in a workplace. It lets a student try out working life while still at school, and the benefits are a common exam question.
[hom-education-s3.3.0] Work experience is part of the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (inside the Preparation for the World of Work link module) and is a standard element of Transition Year.
[hom-education-s3.3.1] A placement lets students test their career preferences and see what a job actually involves, so they can make a better choice about their future.
[hom-education-s3.3.2] Students learn workplace co-operation and how working life differs from school.
[hom-education-s3.3.3] Work experience develops communication, teamwork, planning, organisation, punctuality and problem solving.
[hom-education-s3.3.4] Students learn the expectations of a workplace, including timekeeping, appearance and health and safety.
[hom-education-s3.3.5] Completing a placement builds confidence and responsibility, and gives the student a work record for a curriculum vitae.
[hom-education-s3.4.b] Third level is education after second level. It includes degree courses in colleges and further education courses, and students need a way in and a way to pay.
[hom-education-s3.4.0] Third-level institutions include universities, technological universities and institutes of technology, which offer full-time degree and diploma courses. TU Dublin, created under the Technological Universities Act 2018, is an example of a technological university.
[hom-education-s3.4.1] Most full-time undergraduate places are allocated through the Central Applications Office (CAO) using Leaving Certificate points, which are based on the grades a student earns.
[hom-education-s3.4.2] Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses are full-time further education courses, usually delivered by Education and Training Boards in second-level schools and colleges. They provide training with work experience and can give access to third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.3] The Student Universal Support Ireland (SUSI) scheme provides means-tested grants (based on family income) towards the costs of approved third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.4] The Free Fees Initiative pays tuition for eligible undergraduates, so students pay only a student contribution.
[hom-education-s3.4.5] The Higher Education Access Route (HEAR) and the Disability Access Route to Education (DARE) are reduced-points entry routes for students from disadvantaged backgrounds or with disabilities, so these students can enter with fewer points.
[hom-education-s3.5.b] Adult and second-chance education lets people return to learning at any age, whatever their earlier educational level.
[hom-education-s3.5.0] Adult education courses are available full-time, part-time, modular, day, evening and weekend, and by distance learning, so adults can fit study around work and family.
[hom-education-s3.5.1] Second-chance education gives people who left school early a route to qualifications, such as Youthreach.
[hom-education-s3.5.2] Education and Training Boards (ETBs) run adult, community and further education courses; they replaced the Vocational Education Committees in 2013.
[hom-education-s3.5.3] SOLAS, the Further Education and Training Authority, funds and co-ordinates further education and training programmes across the country.
[hom-education-s3.5.4] Adult literacy schemes provide basic reading, writing and numeracy courses for adults.
[hom-education-s3.5.5] Adult education builds skills for work, supports career change, and helps adults keep pace with new workplace technology.
[hom-education-s3.6.b] Students with special educational needs are supported through a combination of planning, specialist staff and school settings. The aim is that each student gets the help that suits their need.
[hom-education-s3.6.0] An Individual Education Plan (IEP) sets out the goals, teaching approach and supports for a student with special needs, drawn up with the school and the parents.
[hom-education-s3.6.1] The National Council for Special Education (NCSE) plans and co-ordinates special educational provision, and Special Educational Needs Organisers allocate supports to schools.
[hom-education-s3.6.2] Special Needs Assistants (SNAs) provide care and support in class for students who have care needs or who need help to take part in lessons.
[hom-education-s3.6.3] Special education teachers (formerly called learning support and resource teachers) give withdrawal teaching, taking students out of class individually or in small groups, to students with learning difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.6.4] Special classes in mainstream schools, such as autism classes, have a reduced number of pupils per teacher, so each pupil gets more attention.
[hom-education-s3.6.5] Special schools provide for students with significant general or specific learning difficulties, physical disabilities, or sensory impairments.
[hom-education-s3.6.6] Visiting teachers advise and support students who are blind or vision impaired, or deaf or hard of hearing, and the teachers who teach them.
[hom-education-s3.6.7] Assistive technology, such as text-to-speech software, helps students with reading or writing difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.7.b] Beyond the school, specialist professionals assess needs and provide therapy, and the examinations themselves are adjusted for students who need it.
[hom-education-s3.7.0] Educational psychologists assess students' needs and recommend supports, which helps the school decide what help to give.
[hom-education-s3.7.1] The Health Service Executive (HSE) provides speech and language therapy and occupational therapy to children with special needs.
[hom-education-s3.7.2] The State Examinations Commission provides reasonable accommodations in state examinations, such as a reader, a scribe or extra time, so that a student's disability does not stop them showing what they know.

CANDIDATES hom-education-s1: 1. The purpose of education
[hom-education-s1.0.b] Socialisation is the process by which a person learns the norms (accepted rules of behaviour), values, beliefs, language and behaviour of their society so that they can take part in it. The family is the first agent of socialisation, and school is a second, major one.
[hom-education-s1.0.0] Formal socialisation is the planned, stated part of school: the curriculum, timetable, school rules, uniform, codes of conduct, sanctions and rewards. It teaches punctuality, respect for authority, following instructions and responsibility, because pupils must keep to these every day.
[hom-education-s1.0.1] Informal socialisation is the unplanned part, called the hidden curriculum (what pupils pick up without being taught it directly): peer groups, playground rules, friendships, school routines, the school's ethos (its character and values) and the example set by teachers.
[hom-education-s1.0.2] The hidden curriculum teaches sharing, taking turns, cooperation, teamwork and how to get on with others. Remember that it is not the written curriculum: rules and timetable are formal, while peer groups and playground life are informal.
[hom-education-s1.0.3] School is an early and long experience of a group outside the family, so children learn to share, take turns and cooperate with others who are not relatives.
[hom-education-s1.0.4] Education transmits (passes on) the culture of a society, including its language (English and Irish), customs, history, shared values, and the rights and duties of citizens.
[hom-education-s1.0.5] School brings together children from different backgrounds, which helps them fit into wider society.
[hom-education-s1.0.6] Through school, pupils learn to accept responsibility, follow rules and respect the rights of others.
[hom-education-s1.1.b] Physical development is how the body grows stronger, more skilled and healthier. School supports it through activity, practical work and teaching about health.
[hom-education-s1.1.0] Physical education, games, sport and dance develop gross motor skills (large body movements) such as running, jumping, balance and co-ordination, as well as strength, fitness and general body development.
[hom-education-s1.1.1] Practical subjects such as Home Economics, Art, Construction Studies, Engineering and Science develop fine motor skills (small, precise hand movements), manual dexterity and hand-eye coordination. In early years, play with building blocks, jigsaws, drawing and writing does the same.
[hom-education-s1.1.2] Regular physical activity strengthens muscles and bones and builds cardiovascular fitness (a healthy heart and lungs).
[hom-education-s1.1.3] Lessons on nutrition, hygiene, safety and health (in SPHE, Home Economics and PE) give pupils the knowledge to look after their physical health.
[hom-education-s1.1.4] Physical education and school sport can establish exercise habits that a person keeps into adult life, so the benefit lasts beyond school.
[hom-education-s1.2.b] Emotional development is learning to understand and manage feelings and to get on with other people. School gives children many situations that call for this.
[hom-education-s1.2.0] Starting school moves a child towards emotional independence from parents, because they meet other adults and peers outside the home, cope with separation and learn to depend on themselves.
[hom-education-s1.2.1] Group work and class discussion develop empathy, the ability to understand and respond to the feelings of others, and help pupils express their own feelings appropriately.
[hom-education-s1.2.2] Education helps a young person form healthy relationships and friendships.
[hom-education-s1.2.3] Coping with success and failure in tests, sport, performances and tasks builds resilience (the ability to recover from setbacks), confidence, self-esteem and maturity.
[hom-education-s1.2.4] Praise and encouragement from teachers strengthen self-worth.
[hom-education-s1.2.5] The Social, Personal and Health Education (SPHE) programme teaches emotional literacy (recognising and naming feelings), relationships, coping skills and personal safety. It continues into senior cycle as a non-exam programme.
[hom-education-s1.2.6] Guidance counsellors and pastoral care (support for pupils' wellbeing) give extra help.
[hom-education-s1.3.b] Moral development is learning the difference between right and wrong and acting on it. School teaches this through its rules, its subjects and the people in it.
[hom-education-s1.3.0] The ethos of a school sets out its values and the behaviour it expects from pupils.
[hom-education-s1.3.1] School rules, sanctions and rewards teach pupils to distinguish right from wrong, fairness and accountability for their actions, because actions have consequences they must accept.
[hom-education-s1.3.2] Religious education and SPHE address moral questions such as honesty, fairness, respect and tolerance, and subjects such as History and English raise the same questions.
[hom-education-s1.3.3] Moral development is also shaped by teachers as role models and by the example of the peer group.
[hom-education-s1.3.4] Pupils learn to respect the rights, property and opinions of others and to accept responsibility, so becoming responsible citizens.
[hom-education-s1.4.b] Intellectual development is the growth of knowledge and thinking skills. A school's curriculum and assessment are built to promote it.
[hom-education-s1.4.0] A broad curriculum develops literacy, numeracy, languages, scientific reasoning, creativity and artistic skills, and builds knowledge across many subjects.
[hom-education-s1.4.1] Subjects that require analysis, argument and problem solving, and research, develop critical thinking and the ability to apply knowledge.
[hom-education-s1.4.2] Concentration and memory are trained through homework, revision and classroom tasks, because they require pupils to practise focusing and recalling.
[hom-education-s1.4.3] Examinations and continuous assessment (work marked throughout the year) measure learning and progress, and give motivation, goals and a sense of achievement.
[hom-education-s1.4.4] Competition in class, sport, quizzes and extra-curricular activities challenges students to extend their abilities.
[hom-education-s1.4.5] Education also builds curiosity and a habit of lifelong learning.
[hom-education-s1.5.b] Most people spend much of their lives working, and school is where the groundwork is laid. Education gives skills, qualifications and habits that employers look for.
[hom-education-s1.5.0] Literacy, numeracy, communication and computer or ICT skills are basic skills that employers expect in almost every job.
[hom-education-s1.5.1] Qualifications such as Junior Cycle and the Leaving Certificate, and Post Leaving Certificate, further education and third-level awards, show employers what a person has learned and open doors to jobs and higher-paid work.
[hom-education-s1.5.2] Higher levels of education are linked with better employment prospects.
[hom-education-s1.5.3] Punctuality, good attendance, respect for authority, reliability, trustworthiness, responsibility, self-discipline and teamwork are taught through daily school routines and group tasks. Social skills are learned by working with others.
[hom-education-s1.5.4] Work experience (in Transition Year, the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP) and the Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA)), career guidance and subjects chosen for a career help students connect school with the world of work.
[hom-education-s1.5.5] Vocational and practical subjects such as Business Studies teach skills used directly in employment.
[hom-education-s1.5.6] Education matters more as industry changes, because technology and a service-based economy raise the education required for employment. It also prepares people to retrain throughout their working life.
