TARGET hom-carbohydrates-s5: 5. Functions
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.b] Carbohydrate is the body's main and quickest source of energy. Sugars and starch both end up as glucose in the body, and what the body does with that glucose explains each of the functions below.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.0] Heat and energy: glucose is oxidised in the cells (respiration, the process by which cells release energy from glucose using oxygen). This releases energy for movement and growth and heat to keep body temperature at about 37 °C.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.1] The brain and nervous system rely on glucose as their main fuel, and red blood cells depend on it too, so the body needs a steady supply.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.2] Protein sparing: when enough carbohydrate is eaten to supply energy, protein is kept for growth and repair rather than being used as fuel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.3] Glycogen store: glucose not needed at once is converted to glycogen and stored in the liver and muscles as a short-term energy reserve, which can be turned back into glucose when blood glucose falls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.4] Fat store: when the glycogen stores are full, surplus glucose is converted to fat and stored as adipose tissue (body fat) under the skin and around organs, where it also insulates the body and protects organs. Excess energy of any kind is stored this way.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.5] Starch is digested to glucose in the body, so it provides the same energy as sugars, but more slowly.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.0.6] Sugars give a quick supply of energy. Starch gives a slower, steadier release, along with other nutrients, in foods such as cereals and potatoes.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.b] Dietary fibre (non-starch polysaccharide, mainly cellulose) cannot be digested by humans, so it passes through the digestive system. Its value lies in what it does on the way through the bowel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.0] Fibre absorbs water and adds bulk and softness to the contents of the intestine and to the stool, so waste is easier to pass.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.1] The bulk stimulates peristalsis, the wave-like muscular contraction that moves food and waste through the intestine. Waste leaves faster, so there is less time for toxins to form in the bowel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.2] Regular movement of the bowel prevents constipation and reduces the straining that causes haemorrhoids (piles, swollen veins at the anus).
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.3] Low fibre intake is a risk factor for constipation, haemorrhoids, diverticular disease (small pouches forming in the bowel wall, which can become inflamed, called diverticulitis) and colon (bowel) cancer.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.4] Fibre absorbs water and gives a feeling of fullness, which helps control appetite and body weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.5] Soluble fibre, such as pectin and the soluble fibre in oats, beans and lentils, slows the release of glucose into the blood, which helps keep blood sugar steady.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.6] Soluble fibre also helps to lower LDL cholesterol (the type of blood cholesterol that raises the risk of heart disease), so it reduces the risk of heart disease.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.1.7] There are two kinds. Insoluble fibre (cellulose, wheat bran) speeds the movement of waste. Soluble fibre (pectin) slows sugar absorption and lowers LDL cholesterol. Fibre provides little or no usable energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.b] A diet low in fibre and fluid slows the movement of waste through the bowel. Examiners ask for one bowel disease with an account and its symptoms, and constipation is the model answer.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.0] Constipation: stools pass too slowly through the large intestine, so they become hard and difficult to pass, causing discomfort and straining.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.1] Cause: a diet low in fibre and too little water, because without bulk and moisture the stool stays hard and peristalsis is weak.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.2] Effects: hard stools, straining, bloating and discomfort, and a risk of haemorrhoids (piles) from the straining.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.3] Prevention: eat more fibre, drink plenty of fluids and take regular exercise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.4] Haemorrhoids (piles) and diverticular disease are the other bowel conditions to name if asked, and both are linked to low fibre intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.2.5] Bowel-protecting guidelines: choose wholegrain cereals, leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, snack on fresh or dried fruit, nuts and seeds, eat whole fruit rather than juice, and drink water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.b] Sugar does far more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each use below is something sugar does for the food, and exams ask for three or more.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.0] Sweetening and flavouring cakes, desserts, drinks, sauces and sweets, which improves palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat).
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.1] Preserving jam, chutney, syrup and crystallised fruit: sugar in high concentration draws water out of micro-organisms, so spoilage is slowed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.2] Feeding yeast: fermentation of sugar by yeast produces carbon dioxide, which makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.3] Browning and caramel flavour: caramelisation and the Maillard reaction brown the surface of baked goods, glazes and crème caramel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.4] Giving the texture of fudge, fondant and boiled sweets through controlled crystallisation, since sugar crystals form their structure.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.5] Giving volume: creamed sugar and fat trap air in sponge cakes, and sugar helps egg white hold a stable foam in meringues.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.6] Coating: syrups and icing (a solution of sugar) are used for coating, and sugar syrup stops cut fruit in a fruit salad discolouring.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.3.7] Sugar holds moisture (it is hygroscopic), so cakes stay moist and keep for longer.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.b] Starch and pectin are carbohydrates that change how food holds together. Starch mainly thickens, while pectin sets fruit into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.0] Starch is a thickening agent: it gelatinises (swells and thickens) in a hot liquid, thickening sauces, soups, gravies, custards and pie fillings.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.1] In a roux the fat binds with the flour and separates the starch granules, so the sauce is lump-free.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.2] Starch binds and coats: it forms the thickening in batters and coatings and gives body to a sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.3] Starch helps structure in baking: starch in flour gelatinises as a cake, pastry or bread bakes and helps to set the crumb. The elastic structure of bread comes from gluten, a protein in wheat flour, not from starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.4] Starch browns on dry heat (dextrinisation), giving crust colour and flavour to toast and baked goods.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.5] Pectin is the gelling agent in jam, jelly and marmalade: with sugar and acid it sets the fruit mixture into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s5.4.6] Pectin is also used in food manufacture as a gelling agent and stabiliser (additive E440) in confectionery, desserts and yoghurts, and it supports shape in cooked fruit and thickens fruit fillings.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.b] Sugars are the sweet, water-loving carbohydrates, and these two properties explain why they are used so widely in cooking. Starch is the contrast: it is neither sweet nor soluble in cold water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.0] All sugars are sweet, but to different degrees. Fructose is sweeter than sucrose (table sugar), and lactose (milk sugar) is the least sweet of the common sugars.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.1] On a scale where sucrose scores 100, the 2017 marking scheme gives fructose 170 and lactose 15. Learn the order (fructose, then sucrose, then lactose) and use the figures only as that scale.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.2] Sweetness is why sugar is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.3] Sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve readily in water, and solubility increases with temperature, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.4] A concentrated sugar solution is a syrup. Syrups are used to sweeten drinks and sauces, to make icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.5] Starch is different: it is insoluble in cold water, so it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension (tiny solid particles spread through the liquid). It is also not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.b] Starch behaves differently from sugar: it is insoluble in cold water and must be heated with a liquid before it thickens anything. It also takes in moisture from the air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.0] Gelatinisation: when starch is heated in a liquid, the granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, thickening it into a sol (a colloidal solution, where tiny particles stay spread through the liquid).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.1] On cooling, a concentrated starch mixture may set into a gel (a solid-like network that holds the liquid), which is why a cooled custard or sauce can become firm.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.2] Gelatinised starch thickens white sauces, custard, soups, gravies, risotto and fillings for pies and flans.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.3] To prevent lumps, the granules must be separated before they swell. Mix the starch with a little cold liquid first (a cornflour paste), or coat it in fat as in a roux.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.4] A roux is a mixture of fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added. The fat separates the starch granules, and the starch in the flour gelatinises as the liquid is stirred in, giving a base for white sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.5] Starch is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump if stored in damp air. Store them in airtight containers.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.6] Sugar, especially brown sugar, is also hygroscopic, so it can turn hard or lumpy if left open in damp air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.b] How a carbohydrate food is cooked decides what happens to it. Dry heat browns it, while moist heat softens it and thickens liquids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.0] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch makes the granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs: the starch breaks down into brown dextrins. This gives a brown colour and a crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.1] Dry heat on sugar causes caramelisation and the Maillard reaction, which brown the surface of the food and give it flavour and a crisp texture.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.2] Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch causes gelatinisation: the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid, thickening sauces and puddings. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.3] Moist heat softens the cellulose in vegetable cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest. The vegetable keeps its fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.4] Sugar dissolves in the cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when fruit is stewed, so the syrup carries the flavour of the fruit. Sugar also helps the fruit keep its shape.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.b] Enzymes (proteins that speed up chemical reactions) break complex carbohydrates down in stages until they are small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.0] 1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins breaking starch down into smaller polysaccharides and maltose. This enzyme stops working in the stomach acid, so no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.1] 2. Pancreatic amylase, in pancreatic juice released into the small intestine, completes the breakdown of the remaining starch to maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.2] 3. Maltase, sucrase and lactase, made by the wall of the small intestine and found in the intestinal juice, finish the job. Maltase splits maltose into two glucose units, sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.3] Only monosaccharides (single sugar units: glucose, fructose and galactose) are small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.4] Humans have no enzyme that digests cellulose, so it passes through the intestine as dietary fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.b] Sugar does much more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each property has a use, and exam answers pair the two.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.0] Sweetness: sweetens cakes, desserts and drinks. Solubility: dissolves to make syrups and icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.1] Preservation: sugar draws water out of micro-organisms, so they cannot grow and spoilage is slowed in jam, jelly, chutney and crystallised fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.2] Fermentation: sugar is the food for yeast, which produces carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.3] Aeration (trapping air): creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures such as sponge cakes. Whisking traps air in egg white, and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable, which makes meringue.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.4] Maillard reaction: sugar reacts with amino acids (the units that make up protein) when heated, a non-enzymic browning that gives brown colour and flavour to roast meat, toast, roast potatoes and baked goods. Caramelisation is different because it involves sugar alone.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.5] Gel formation: sugar, acid and pectin together set jam into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.6] Caramelisation gives crème caramel and toffee, crystallisation gives confectionery, and inversion is used in jam and boiled sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.b] Caramelisation, the Maillard reaction and dextrinisation all brown food, so they are easy to confuse. What differs is what is being heated and with what.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.0] Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry until it melts and browns, so it needs no protein, as in caramel sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.1] The Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids together, heated dry, so it browns foods that contain both, such as toast and roast meat.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.2] Dextrinisation is starch, not sugar, broken into brown dextrins by dry heat, which is why toast browns even though bread is mostly starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.3] Gelatinisation is a different change again: starch heated in a liquid (moist heat) swells and thickens it, while inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by acid or enzyme.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.b] Hydrolysis is the reaction that splits big sugars into small ones, and it is the same reaction that happens in digestion. Inversion is the form of it that cooks use on sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: the bond between two units is split and water is added across it. It is the reverse of condensation (joining units with the loss of water).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.1] A disaccharide reacts with water, with enzymes or with heat and acid, and splits into its monosaccharide units. Sucrose gives glucose and fructose, lactose gives glucose and galactose, and maltose gives two glucose units.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.2] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. It produces invert sugar, a mixture of glucose and fructose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.3] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise than sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.4] This is why invert sugar is used in jam and boiled sweets, and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.b] Heating and cooling sugar and starch changes their colour, flavour and texture in three different ways. Each is wanted in some foods and avoided in others.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.0] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a distinctive, pleasant flavour. Overheating makes it dark and bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.1] Caramel is used in caramel sauce, crème caramel, toffee and brûlée.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.2] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution) and the mixture cools, sugar crystals form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.3] Controlled crystallisation gives the texture of fudge, fondant and sweets, while unwanted large crystals make jam grainy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.4] Crystallisation is prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (which causes inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.5] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains of glucose units called dextrins, which are brown. The 2005 scheme names the brown compound pyrodextrin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.6] Dextrinisation gives toast and the crust of baked bread their brown colour and flavour. Browned flour used for a roux thickens less, because its starch has already been broken into shorter chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.7] Dextrinisation is a property of starch, not sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.b] Jam sets because of pectin, a substance in fruit cell walls. Making jam depends on getting the pectin out of the fruit and then giving it the right conditions to set.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.0] Pectin is extracted by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft. Acid, such as lemon juice, helps release the pectin from the cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.1] Fruit high in pectin includes cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit (especially the peel), gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants. Strawberries are low in pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.2] Unripe fruit contains more pectin than ripe fruit, so under-ripe apples are a good source of pectin for jam.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.3] A gel forms only when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions and the mixture is boiled and then cooled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.4] During cooling, the pectin molecules link into a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.5] Too little pectin, as in strawberries, gives a runny jam. Add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit to fix this.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.6] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.b] This simple test shows that starch does not thicken a cold liquid but does thicken it when heated.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.0] Aim: to show the effect of heat on starch mixed with a liquid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.1] Method: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water and observe that it does not dissolve and does not thicken. Then heat the mixture while stirring and observe that it thickens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.2] Variable: temperature is the variable that changes (cold, then heated).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.3] Control and comparison: the unheated cornflour and cold water mixture is compared with the heated one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.4] Result: cold, the starch settles as a cloudy suspension and the mixture stays thin; heated, the granules swell, burst and absorb the water, and the mixture thickens into a sol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.b] Heating sugar on its own, with no water, shows caramelisation happening step by step.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.0] Aim: to observe how granulated sugar changes when it is heated on its own, with no water added.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.1] Method: place a measured quantity of granulated sugar in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat, stirring gently as it melts, and note the colour and smell at regular intervals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.2] Variables: heating time is the independent variable; colour, smell and taste are the dependent variables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.3] Control: a sample of the same sugar left unheated, for comparison.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.4] Safety: molten sugar is extremely hot and causes severe burns, so use heatproof utensils and keep hands and skin away from the pan.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.5] Expected results: the colour darkens gradually from pale to golden, then deep brown, and the caramel smell strengthens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.b] These two tests show a change in starch caused by dry heat and a change in sucrose caused by acid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.0] Dextrinisation, aim: to show that dry heat changes starch. Method: toast white bread, or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.1] Dextrinisation, result: the browned starch is darker and thickens less than unbrowned starch, because its chains are shorter. Safety: the pan is hot.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.2] Inversion, aim: to show that acid changes sucrose. Method: boil a sucrose solution with a little lemon juice and cool it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.3] Inversion, result: the invert sugar does not crystallise as readily and is sweeter.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.4] A Benedict's test shows the invert sugar: reducing sugars give an orange-red colour, while sucrose does not.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s2: 2. Composition, structure and classification
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.b] Every carbohydrate molecule is built from just three elements (a compound is a substance made of two or more elements, and a formula shows the atoms in one molecule). This small set of elements is what separates carbohydrates from proteins.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.0] Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. They contain no nitrogen, which is how they differ from proteins, because proteins do contain nitrogen.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.1] In sugars the hydrogen and oxygen atoms are in a 2:1 ratio, the same as in water, which is why carbohydrates are described as hydrated carbon (carbon combined with water's elements).
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.2] The 2:1 ratio does not mean a carbohydrate contains water; it only means the hydrogen and oxygen atoms are present in the same proportion as in water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.b] Carbohydrates are grouped by the number of sugar units (saccharide units) in each molecule. One unit makes a monosaccharide, two make a disaccharide and many make a polysaccharide.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.0] Monosaccharides (simple sugars) have one sugar unit: glucose, fructose and galactose, each with the formula C₆H₁₂O₆.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.1] Disaccharides (double sugars) have two sugar units: sucrose, lactose and maltose, each with the formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.2] Polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates) have many sugar units joined in long chains: starch, glycogen, cellulose and pectin, with the general formula (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ. Pectin is a polysaccharide found in fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.3] Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all made of glucose units, so cellulose is a polysaccharide and therefore a carbohydrate even though people cannot digest it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.4] The three monosaccharides share one formula and the three disaccharides share one formula, so they differ in how their atoms are arranged, not in how many atoms they have.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.5] Sugars (monosaccharides and disaccharides) are sweet and soluble, while polysaccharides are not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.6] Dietary language uses a simpler three-way grouping: sugar, starch and fibre (cellulose). Chemical language uses monosaccharide, disaccharide and polysaccharide, so learn both.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.7] Revision table: monosaccharide, one unit, C₆H₁₂O₆, glucose, fructose, galactose. Disaccharide, two units, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, sucrose, lactose, maltose. Polysaccharide, many units, (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ, starch, glycogen, cellulose, pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.b] A monosaccharide is the building block of all the larger carbohydrates. Knowing its formula and ring shape explains how sugar units later link together.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.0] A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit that cannot be split by hydrolysis (breaking a bond by adding water) into a simpler sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.1] Glucose has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆, which means six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms per molecule.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.2] In solution, glucose forms a six-membered ring: five carbon atoms and one oxygen atom make up the ring, and the sixth carbon is a CH₂OH group above the ring.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.3] Hydroxyl (–OH) groups are attached to the other carbon atoms of the ring; these groups take part in linking sugar units together.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.4] Fructose and galactose have the same formula as glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, but their atoms are arranged differently (they are isomers). Fructose can form a five-membered ring, as in sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.5] Diagram: glucose molecule drawn as a ring, with the ring oxygen, the carbon atoms numbered 1 to 6, the CH₂OH group and the hydroxyl groups labelled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.6] In the usual ring diagram (alpha-glucose) the –OH on carbon 1 points down, on carbon 2 down, on carbon 3 up and on carbon 4 down, with CH₂OH up on carbon 5.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.7] A simpler ring of C, C, C, C and O with H and OH groups and a CH₂OH group is also accepted.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.b] Sugar units are joined by a reaction that gives off water. Repeating it once makes a disaccharide, and repeating it many times makes a polysaccharide, a polymer (a long molecule built from many repeating units).
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.0] Two monosaccharides join by a condensation reaction, in which one molecule of water is eliminated as the bond forms: C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆ → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.1] The bond between two sugar units is called a glycosidic link, the –O– bridge formed where the water is lost.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.2] Glucose + glucose gives maltose; glucose + fructose gives sucrose; glucose + galactose gives lactose. Fructose is not in lactose or maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.3] Polysaccharides form in the same way: many monosaccharide units (glucose) join in a long chain, with a molecule of water lost at each link. Chains may be straight or branched.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.4] Starch is a mixture of amylose, which has straight chains of glucose, and amylopectin, which has branched chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.5] Glycogen is a highly branched polymer of glucose, the form in which animals store carbohydrate, in the liver and muscles.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.6] Cellulose has straight, unbranched chains of glucose units held side by side by hydrogen bonds, forming strong fibres in plant cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.7] Diagram: two glucose units joined by a glycosidic link, labelled with the water molecule released, and the resulting maltose molecule. For a polysaccharide, draw a longer chain of blocks, one straight and one branched.

CANDIDATES hom-digestion-metabolism-s2: 2. Digestion and absorption of carbohydrates
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.b] Food carbohydrates are too big to pass into the blood, so digestion chops them into small sugar units. This is done by hydrolysis, the splitting of the glycosidic bonds (the links) that join sugar units together.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.0] Digestion turns large food molecules into small soluble ones by hydrolysis, using enzymes. Enzymes are biological catalysts, each specific to one substrate, and they work at about 37 °C, body temperature.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.1] Starch is a polysaccharide made of many glucose units joined by glycosidic bonds, so it is a very large molecule that must be broken down before it can be absorbed.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.2] Maltose is two glucose units joined together; sucrose is glucose joined to fructose; lactose is glucose joined to galactose. These three are disaccharides (double sugars).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.3] Hydrolysis adds water across a glycosidic bond, splitting a disaccharide into two monosaccharides or a polysaccharide into smaller units. The end products are the monosaccharides glucose, fructose and galactose, the only forms small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.4] Each carbohydrate-digesting enzyme acts on one substrate, and its name ends in -ase and names that substrate: amylase on starch, maltase on maltose, sucrase on sucrose and lactase on lactose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.5] Diagram: glucose unit linked to a second glucose unit by a glycosidic bond, with the water added during hydrolysis labelled, giving two monosaccharides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.b] Carbohydrate digestion starts in the mouth, where chewing and saliva begin to break down starch.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.0] Chewing breaks food into smaller pieces (mechanical digestion), which gives the enzymes more surface to work on.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.1] Organ and secretion: the salivary glands secrete saliva, which contains salivary amylase.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.2] Substrate and product: salivary amylase hydrolyses starch into dextrins (shorter chains of glucose units) and maltose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.3] Digestion in the mouth is brief, because food is chewed and swallowed quickly, so much of the starch reaches the small intestine undigested.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.4] Diagram: the alimentary canal, with the mouth, salivary glands and oesophagus labelled.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.b] Starch digestion pauses in the stomach, because the conditions there do not suit the enzyme that arrives from the mouth.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.0] The acid of the stomach, hydrochloric acid, denatures salivary amylase (changes its shape so it no longer works) and stops its action on starch.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.1] The stomach secretes no enzyme that digests carbohydrate.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.2] Starch, dextrins and maltose leave the stomach as part of the chyme (the semi-liquid mixture of food and gastric juice) and pass into the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.b] Most starch is digested in the small intestine. Pancreatic juice finishes the starch, then enzymes from the intestine wall split the disaccharides into single sugars.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.0] Organ and secretion: the pancreas, a gland, secretes pancreatic juice, which contains pancreatic amylase. It enters the duodenum by the pancreatic duct.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.1] Pancreatic amylase hydrolyses the remaining starch and dextrins into maltose. It is a different enzyme from salivary amylase but acts on the same substrate.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.2] Pancreatic juice is alkaline, which neutralises the acidic chyme so the intestinal enzymes can work.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.3] The wall of the small intestine secretes intestinal juice from glands in its wall. It contains maltase, sucrase and lactase, which finish the job, and each acts only on its own substrate.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.4] Maltase hydrolyses maltose into two glucose molecules.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.5] Sucrase hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.6] Lactase hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose, so the products of the disaccharidases are not all glucose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.7] Non-starch polysaccharides (dietary fibre) are not hydrolysed by human enzymes, so they pass into the large intestine. Fibre is the carbohydrate that is not digested.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.b] Putting the stages in order shows how starch travels from the mouth to the blood and on to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.0] Mouth: salivary amylase begins starch digestion.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.1] Stomach: acid stops amylase action, and food is mixed to form chyme.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.2] Duodenum: pancreatic amylase and intestinal juice complete digestion to monosaccharides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.3] Villi of the small intestine: monosaccharides are absorbed into the capillaries inside each villus.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.4] Hepatic portal vein: carries all the monosaccharides, glucose, fructose and galactose, from the small intestine to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.5] Liver: converts fructose and galactose to glucose, and glucose then enters the general circulation.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.6] Diagram: the digestive system, labelled mouth, salivary glands, stomach, pancreas, small intestine, liver and hepatic portal vein, with where each enzyme acts.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.b] Only monosaccharides are small enough to cross the wall of the small intestine. The wall is built to absorb them quickly and has two different ways of moving them across.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.0] The small intestine is very long and its lining is folded into villi, with microvilli on their cells, so the surface area for absorption is large.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.1] Each villus has a thin wall, so sugars cross quickly, and a capillary network that carries them away. It also has a central lacteal, which is used for fat.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.2] Glucose and galactose are absorbed by active transport, which uses energy and carrier proteins and is coupled with sodium ions.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.3] Fructose is absorbed by facilitated diffusion, which moves it down its concentration gradient through carrier proteins without using energy.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.4] Monosaccharides leave the villi in the blood of the capillaries, which join to form the hepatic portal vein. The vein carries all of them to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.5] Diagram: cross-section of a villus showing the capillary network, the lacteal and the direction of glucose and fructose movement.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.b] Once glucose is in the blood it has several possible fates: it is used for energy, stored, or converted to fat. Two pancreatic hormones keep the blood glucose level steady.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.0] Glucose in the blood is carried to the cells, where cells oxidise it in respiration, releasing heat and energy, some of which is stored in ATP. It is the main fuel for the brain and red blood cells.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.1] Carbohydrate spares protein: because glucose supplies the energy, protein can be used for growth and repair instead.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.2] Surplus glucose is stored as glycogen, a branched polysaccharide of glucose, in the liver and muscles. Glycogen is the animal store, whereas starch is the plant store.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.3] Insulin, released from the beta cells of the pancreas, promotes the uptake of glucose into cells and its storage as glycogen, which lowers blood glucose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.4] Glucagon, released from the alpha cells of the pancreas, causes the liver to break down glycogen and release glucose, which raises blood glucose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.5] When glycogen stores are full, the liver converts surplus glucose into fatty acids and triglycerides, which are stored as fat in adipose tissue.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.6] Insulin and glucagon are hormones released into the blood, not digestive secretions, so they are a different job of the pancreas from making pancreatic juice.
