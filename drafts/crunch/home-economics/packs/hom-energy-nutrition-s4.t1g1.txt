TARGET hom-energy-nutrition-s4: 4. Dietary guidelines
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.b] Dietary guidelines are official advice, based on scientific evidence, on the types and amounts of food that keep a population healthy. They turn nutrition science into practical advice for the general healthy population, and are revised as evidence changes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.0] Evidence comes from nutrition research, national food consumption surveys (which record what people actually eat) and health data on diet-related diseases.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.1] Expert committees review this evidence and turn it into practical advice on foods rather than nutrients, setting the types of food to eat and the amounts to limit. The guidelines are reviewed as new evidence appears.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.2] The Department of Health publishes the healthy eating guidelines for Ireland and the Food Pyramid, which is one of the tools used to explain them (the current pyramid is called 'Healthy Food for Life', 2016).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.3] The HSE supports the guidelines, and the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) and safefood are other Irish bodies in this field.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.4] The guidelines are written for the general healthy population, so they are not a substitute for advice for individuals with special medical needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.5] The aims are to make sure people get enough nutrients, to prevent deficiency, and to reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, some cancers and osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.6] One consistent message also helps consumers, schools and food planners, because everyone is working from the same advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.b] The Irish guidelines can be given as about eight principles. Together they say which foods to eat most, which to eat in moderation and which to keep small.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.0] Eat plenty of vegetables, salad and fruit: about five to seven portions a day, spread across meals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.1] Base meals on starchy foods, choosing wholegrain bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.2] Include protein from lean meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans, lentils and nuts, with fish eaten regularly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.3] Choose milk, yoghurt and cheese in lower-fat forms, as they supply calcium and HBV protein (high biological value protein, which contains all the essential amino acids).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.4] Use fats and oils sparingly, and prefer unsaturated oils such as rapeseed and olive oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.5] Limit foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, which are not needed for health and should be occasional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.6] Drink water as the main drink, and keep alcohol within recommended limits.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.7] Eat regular meals and prepare food with methods that add little fat, such as steaming, grilling and baking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.b] Each guideline is linked to a health benefit or a health risk. Examiners want the guideline and its reason together.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.0] Fruit and vegetables supply fibre, vitamins, minerals and antioxidants (substances that protect cells from damage), which protect against chronic disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.1] Wholegrain starchy foods give more fibre and nutrients than refined white flour products, and promote bowel health because fibre helps prevent constipation and bowel disorders.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.2] Saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.3] Excess salt is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of stroke and heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.4] Sugar adds energy without nutrients, contributes to obesity, and causes tooth decay.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.b] An RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) is a planning value: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of nearly all healthy people in a group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.0] An RDA is set for each nutrient and differs by age, sex and life stage, such as growth, pregnancy, lactation (breastfeeding) or adolescence.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.1] RDAs are used to plan menus and to check that a diet supplies enough of each nutrient over the day, by comparing the day's nutrient total with the RDA. They also guide food-labelling and supplement standards and institutions such as schools and hospitals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.2] An RDA is a guide for a group; it is not a precise target for an individual.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.3] Intake far above the RDA does not give extra benefit and can be harmful for some nutrients, so supplements should be used with care.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.4] Nutrients that are often low in Irish diets, such as iron and calcium, are the ones to check against the RDA when planning a menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.b] A food composition table is a reference book of food values. It lets you turn a menu into numbers, so you can check it against the RDAs and the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.0] A food composition table gives the nutrient and energy content of a food, usually per 100 g of edible food. Irish tables are produced from national food analysis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.1] Tables state whether values are for raw or cooked food, so the matching version must be used because cooking changes the nutrient content.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.2] Method: for each food, work out the grams eaten, divide by 100, then multiply by the value per 100 g.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.3] Example (sample data): 150 g of a food with 3 g fibre per 100 g supplies 150 / 100 x 3 = 4.5 g fibre.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.4] Add the results for all foods in the menu to give the day's total for each nutrient and for energy, then compare the total with the RDA. Where a total is low or high, adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.b] A computer nutrition program does the same job as a food composition table, but automatically. It is quick to use, though it is only as good as the information put in.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.0] Nutrition programs contain a built-in food composition database; the user enters each food and the quantity eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.1] The program totals the energy and nutrients for the day and compares them with the guidelines or RDAs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.2] Check that the portion size entered matches the food as eaten, and that the correct cooking method is selected, because the results depend on correct portions and the right cooking state.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.3] A program shows which nutrients are low or high, which helps the planner adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.4] Advantages are that it is fast and accurate and holds many foods and recipes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.b] The Food Pyramid turns the healthy eating guidelines into the types and relative amounts of food needed each day. Foods with similar nutrients are grouped on shelves, with the base shelf eaten most and the top shelf least.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.0] The current Irish pyramid has six shelves. At the base are vegetables, salad and fruit, with wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice above them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.1] Next come milk, yogurt and cheese, then meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (including lean choices), then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, to be used sparingly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.2] The top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt. These are not needed for health, have no recommended servings, and should be occasional. Water is the main drink.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.3] Servings: five to seven servings of vegetables, salad and fruit a day, three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese (five for ages 9 to 18), and three to five servings of wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.4] The number and size of servings from the other shelves are adjusted for age, sex, health, activity level and life stage, for example children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.5] To plan meals, take most foods from the large lower shelves, some from the middle and very little from the top, and vary the foods within each shelf. Include vegetables or fruit at every meal, and choose wholemeal and lower-fat versions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.6] Spread the choices across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks so the day covers every shelf and matches the person's portions. This supports energy balance and prevents both deficiency and excess.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.7] The pyramid guides what to eat and roughly how much, not an exact diet, and it does not replace advice for medical conditions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.8] Diagram: draw the Food Pyramid, label each shelf with the main foods on it, and mark the base shelf and the top shelf.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.b] A serving (portion) is a standard measure of food used to plan and count intake against the pyramid. It makes diets easy to compare, because everyone counts in the same units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.0] A portion is a standard amount of food used for planning, counting and comparing diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.1] One piece of fruit, such as an apple, or one serving of vegetables counts as one portion of fruit or vegetables.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.2] One glass of milk counts as one portion of milk, yoghurt or cheese in the dairy group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.3] Portion size should be adjusted to the person's age and activity: teenagers and active adults need larger portions from the starchy foods shelf than older, less active adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.4] Serving sizes and numbers rise for growing teenagers and active people and fall for older, less active adults. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra servings of some groups.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.b] Food supplements are concentrates of important nutrients. They can fill a gap in a diet, but they cannot do everything that food does.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.0] Food supplements are concentrates of nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, omega-3 fatty acids (polyunsaturated fatty acids) and amino acids, sold in tablet, capsule or powder form.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.1] They may be used to fill gaps where diet is restricted, where allergies or intolerances exclude foods, or where a person has special needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.2] Folic acid prevents neural tube defects (defects of the baby's brain and spine) in a developing baby when taken before conception and in early pregnancy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.3] Iron supplements can treat anaemia caused by low iron intake, under medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.4] Older people may absorb nutrients less efficiently, so they may need supplements, particularly vitamin D.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.5] Their popularity is growing for reasons such as busy lifestyles, poor diets, media and advertising, a wish to prevent illness, special needs (vegans, restricted diets, illness), sports performance and convenience.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.6] Supplements cannot replace a varied diet, because food supplies fibre and other nutrients that supplements do not contain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.7] High doses of some vitamins and minerals can be harmful, especially fat-soluble vitamins, so supplements should not be taken above the recommended levels. They can also cost a lot, so take medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.b] Healthy eating is only part of staying healthy. The lifestyle guidelines cover activity, mental wellbeing and habits that harm the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.0] Regular physical activity: build activity into daily life, such as brisk walking, cycling, sport or active housework, and include muscle-strengthening and weight-bearing exercise (which loads the bones, such as walking or running).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.1] Physical activity improves cardiovascular fitness, maintains muscle and bone, supports weight control and mental wellbeing, and may raise protective HDL cholesterol (the protective form of cholesterol).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.2] Mental health: get adequate sleep and rest, keep a realistic workload, and use stress management, relaxation and social contact.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.3] Avoid tobacco and second-hand smoke, which damage the lungs and blood vessels and greatly raise the risk of cancer and coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.4] Limit alcohol: it supplies empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrients), harms the liver and judgement, and must never be used to manage stress.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.5] Drink plenty of water and keep a healthy body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.6] Regular health checks and good personal hygiene complete the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.7] Together these habits reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension (high blood pressure) and heart disease.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s3: 3. Energy value of the nutrients
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.b] Only three nutrients supply energy in food: protein, carbohydrate and fat (lipid). Each releases a fixed amount of energy, measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal), for every gram eaten, and these values are the basis of every energy calculation.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.0] Protein supplies 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, carbohydrate supplies 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram and fat (lipid) supplies 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram. A simple table of these three values is the best way to learn them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.1] Fat is the most concentrated energy source, giving more than twice the energy per gram of protein or carbohydrate. This is why a high-fat diet is energy-dense and contributes strongly to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.2] Vitamins, minerals and water supply no energy, so they are not energy nutrients, even though the body cannot work without them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.3] Alcohol is not a nutrient, but it does supply energy, at 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. It supplies no protein, vitamins or minerals, so it is a source of empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrition).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.4] Fibre is a carbohydrate that the body cannot digest, so it supplies very little usable energy. Do not count it with the starches and sugars, and do not give it 16 kJ per gram.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.5] 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, so dividing kJ by 4.2 gives kcal. Check: 37 kJ is about 9 kcal, 17 kJ is about 4 kcal, 16 kJ is about 3.75 kcal and 29 kJ is about 7 kcal.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.b] A food's energy value is worked out from how many grams of each energy nutrient it contains. Food composition tables and labels give these amounts per 100 g, so the method is multiply, add, then state the unit.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.0] 1. Find the grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat in the amount eaten, from the food label or a food composition table (which gives values per 100 g).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.1] 2. Multiply the grams of each nutrient by its energy value per gram: protein by 17, carbohydrate by 16 and fat by 37.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.2] 3. Add the three results to give the total energy value of the food.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.3] 4. Give the unit, kJ, and convert to kcal if asked by dividing by 4.2.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.4] Scaling to a portion: if the table gives values per 100 g and the portion is 150 g, multiply each per-100 g value by 150 divided by 100 (or 1.5).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.5] Without scaling, the answer is for 100 g, not for the portion eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.b] Seeing the method on sample figures shows where the marks are: each multiplication, the total and the unit. These are invented sample foods, not facts about any named product.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.0] Worked example (sample data): 100 g of a food with 12 g protein, 60 g carbohydrate and 8 g fat gives 12 x 17 = 204 kJ, 60 x 16 = 960 kJ and 8 x 37 = 296 kJ.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.1] These add to a total of 1460 kJ. In kcal this is about 348 (1460 divided by 4.2).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.2] Second worked example (sample data): 100 g of a food with 20 g protein, 50 g carbohydrate and 10 g fat gives 20 x 17 = 340 kJ, 50 x 16 = 800 kJ and 10 x 37 = 370 kJ.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.3] These add to a total of 1510 kJ, which is about 360 kcal.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.4] Percentage contribution: divide the energy from one nutrient by the total energy, then multiply by 100. In the 1460 kJ sample, fat gives 296 out of 1460, which is about 20 per cent of the energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.b] Section B data questions give a table of composition per 100 g for several foods, such as protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre, calcium and iron. The sum is only half the answer, because you must also say what the figures mean for a diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.0] To rank foods by energy value, calculate the kJ for each food using the method above, then put them in order and name the highest and the lowest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.1] A high-fibre food helps bowel health, because fibre adds bulk and keeps waste moving through the gut.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.2] A high-fat food is a disadvantage on a low-fat diet, because fat has the most energy per gram and contributes to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.3] A high-carbohydrate food is a good source of energy for the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.4] The 2007H bread comparison is an example of this type of question: calculate, rank, then comment on how suitable each bread is for a given diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.b] Carbohydrate supplies the largest share of energy in the diet. It is the body's main and most readily available energy source.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.0] Sugars and starches are digested to glucose, which cells use for energy. Any glucose not needed straight away is stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.1] Starchy foods such as bread, potatoes, rice and pasta supply carbohydrate together with fibre, vitamins and minerals, so they give energy with useful nutrition. Cereals and fruit are also sources.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.2] Sugary foods and drinks supply carbohydrate with little nutrition, so they are a source of empty kilocalories.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.3] Healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods, preferably wholegrain, with less from added sugars.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.b] Fat packs far more energy into each gram than the other nutrients, so it contributes strongly to the energy in the diet even when it is a smaller part of the weight of the food. It is the second largest source of energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.0] Fats and oils are lipids, mostly triglycerides, made of glycerol and fatty acids.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.1] Fat supplies the most energy per gram of the three energy nutrients, more than twice that of carbohydrate or protein.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.2] Surplus energy from fat is stored as adipose tissue, an energy reserve, which is why high-fat diets contribute strongly to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.3] Saturated fats come mainly from animal foods and some plant oils, such as palm oil. Unsaturated fats come from vegetable oils, nuts, seeds and oily fish.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.4] Sources of fat include butter, oils, fatty meat, nuts, cheese and oily fish.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.b] Protein can supply energy, but this is not its main job. It makes the smallest contribution to the energy in the diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.0] Protein is made of amino acids. The body uses it mainly for growth and repair of tissue, but it can also supply energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.1] Protein is not stored in the body as protein, so surplus protein is broken down and used for energy or converted to fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.2] When carbohydrate and fat are short, protein is used for energy at the expense of its building role.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.3] High biological value (HBV) protein, from meat, fish, eggs, milk and cheese, contains all essential amino acids. Beans are also a protein source.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.4] Protein is not a major energy source in a healthy diet: its value per gram is close to that of carbohydrate, but it is eaten in smaller amounts.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.b] How much each nutrient contributes to a diet depends on how much of it is eaten and on how much energy each gram of the food carries.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.0] Energy density is the energy per gram of a food. Foods rich in fat and sugar are the most energy-dense.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.1] Wholegrain starchy foods, fruit, vegetables and lean protein give nutrients with less energy per gram, so they are a better choice than empty kilocalories.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.2] Healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods, preferably wholegrain, with less from fat, especially saturated fat, and less from added sugars.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.3] The typical Irish diet gets more of its energy from saturated fat and from added sugar in drinks and processed foods than the guidelines recommend.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.4] Calculating the contribution of each nutrient: multiply the grams eaten in a day by the energy value per gram, then divide each result by the total and multiply by 100 to give its percentage share.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s6: 6. Energy value and dietary fibre targets
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.b] Every nutrient releases a set amount of energy per gram, so we can work out how much energy a food supplies from its nutrient content. Carbohydrate is the main energy nutrient in most diets, so you need its figure and how to use it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.0] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram. Sugars and starch have the same energy value per gram, so a gram of sugar gives no more energy than a gram of starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.1] For comparison, protein provides 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, fat 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram and alcohol 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. Fat gives more than twice the energy of carbohydrate for the same weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.2] Fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) is not digested by human enzymes, so it contributes little usable energy. Say 'little', not 'none'.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.3] To calculate the energy from a food, multiply the grams of carbohydrate by 16 to get kilojoules (kJ), or by 3.75 to get kilocalories (kcal).
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.4] Worked example: 30 g of sugar provides 30 × 16 = 480 kJ, or 30 × 3.75 = 112.5 kcal. One kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ), which is why the kcal figure is smaller.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.5] Label point: the nutrition declaration on a food label states carbohydrate and, of which, sugars; fibre is an optional item. The energy figure is calculated by the manufacturer, so it may not match your own calculation exactly.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.6] Settings, comparative study: compare two breakfast cereals, one with added sugar and one without, using the label's carbohydrate, sugar and energy per 100 g. Discuss which supplies more energy and which supplies more fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.7] Settings, activity comparison: work out the energy in a food such as a bar of chocolate and compare it with the time an activity such as walking or running takes to use it up. Use the energy values from the source given with the question.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.b] Carbohydrate, mostly as starch, is the main source of energy in most diets, and it should supply the largest share of the energy in the diet, with fat and protein supplying the rest. The kind of carbohydrate matters, because some carries other nutrients and some does not.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.0] Healthy eating guidelines use the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid (Department of Health and HSE, 2016). Its base shelf is vegetables, salad and fruit, and the second shelf up is wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.1] The top shelf holds foods high in fat, sugar and salt. These are eaten in small amounts and not every day, so they should not be the main source of energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.2] Choose wholemeal and wholegrain starchy foods, because they supply energy together with fibre, vitamins and minerals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.3] Foods with a lot of added sugar supply energy but few other nutrients, so they are described as energy-dense and nutrient-poor ('empty calories').
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.4] Replacing sugary foods and refined starchy foods with wholegrain starchy foods, fruit and vegetables raises the intake of fibre and nutrients without raising the energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.b] Fibre is the part of plant foods that humans cannot digest, and adults are advised to eat a set amount each day. Many Irish adults do not reach it, so you should know the target and the everyday ways to meet it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.0] The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommends a daily intake of dietary fibre for adults of 25 g a day. This is its adequate intake, set in 2010 for normal bowel function.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.1] Irish healthy eating guidance from the Department of Health and the HSE (the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid) also advises eating wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetables every day.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.2] Breakfast: choose a high-fibre cereal (look for at least 3 g of fibre per 100 g on the label), porridge, bran or wholegrain bread or toast. Check the fibre per 100 g when choosing cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.3] Choose wholegrain or wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta and brown flour in baking in place of white versions, because the fibre is in the outer part of the grain that white versions have lost.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.4] Add pulses such as beans, lentils, peas and chickpeas to soups, stews and salads. Half a tin of beans gives about 7.5 g of fibre, and chickpeas, kidney beans and butter beans give about 10 g.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.5] Add two tablespoons of seeds a day, such as sunflower, sesame, pumpkin and linseed, or bran or porridge oats, to cereal, yoghurt, bread and salads.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.6] Eat vegetables at lunch and dinner, about a third of each meal; frozen vegetables are as good as fresh. Leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, and bake or boil potatoes in their jackets, since most of the fibre in a potato is in the skin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.7] Eat 2-3 pieces of fruit a day (a piece gives about 2 g of fibre), and snack on fresh fruit, dried fruit or a handful of nuts instead of biscuits or crisps. Increase fibre gradually and drink plenty of water, because fibre absorbs water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.b] Added sugar supplies energy with few other nutrients, so cutting it down is a main healthy eating target. Strategies fall into two groups: what you buy, and how you prepare and eat food.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.0] Purchasing: read labels and ingredient lists, because added sugars appear under names such as sucrose, glucose syrup, fructose, honey, cane sugar, brown sugar, corn syrup and fruit juice concentrate.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.1] Choose reduced-sugar, lower-sugar or no-added-sugar versions of drinks, jams, cereals and yoghurts, choose fruit canned in juice rather than syrup, and avoid sugar-coated cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.2] Front-of-pack colour (traffic-light) labelling, where used, shows at a glance whether a food is high, medium or low in sugars. It is voluntary, so not every product carries it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.3] Preparing: reduce the sugar in recipes and use spices, vanilla or ripe fruit for flavour. Pureed fruit, banana, dates or apple can sweeten recipes instead of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.4] Do not add sugar to tea, coffee or breakfast cereals. Replace fizzy drinks with water, or with fruit juice diluted with sparkling water, which cuts the sugar per glass.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.5] Eat whole fruit rather than fruit juice or smoothies. Juice contains little fibre, and the sugar in juices and smoothies is released from the fruit's cells so it counts as free sugar, whereas whole fruit keeps its fibre and fills you up.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.6] Snacks and treats: limit sweets, biscuits, cakes, sweet sauces and sugary drinks, and keep fresh fruit, fruit or vegetable sticks, nuts and natural yoghurt as snacks instead.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.b] A diet high in sugar causes harm in several ways, some directly on the teeth and others through the energy and nutrients the diet supplies. Each effect needs its reason to earn marks.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.0] Tooth decay: bacteria in plaque (the sticky film on teeth) ferment sugar to produce acid, which dissolves the enamel of the teeth.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.1] Weight gain and obesity: sugary foods are energy-dense and nutrient-poor, so regular intake adds to excess energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.2] Type 2 diabetes: a diet high in sugar and energy is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes, which is linked to excess body weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.3] Heart disease and high blood pressure: high sugar intake is associated with a higher risk of heart disease and raised blood pressure. High fructose intake has been linked with higher blood pressure, and refined carbohydrate with excess insulin that can raise LDL cholesterol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.4] Nutritional deficiency: foods high in sugar displace fruit, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods from the diet, so vitamins and minerals fall short.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.5] Sugary foods are also low in fibre, so they do little for bowel health.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.b] Artificial sweeteners give a sweet taste without the energy of sugar, or with less of it. They fall into two types, intense and bulk, and each has its uses and limits.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.0] Intense sweeteners, such as aspartame and saccharin, are many times sweeter than sugar, so only tiny amounts are needed and they add almost no energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.1] Bulk sweeteners, such as sorbitol, mannitol and xylitol, have a similar bulk to sugar and provide some energy, though less than sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.2] Uses in food manufacture: they sweeten and improve palatability, and they are used in low-calorie, sugar-free, slimming and reduced-sugar foods, soft drinks and confectionery.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.3] Sorbitol and other bulk sweeteners act as humectants: they retain moisture and stop products drying out.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.4] Bulk sweeteners are used where sugar's bulk is needed, as in confectionery and fudge. Xylitol and sorbitol do not cause tooth decay, so they are used to avoid it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.5] Sweeteners cannot replace all of sugar's functions: they do not provide the bulk, browning, preserving or bulk-texture roles of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.6] Sweeteners are food additives with E numbers. Labels must say 'contains sweetener', and aspartame carries a warning for people with phenylketonuria.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.7] Excess bulk sweeteners can have a laxative effect, so their labels carry a warning.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s5: 5. Functions and energy value
[hom-lipids-s5.0.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do far more in the body than provide energy. Each function below follows from where fat sits in the body or what it does as it passes through the digestive system.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.0] Lipids are a concentrated source of energy, supplying the body with more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein, so a small amount of fat gives a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.1] Excess energy from any food is stored as adipose tissue (the body's stored fat), which is a reserve of energy for when food intake is low.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.2] Adipose tissue lies under the skin and insulates the body against heat loss, which helps to keep body temperature constant.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.3] Adipose tissue around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against knocks and pressure, and holds them in place.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.4] Lipids slow the emptying of the stomach, which helps to delay hunger, so meals containing fat are more satisfying.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.b] Beyond energy and body protection, lipids carry nutrients, build cell structures and affect how food tastes.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.0] Lipids carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K and help the body absorb them, because these vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water. Dairy fat, oily fish and egg yolk supply them.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.1] Lipids are a source of essential fatty acids, the fatty acids the body cannot make for itself and must get from food.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.2] Phospholipids (fat-like lipids containing phosphorus) and cholesterol form part of the structure of every cell membrane, the thin layer around each cell.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.3] Fat also adds flavour and a smooth texture to food, although this is a cooking benefit and not a biological function.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.4] Digestion link: the enzyme lipase digests triglycerides (the main fats in food) to fatty acids and glycerol, and any excess is stored as adipose tissue.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.b] Cholesterol is a lipid that is often blamed for heart disease, yet the body needs it. It is different from the saturated fat in food, and students often confuse the two.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.0] Cholesterol is a fat-like substance called a sterol, found only in animal foods such as egg yolk, offal (liver and kidney), meat, butter, cream, cheese, prawns and other shellfish. Plant foods contain none.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.1] The liver makes cholesterol itself, in fact most of what the body has, so the amount eaten has less effect on blood cholesterol than the saturated fat in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.2] Saturated fat in the diet raises blood LDL cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol does. A plant oil such as coconut oil is cholesterol-free but can still be high in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.3] The body uses cholesterol to make cell membranes, bile salts that emulsify fat (break it into tiny droplets) for digestion, and steroid hormones such as oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone and cortisol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.4] Vitamin D is made in the skin from a derivative of cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.5] Too much cholesterol in the blood, especially LDL, builds up on artery walls and narrows them, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke. It is also a main constituent of most gallstones.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.6] To lower blood cholesterol, eat less saturated and trans fat and more unsaturated fat, oily fish, oats, pulses, fruit and vegetables (their fibre helps). Keep to a healthy weight, be active and do not smoke.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.7] Functional spreads containing plant stanols or sterols also help to lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.b] Foods high in saturated fat are energy-dense and raise blood LDL cholesterol. A diet high in them over years raises the risk of several diseases, and each has its own mechanism.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.0] Obesity: saturated-fat foods are energy-dense, and surplus energy from them is stored as adipose tissue, which can lead to obesity.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.1] Coronary heart disease and high blood pressure: raised LDL cholesterol narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis), increasing the risk of coronary heart disease, high blood pressure (hypertension) and stroke.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.2] Type 2 diabetes: excess body fat reduces the effectiveness of insulin (the hormone that controls blood sugar), which is called insulin resistance, so the risk of Type 2 diabetes increases.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.3] Gallstones: cholesterol is a main component of gallstones, which can form in the gall bladder.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.4] Varicose veins and infertility are also linked to a high-fat diet; the first four effects carry the most weight.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.5] Lifestyle changes recommended for someone at risk of CHD: a low-fat, low-cholesterol, low-salt diet; losing weight; not smoking; limiting alcohol; exercise; and managing stress.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.6] Irish healthy eating guidelines from the Department of Health advise replacing saturated fats with unsaturated (mono- and polyunsaturated) fats and using fats and oils in small amounts, which is the fats, spreads and oils shelf of the Food Pyramid.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.7] Other advice: choose lean meat and low-fat dairy, and grill, steam or bake instead of frying. Saturated fat should supply less than 10% of daily energy (HSE and Department of Health guidelines).
[hom-lipids-s5.4.b] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal). Fat has the highest energy value per gram of the three nutrients, which is why fatty foods are so energy-dense.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.0] Fat provides 37 kJ or 9 kcal of energy per gram, which is more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein. The true/false statement that 1 gram of fat provides 20 kilocalories is false.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.1] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ or 3.75 kcal per gram; protein provides 17 kJ or 4 kcal per gram; alcohol provides 29 kJ or 7 kcal per gram.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.2] Energy from fat is calculated by multiplying the grams of fat by 37 kJ, or by 9 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.3] Worked example (sample data): 20 g of fat gives 20 × 37 = 740 kJ, or 20 × 9 = 180 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.4] A large part of the energy in the average Irish diet comes from fat, much of it hidden in processed and convenience foods, meat products, dairy, cakes, pastries, biscuits, chocolate, fried foods and takeaways, and much of that is saturated.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.5] Guidance is to reduce total fat, especially saturated and trans fat, replacing them with unsaturated fat and complex carbohydrate. Over-consumption of fat leads to obesity because fat is energy-dense and stores readily.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s4: 4. Mineral elements
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.b] Minerals are the part of food that is not made by living things at all. The body needs them in small amounts to build structures, run processes and make enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.0] Minerals are inorganic elements (not made by living things) that the body cannot make, so they must be obtained from food. They build body structures, regulate body processes and form parts of enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.1] Major (macro) minerals are needed in larger amounts, above 100 mg a day. They are calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium and sulphur.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.2] Minerals are not destroyed by cooking, because they are elements. They can, however, be lost into cooking water and in the trimming of food. For example, boiling vegetables in a lot of water washes minerals out.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily amount) is the daily intake that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, so some groups need more than others.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.4] Phosphorus works with calcium to harden bones and teeth and forms part of ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and cell membranes. Sources are milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs, wholegrains and nuts, and deficiency is rare.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.b] Calcium is the most important mineral for the skeleton, but it also keeps blood, muscles and nerves working. Because bone is built in childhood and lost in later life, some groups need more of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.0] Milk, cheese and yoghurt are the richest dietary sources of calcium. This is why dairy foods matter for bone health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.1] Canned sardines and canned salmon eaten with their bones, green leafy vegetables such as kale, broccoli and cabbage, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks and fortified breakfast cereals are further sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.2] Calcium works with phosphorus to form the mineral salts that give bones and teeth their hardness. This is why a diet low in calcium leaves bones weak.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.3] Calcium is needed for blood clotting, normal muscle contraction (including the heartbeat) and nerve impulse transmission. It also activates some enzymes and helps regulate blood pressure.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.4] Rickets in children (soft, bent bones and poor teeth) and osteomalacia in adults result from too little calcium, phosphorus or vitamin D.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.5] Osteoporosis, which develops over years, makes bones thin, porous, brittle and prone to fracture, with loss of height and a stooped back. Other effects of low calcium are tooth decay, slow clotting, and muscle cramps (tetany) and poor nerve function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.6] Osteoporosis risk is raised by low calcium and vitamin D intake, lack of weight-bearing exercise, smoking, alcohol, the fall in oestrogen after menopause and family history.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.7] Need is highest when bone is being built (childhood, teenage years, pregnancy, breastfeeding) and when it is lost (women after menopause). Bone density peaks in the late teens and twenties, so teenage intake matters.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.b] Iron is needed to carry oxygen, so a shortage leaves the whole body short of energy. It comes in two forms, and the form decides how easily the body absorbs it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.0] Haem iron comes from animal foods and is absorbed easily. Sources are red meat, liver and offal, kidney, black pudding, poultry and oily fish.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.1] Non-haem iron comes from plant foods and eggs and is absorbed less easily. Sources are eggs, wholegrain and fortified cereals, wholemeal bread, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried apricots and other dried fruit, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.2] Iron is an essential part of haemoglobin, the red protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs around the body to all cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.3] Iron is part of myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle cells, and part of enzymes that release energy from food. It also supports immunity and concentration. This is why a shortage leaves people tired.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.4] Iron deficiency causes anaemia (too few red blood cells or too little haemoglobin). Effects are tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, dizziness, irritability, poor concentration, feeling cold and reduced resistance to infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.5] Causes of iron deficiency are a diet low in iron or poor in vitamin C, blood loss (menstruation, ulcers, operations), high needs in growth spurts, pregnancy and infancy, and poor absorption.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.6] Corrective measures are eating more iron-rich foods together with vitamin C, avoiding tea with meals, and taking an iron supplement on medical advice. This is because vitamin C improves absorption and tea blocks it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.7] Groups at risk are teenage girls, menstruating and pregnant women, infants and toddlers, people with heavy blood loss, and vegetarians and vegans, who eat only non-haem iron. Children are at risk because of fast growth and low-iron foods. For example, vegetarians miss out on the more easily absorbed haem form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.b] Trace minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, yet the body cannot do without them. Each has a job that no other mineral can do.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.0] Trace (micro) minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms a day, and the diet must supply them. They are tiny in amount yet essential. For example, zinc and iodine are needed in milligrams or micrograms.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.1] The trace minerals are iron, zinc, iodine, fluoride, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium and cobalt. Iron is a trace mineral by the amount needed, but it is studied in detail at Ordinary Level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.2] Iron, zinc and iodine each play a specific role in the body that the other minerals cannot replace. Zinc and iodine are set out in the blocks that follow.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.b] Zinc is a trace mineral that works inside enzymes, so it affects growth, healing and defence against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.0] Zinc sources include oysters and other shellfish, red meat, poultry, liver, dairy products, eggs, wholegrain cereals, pulses, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.1] Zinc is a component of many enzymes and supports growth and cell division, which is why a shortage slows growth in children.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.2] Zinc supports wound healing and a healthy immune system. This is because new cells must be made to repair the wound.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.3] Zinc contributes to a normal sense of taste and smell, and to healthy skin and hair and reproductive health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.4] Deficiency causes poor growth, delayed wound healing, dry flaky skin and rashes, loss of taste and appetite, reduced immunity and hair loss.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.b] Iodine is needed to make a hormone that sets the body's pace. Without it the thyroid gland cannot do its job.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.0] Iodine sources include sea fish and shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy products and eggs. Iodised salt also exists as a source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.1] Iodine is part of thyroxine, the hormone from the thyroid gland that controls the rate of metabolism (and growth and brain development).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.2] Deficiency causes goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck as it works harder to make more thyroxine. This is because the gland enlarges as it works harder.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.3] Iodine deficiency causes tiredness and sluggishness (lethargy) and weight gain in adults. It causes delayed mental and physical development in children, and in babies of deficient mothers.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.b] Potassium and sodium work as a pair, one inside cells and one outside, to keep fluid, nerves and muscles balanced.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.0] Potassium sources include bananas, potatoes eaten in their skins, tomatoes, dried fruit, orange juice, pulses, green and leafy vegetables, milk, meat, fish and nuts.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.1] Potassium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) inside cells and, with sodium, helps regulate fluid balance in and around cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.2] Potassium is needed to transmit nerve impulses and for muscle contraction, including the heartbeat. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.3] Potassium works with sodium to regulate blood pressure, and a high potassium intake helps lower it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.4] Deficiency (hypokalaemia) causes muscle weakness, cramps, an irregular heartbeat and tiredness. It is usually caused by severe vomiting, diarrhoea or some medicines rather than by normal diets.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.b] Sodium is essential, and the problem in Ireland is eating too much of it, not too little. Most of it comes from salt, which is sodium chloride.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.0] Sodium is mainly consumed as salt. Table salt, cured and smoked meats and fish such as bacon and ham, cheese, bread, crisps and snack foods, processed and ready meals, sauces, stock cubes and canned foods supply most of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.1] Fresh, unprocessed foods contain only small amounts of naturally occurring sodium. This is why processed foods, such as bacon and ready meals, are the main source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.2] Sodium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) outside cells and, with potassium, regulates fluid balance and blood volume.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.3] Sodium is needed to transmit nerve impulses, for muscle contraction and for enzyme function. It helps the small intestine absorb glucose and some amino acids. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.4] Excess sodium raises blood pressure (hypertension), which increases the risk of stroke, heart disease and kidney problems. Most people in Ireland eat more salt than advised, mostly from processed foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.5] To cut salt, cook from fresh food, do not add salt at the table, read labels (salt is on the nutrition declaration) and choose low-salt products.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.6] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) began a voluntary Salt Reduction Programme in 2003 to cut salt in processed food. Later reformulation work continues under the FSAI-Healthy Ireland Food Reformulation Task Force.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.7] Deficiency is uncommon but causes muscle cramps, headache, nausea and confusion. It can follow heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea.
