TARGET hom-food-preservation-s2: 2. Freezing
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.b] Freezing preserves food by lowering its temperature far enough to stop enzyme action and microbial growth, and by turning free water into ice that micro-organisms cannot use. It is popular because no high heat is needed, so food stays close to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.0] Low temperature slows enzyme reactions (enzymes are the natural chemicals in food that make it spoil or change) and stops micro-organisms from reproducing, but it does not kill most of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.1] Frozen food is not sterile: micro-organisms become dormant (inactive) and start growing again when the food thaws, so frozen food is never safe indefinitely.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.2] Turning free water into ice removes the water micro-organisms need, because ice is unavailable to them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.3] Pure water freezes at 0 °C, but food freezes below this because its dissolved sugars and salts lower its freezing point.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.4] Water expands by about 9 per cent when it freezes, which is why liquids need headspace (an empty gap at the top) in their containers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.5] Freezing itself does little harm to nutritive value, and colour, flavour and texture are kept better than with most other methods because no high heat is used, except in blanching; most nutrient losses happen during blanching and thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.b] The speed of freezing decides how big the ice crystals are, and crystal size decides how much damage the food suffers. Quick freezing makes small crystals; slow freezing makes large ones.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.0] Quick freezing takes place at about −25 °C (the 2013 Higher Level scheme gives −25 °C to −40 °C) in the fast-freeze section of a freezer, so the food passes through the freezing range rapidly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.1] Quick freezing forms small ice crystals within the cells, which causes little damage to the cell walls, so there is little drip (juice lost) on thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.2] For this reason quick freezing retains the texture, colour, flavour and nutritive value of food better than slow freezing does.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.3] Slow freezing takes place at a higher temperature than −25 °C, as in an ordinary freezer compartment, where ice forms gradually.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.4] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals, mostly between the cells, that pierce and rupture cell walls, allowing juices to escape.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.5] As a result slow-frozen food is limp and soft when thawed, and it loses colour and flavour and drip containing soluble vitamins B1 and C and minerals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.6] The fast-freeze setting should be switched on about 3 to 4 hours before food is placed in the freezer, so the freezer is cold enough to freeze the new food quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.7] Diagram: draw two cells side by side, one with small crystals inside an intact cell wall (quick) and one with large crystals and a ruptured wall (slow).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.b] Blanching is a brief heat treatment given to vegetables before freezing. Enzymes keep working slowly even at freezer temperatures, so vegetables would spoil in storage without it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.0] 1. The vegetables are plunged into boiling water for a short, set time that depends on the vegetable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.1] 2. They are plunged immediately into ice-cold water for the same length of time, which stops the cooking so the vegetables do not go soft.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.2] 3. They are drained and dried, because surface water left on them would form ice crystals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.3] Blanching inactivates enzymes that would otherwise cause loss of colour, flavour, texture and nutrients during frozen storage.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.4] Blanching also cleans the vegetable, destroys some micro-organisms on its surface, softens it slightly so it packs well, and keeps its bright colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.5] Blanching loses some water-soluble vitamins, particularly vitamin C and vitamin B1, into the water and through heat. Blanch small batches in plenty of fast-boiling water so the water returns to the boil quickly and the timing stays short and accurate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.6] Fruit is not blanched; soft fruit is open-frozen on trays or packed in sugar or sugar syrup. Some foods, such as soups, sauces and casseroles, are frozen cooked after cooling.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.b] Good results at home depend on following a set of steps, and each step has a reason. Most of them aim either to freeze the food quickly or to keep air and moisture away from it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.0] Choose fresh, best-quality food in perfect condition and freeze it soon after harvest or purchase, because freezing keeps quality but cannot improve it. Wash, peel, trim or cut as needed, blanch vegetables, and cool cooked food quickly before it goes into the freezer.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.1] Portion food into usable (family-sized) quantities so that only what is needed is thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.2] Open-freeze (tray-freeze) sticky or delicate items, such as strawberries or raspberries, or items to be kept separate, such as sausages and burgers: spread them in a single layer on a tray, freeze hard, then bag them so that they stay separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.3] Pack food in strong, moisture-proof and vapour-proof freezer bags, foil or rigid plastic containers, and remove as much air as possible so that the food does not dry out. Leave headspace in containers holding liquids to allow for expansion as the food freezes.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.4] Seal tightly and label with contents and date. Place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze compartment, as the freezer instructs, and keep them apart from already frozen food until frozen, so they freeze quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.5] Do not overload the freezer: freeze no more than about one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24 hours, because overloading raises the temperature and slows freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.6] Store frozen food at −18 °C or colder, using the oldest first. The safe storage time depends on the food: fatty foods and oily fish keep for a shorter period, and fatty foods stored too long can turn rancid.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.7] Foods that freeze well include peas, beans, carrots, broccoli, apples, strawberries, meat, fish, bread, cakes, soups, sauces and prepared meals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.8] Foods that freeze poorly include lettuce, whole tomatoes and other high-water salad foods, bananas, melons, pears, whole eggs in the shell, low-fat cream and mayonnaise-based foods, which go limp, watery or separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.b] Problems with frozen food usually come from air reaching it or from careless thawing. Both are preventable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.0] Freezer burn shows as dry, discoloured, tough patches where air has reached the surface of the food, caused by dehydration and oxidation, especially in protein foods such as meat and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.1] Airtight packaging with the air removed prevents freezer burn.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.2] Thaw food in a fridge, or cook it from frozen where the food allows, such as vegetables, so that the food stays below the danger zone while it thaws. Thaw meat and poultry fully before cooking unless the pack says otherwise.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.3] Thawed food should be used promptly and not refrozen as it is, because quality declines and micro-organisms may have multiplied. Raw food thawed in the fridge can be cooked and then frozen as a cooked dish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.4] In a freezer fault or power cut, keep the door closed and do not open it, because frozen food keeps for a time if the cold is held in.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.b] Commercial freezing is faster and colder than home freezing, so ice crystals are smaller and quality is higher. Food is frozen soon after harvesting or catching, often on the farm or on a factory ship. Home freezers are not as cold or as fast, so home freezing does not match these methods.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.0] Air blast freezing: a very cold air stream at about −30 °C is blown over food, often on a conveyor belt or through a tunnel; suitable for most foods, such as fruit, vegetables, meat and bakery products.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.1] Plate (contact) freezing: food is pressed between two hollow metal plates cooled by refrigerant; suitable for thin, flat foods such as fish fillets, flat pieces of meat and burgers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.2] Flow (fluidised bed) freezing: cold air is blown up through a perforated conveyor belt so that small pieces float and freeze individually, staying free-flowing and not sticking together; suitable for peas, sweetcorn and berries.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.3] Cryogenic freezing: food is exposed to liquid nitrogen or carbon dioxide at extremely low temperature, which freezes it almost instantly; suitable for delicate or high-value foods such as strawberries, prawns and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.4] The cold chain keeps frozen food at −18 °C or colder in cold stores, refrigerated lorries and supermarket freezer cabinets until it reaches the home.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.5] When buying frozen food, check that the pack is intact with no ice crystals or frost inside, that it is below the load line on the cabinet, and take it home quickly in a cool bag.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.b] Freezing helps a household plan meals, save money and cut waste. These benefits are the points examiners expect when they ask about frozen foods in meal planning.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.0] Freezing surplus food before it spoils reduces household waste, and leftovers can be frozen for reuse as ready-made portions.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.1] Bulk cooking and freezing portions saves time and fuel, since a large batch is cooked once.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.2] A freezer stock of meals and ingredients provides emergency food and convenience on busy days, including for households with different eating times.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.3] Buying in bulk or when produce is in season is more economical, and freezing keeps it for a longer period (a longer shelf life).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.4] Frozen foods provide a wide variety of vegetables, fish and fruit all year, including items not available fresh locally.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.5] Freezing retains colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value well compared with heat processing; frozen vegetables keep more vitamin C than canned ones, as one past-paper table shows for peas (25 mg fresh, 17 mg frozen, 8 mg canned).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.6] Fruit and salad foods go soft after thawing because of cell damage, so they suit cooking better than eating raw.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.7] Labelling each pack with contents, date and portion size lets older items be used first, which helps planned menus.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s1: 1. Principles of food preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.b] Food starts to spoil as soon as it is harvested or slaughtered. Preservation means treating food so that spoilage is slowed or stopped and the food can be kept, moved and eaten later, with colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as near as possible to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.0] It reduces waste by keeping surplus produce, such as a glut of home-grown fruit or vegetables, usable for months rather than days, instead of being thrown away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.1] Because less food is thrown away, preservation also reduces the environmental footprint of food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.2] It makes food available out of season and over long distances, so perishable foods (those that spoil quickly) can be transported and stored.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.3] It makes food safer by destroying food poisoning micro-organisms or stopping them from growing, and it lengthens shelf life (the time food stays good to eat).
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.4] It creates foods with distinct textures and flavours, such as jam, chutney, dried fruit, smoked fish and cured meat, which increases variety and choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.b] Spoilage is not caused only by micro-organisms. Several separate processes make food unusable, and each has its own visible effect.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.0] Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions and are naturally present in food. After harvest or slaughter they carry on working, so food ripens, softens, darkens and changes flavour even when no micro-organism is present. Cut apple, banana and potato brown this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.1] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) grow on food, causing decay, rot, souring, off-odours and slime, and in some cases food poisoning. Moulds give fuzzy growth on bread, cheese and jam; yeasts ferment fruit juice and jam, with bubbling and an alcoholic smell; bacteria cause souring, putrefaction and food poisoning.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.2] Putrefaction is the breakdown of proteins by bacteria, producing foul-smelling products.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.3] Oxidation occurs when oxygen reacts with fats, causing rancidity (an unpleasant, stale taste and smell), and with the cut surfaces of fruit and vegetables, causing browning. Vitamin C is also lost by oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.4] Moisture loss through the skins of fruit and vegetables causes shrivelling, wilting, wrinkling and loss of weight. It also dries and hardens bread and cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.5] Incorrect storage temperature allows micro-organisms and enzymes to work quickly, because warmth speeds both of them up.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.6] Poor hygiene and careless handling carry micro-organisms from unwashed hands, equipment, pests or raw food onto prepared food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.7] Chemical contaminants, such as insecticide or pesticide residues, antibiotics, metals and residues from cleaning products, can make food unsafe. Physical damage such as bruising also opens food to spoilage.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.b] Micro-organisms need six conditions to grow and multiply. Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.0] The six conditions are food (nutrients), moisture (available water), warmth (a suitable temperature), time (to multiply), a suitable pH or acidity and, for aerobic ones (those that need air), oxygen. Most bacteria prefer a neutral or slightly acid pH.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.1] Bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply rapidly in the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C, which is why chilled and hot food is kept outside it. Below 5 °C growth is slowed; above 63 °C it is stopped and most are killed by sustained heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.2] Under good conditions one bacterial cell divides about every 20 minutes (binary fission, splitting in two), so numbers rise very fast.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.3] Moulds and yeasts tolerate acid, sugar and low moisture better than most bacteria, so they spoil jam and dried fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.4] Moulds appear as fuzzy colonies or threads; yeasts produce bubbling, gas and a sour or alcoholic smell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.5] Enzymes work best at a moderate temperature and are destroyed (denatured) by heat or strong acid; cold slows them but does not stop them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.6] Micro-organisms cannot multiply in food with little available water, which is why drying and concentrated sugar or salt preserve food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.b] Three groups of micro-organism spoil food, and spores and enzymes also have to be understood. Each acts differently, which is why different methods suit different foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.0] Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause food poisoning and others spoil food by producing acid and gas.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.1] Yeasts are single-celled fungi that ferment sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.2] Moulds are multicellular fungi that grow as visible threads and spread by airborne spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.3] Spores are dormant (resting), resistant forms produced by some bacteria and all moulds; they survive ordinary heat and cold and start growing again when conditions improve.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.4] Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein, and each one acts on a specific substance in food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.b] Osmosis explains why strong sugar or salt solutions preserve food. It is the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane (one that lets water through but not most dissolved substances) from a weaker solution to a stronger one.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.0] The cell membrane of a micro-organism is partially permeable, so water leaves the cell when the surrounding solution is concentrated.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.1] Loss of water makes the cell shrink and stop growing, and at high concentrations it can kill the cell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.2] The same process draws water out of food, which is why salted meat and fish and sugared fruit keep. A strong sugar or salt solution outside a microbial cell draws water out, so the cell shrivels and cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.b] Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of the conditions that micro-organisms and enzymes need, and by preventing re-contamination. There are only a few principles, and each method is an application of one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.0] Destroy micro-organisms and inactivate enzymes with heat, as in canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching. Irradiation also destroys organisms and some enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.1] Slow or stop activity with low temperature: refrigeration slows growth and enzyme action, and freezing makes organisms dormant (inactivated, not killed) and makes water unavailable as ice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.2] Remove available water: dehydration (drying, freeze drying) takes water away, while sugar and salt tie it up and draw it out of organisms by osmosis, as in jam, salted fish and cured meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.3] Alter the pH with acid: vinegar (acetic acid) in pickles and chutney, or acids produced in fermentation, such as lactic acid in yoghurt and sauerkraut and acetic acid in vinegar. This prevents the growth of many spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.4] Use chemical preservatives that inhibit organisms: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives such as sulphur dioxide, sorbates, benzoates and nitrite.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.5] Exclude oxygen and prevent re-entry of organisms with airtight packaging, vacuum sealing, sealed hot jars (a vacuum forms as the contents cool) and moisture-proof wraps for the freezer. Removing oxygen also inactivates enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.6] Prevent re-contamination after treatment, using sterilised jars and lids, clean utensils, good hygiene and sealing straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.7] Most methods combine principles. Jam uses heat, sugar (osmosis), acid and pectin, and a sealed jar; chutney uses heat, vinegar and sugar; smoked fish uses salt, drying and smoke chemicals.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.b] The methods of preservation fall into groups according to the principle they use. Knowing the groups shows the structure of food preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.0] Low temperature: refrigeration and freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.1] Heat: canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.2] Water removal: drying and freeze drying, and sugar or salt that bind water.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.3] Chemicals: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives, with sugar, vinegar, salt, smoking and alcohol being the traditional natural preservatives.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.4] Fermentation and irradiation.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s6: 6. Safe food preparation
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.b] Almost every rule for handling food comes from two facts about food poisoning bacteria: they multiply fastest in warm food, and they travel easily from one food to another. Knowing these makes the individual rules easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.0] Food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest in the danger zone, 5°C to 63°C. This is why safe preparation keeps food cold, hot, or moving quickly through that range.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.1] Cross-contamination is the transfer of bacteria from raw food to cooked or ready-to-eat food, directly or by hands, chopping boards, cloths, utensils or meat juices. This is why raw and cooked foods are kept apart.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.2] Freezing does not kill bacteria, it only stops them multiplying, and thawing lets them start multiplying again. So frozen food must be handled with care once it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.b] Storage aims to keep food cold or frozen so bacteria cannot multiply, and to stop bacteria passing from one food to another. Short questions usually ask for three to six storage guidelines.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.0] Cover all food and store it in sealed containers, which prevents drying out, odour transfer and cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.1] Keep perishable food (food that spoils quickly) in the fridge at 5°C or below, which slows the growth of bacteria. Check the fridge and freezer temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.2] Store raw meat, poultry and fish on the lowest shelf, on a plate and loosely covered with the shop wrapping removed, so that juices cannot drip onto cooked or ready-to-eat food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.3] Keep raw and cooked foods apart, covered or in sealed containers, so bacteria cannot pass between them.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.4] Cool cooked food quickly before refrigerating or freezing it, and do not overpack the fridge, so that cold air can circulate around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.5] Check the use-by or best-before date of each food and follow the storage instructions on its label. Use-by is a safety date for highly perishable food, while best-before is a quality date.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.6] Remove fruit and vegetables from plastic wrapping, which encourages mould, and check them regularly for mould.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.7] Store dry foods in a cool, dry, well-ventilated cupboard, in vermin-proof containers, and keep storage areas clean.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.8] Keep perishable food out of the fridge for the shortest possible time while it is being prepared, because food left in warm air soon enters the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.b] Freezing keeps food safe for a long time, but only if it is frozen and thawed properly. Bacteria are not killed by freezing, so the care taken when thawing matters.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.0] Freeze food quickly at −25°C, then store it at −18°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.1] Defrost frozen food completely before cooking, preferably in the fridge on the bottom shelf, so that it thaws slowly and any drips cannot reach other food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.2] Never refreeze food that has thawed, because bacteria multiply while it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.b] Cooking and reheating must heat food right through to kill bacteria, and hot food must then stay hot or be eaten promptly. Otherwise bacteria multiply again in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.0] Cook food thoroughly, using the right time and temperature, and turn or stir it so that heat reaches the centre. Cook meat, poultry and minced meat right through, to a core temperature of at least 72°C for poultry.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.1] Reheat food quickly until it is above 100°C throughout, and reheat it only once.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.2] High-risk foods such as stews and gravies should be brought to boiling when reheated, because their moist, protein-rich nature suits bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.3] Cover food when reheating. In a microwave, stir or turn the food and allow standing time, so that heat reaches all parts evenly, and follow the appliance's instructions.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.4] Serve cooked food immediately. If holding it hot, keep it above 63°C, because bacteria multiply rapidly between 5°C and 63°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.5] Eat food that has been held hot within about 90 minutes (1.5 hours).
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.6] Do not keep food in the danger zone. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate them and use them soon.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.b] People carry food poisoning bacteria on their hands, hair, skin and clothes, so the person preparing food is a major source of contamination. Examiners usually ask for four to six personal hygiene points.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.0] Wash hands in hot water with liquid soap, and a nailbrush for the nails, before handling food, because hands carry food poisoning bacteria. Dry them on a clean towel or paper towel.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.1] Wash hands after the toilet, sneezing or coughing, changing nappies, handling pets, handling rubbish and handling raw meat.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.2] Keep nails short and clean and remove jewellery before preparing food, because dirt and bacteria collect under nails and around jewellery.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.3] Tie hair back or cover it. Wear a clean apron or clothing, and change it if it becomes soiled.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.4] Cover cuts, boils and sores with a brightly coloured waterproof plaster, so that infection cannot pass to food and the plaster can be seen if it falls into the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.5] Do not cough or sneeze over food, and do not taste food with your fingers or lick your fingers.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.6] Do not prepare or serve food for others while suffering from diarrhoea or vomiting, because the illness can pass to the people eating it.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.b] A clean kitchen with sensible habits stops bacteria building up on surfaces and equipment and passing to food. Four kitchen hygiene practices is a common exam question.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.0] Clean and disinfect work surfaces often, and wash equipment in hot water and detergent. Clean as you go.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.1] Use colour-coded chopping boards and separate utensils for raw and cooked foods, to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.2] Never prepare raw and cooked foods on the same surface.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.3] Handle food as little as possible during preparation, and keep it covered, because every touch can pass on bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.4] Floors, walls and work surfaces should be easy to clean, and the kitchen should have good lighting.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.5] Effective ventilation reduces condensation, which encourages the growth of micro-organisms.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.6] The kitchen should have a clean water supply and no access for pets or pests.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.7] Keep a covered waste bin and empty it regularly, and change clean cloths and dishtowels often.
