TARGET hom-food-preservation-s5: 5. Chemical preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.b] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) need available water, a suitable acidity and working enzymes to grow. Chemical preservatives attack one or more of these, which is why preserved food keeps for much longer.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.0] Some preservatives draw water out of food and micro-organisms by osmosis (water moving out through a cell wall towards a stronger solution), so the organisms cannot multiply. Sugar and salt work this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.1] Some lower the pH (make the food more acidic) so that most spoilage and food poisoning bacteria cannot grow. Vinegar and other acids work this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.2] Some directly poison or inhibit micro-organisms or their enzymes, such as sulphur dioxide, benzoates, sorbates, nitrite and the chemicals in smoke. Others act as antioxidants (stopping oxygen damaging the food) or inhibit mould.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.3] Sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke and alcohol are the traditional (natural) preservatives. Sugar, salt and vinegar are the traditional home preservatives, and smoke was the traditional preservative for fish and meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.4] The permitted additive preservatives are sorbic acid, benzoic acid, sulphur dioxide and sulphites, and nitrites and nitrates.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.5] Many chemical methods are combined with heat, as in jam and chutney, or with drying, as in smoked and salted fish. Using two methods together makes the food safer than either alone.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.0.6] Fruit can also be preserved in alcohol, for example fruit kept in brandy or rum.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.b] A strong sugar solution holds on to water so tightly that micro-organisms cannot use it. This is why jam keeps in a sealed jar.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.0] At high concentration, sugar binds water and draws it out of microbial cells by osmosis, so the micro-organisms cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.1] Sugar is the preservative in jam, marmalade, syrups, crystallised and candied fruit and condensed milk.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.2] The concentration must be high enough. Low-sugar jam can ferment or grow mould, so a strong sugar solution is essential.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.3] Yeasts and moulds tolerate sugar better than most bacteria, so sugared products still need sealed, clean containers to keep them out.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.4] Sugar also has other roles in jam: it forms a gel with pectin (the setting substance in fruit), and it adds sweetness and colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.1.5] The Department of Health healthy eating guidelines advise limiting foods and drinks high in added sugar, since excess sugar is linked to tooth decay and excess energy intake. Preserves contain a lot of added sugar.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.b] Salt works like sugar, pulling water out of food and out of any micro-organisms on it. It has been used on fish and meat, but it has a health cost.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.0] Salt draws water out of food and micro-organisms by osmosis, and it makes the remaining water unavailable to organisms and to enzyme action.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.1] Dry salting: the food is packed in salt, which draws out moisture. This method is used for salt cod, salt pork, bacon and salted herring.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.2] Brining: the food is immersed or soaked in a strong salt solution, as used for olives, ham, pickled meat and brine pickles of vegetables such as cucumbers.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.3] Curing combines salt with sodium nitrite or nitrate, and sometimes sugar, to preserve meat such as bacon, ham and corned beef. The nitrite inhibits Clostridium botulinum (a dangerous food poisoning bacterium) and keeps the meat pink.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.4] Salt-cured foods must be stored in cool, dry conditions, since moisture in the air can cause surface mould.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.5] Salted foods need soaking or rinsing to remove the salt before they are used.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.2.6] Excess salt (sodium) raises blood pressure, and salted and cured foods are high in sodium. The Department of Health healthy eating guidelines advise reducing salt in the diet.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.b] Vinegar does not dry food out. It preserves by making the food too acidic for most bacteria to survive, and it adds flavour at the same time.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.0] Vinegar is a dilute solution of acetic acid, which lowers the pH of food and inhibits most spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.1] Vinegar pickling: vegetables such as onions, cucumbers, beetroot and red cabbage, and also eggs, are packed in spiced vinegar, which acts as the preservative. It is also used in chutneys, relishes and sauces, sometimes with sugar and spices.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.2] Vinegar also gives sharpness and flavour, and its effectiveness depends on its acetic acid strength. It must be strong enough that the water in the food does not dilute it.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.3] Spiced vinegar, made cold or hot, is poured over vegetables packed into sterilised jars. Malt vinegar is the usual home choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.3.4] Use non-metallic lids on jars of vinegar foods, because the acid corrodes metal and spoils the seal.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.b] Chutney and pickle both rely on vinegar, but they are different products. Chutney is cooked down to a thick mixture, while pickle keeps its pieces whole in a liquid.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.0] Chutney is made by chopping fruit and/or vegetables (apples, tomatoes, onions, marrow, plums) and cooking them slowly with vinegar, sugar and spices until the mixture is thick. It needs no pectin, unlike jam.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.1] Each ingredient has a job. The cooking destroys micro-organisms and enzymes, the vinegar (an acid) and sugar prevent any survivors from growing, and spices such as mustard seed, chilli and ginger add flavour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.2] Chutney method: 1. Sterilise the jars. 2. Prepare and chop the fruit and vegetables. 3. Cook slowly with vinegar, sugar and spices until thick and no liquid vinegar shows when a spoon is drawn across. 4. Pot into hot jars. 5. Seal with vinegar-proof lids and label.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.3] Chutney is poured hot into sterilised jars, sealed and stored in a cool, dark place, where it matures and improves in flavour after weeks. Once opened it is kept in the fridge.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.4] Pickles are raw, lightly cooked or brined vegetables, eggs or fruit packed whole or in pieces in vinegar or brine. Vinegar pickles are covered with spiced vinegar and sugar and sealed.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.4.5] Chutney spoils if there is too little vinegar or sugar, if it is not cooked long enough, if jars or lids are not sterile or the seal is faulty, or if a metal lid is corroded by the vinegar.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.b] Smoking preserves food by exposing it to smoke from smouldering wood, and it adds flavour and colour as well. It is used mostly on fish and meat, and also on cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.0] Food is exposed to smoke from smouldering wood (such as oak, beech or hickory) or sawdust. The smoke deposits chemicals (phenols, formaldehyde and organic acids) that inhibit micro-organisms and slow oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.1] Smoke also dries the surface of the food, which slows micro-organism growth and gives flavour and colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.2] Smoking is almost always combined with salting first, by brining or dry salting, because smoking gives limited shelf life on its own. Smoked meat and fish are usually also salted or cured.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.3] Cold smoking is carried out at a low temperature, not exceeding about 27 °C, so the food is flavoured and partly preserved but not cooked and requires further cooking. Smoked salmon is an example.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.4] Hot smoking raises the temperature gradually to about 80 °C, so the food is cooked as it is smoked and is ready to eat, as in smoked mackerel.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.5] Smoking fish, in order: 1. Fillet and trim. 2. Brine or dry-salt. 3. Rinse and dry. 4. Smoke at the chosen temperature. 5. Cool.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.5.6] Smoked foods have a limited shelf life unless chilled, and they are high in salt. Some smoke compounds (tars) are a health concern if eaten in large amounts.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.b] Besides the traditional preservatives, food manufacturers may add certain chemical preservatives. They are tightly controlled, and each one works best against particular organisms in particular foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.0] They are controlled in the EU by Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives: only listed substances may be used, in listed foods, up to maximum levels. They are given E numbers (E200 to E299 are the preservatives) and are tested for safety by the European Food Safety Authority.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.1] Sorbic acid (E200) and potassium sorbate (E202) inhibit moulds and yeasts in foods such as cheese, baked goods and soft drinks.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.2] Benzoic acid (E210) and sodium benzoate (E211) inhibit yeasts, moulds and bacteria in acidic foods such as soft drinks, pickles and fruit products.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.3] Sulphur dioxide (E220) and sulphites inhibit micro-organisms and prevent browning in products such as dried fruit, wine and fruit juice.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.4] Sodium nitrite (E250) and sodium nitrate (E251) are used in cured meat. Nitrite inhibits Clostridium botulinum and gives cured meat its pink colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.5] Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information to consumers, a preservative in the ingredients list is given with its function ("preservative") followed by its name or E number. Sulphur dioxide and sulphites above the stated level are among the 14 allergens that must be emphasised.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.6] Benefits are longer shelf life, safety, less waste and lower cost. Concerns are that some people react to preservatives (asthmatics may react to sulphites), consumer preference for fewer additives, and high sodium in salt-based preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s5.6.7] An E number shows only that an additive is approved, not that it is natural or unsafe.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s1: 1. Principles of food preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.b] Food starts to spoil as soon as it is harvested or slaughtered. Preservation means treating food so that spoilage is slowed or stopped and the food can be kept, moved and eaten later, with colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as near as possible to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.0] It reduces waste by keeping surplus produce, such as a glut of home-grown fruit or vegetables, usable for months rather than days, instead of being thrown away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.1] Because less food is thrown away, preservation also reduces the environmental footprint of food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.2] It makes food available out of season and over long distances, so perishable foods (those that spoil quickly) can be transported and stored.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.3] It makes food safer by destroying food poisoning micro-organisms or stopping them from growing, and it lengthens shelf life (the time food stays good to eat).
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.4] It creates foods with distinct textures and flavours, such as jam, chutney, dried fruit, smoked fish and cured meat, which increases variety and choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.b] Spoilage is not caused only by micro-organisms. Several separate processes make food unusable, and each has its own visible effect.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.0] Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions and are naturally present in food. After harvest or slaughter they carry on working, so food ripens, softens, darkens and changes flavour even when no micro-organism is present. Cut apple, banana and potato brown this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.1] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) grow on food, causing decay, rot, souring, off-odours and slime, and in some cases food poisoning. Moulds give fuzzy growth on bread, cheese and jam; yeasts ferment fruit juice and jam, with bubbling and an alcoholic smell; bacteria cause souring, putrefaction and food poisoning.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.2] Putrefaction is the breakdown of proteins by bacteria, producing foul-smelling products.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.3] Oxidation occurs when oxygen reacts with fats, causing rancidity (an unpleasant, stale taste and smell), and with the cut surfaces of fruit and vegetables, causing browning. Vitamin C is also lost by oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.4] Moisture loss through the skins of fruit and vegetables causes shrivelling, wilting, wrinkling and loss of weight. It also dries and hardens bread and cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.5] Incorrect storage temperature allows micro-organisms and enzymes to work quickly, because warmth speeds both of them up.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.6] Poor hygiene and careless handling carry micro-organisms from unwashed hands, equipment, pests or raw food onto prepared food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.7] Chemical contaminants, such as insecticide or pesticide residues, antibiotics, metals and residues from cleaning products, can make food unsafe. Physical damage such as bruising also opens food to spoilage.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.b] Micro-organisms need six conditions to grow and multiply. Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.0] The six conditions are food (nutrients), moisture (available water), warmth (a suitable temperature), time (to multiply), a suitable pH or acidity and, for aerobic ones (those that need air), oxygen. Most bacteria prefer a neutral or slightly acid pH.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.1] Bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply rapidly in the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C, which is why chilled and hot food is kept outside it. Below 5 °C growth is slowed; above 63 °C it is stopped and most are killed by sustained heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.2] Under good conditions one bacterial cell divides about every 20 minutes (binary fission, splitting in two), so numbers rise very fast.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.3] Moulds and yeasts tolerate acid, sugar and low moisture better than most bacteria, so they spoil jam and dried fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.4] Moulds appear as fuzzy colonies or threads; yeasts produce bubbling, gas and a sour or alcoholic smell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.5] Enzymes work best at a moderate temperature and are destroyed (denatured) by heat or strong acid; cold slows them but does not stop them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.6] Micro-organisms cannot multiply in food with little available water, which is why drying and concentrated sugar or salt preserve food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.b] Three groups of micro-organism spoil food, and spores and enzymes also have to be understood. Each acts differently, which is why different methods suit different foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.0] Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause food poisoning and others spoil food by producing acid and gas.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.1] Yeasts are single-celled fungi that ferment sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.2] Moulds are multicellular fungi that grow as visible threads and spread by airborne spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.3] Spores are dormant (resting), resistant forms produced by some bacteria and all moulds; they survive ordinary heat and cold and start growing again when conditions improve.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.4] Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein, and each one acts on a specific substance in food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.b] Osmosis explains why strong sugar or salt solutions preserve food. It is the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane (one that lets water through but not most dissolved substances) from a weaker solution to a stronger one.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.0] The cell membrane of a micro-organism is partially permeable, so water leaves the cell when the surrounding solution is concentrated.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.1] Loss of water makes the cell shrink and stop growing, and at high concentrations it can kill the cell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.2] The same process draws water out of food, which is why salted meat and fish and sugared fruit keep. A strong sugar or salt solution outside a microbial cell draws water out, so the cell shrivels and cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.b] Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of the conditions that micro-organisms and enzymes need, and by preventing re-contamination. There are only a few principles, and each method is an application of one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.0] Destroy micro-organisms and inactivate enzymes with heat, as in canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching. Irradiation also destroys organisms and some enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.1] Slow or stop activity with low temperature: refrigeration slows growth and enzyme action, and freezing makes organisms dormant (inactivated, not killed) and makes water unavailable as ice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.2] Remove available water: dehydration (drying, freeze drying) takes water away, while sugar and salt tie it up and draw it out of organisms by osmosis, as in jam, salted fish and cured meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.3] Alter the pH with acid: vinegar (acetic acid) in pickles and chutney, or acids produced in fermentation, such as lactic acid in yoghurt and sauerkraut and acetic acid in vinegar. This prevents the growth of many spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.4] Use chemical preservatives that inhibit organisms: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives such as sulphur dioxide, sorbates, benzoates and nitrite.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.5] Exclude oxygen and prevent re-entry of organisms with airtight packaging, vacuum sealing, sealed hot jars (a vacuum forms as the contents cool) and moisture-proof wraps for the freezer. Removing oxygen also inactivates enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.6] Prevent re-contamination after treatment, using sterilised jars and lids, clean utensils, good hygiene and sealing straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.7] Most methods combine principles. Jam uses heat, sugar (osmosis), acid and pectin, and a sealed jar; chutney uses heat, vinegar and sugar; smoked fish uses salt, drying and smoke chemicals.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.b] The methods of preservation fall into groups according to the principle they use. Knowing the groups shows the structure of food preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.0] Low temperature: refrigeration and freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.1] Heat: canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.2] Water removal: drying and freeze drying, and sugar or salt that bind water.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.3] Chemicals: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives, with sugar, vinegar, salt, smoking and alcohol being the traditional natural preservatives.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.4] Fermentation and irradiation.

CANDIDATES hom-food-additives-s4: 4. Classes of food additives
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.b] A food additive is a natural or artificial substance added to food during manufacture to improve colour, taste, texture, appearance, keeping qualities or nutritive value, or to aid processing. Because each additive is added for a particular job, additives are sorted into classes by that job.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.0] Additives are grouped into classes by their function: colourings, flavourings (including flavour enhancers), sweeteners, preservatives, antioxidants, nutritional supplements and, at Higher Level, physical conditioning agents.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.1] Each class has natural examples (from plants, animals or minerals) and artificial examples (made by chemical synthesis), and the function of the additive, not its origin, is the basis for its class.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.2] Natural does not mean safer: both natural and artificial additives must be approved before they can be used in food.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.3] Settings: examine a food label to find the additives used, identify the class of each from its name or E number (a code given to an approved additive), and suggest the reason it was added.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.4] Additives have disadvantages: some people react to them (allergy, intolerance, or a hyperactivity warning on certain colours), and they may mask poor-quality food or encourage a diet of processed foods.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.5] Two further disadvantages are specific to one additive each: sulphur dioxide destroys thiamine (vitamin B1), and polyols (bulk sweeteners) can cause diarrhoea if eaten in excess.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.b] Processing, such as heating or canning, can fade or change the natural colour of food, and shoppers judge food by its look. Colourings are added to put this right.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.0] Function: colourings restore colour lost in processing, make food more attractive and give a consistent, uniform appearance from batch to batch.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.1] Natural colourings include caramel (E150), chlorophyll (E140), carotene (E160a), cochineal (E120) and turmeric (E100, also called curcumin).
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.2] Artificial colourings include tartrazine (E102), a yellow dye used in soft drinks and sweets.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.3] Colourings are used in foods such as sweets, soft drinks, desserts, baked goods and cheese products.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.4] Tartrazine and some other azo colours (a group of synthetic dyes) must carry a warning that they may affect activity and attention in children, which is one reason some people avoid them.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.b] Some foods taste bland to begin with and others lose flavour during processing. Flavourings put taste back or add it, while flavour enhancers work differently by making a flavour already there seem stronger.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.0] Function: flavourings add flavour to bland food, replace flavour lost in processing, and (as enhancers) strengthen an existing flavour.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.1] Natural flavourings include herbs, spices, sugar and salt, used in foods such as jam, tinned beans, cereals, meat products, sauces, stock cubes, cheese and butter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.2] Artificial flavourings are chemical compounds made by synthesis, such as esters and aldehydes; ethyl acetate gives a rum flavour, amyl acetate a pear flavour, benzaldehyde a cherry flavour, and maltol is another example. They are used in baked goods and cakes.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.3] Flavour enhancers (in the E600 to E699 range) improve an existing flavour and make it seem stronger; monosodium glutamate (E621) is the most common example.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.4] Monosodium glutamate is used in soups, sauces, stock cubes and prepared meals.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.5] Flavourings in the narrow sense carry no E number, but flavour enhancers do, so do not say that every additive has an E number.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.b] Bacteria, yeasts and moulds spoil food. Preservatives slow this down so that food stays safe and edible for longer.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.0] Preservatives inhibit the growth of micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds), which delays spoilage and extending shelf life.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.1] Natural preservatives include salt, sugar, vinegar and smoke, which are accepted as natural preservatives.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.2] Chemical preservatives include sulphur dioxide (E220) and potassium sorbate (E202), used in soft drinks, jams, baked goods, dried fruit and wine.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.3] Sulphur dioxide destroys some thiamine (vitamin B1), so its use reduces the nutritive value of the food.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.4] Sulphur dioxide must be declared on the label as an allergen, because some people react to it.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.b] Sweeteners give a sweet taste, but they fall into very different groups. Nutritive sweeteners supply energy, intense sweeteners replace sugar with almost no energy, and bulk sweeteners (polyols) sweeten while also adding body.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.0] Nutritive sweeteners such as sugar, honey, glucose syrup and fructose supply energy as well as sweetness.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.1] Intense sweeteners (high-intensity, non-nutritive sweeteners) are many times sweeter than sugar, so only a tiny amount is needed and they contribute negligible energy.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.2] Intense sweeteners include saccharin (E954), aspartame (E951), acesulfame K (E950) and sucralose (E955).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.3] Because the quantity used is so small, intense sweeteners do not provide sugar's bulk, browning, preservation or texture, so a bulking agent may also be needed.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.4] Intense sweeteners are used in low-energy and diet soft drinks, tabletop sweeteners, yoghurts, desserts and foods for people reducing their sugar intake.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.5] Products containing aspartame carry a label warning for people with phenylketonuria, as aspartame is a source of phenylalanine (an amino acid they cannot process).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.6] Bulk sweeteners, or polyols, include sorbitol (E420), mannitol (E421) and xylitol; they are less intensely sweet, are used in larger amounts, give bulk and texture, and also act as humectants (they hold moisture in food).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.7] Polyols are found in sugar-free confectionery and chewing gum, and excessive amounts can have a laxative effect (loosen the bowel).
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.b] Intense sweeteners are used wherever sugar must be cut, but that does not make the food automatically healthy or suitable for everyone.
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.0] Artificial sweeteners are useful in slimming and diabetic products because they reduce sucrose, although the energy and portion size of the whole product still matter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.1] Sweeteners are not permitted in food for infants and young children.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.b] Fats and oils go rancid (stale and unpleasant-tasting) when oxygen attacks them. Antioxidants take up the oxygen first so the fat is protected.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.0] Antioxidants react with oxygen, so it cannot attack the fats and oils in food, preventing oxidative rancidity.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin C (E300, ascorbic acid) and vitamin E (E306, tocopherols).
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.2] Artificial antioxidants include BHA (E320) and BHT (E321).
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.3] Antioxidants are used in fats, cooking oils, crisps, biscuits, stock cubes, cheese spread, chewing gum, tinned fish, fruit drinks and jams.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.4] By slowing rancidity, antioxidants extend shelf life and reduce food waste, because less food spoils before it can be eaten.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.b] Some additives are added for nutrition rather than for how the food looks, tastes or keeps.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.0] Nutritional supplements are vitamins, minerals and other nutrients added to food to raise or restore its nutrient content.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.1] They are the additives used for fortification, so the food supplies more of a nutrient than it naturally contains.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.2] Unlike sweeteners, preservatives and colourings, their purpose is nutritional rather than technological.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.b] Physical conditioning agents are additives that change or maintain the physical properties of food, such as texture, consistency, moisture, stability and appearance. Emulsifiers and stabilisers work as a pair to keep mixtures like mayonnaise smooth.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.0] An emulsifier holds two immiscible liquids (liquids that do not mix), such as oil and water, together so that they do not separate.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.1] An emulsifier molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) head that attaches to water and a hydrophobic (oil-loving) tail that attaches to oil, so it links the two phases.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.2] Examples are lecithin (E322) and mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471).
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.3] Emulsifiers are used in mayonnaise, ice cream, sauces, margarine and chocolate.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.4] A stabiliser prevents an emulsion (a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix) from separating over time; alginates (E401), carrageenan (E407), guar gum (E412) and pectin (E440) are examples, with stabilisers and thickeners used in ice cream, sauces and desserts.

[hom-food-additives-s4.9.b] These agents protect the texture of food by controlling moisture, lumping and firmness. One function in a single clause is enough when you are asked to name an agent and give its function.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.0] A humectant attracts and retains water, preventing food from losing moisture, drying out and hardening; sorbitol and mannitol are examples.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.1] Humectants are used in marzipan, cakes and sweets, so that they stay soft.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.2] An anti-caking agent prevents lumps forming in dried or powdered foods such as cake mixes and dried milk; polyphosphates (E450 to E452) are an example that also increases water retention.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.3] A setting agent helps a food to set; pectin aids the setting of jams.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.4] A firming agent keeps fruit and vegetables firm during processing.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.b] Some agents control what happens in the pan, in the packet or on the processing line, rather than in the food itself.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.0] Anti-foaming agents prevent scum and froth forming on the surface of food as it boils.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.1] Anti-spattering agents keep water droplets apart in cooking oils, preventing spatter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.2] Buffers maintain (control) the pH of a food at the level required for processing or taste.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.3] Packaging gases, such as nitrogen, are sealed into sachet foods to replace air and prevent oxidation.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.4] Release agents form a coating, so food does not stick: it stops foods from sticking to each other or to equipment.
