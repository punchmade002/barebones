TARGET hom-fruit-vegetables-s4: 4. Processing
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.0.b] Fresh fruit and vegetables spoil quickly, so manufacturers process them, often close to the farm soon after harvest. Processing turns a perishable crop into a product that lasts and is easy to use.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.0.0] Processing changes fresh produce into products with a longer shelf life, greater convenience, availability all year and less waste, or a mixture of these.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.0.1] The three main commercial methods for fruit and vegetables are freezing, canning and drying (also called dehydration).
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.0.2] Steps common to the methods are sorting, washing, peeling, cutting, blanching or cooking (blanching is a short dip in boiling water or steam), packaging and labelling. Brine, syrup, juice or additives may also be added.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.0.3] Spoilage is caused by enzymes (natural chemicals in the food that make it deteriorate) and micro-organisms such as bacteria and moulds. Every preservation method works by inactivating, killing or preventing the activity of one or both.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.0.4] Processed products must be labelled under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information to consumers, which applies in Ireland through the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.b] Freezing keeps food by cold rather than heat, so the fresh colour and flavour are largely kept. The way the food is frozen decides whether its texture survives.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.0] Process: the fruit or vegetables are washed and prepared, vegetables are blanched, then they are frozen quickly and packed in moisture-proof packaging. They are stored and distributed at -18 degrees Celsius or lower.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.1] Principle: low temperature stops micro-organisms multiplying and slows enzymes, and the water in the food becomes ice, which micro-organisms cannot use. Freezing does not kill them, it only stops their growth and activity.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.2] Vegetables are blanched before freezing to destroy the enzymes that would otherwise keep working and cause deterioration. Blanching does not sterilise the food and does not kill all micro-organisms.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.3] Quick freezing at about -25 degrees Celsius forms small ice crystals, which damage the cell walls very little, so the food keeps its texture.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.4] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals that rupture the cell walls, so the food becomes limp and loses juice (called drip) on thawing.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.5] Air blast freezing blows very cold air through a tunnel over food carried on a conveyor belt.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.6] Plate (contact) freezing presses the food between two cold metal plates, which suits flat items.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.7] Flow (fluidised bed) freezing blows cold air up through a perforated belt so that each piece freezes separately and does not stick together. It suits peas.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.1.8] Cryogenic freezing immerses or sprays food with liquid nitrogen and suits delicate foods such as strawberries.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.2.b] Frozen vegetables keep most of their goodness, but a few vitamins are lost along the way. Knowing where they go is a favourite exam point.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.2.0] Vitamin C and B vitamins are lost into the blanching water and into the drip on thawing, so thaw as little as possible and cook vegetables from frozen.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.2.1] Colour, flavour and most nutrients are well retained when freezing is carried out properly. Frozen produce can contain more vitamin C than 'fresh' produce stored for several days.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.2.2] Foods with a high water content and delicate texture, such as lettuce and bananas, do not freeze well because they go soft and mushy on thawing.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.2.3] Frozen foods are convenient, with little waste and no salt added.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.b] Canning keeps food by heat. The food is sealed in a container and sterilised, so it keeps for a long time with no fridge, but the heat costs some nutrients and texture.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.0] Process: produce is washed, graded, peeled and cut, then blanched or cooked briefly. It is packed into cans or jars with brine, syrup, juice or water, sealed with the air removed, heated in a retort (an industrial pressure cooker heated above boiling point) to sterilise it, then cooled.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.1] Heat sterilisation destroys micro-organisms and enzymes, and the airtight seal stops them re-entering. Canned food therefore has a long shelf life without refrigeration until opened.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.2] Canning causes loss of water-soluble vitamins (vitamin C, thiamine and folate) through the heat and by leaching (dissolving out) into the liquid, so the liquid should be used.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.3] Canning changes the colour, texture and flavour of fruit and vegetables, which become softer and duller.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.4] Canned vegetables contain added salt in the brine, which raises their sodium content. Vegetables in water or with no added salt are lower in sodium.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.5] Syrup raises the sugar content of canned fruit. People controlling their blood glucose should choose fruit in natural juice or water, which has less added sugar.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.6] Fibre, minerals, carotene and protein are well kept in canned foods.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.7] Advantages: long shelf life, ready to use, cheap, available all year, and safe from spoilage if the can is undamaged.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.3.8] Disadvantages: added salt or sugar, loss of vitamin C and thiamine, and altered texture.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.b] Drying keeps food by taking its water away. Without water, spoilage cannot happen, so dried foods are light, compact and cheap to store and transport.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.0] Process: the produce is washed, cut and usually blanched or treated with a preservative. Most of the water is removed by controlled warm air (hot-air drying), or by sun drying, spray drying or freeze-drying. It is then sealed in moisture-proof packaging.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.1] Removing water leaves none available to micro-organisms and enzymes, so micro-organisms cannot grow and enzymes cannot work. Dried food keeps for months without refrigeration.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.2] Dehydration may reduce B vitamins, vitamin A and vitamin E, and it alters colour, texture and flavour. The food shrinks and becomes chewy.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.3] Vitamin C is lost unless a sulphite preservative is used.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.4] Dried fruit such as raisins, sultanas, apricots and prunes is concentrated in sugar, energy and fibre because most of the water has been removed.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.5] Dried peas, beans and lentils are pulses that keep for long periods and are rehydrated (soaked or cooked) before use.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.4.6] Other examples are instant mashed potato and dried herbs.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.b] Processing can spoil the colour of produce, let it brown or take out nutrients. Additives are substances added to food to put these right and to help it keep.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.0] Additives improve colour, flavour, texture and keeping quality, replace nutrients lost in processing, and make processing and storage easier. Approved additives are identified by E numbers.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.1] Food additives are approved under Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008 on food additives, and the label must list them by function and name or number.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.2] Colourings, in the E100 to E180 range, restore or enhance colour lost during processing, for example in canned peas and other canned vegetables.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.3] Preservatives prevent micro-organisms from growing. Sulphur dioxide (E220) is used as a preservative in some dried fruit, and sugar and salt also act as preservatives.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.4] Antioxidants prevent browning. Ascorbic acid, which is vitamin C (E300), is used to stop cut or frozen fruit darkening.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.5] Flavourings, sweeteners, acids, firming agents and bulking agents are added to some canned and processed fruit and vegetables, and must be declared on the label.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.5.6] Nutrient fortification (adding nutrients) improves nutritive value where nutrients are lost in processing.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.b] Processed fruit and vegetables save time and effort, but what is added or taken out during processing changes their nutritional value.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.0] Examples: frozen peas and mixed vegetables, canned beans, tomatoes and sweetcorn, dried fruit, bagged salad and stir-fry mixes, fruit juices, smoothies, fruit salads in juice, fruit yoghurts, jam, vegetable soups, ready meals and sauces with vegetables, and potato products (chips, wedges, crisps, instant mash).
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.1] Advantages: they save time, skill and effort, are available all year, have a long shelf life, cause little waste and can be fortified. Some, such as frozen vegetables, retain nutrients well.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.2] Disadvantages: they are often higher in salt, sugar and fat than fresh produce and lower in fibre and vitamins. They are also more expensive, create packaging waste, have lower sensory quality, and can unbalance the diet if eaten too often.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.3] Vegetable crisps and wedges are high in fat and salt, so they are better counted as snacks than as a serving of vegetables.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.4] Vegetables in soups, sauces and ready meals often contain added salt, fat and sugar, which lowers their nutritional value compared with fresh vegetables.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.5] Fruit juice, whether fresh natural juice or made from concentrate, has the sugars of the fruit with little fibre. Juice and smoothies therefore lack the fibre of whole fruit and are not a full replacement for it.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.6] Unsweetened juice counts as at most one serving a day. Check labels for added sugar.
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.7] Processed products must carry an ingredients list (in descending order of weight), additives with their functions, net quantity, a date mark (best before or use by), storage instructions, cooking instructions where needed, and nutritional information (energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt).
[hom-fruit-vegetables-s4.6.8] Allergens must be shown in bold or otherwise emphasised in the ingredients list. The law is Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, in force in Ireland through the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s2: 2. Freezing
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.b] Freezing preserves food by lowering its temperature far enough to stop enzyme action and microbial growth, and by turning free water into ice that micro-organisms cannot use. It is popular because no high heat is needed, so food stays close to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.0] Low temperature slows enzyme reactions (enzymes are the natural chemicals in food that make it spoil or change) and stops micro-organisms from reproducing, but it does not kill most of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.1] Frozen food is not sterile: micro-organisms become dormant (inactive) and start growing again when the food thaws, so frozen food is never safe indefinitely.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.2] Turning free water into ice removes the water micro-organisms need, because ice is unavailable to them.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.3] Pure water freezes at 0 °C, but food freezes below this because its dissolved sugars and salts lower its freezing point.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.4] Water expands by about 9 per cent when it freezes, which is why liquids need headspace (an empty gap at the top) in their containers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.0.5] Freezing itself does little harm to nutritive value, and colour, flavour and texture are kept better than with most other methods because no high heat is used, except in blanching; most nutrient losses happen during blanching and thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.b] The speed of freezing decides how big the ice crystals are, and crystal size decides how much damage the food suffers. Quick freezing makes small crystals; slow freezing makes large ones.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.0] Quick freezing takes place at about −25 °C (the 2013 Higher Level scheme gives −25 °C to −40 °C) in the fast-freeze section of a freezer, so the food passes through the freezing range rapidly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.1] Quick freezing forms small ice crystals within the cells, which causes little damage to the cell walls, so there is little drip (juice lost) on thawing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.2] For this reason quick freezing retains the texture, colour, flavour and nutritive value of food better than slow freezing does.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.3] Slow freezing takes place at a higher temperature than −25 °C, as in an ordinary freezer compartment, where ice forms gradually.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.4] Slow freezing forms large ice crystals, mostly between the cells, that pierce and rupture cell walls, allowing juices to escape.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.5] As a result slow-frozen food is limp and soft when thawed, and it loses colour and flavour and drip containing soluble vitamins B1 and C and minerals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.6] The fast-freeze setting should be switched on about 3 to 4 hours before food is placed in the freezer, so the freezer is cold enough to freeze the new food quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.1.7] Diagram: draw two cells side by side, one with small crystals inside an intact cell wall (quick) and one with large crystals and a ruptured wall (slow).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.b] Blanching is a brief heat treatment given to vegetables before freezing. Enzymes keep working slowly even at freezer temperatures, so vegetables would spoil in storage without it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.0] 1. The vegetables are plunged into boiling water for a short, set time that depends on the vegetable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.1] 2. They are plunged immediately into ice-cold water for the same length of time, which stops the cooking so the vegetables do not go soft.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.2] 3. They are drained and dried, because surface water left on them would form ice crystals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.3] Blanching inactivates enzymes that would otherwise cause loss of colour, flavour, texture and nutrients during frozen storage.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.4] Blanching also cleans the vegetable, destroys some micro-organisms on its surface, softens it slightly so it packs well, and keeps its bright colour.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.5] Blanching loses some water-soluble vitamins, particularly vitamin C and vitamin B1, into the water and through heat. Blanch small batches in plenty of fast-boiling water so the water returns to the boil quickly and the timing stays short and accurate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.2.6] Fruit is not blanched; soft fruit is open-frozen on trays or packed in sugar or sugar syrup. Some foods, such as soups, sauces and casseroles, are frozen cooked after cooling.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.b] Good results at home depend on following a set of steps, and each step has a reason. Most of them aim either to freeze the food quickly or to keep air and moisture away from it.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.0] Choose fresh, best-quality food in perfect condition and freeze it soon after harvest or purchase, because freezing keeps quality but cannot improve it. Wash, peel, trim or cut as needed, blanch vegetables, and cool cooked food quickly before it goes into the freezer.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.1] Portion food into usable (family-sized) quantities so that only what is needed is thawed.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.2] Open-freeze (tray-freeze) sticky or delicate items, such as strawberries or raspberries, or items to be kept separate, such as sausages and burgers: spread them in a single layer on a tray, freeze hard, then bag them so that they stay separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.3] Pack food in strong, moisture-proof and vapour-proof freezer bags, foil or rigid plastic containers, and remove as much air as possible so that the food does not dry out. Leave headspace in containers holding liquids to allow for expansion as the food freezes.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.4] Seal tightly and label with contents and date. Place new packs against the cold surfaces or in the fast-freeze compartment, as the freezer instructs, and keep them apart from already frozen food until frozen, so they freeze quickly.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.5] Do not overload the freezer: freeze no more than about one-tenth of the freezer's capacity in any 24 hours, because overloading raises the temperature and slows freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.6] Store frozen food at −18 °C or colder, using the oldest first. The safe storage time depends on the food: fatty foods and oily fish keep for a shorter period, and fatty foods stored too long can turn rancid.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.7] Foods that freeze well include peas, beans, carrots, broccoli, apples, strawberries, meat, fish, bread, cakes, soups, sauces and prepared meals.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.3.8] Foods that freeze poorly include lettuce, whole tomatoes and other high-water salad foods, bananas, melons, pears, whole eggs in the shell, low-fat cream and mayonnaise-based foods, which go limp, watery or separate.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.b] Problems with frozen food usually come from air reaching it or from careless thawing. Both are preventable.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.0] Freezer burn shows as dry, discoloured, tough patches where air has reached the surface of the food, caused by dehydration and oxidation, especially in protein foods such as meat and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.1] Airtight packaging with the air removed prevents freezer burn.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.2] Thaw food in a fridge, or cook it from frozen where the food allows, such as vegetables, so that the food stays below the danger zone while it thaws. Thaw meat and poultry fully before cooking unless the pack says otherwise.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.3] Thawed food should be used promptly and not refrozen as it is, because quality declines and micro-organisms may have multiplied. Raw food thawed in the fridge can be cooked and then frozen as a cooked dish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.4.4] In a freezer fault or power cut, keep the door closed and do not open it, because frozen food keeps for a time if the cold is held in.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.b] Commercial freezing is faster and colder than home freezing, so ice crystals are smaller and quality is higher. Food is frozen soon after harvesting or catching, often on the farm or on a factory ship. Home freezers are not as cold or as fast, so home freezing does not match these methods.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.0] Air blast freezing: a very cold air stream at about −30 °C is blown over food, often on a conveyor belt or through a tunnel; suitable for most foods, such as fruit, vegetables, meat and bakery products.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.1] Plate (contact) freezing: food is pressed between two hollow metal plates cooled by refrigerant; suitable for thin, flat foods such as fish fillets, flat pieces of meat and burgers.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.2] Flow (fluidised bed) freezing: cold air is blown up through a perforated conveyor belt so that small pieces float and freeze individually, staying free-flowing and not sticking together; suitable for peas, sweetcorn and berries.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.3] Cryogenic freezing: food is exposed to liquid nitrogen or carbon dioxide at extremely low temperature, which freezes it almost instantly; suitable for delicate or high-value foods such as strawberries, prawns and fish.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.4] The cold chain keeps frozen food at −18 °C or colder in cold stores, refrigerated lorries and supermarket freezer cabinets until it reaches the home.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.5.5] When buying frozen food, check that the pack is intact with no ice crystals or frost inside, that it is below the load line on the cabinet, and take it home quickly in a cool bag.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.b] Freezing helps a household plan meals, save money and cut waste. These benefits are the points examiners expect when they ask about frozen foods in meal planning.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.0] Freezing surplus food before it spoils reduces household waste, and leftovers can be frozen for reuse as ready-made portions.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.1] Bulk cooking and freezing portions saves time and fuel, since a large batch is cooked once.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.2] A freezer stock of meals and ingredients provides emergency food and convenience on busy days, including for households with different eating times.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.3] Buying in bulk or when produce is in season is more economical, and freezing keeps it for a longer period (a longer shelf life).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.4] Frozen foods provide a wide variety of vegetables, fish and fruit all year, including items not available fresh locally.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.5] Freezing retains colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value well compared with heat processing; frozen vegetables keep more vitamin C than canned ones, as one past-paper table shows for peas (25 mg fresh, 17 mg frozen, 8 mg canned).
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.6] Fruit and salad foods go soft after thawing because of cell damage, so they suit cooking better than eating raw.
[hom-food-preservation-s2.6.7] Labelling each pack with contents, date and portion size lets older items be used first, which helps planned menus.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s3: 3. Heat processing
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.b] Food spoils because micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) and enzymes (natural proteins in the food that break it down) keep working. Heat processing stops both, and a clean airtight container then keeps new micro-organisms out.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.0] Heat destroys vegetative bacteria (bacteria in their active, growing form), yeasts and moulds, and denatures (permanently changes the shape of) the enzymes that cause spoilage, so the food stops breaking down.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.1] Sealing the food in a clean airtight container while it is hot, or sealing before heating, prevents re-contamination, because otherwise new micro-organisms would simply re-enter after the heating.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.2] Spores (tough resting forms) of some bacteria, such as Clostridium botulinum, are very heat-resistant and survive boiling at normal pressure, so low-acid foods need higher temperatures held for longer, in a pressure cooker or retort (a large industrial pressure cooker).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.3] Acid foods such as fruit, tomatoes and pickles are easier to preserve by heat because acid hinders micro-organisms and their spores. Low-acid foods such as peas, beans, meat and fish need a higher temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.4] Heat also changes the colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value of food, which is why heat-processed fruit and vegetables differ from fresh produce.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.5] Heat-sensitive, water-soluble vitamins (vitamin C and the B group) are reduced by heat processing.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.b] Heat treatments differ in how much they kill. A mild treatment leaves some organisms alive so the food must be chilled, while a severe one kills everything so the sealed food keeps at room temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.0] Pasteurisation is a moderate heat treatment that destroys most food poisoning (pathogenic) bacteria and reduces spoilage organisms, but not all spores, so pasteurised products keep for a limited time and still need refrigeration.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.1] Milk is pasteurised at 72 °C for 15 seconds, then cooled rapidly. Pasteurisation is also used for cream, fruit juice, beer and some canned foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.2] Sterilisation uses a higher temperature for longer, for example milk sterilised in the bottle and canned foods. It destroys all micro-organisms including spores, so the sealed product can be stored at room temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.3] The price of sterilisation is that flavour and colour change and vitamin losses are greater than with pasteurisation.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.4] UHT (ultra-heat treatment) heats milk to about 135 °C for a few seconds, and the milk is then packed aseptically (in a germ-free way) into sterile cartons.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.5] UHT milk keeps for months unopened at room temperature, must be refrigerated after opening, and has a slight cooked flavour.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.6] Three processing methods make milk safe: pasteurisation, sterilisation or UHT, and evaporation or dehydration (drying).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.b] Canning seals food in a container and heats it hard enough to sterilise it, so it keeps for a long time without a fridge. Commercial canning in tin cans is different from home bottling in glass jars.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.0] Commercial canning has stages: food is prepared (washed, peeled, trimmed, blanched where needed), packed into cans, covered with brine, syrup or oil, and air is removed (exhausting).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.1] Then the can is sealed with a double seam, heated in a retort at a high temperature for a set time, and cooled quickly. The heat sterilises the contents and the sealed can prevents re-contamination.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.2] Cans are lined with a lacquer, a protective coating, to prevent the metal reacting with the food.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.3] Canned food is shelf-stable, so it keeps at room temperature for a long time without refrigeration, and it can be eaten straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.4] Foods suited to canning include fruit, tomatoes, vegetables such as peas and beans, fish (tuna, sardines, salmon), meat, soup and baked beans.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.5] Low-acid vegetables such as peas, beans and carrots must be processed in a pressure canner (a sealed pot that heats above 100 °C), because boiling water at normal pressure, which is 100 °C, does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.6] A bulging can end or lid indicates gas produced by micro-organisms. Hissing, leaking, rust, deep dents or an off smell are also warning signs, so the contents must be discarded unopened and never tasted.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.b] The very high temperature and long processing time that make canned food safe also change it. Examiners ask what is lost and what is gained.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.0] Vitamin C is heat-sensitive and water-soluble, so it is lost during high-temperature canning, especially into the canning liquid.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.1] Thiamine (vitamin B1) is unstable at high temperature and is reduced by canning.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.2] Canned vegetables are softer, paler and less bright in colour than fresh or frozen produce, and the flavour changes too.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.3] Canned vegetables gain sodium from added salt (brine), so no-added-salt versions are preferred on a low-salt diet.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.4] Fruit canned in heavy syrup gains sugar, and food packed in oil gains fat, which raises the energy content.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.5] Heat destroys micro-organisms and enzymes in the can, which is the purpose of canning. Minerals and fibre are largely kept, and the liquid can be used in cooking to keep the nutrients it holds.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.6] One past-paper table showed vitamin C in peas falling from 25 mg per 100 g fresh to 8 mg canned, and sodium rising from 1 mg to 230 mg. The reasons were the very high temperature and long time, and the salt used in canning.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.b] Bottling is the home version of heat preservation. Fruit is heated in glass jars so that the contents are sterilised and sealed.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.0] Bottling packs fruit (and tomatoes) into clean sterilised glass jars with sugar syrup or water, seals them, and heats them in a water bath (or oven) until the contents reach a temperature that destroys organisms and enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.1] The seal closes as the jar cools and a vacuum forms, which holds the lid down and keeps new micro-organisms out.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.2] Jars must be filled hot, leaving headspace, and sealed at once; the contents cool and form a vacuum that holds the lid down.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.3] Jars must be sterilised and undamaged with new or sound lids, and filled leaving headspace (a gap at the top). Hot jars must be lifted carefully.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.4] Label bottled food with the date and store it in a cool, dark, dry place. Discard any jar whose lid is not tight or domed.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.5] Low-acid vegetables and meat should not be bottled in a boiling-water bath at home, because boiling at normal pressure does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores. They need pressure processing, so home bottling is restricted to fruit and acid foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.6] Fruit is routinely bottled in a water bath because acid foods are easier to preserve by heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.b] Jam is fruit boiled with sugar until it sets. It keeps because of heat, a high concentration of sugar and a sealed jar, and it sets because pectin, acid and sugar combine.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.0] Heat: boiling at about 100 °C or higher destroys micro-organisms and inactivates enzymes in the fruit. The fruit is first simmered gently to soften its tissues and release pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.1] Sugar preserves by osmosis: at high concentration it draws water out of microbial cells, so they cannot multiply. Sugar also helps the gel to form and gives sweetness.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.2] Pectin is a natural gelling substance, a carbohydrate found in the cell walls of fruit, mostly in the skin and core. It forms a gel with sugar and acid as the jam cools, so the jam sets. Pectin is not a preservative.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.3] Apples, blackcurrants, redcurrants, gooseberries and lemons or other citrus fruit are high in pectin. Strawberries, raspberries, cherries and pears are low, so add pectin-rich fruit or a commercial pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.4] Acid, from lemon juice or the fruit itself, releases pectin from the cell walls and helps the pectin set. It also improves colour and flavour, and lemon juice is added for low-acid or low-pectin fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.5] Choose just ripe, firm, sound fruit. Under-ripe fruit contains more pectin, whereas over-ripe fruit has less pectin and spoils the set.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.6] Sealing: pour the jam hot into clean sterilised jars and cover at once so micro-organisms cannot re-enter. A vacuum forms as the jam cools, and a waxed disc plus cover is the traditional method.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.7] Jam loses some heat-sensitive vitamin C in boiling, its sugar concentration is high, and its flavour and colour change, so it no longer tastes fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.b] A jam method runs in a set order, and each stage protects either the set or the keeping quality. Jam spoils when one of these protections fails.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.0] 1. Wash and sterilise the jars, and prepare the fruit. 2. Simmer the fruit in a heavy pan to soften it and release pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.1] 3. Add the sugar in the correct proportion, with lemon juice if needed, and dissolve it fully before the mixture is boiled rapidly to setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.2] Setting point is confirmed by the wrinkle test: a spoonful of jam dropped onto a cold saucer wrinkles when pushed with a finger. A sugar thermometer reads about 105 °C (220 °F) at setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.3] 4. Skim the scum from the surface with a clean dry spoon. 5. Pot the jam into hot sterilised jars, seal at once, label and store in a cool, dry place.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.4] Jam ferments or grows mould if too little sugar is used, setting point is not reached, jars or lids are not sterilised, the seal fails, or a dirty or wet spoon introduces yeasts and moulds.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.5] Storage in a warm or damp place also spoils jam. Mould on the surface or fermentation (bubbles and an alcoholic smell) is the result.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s4: 4. Dehydration
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.b] Dehydration (drying) removes most of the water from food. Micro-organisms and enzymes both need water to work, so taking it away keeps the food from spoiling.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.0] Micro-organisms need available water to grow. With most of the water removed, they cannot multiply, and enzymes (the natural chemicals in food that cause ripening and decay) cannot act either.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.1] Drying to a low moisture level stops micro-organisms growing but does not kill all of them. They are only inactivated, so they can grow again if the food takes in moisture.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.2] Fresh fruit and vegetables contain roughly 70 to 95 per cent water, so they lose most of their weight when dried and become much lighter and smaller.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.3] Dried food is light and compact, which reduces its storage space and the cost of transporting it.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.4] Damp dried food can re-absorb moisture from the air and go mouldy, so it must be stored dry and airtight.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.0.5] Blanching vegetables (dipping them briefly in boiling water) before drying inactivates enzymes that would otherwise cause darkening and off-flavours during storage. Fruit is treated differently: it is dipped in lemon juice or an ascorbic acid solution, or sulphured, to stop browning.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.b] Home drying uses gentle heat and moving air to carry moisture away from thin pieces of food. The methods differ in how much control you have over temperature and drying time.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.0] Preparation: choose ripe, sound, undamaged produce and wash it thoroughly. Cut it into even, thin pieces so that they all dry at the same rate, blanch vegetables, and arrange the pieces in a single layer.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.1] Oven drying: food is spread in a single layer on trays and dried at a very low temperature, with the oven door slightly open so moisture can escape. It takes several hours.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.2] Electric dehydrator: a fan circulates warm air at a controlled low temperature over trays, so food dries evenly and reliably.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.3] Sun drying: food is laid on trays or racks in strong, dry heat. It is slow and depends on the weather, and exposes food to dust, insects and moisture, so it suits hot, dry countries rather than the damp Irish climate.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.4] Sun drying is common for fruit in hot countries, such as raisins, apricots and sun-dried tomatoes.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.1.5] Air drying: herbs are tied in small bunches and hung in a warm, dry, airy place away from direct sunlight, which would fade them. Beans and pulses can also be left to dry on the plant.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.2.b] Food that looks dry may still hold pockets of moisture, and moisture is what lets mould return. So dried food is tested, conditioned and then packed carefully.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.2.0] Test dryness: dried fruit should be leathery and pliable, with no moist centre or sticky patches, which would let micro-organisms grow.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.2.1] Dried vegetables should be brittle and snap cleanly when bent.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.2.2] Condition dried food by packing it loosely in a jar for several days and shaking the jar daily. Condensation on the inside of the jar means the food needs more drying.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.2.3] Store dried food in airtight, moisture-proof containers in a cool, dark, dry place, since light and moisture reduce its quality and damp lets mould grow.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.3.b] Many foods dry well, especially those that are mostly water and have a flavour that survives the process.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.3.0] Fruit such as apples, apricots, pears, bananas and grapes (which become raisins).
[hom-food-preservation-s4.3.1] Vegetables such as onions, carrots, tomatoes, peas and beans.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.3.2] Herbs such as mint, parsley, thyme and rosemary, which keep their flavour well when dried.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.3.3] Pulses and cereals, which are naturally dry foods that keep well.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.3.4] Meat and fish, which are salted before drying or smoked and dried, as in stockfish (dried fish) and jerky (dried strips of meat).
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.b] Factories dry food on a large scale, often by turning liquid foods into powders or flakes. The most advanced method, accelerated freeze drying, gives the best quality but costs the most. Freeze-dried foods keep their shape, colour and flavour better than air-dried foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.0] Spray drying: liquid food such as milk, coffee, eggs or soup is sprayed as a fine mist into a stream of hot air. The water evaporates in seconds and the powder falls. It is used for milk powder, instant coffee and infant formula.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.1] Drum (roller) drying: a thin layer of liquid or purée is spread on a heated rotating drum, dries, and is scraped off as flakes. It is used for potato flakes (instant mashed potato) and baby cereals.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.2] Tunnel or cabinet drying: trays or a conveyor belt of food pass through a tunnel of hot air. It is used for fruit and vegetables.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.3] Accelerated freeze drying (AFD): 1. The food is frozen quickly. 2. It is placed in a vacuum chamber and gently heated. 3. The ice turns directly into water vapour without melting, a change called sublimation, and the vapour is drawn off.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.4] 4. This leaves a dry, porous food that keeps its shape.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.5] AFD food is packed airtight, often with nitrogen, and is used for coffee, fruit pieces, vegetables in instant soups, camping and ready meals, and herbs.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.6] AFD is valued because the food keeps its colour, flavour, shape and nutrients well (little heat is used, and no liquid water moves to the surface). It rehydrates quickly and fully, is very light, and has a long shelf life.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.7] AFD is limited because the equipment and energy are expensive, so the food costs more.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.4.8] Diagram: draw AFD in three labelled stages: frozen food; vacuum chamber with ice changing to vapour (sublimation); condenser and dry product.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.b] Drying keeps food safe for a long time, but heat and the loss of water change its nutrients, appearance and texture. These effects are examined, usually with fruit and vegetables.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.0] Water-soluble vitamins, especially vitamin C and the B vitamins, are lost during drying.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.1] Vitamins A and E are reduced, because they are sensitive to heat and oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.2] Colour darkens or fades, especially in fruit left in the sun, because of oxidation and browning reactions. Vegetables are blanched, and fruit is dipped in lemon juice, to reduce this.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.3] Dried fruit is concentrated in sugar, so a small portion gives much more sugar and energy than the same weight of fresh fruit. Sticky dried fruit can also cause dental decay.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.4] Texture becomes leathery, chewy or brittle, and the flavour becomes more concentrated. Dried food also shrinks and wrinkles.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.5] Minerals and fibre are not destroyed by drying, and protein and carbohydrate are also kept.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.6] Most dried foods must be soaked or cooked in water (rehydrated) before use. Spray-dried and freeze-dried foods rehydrate easily, and the soaking water contains vitamins and can be used.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.7] Advantages: long shelf life without refrigeration, light and compact, no freezer needed, cheap to transport and store, and concentrated flavour.
[hom-food-preservation-s4.5.8] Disadvantages: vitamin loss, changed colour and texture, slow, needs correct storage, some foods do not rehydrate well, and high sugar in dried fruit.

CANDIDATES hom-food-additives-s4: 4. Classes of food additives
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.b] A food additive is a natural or artificial substance added to food during manufacture to improve colour, taste, texture, appearance, keeping qualities or nutritive value, or to aid processing. Because each additive is added for a particular job, additives are sorted into classes by that job.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.0] Additives are grouped into classes by their function: colourings, flavourings (including flavour enhancers), sweeteners, preservatives, antioxidants, nutritional supplements and, at Higher Level, physical conditioning agents.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.1] Each class has natural examples (from plants, animals or minerals) and artificial examples (made by chemical synthesis), and the function of the additive, not its origin, is the basis for its class.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.2] Natural does not mean safer: both natural and artificial additives must be approved before they can be used in food.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.3] Settings: examine a food label to find the additives used, identify the class of each from its name or E number (a code given to an approved additive), and suggest the reason it was added.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.4] Additives have disadvantages: some people react to them (allergy, intolerance, or a hyperactivity warning on certain colours), and they may mask poor-quality food or encourage a diet of processed foods.
[hom-food-additives-s4.0.5] Two further disadvantages are specific to one additive each: sulphur dioxide destroys thiamine (vitamin B1), and polyols (bulk sweeteners) can cause diarrhoea if eaten in excess.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.b] Processing, such as heating or canning, can fade or change the natural colour of food, and shoppers judge food by its look. Colourings are added to put this right.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.0] Function: colourings restore colour lost in processing, make food more attractive and give a consistent, uniform appearance from batch to batch.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.1] Natural colourings include caramel (E150), chlorophyll (E140), carotene (E160a), cochineal (E120) and turmeric (E100, also called curcumin).
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.2] Artificial colourings include tartrazine (E102), a yellow dye used in soft drinks and sweets.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.3] Colourings are used in foods such as sweets, soft drinks, desserts, baked goods and cheese products.
[hom-food-additives-s4.1.4] Tartrazine and some other azo colours (a group of synthetic dyes) must carry a warning that they may affect activity and attention in children, which is one reason some people avoid them.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.b] Some foods taste bland to begin with and others lose flavour during processing. Flavourings put taste back or add it, while flavour enhancers work differently by making a flavour already there seem stronger.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.0] Function: flavourings add flavour to bland food, replace flavour lost in processing, and (as enhancers) strengthen an existing flavour.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.1] Natural flavourings include herbs, spices, sugar and salt, used in foods such as jam, tinned beans, cereals, meat products, sauces, stock cubes, cheese and butter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.2] Artificial flavourings are chemical compounds made by synthesis, such as esters and aldehydes; ethyl acetate gives a rum flavour, amyl acetate a pear flavour, benzaldehyde a cherry flavour, and maltol is another example. They are used in baked goods and cakes.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.3] Flavour enhancers (in the E600 to E699 range) improve an existing flavour and make it seem stronger; monosodium glutamate (E621) is the most common example.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.4] Monosodium glutamate is used in soups, sauces, stock cubes and prepared meals.
[hom-food-additives-s4.2.5] Flavourings in the narrow sense carry no E number, but flavour enhancers do, so do not say that every additive has an E number.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.b] Bacteria, yeasts and moulds spoil food. Preservatives slow this down so that food stays safe and edible for longer.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.0] Preservatives inhibit the growth of micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds), which delays spoilage and extending shelf life.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.1] Natural preservatives include salt, sugar, vinegar and smoke, which are accepted as natural preservatives.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.2] Chemical preservatives include sulphur dioxide (E220) and potassium sorbate (E202), used in soft drinks, jams, baked goods, dried fruit and wine.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.3] Sulphur dioxide destroys some thiamine (vitamin B1), so its use reduces the nutritive value of the food.
[hom-food-additives-s4.3.4] Sulphur dioxide must be declared on the label as an allergen, because some people react to it.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.b] Sweeteners give a sweet taste, but they fall into very different groups. Nutritive sweeteners supply energy, intense sweeteners replace sugar with almost no energy, and bulk sweeteners (polyols) sweeten while also adding body.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.0] Nutritive sweeteners such as sugar, honey, glucose syrup and fructose supply energy as well as sweetness.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.1] Intense sweeteners (high-intensity, non-nutritive sweeteners) are many times sweeter than sugar, so only a tiny amount is needed and they contribute negligible energy.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.2] Intense sweeteners include saccharin (E954), aspartame (E951), acesulfame K (E950) and sucralose (E955).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.3] Because the quantity used is so small, intense sweeteners do not provide sugar's bulk, browning, preservation or texture, so a bulking agent may also be needed.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.4] Intense sweeteners are used in low-energy and diet soft drinks, tabletop sweeteners, yoghurts, desserts and foods for people reducing their sugar intake.
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.5] Products containing aspartame carry a label warning for people with phenylketonuria, as aspartame is a source of phenylalanine (an amino acid they cannot process).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.6] Bulk sweeteners, or polyols, include sorbitol (E420), mannitol (E421) and xylitol; they are less intensely sweet, are used in larger amounts, give bulk and texture, and also act as humectants (they hold moisture in food).
[hom-food-additives-s4.4.7] Polyols are found in sugar-free confectionery and chewing gum, and excessive amounts can have a laxative effect (loosen the bowel).
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.b] Intense sweeteners are used wherever sugar must be cut, but that does not make the food automatically healthy or suitable for everyone.
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.0] Artificial sweeteners are useful in slimming and diabetic products because they reduce sucrose, although the energy and portion size of the whole product still matter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.5.1] Sweeteners are not permitted in food for infants and young children.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.b] Fats and oils go rancid (stale and unpleasant-tasting) when oxygen attacks them. Antioxidants take up the oxygen first so the fat is protected.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.0] Antioxidants react with oxygen, so it cannot attack the fats and oils in food, preventing oxidative rancidity.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin C (E300, ascorbic acid) and vitamin E (E306, tocopherols).
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.2] Artificial antioxidants include BHA (E320) and BHT (E321).
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.3] Antioxidants are used in fats, cooking oils, crisps, biscuits, stock cubes, cheese spread, chewing gum, tinned fish, fruit drinks and jams.
[hom-food-additives-s4.6.4] By slowing rancidity, antioxidants extend shelf life and reduce food waste, because less food spoils before it can be eaten.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.b] Some additives are added for nutrition rather than for how the food looks, tastes or keeps.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.0] Nutritional supplements are vitamins, minerals and other nutrients added to food to raise or restore its nutrient content.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.1] They are the additives used for fortification, so the food supplies more of a nutrient than it naturally contains.
[hom-food-additives-s4.7.2] Unlike sweeteners, preservatives and colourings, their purpose is nutritional rather than technological.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.b] Physical conditioning agents are additives that change or maintain the physical properties of food, such as texture, consistency, moisture, stability and appearance. Emulsifiers and stabilisers work as a pair to keep mixtures like mayonnaise smooth.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.0] An emulsifier holds two immiscible liquids (liquids that do not mix), such as oil and water, together so that they do not separate.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.1] An emulsifier molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) head that attaches to water and a hydrophobic (oil-loving) tail that attaches to oil, so it links the two phases.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.2] Examples are lecithin (E322) and mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids (E471).
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.3] Emulsifiers are used in mayonnaise, ice cream, sauces, margarine and chocolate.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.4] A stabiliser prevents an emulsion (a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix) from separating over time; alginates (E401), carrageenan (E407), guar gum (E412) and pectin (E440) are examples, with stabilisers and thickeners used in ice cream, sauces and desserts.
[hom-food-additives-s4.8.5] Diagram: an emulsifier molecule with a hydrophilic head and a hydrophobic tail, shown at the boundary between an oil droplet and water.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.b] These agents protect the texture of food by controlling moisture, lumping and firmness. One function in a single clause is enough when you are asked to name an agent and give its function.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.0] A humectant attracts and retains water, preventing food from losing moisture, drying out and hardening; sorbitol and mannitol are examples.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.1] Humectants are used in marzipan, cakes and sweets, so that they stay soft.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.2] An anti-caking agent prevents lumps forming in dried or powdered foods such as cake mixes and dried milk; polyphosphates (E450 to E452) are an example that also increases water retention.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.3] A setting agent helps a food to set; pectin aids the setting of jams.
[hom-food-additives-s4.9.4] A firming agent keeps fruit and vegetables firm during processing.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.b] Some agents control what happens in the pan, in the packet or on the processing line, rather than in the food itself.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.0] Anti-foaming agents prevent scum and froth forming on the surface of food as it boils.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.1] Anti-spattering agents keep water droplets apart in cooking oils, preventing spatter.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.2] Buffers maintain (control) the pH of a food at the level required for processing or taste.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.3] Packaging gases, such as nitrogen, are sealed into sachet foods to replace air and prevent oxidation.
[hom-food-additives-s4.10.4] Release agents form a coating, so food does not stick: it stops foods from sticking to each other or to equipment.
