TARGET hom-appliances-equipment-s3: 3. An appliance with a motor
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.0.b] Some appliances exist to make something move, such as a blade, a drum or a fan. They use an electric motor, which turns electrical energy into movement (kinetic energy), rather than a heating element, which turns electricity into heat.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.0.0] Common motor appliances are the food processor, blender, hand mixer, vacuum cleaner, washing machine, dishwasher pump, extractor fan and the fan in a hairdryer, because each one needs something to spin or move.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.0.1] Kettles, toasters and irons work differently: they have a heating element that produces heat, not a motor that produces movement.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.0.2] Some appliances have both, so a washing machine, hairdryer and dishwasher each contain a motor for movement and an element for heat.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.0.3] Classification check: a food processor has a motor, a kettle has an element and a toaster has an element.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.b] A food processor is a typical motor-driven appliance: the motor sits in the base and drives interchangeable attachments. Each part of its design is there for performance or safety, and exam answers should name the part and its purpose.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.0] The heavy motor is enclosed in a stable base with rubber (non-slip) feet, which keeps the centre of gravity low and prevents the appliance moving during use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.1] A spindle (drive shaft) transfers rotary motion from the motor to the blade or disc fitted in the bowl, so the attachment turns when the motor runs.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.2] The bowl is transparent and graduated (marked with a measuring scale), so the user can see the quantity and texture of the food being processed. It is made of heat-resistant, impact-resistant plastic or glass.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.3] The lid locks onto the bowl and has a feed tube with a pusher, which keeps fingers out of the tube. A safety interlock stops the motor from starting unless the bowl and lid are correctly fitted.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.4] Further safety features: an overload cut-out protects the motor from overheating, and the blade comes to a stop quickly when switched off.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.5] Interchangeable attachments include the chopping (S-) blade, slicing and shredding (grating) discs, a dough blade and a whisk. Blades are stainless steel, which resists rust and stays sharp.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.6] Controls usually include on/off, variable speeds and a pulse button that gives short, controlled bursts of power.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.7] Removable, dishwasher-safe components and a smooth, sealed base make cleaning easier, and internal cord storage keeps the base tidy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.1.8] Diagram: food processor in section, showing the base with motor and spindle (drive shaft), the bowl, the blade, the lid with feed tube and pusher, and the interlock position.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.2.b] When choosing a motor appliance, some factors matter that do not apply to other appliances. They all come down to whether the machine suits the jobs you will actually do and is safe and easy to live with.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.2.0] Capacity and motor power (wattage) decide how much food it can handle and how well it copes with heavy tasks such as dough.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.2.1] Choose a range of attachments you will actually use, because extra attachments add cost and storage problems if they are never needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.2.2] Consider noise, cord length and storage, and how easy it is to assemble and clean, because these decide whether it will be used often.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.2.3] Look for safety features such as an interlock, and for a guarantee and service availability in case the motor fails.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.b] A food processor has fast-moving sharp blades and an electric motor, so most guidelines prevent cuts, electric shock or damage to the motor. Each guideline is worth stating together with the hazard it prevents.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.0] Read the manufacturer's instructions, assemble correctly and fit the correct attachment before connecting the appliance to the electricity supply, so the machine is used as designed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.1] Check that the plug, flex and bowl are undamaged, and place the processor on a dry, level surface, away from water and the edge of the worktop so it cannot fall or cause shock.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.2] Lock the bowl and lid before switching on, and never try to defeat the safety interlock, which exists to stop the blade turning while it is exposed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.3] Cut food into even pieces, do not exceed the maximum fill line, and use the speed recommended for the ingredient, so the motor is not strained.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.4] Do not run the machine for longer than the instructions allow, because the motor can overheat; pause to let it cool.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.5] Feed food only with the supplied pusher, never with fingers, knives or spoons, and keep hands, hair and loose clothing away from moving parts, because the blade can cut or catch them.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.6] Use pulse control to avoid over-processing and to keep control of the texture of the food.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.3.7] Switch off and unplug before changing attachments, removing the lid, scraping the bowl or cleaning, and wait until the blade has stopped completely, so it cannot start or cut by accident.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.b] Looking after the appliance keeps it safe and working. The rules about unplugging, water and damage matter most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.0] Lift sharp blades by the centre hub, wash and dry them carefully, and store them safely and out of reach of young children.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.1] Wash the bowl, lid and attachments in warm soapy water, or in the dishwasher if the manufacturer allows it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.2] Wipe the motor base with a damp cloth; never immerse it or hold it under running water, because water entering the motor and wiring can cause electric shock.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.3] Wind or store the flex neatly, and do not use the appliance if the plug, flex, bowl or interlock is damaged; have it repaired by a qualified person before use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.4] Dry your hands before touching the plug, and never overload a socket, because wet hands and overloaded sockets risk shock and fire.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.4.5] Use the correct fuse in the plug: 3 A for low-power appliances and 13 A for high-power ones, as the manufacturer states. A fuse melts if too much current flows, which protects the appliance and its flex.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.5.b] Other motor appliances have their own particular hazards, so each has a rule matched to what it does.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.5.0] Hand mixers: start on the lowest speed to avoid splashing, then increase speed, and switch off before removing the beaters.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.5.1] Blenders: add liquids first and never run the blender empty. Do not blend hot liquid in a sealed container, because steam pressure can force the lid off.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.5.2] Vacuum cleaners: empty the dust container or change the bag before it is full, since a blocked airflow strains the motor; clear blockages with the machine switched off.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.5.3] Washing machines: do not overload the drum, use the detergent amount recommended by the manufacturer, and choose a programme suited to the fabric.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.6.b] Motor appliances use little energy compared with heating appliances because they run for short periods. Using them well keeps running costs low and saves energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.6.0] Prepare several ingredients in sequence, starting with dry or clean foods, so the appliance is used once for many tasks rather than washed between each one.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.6.1] Use the appliance only for the job it is designed for, use the right size for the task, and switch it off as soon as the task is complete rather than letting it run on.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.6.2] Switch off at the socket when not in use, and choose an efficient, correctly sized model.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.6.3] The cost to run an appliance is its power in kW multiplied by the hours used and the unit price, so a short-use appliance costs very little.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.6.4] Appliances with a motor that run for long periods, such as the washing machine, dishwasher and fridge, are where the energy label matters. Use eco programmes and full loads to save energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.b] A motor converts electrical energy into kinetic energy by using the magnetic force on a current-carrying conductor. Understanding this force explains why a coil of wire can be made to spin.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.0] A wire carrying a current in a magnetic field experiences a force, the motor effect, which is greatest when the wire is at right angles to the field.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.1] The direction of the force is given by Fleming's left-hand rule: the thumb shows the force (motion), the first finger the magnetic field and the second finger the current.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.2] In a simple DC motor, a coil sits between the poles of a permanent magnet or electromagnet. The forces on opposite sides of the coil act in opposite directions, creating a turning effect, so the coil turns.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.3] The commutator is a split ring on the shaft; carbon brushes press on it and reverse the current in the coil every half turn, so the coil keeps turning the same way instead of stopping.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.4] Electrical energy is the input and kinetic energy the output; some is wasted as heat from the resistance of the coil and friction in the bearings.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.5] The shaft's rotation is passed to the blade, drum or fan by a drive shaft, and in some designs by gears or a drive belt.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.7.6] Diagram: a simple motor showing the magnet poles, the coil, the commutator and brushes, and the supply, with arrows for current direction and the forces on each side of the coil.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.8.b] Household appliances use different kinds of motor depending on whether they need high speed and light weight or steady, quiet running.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.8.0] A universal motor (a series-wound motor) is the common motor in small kitchen appliances and vacuum cleaners. It runs on either AC or DC, spins at high speed and is lightweight, which suits portable appliances such as food processors and hand mixers.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.8.1] The carbon brushes of a universal motor wear and need replacing.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.8.2] Speed control adjusts the voltage or current supplied to the motor, so a variable-speed controller changes the speed of a universal motor.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.8.3] An induction motor runs on AC and has no brushes or commutator; the rotating magnetic field of the stator induces currents in the rotor, which turns it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s3.8.4] Induction motors are robust, quiet, long-lived and need little maintenance, which suits uses where steady, quieter running is wanted, such as dishwasher pumps, some older washing machines and fridge compressors.

CANDIDATES hom-appliances-equipment-s2: 2. Selecting a large and a small household appliance
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.b] Before buying, compare the cost, size and capacity of each model against what the household needs. A good choice is value for money, which means quality against price, not simply the lowest price.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.0] Initial cost: compare the purchase price, including delivery and installation, across several shops and brands, so you know the true cost of getting the appliance working in your home.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.1] Running cost: this is the energy and water the appliance uses over its life. Compare it with the purchase price, since a cheap appliance can cost more to run, and the energy label shows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.2] Size and space: the appliance must fit the available space and allow room for doors to open, air to circulate (ventilation) and access to plumbing and power points.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.3] Capacity: match it to household size, such as litres of fresh and frozen food for a fridge or kilograms of laundry for a washing machine, and litres for a kettle.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.4] Payment method: compare cash, debit or credit card and credit agreements, and the total amount payable under any credit, because credit adds to the final cost.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.b] Cost and size are not the whole story. A buyer also checks how long the appliance will last, who will fix it, and how easy and safe it is to use every day.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.0] Brand reputation and reliability: established brands with a record of durable products, good reviews and available spare parts are less likely to need early repair.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.1] Guarantee and after-sales service: check the length of the guarantee, what it covers, and how quickly repairs, servicing, spare parts, delivery and installation are available.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.2] Ease of operation and cleaning: clear controls, removable parts and filters, smooth surfaces, dishwasher-safe parts and cord storage reduce the time and effort of use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.3] Safety features: automatic cut-outs, interlocks, child lock, cool-touch surfaces and recognised safety marks reduce the risk of injury or fire.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.4] Family needs and modern features: choose features the household will actually use, such as programmes, timers and smart controls, rather than paying for extras that stay unused.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.b] The same criteria apply to every appliance, but how much each one matters depends on the size and price of what you are buying. Apply them to one large and one small example.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.0] Large appliances (cooker, fridge, washing machine, dishwasher, tumble dryer) are expensive and long-lived and usually delivered and installed, so cost, energy efficiency, size and servicing weigh most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.1] Small appliances (kettle, toaster, iron, food processor, mixer) are cheaper and portable, so storage, cleaning and cord length weigh more.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.2] Examples to use for the individual studies: a washing machine, fridge-freezer or dishwasher as the large appliance, and a kettle, toaster, food processor or iron as the small one.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.b] Design is how an appliance looks and works, and construction is what it is made of and how well it is built. Together they decide how safe, durable and easy to clean it is.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.0] Design affects safety, durability, ease of use and cleaning, and how well the appliance fits the style and colour of the kitchen.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.1] Controls should be clearly labelled, easy to reach and simple to read; large, simple controls suit older users and children.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.2] Weight, handles and grip should suit the person using the appliance, so that lifting and pouring are safe, and non-slip feet give stability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.3] Robust construction and good-quality materials, such as stainless steel for surfaces and blades, increase durability. Other materials used include heat-resistant or cool-touch plastic, glass and enamelled steel.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.4] Smooth, sealed surfaces and removable parts are easier to clean and give fewer places for food and bacteria to collect. Sealed joints and a strong flex and plug add durability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.5] Design for safety and efficiency: insulation, interlocks, automatic cut-off, energy-saving settings and quiet running make the appliance safer and cheaper to use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.6] Examples of safe design: an interlock stops a machine working while its door is open, so fingers cannot reach moving parts.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.b] The energy label lets buyers compare the energy efficiency of appliances at a glance, on a common scale. An efficient appliance costs less to run and does less harm to the environment.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.0] The EU energy label rates an appliance from A to G, where A is the most efficient and G the least efficient. This scale applies to fridges, freezers, washing machines, washer-dryers and dishwashers from 1 March 2021, and to tumble dryers from 1 July 2025.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.1] The label is displayed on refrigerating appliances (fridges, freezers and fridge-freezers), washing machines, washer-dryers, dishwashers and tumble dryers. Two named appliances are enough if asked to name two.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.2] A-rated appliances use the least electricity, so they cost least to run and cause the least harm to the environment; G-rated appliances use the most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.3] The label gives annual energy consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which indicates likely running cost and environmental impact. Washing machine and dishwasher labels give energy per 100 cycles.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.4] It also gives, depending on the appliance, the noise level in decibels, capacity and water use. A refrigeration label shows the fresh and frozen capacity in litres.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.5] The current label carries a QR code that links to the product's entry in the EU product database (EPREL) for more detail.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.6] Energy in kWh equals power in kW multiplied by hours used. Higher Level answers can use this to compare the running costs of two appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.7] Efficiency in use is also the consumer's job: use full loads, eco programmes and lower temperatures, switch off standby, and choose the correct size.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.b] A star rating on a fridge-freezer or freezer tells you how cold the frozen-food compartment is and how long frozen food keeps in it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.0] The star rating applies to the frozen-food compartment of fridges and fridge-freezers and to freezers, not to the fresh-food section.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.1] One star: the compartment holds frozen food at −6°C for up to one week.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.2] Two stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −12°C for up to one month.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.3] Three stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C for up to three months.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.4] Four stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C to −25°C for up to one year, and fresh food can be frozen in it. Only four stars allows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.5] The star rating measures storage performance, not energy efficiency, so the energy label is used to compare running costs.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.b] Marks on an appliance or its packaging tell the buyer that a standard has been met. Each mark means something different, so know what each one does and does not show.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.0] The CE mark is the manufacturer's declaration that the product meets the health, safety and environmental requirements of the European Union. It is not independent testing.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.1] The double insulation symbol, a square inside a square, shows that the appliance has two layers of insulation (a Class II appliance), so it needs no earth wire and its plug has no earth connection. It is common on plastic-bodied small appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.2] The Irish Standard mark of the National Standards Authority of Ireland (NSAI) shows that the product has been certified to a standard.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.3] An ISO 9000 quality mark shows that the maker's quality-management system has been assessed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.4] Marks should appear on the appliance or its packaging, and a mark that cannot be found on the product does not show certification.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.5] Safety marks show that a product has been tested to a standard, but they do not guarantee its performance or reliability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.6] A manufacturer's guarantee or a retailer's own-brand label is not an independent mark.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.b] Before buying, a consumer gathers information from several sources. Some are independent, some come from sellers and may be biased, and some are personal experience.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.0] Independent sources: consumer magazines and websites that test and compare products publish independent results on appliance models.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.1] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC), the body for consumer protection and information, publishes guidance for consumers on their rights when buying appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.2] Citizens Information offers free, independent information on consumer rights and on buying goods and services. The European Consumer Centre Ireland helps with cross-border purchases, and libraries also hold information.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.3] Manufacturers' leaflets and brochures give specifications, guidelines for use and guarantee terms, but they are promotional documents.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.4] Product labels and packaging show the energy rating, safety marks, capacity and guarantee details, so you can compare models for example in the shop.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.5] Sales staff can explain features, but their advice may be influenced by the stock and margins of the shop. Advertisements highlight strengths, so check their claims against independent sources.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.6] Word of mouth, online user reviews and in-shop demonstrations reflect other people's experience; read them critically because they can be unreliable.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.7] Benefits of getting information before buying: compare price, installation and running cost; find the most energy-efficient model; compare features, design, size and ease of operation; reduce the influence of advertising; avoid a costly mistake.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.b] An appliance affects the environment at three points: when it is chosen, while it is used and when it is thrown out. A responsible consumer thinks about all three.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.0] Choosing: pick the best energy class you can afford and the correct size, and prefer repairable, durable appliances with eco and low-water programmes and little packaging.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.1] Using: run full loads, use eco and short cycles and lower temperatures, switch off standby, defrost freezers, and do not leave fridge doors open, because each wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.2] Maintaining: descale and maintain appliances so that they keep working efficiently.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.3] Disposing: repair, sell or donate an appliance while it still works, so it is not wasted.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.4] Maintaining also helps because limescale and ice build-up make an appliance work harder and use more energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.5] Under the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014, a retailer must take back an old appliance of similar type free of charge, one for one, when a new one is bought, so old appliances do not end up in landfill.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.6] Collection is on delivery if notice was given, and larger shops must accept very small items without a purchase.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.7] Electrical waste can also go to a civic amenity site. Never put it in the household bin, which the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol warns.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.b] A washing machine shows how the selection criteria apply to a large appliance, where cost, efficiency, size and installation all matter.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.0] Capacity: the drum size, stated in kilograms of dry laundry, should match the number of people in the household.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.1] Spin speed, measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), affects how dry clothes are after washing and so how much drying is needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.2] Eco and low-temperature programmes reduce water and energy use, and the energy label shows the rating for the model.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.3] Installation needs: a water supply and drain, a suitable power point, and a level floor must all be available where the machine is placed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.b] A kettle shows how the criteria apply to a small appliance, where capacity, cleaning and safety features matter most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.0] Capacity is stated in litres; buy a kettle that holds the amount the household needs, not more, because heating extra water wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.1] A concealed element in the base is easier to clean than an exposed element, where limescale collects.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.2] Automatic cut-off switches the kettle off when the water boils, and boil-dry protection switches it off if the water level is too low.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s5: 5. Food preparation and cooking equipment
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.b] Good equipment makes cooking easier and safer, but only if it suits the job. Choose for the quantity of food, the cooking method, the type of hob, durability, ease of cleaning and cost, and check how the material conducts heat (passes heat through it).
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.0] Buy equipment you will use often and can store easily, because a gadget used once a year wastes money and cupboard space.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.1] A heavy, flat, thick base spreads heat evenly and reduces hot spots that scorch food and make it stick; a thin base heats unevenly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.2] Aluminium and copper conduct heat quickly and evenly, so they respond fast when the heat changes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.3] Stainless steel is durable and does not react with acidic foods, but it conducts heat less well, so good pans have a base bonded to copper or aluminium. It is not the best conductor.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.4] Cast iron retains heat well and suits slow, even cooking such as braising, but it is heavy and rusts if left damp. Glass and ceramic dishes suit oven cooking and conduct heat slowly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.5] A non-stick coating allows frying with little fat, but it is scratched by metal utensils and damaged by overheating, for example heating an empty pan.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.6] A close-fitting lid retains steam, so food simmers with less energy and in less time. The pan base should match the ring size, so that the full base makes contact with the heat source.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.7] Induction hobs heat the pan by magnetism, so they need a pan with a magnetic (ferrous) base; aluminium and copper pans without one will not heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.0.8] Handles should be firm, heat-resistant and fixed securely; plastic and wooden handles are not suitable for the oven unless the manufacturer states it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.b] A practical kitchen needs a core set of tools, and recipes only work if ingredients are measured accurately.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.0] Pans and dishes: saucepans of different sizes, a frying pan, a roasting tin, a casserole, and baking tins and trays.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.1] Preparation tools: mixing bowls, a sieve, a colander, wooden spoons, a spatula, a whisk, a grater, a rolling pin, a peeler and a tin opener.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.2] Measuring and checking tools: a measuring jug, scales, measuring spoons, a timer and a thermometer.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.3] Weigh and measure accurately with scales, jugs and spoons, because too much or too little of an ingredient can change how a dish turns out.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.1.4] Ingredients are measured in metric, and spoon measures are level, not heaped, so that every cook gets the same amount.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.b] Knives are the most dangerous everyday tool in the kitchen, so choosing the right one and handling it correctly prevents most cuts. Chopping boards need care too, because they can pass germs between foods.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.0] A cook's knife is used for general chopping, a paring knife for small, detailed work and peeling, a serrated (bread) knife for bread and soft-skinned foods, and a carving knife for carving cooked meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.1] A sharp knife is safer than a blunt one because it cuts with less force and is less likely to slip.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.2] The claw grip curls the fingertips of the guiding hand under, so the knuckles guide the blade and the fingertips stay clear of it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.3] Place the chopping board on a damp cloth or non-slip mat so that it cannot move while cutting.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.4] Colour-coded boards (for raw meat, fish, vegetables and cooked foods) keep them apart, preventing cross-contamination (germs spreading from raw to ready-to-eat food).
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.5] Wash boards well after raw meat, and replace badly scored boards because germs can lodge in the cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.6] Carry a knife by its handle with the blade pointing down and away from the body; never leave a knife in a sink of washing-up water, where it cannot be seen.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.2.7] Store knives in a block, on a rack or on a magnetic strip, so that the edges are protected and the blades are not loose in a drawer.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.b] Most kitchen accidents are burns, scalds, cuts and electric shocks, and most are avoidable with a few habits. Each habit below removes a specific danger.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.0] Hob: turn pan handles inwards over the hob so they cannot be knocked or pulled, use a ring that matches the pan size, and never leave hot oil or fat unattended.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.1] Keep tea towels and flexes away from the hob, where they can catch fire or melt.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.2] Lift lids away from the face so that escaping steam does not scald.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.3] Oven: place the shelves before heating, open the door carefully, and use a stable tray for hot dishes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.4] Use dry oven gloves or a dry cloth to handle hot dishes; a damp cloth conducts heat and causes scalds.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.5] Electrical: keep the flex of appliances away from hot surfaces and water, never handle an appliance with wet hands, and don't overload sockets.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.6] Switch off and unplug an appliance before cleaning its blades or removing food from them, and do not use one with a damaged flex or plug; it must be repaired or replaced before use.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.3.7] Pressure cooker: check that the sealing ring and valve are clean and undamaged, never force the lid, and open it only after the pressure has fallen. Microwave: use microwave-safe containers, never put metal in the cavity, and cover and stir food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.b] Beyond individual appliances, a few habits keep the whole kitchen safe, especially when something goes wrong.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.0] Clean up spills at once, because a wet or greasy floor causes slips and falls.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.1] Keep a fire blanket in the kitchen and know how to use it, so a small pan fire can be smothered quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.4.2] Never use water on a fat fire, because it causes the burning fat to flare up and spread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.b] Looking after equipment makes it last longer and keeps food safe. The right care depends on the material, so follow the manufacturer's care instructions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.0] Wash equipment in hot soapy water or in a dishwasher, then rinse, dry thoroughly and store it clean and dry, so that bacteria cannot grow and metal does not rust.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.1] Do not run cold water into a hot pan; the sudden change in temperature can warp the base and crack a non-stick or ceramic surface. Soak burnt pans before scrubbing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.2] Cast iron must be dried straight after washing and wiped with a thin film of oil to prevent rust.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.3] Use wooden or silicone utensils on non-stick surfaces, whereas metal utensils scratch them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.4] Use abrasive pads only on surfaces that tolerate them; stainless steel and cast iron tolerate them better than non-stick coatings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.5] Wooden items are not left to soak, because the wood absorbs water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.6] Dishcloths, sponges, tea towels and brushes can carry bacteria and should be changed or washed regularly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.5.7] Store knives safely, and stack pans with protection between them so non-stick surfaces are not scratched.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.6.b] In the practical coursework, equipment is part of what you are marked on, because it shows you can plan and work like a competent cook.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.6.0] Area B assignments ask you to describe new equipment or a new skill, and the planning section lists the key equipment you will use.
[hom-cooking-methods-s5.6.1] You should be able to name the equipment for a dish, say why it was chosen, and explain how it was used safely and hygienically.
