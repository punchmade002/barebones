TARGET hom-cooking-methods-s4: 4. Soups and sauces
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.b] Soups are grouped by their thickness and by the temperature they are served at. Knowing the class tells you what the soup is like and how it is made, and each class has typical examples.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.0] Clear (thin) soups are thin and transparent. Consommé is a clear soup made from clarified stock, which is stock cleared of cloudy particles using egg white and minced meat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.1] Broths are thin, clear soups made by simmering meat or vegetables in water, such as chicken broth or vegetable broth.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.2] Purée soups are thick soups in which cooked vegetables are sieved or blended to a smooth texture, for example carrot, or leek and potato. The vegetables themselves make the soup thick.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.3] Thickened soups are thickened with an added agent such as a roux (a cooked mixture of fat and flour), cornflour, puréed potato, or a liaison, which is a mixture of egg yolk and cream.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.4] Cold soups are served chilled. Gazpacho is a Spanish cold soup of raw tomato, pepper and cucumber.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.5] Stock is the base of most soups. It is a liquid made by simmering bones, meat or vegetables with herbs in water, and it gives the soup its flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.0.6] To make a good stock, skim off the scum that rises to the surface, do not boil it hard, and strain it at the end so that the finished liquid is clear.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.b] Most soups follow the same order: prepare the vegetables, soften them, add liquid and simmer, then finish. Each step has a reason, which is what examiners look for.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.0] Wash, peel and cut the vegetables into even pieces so that they all cook at the same rate and none is left hard or turned to mush.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.1] Sweating the vegetables means to cook them gently in a little fat in a covered pan until soft, without browning, to release their flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.2] Add the stock or liquid, bring the soup to the boil, then simmer until the vegetables are tender. Boiling hard breaks up the vegetables and loses flavour. Thicken the soup if needed.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.3] Purée soups are sieved or blended until smooth. If using a blender, do not overfill it and hold the lid down, because hot liquid can force the lid off.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.4] After puréeing, reheat the soup and season it. Add seasoning at the end and taste first, because the flavour concentrates as the soup reduces and cooks also season in stages.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.5] Add cream, milk or a liaison (egg yolk and cream) off the boil so that the soup does not curdle. Adjust the consistency at the end too.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.1.6] For safe practice, cool and chill leftover soup quickly, and reheat it until piping hot.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.2.b] A soup is served at the right temperature and finished with a garnish, a small decoration added for colour and flavour. The garnish and bowl are chosen to suit the type of soup.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.2.0] Serve hot soup hot and in warm bowls so that it does not cool quickly. Thick soups are garnished with croutons, chopped parsley or chives, a swirl of cream or grated cheese.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.2.1] Clear soups are garnished with thin strips of vegetable or chopped herbs, which keep the soup looking light and clear.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.2.2] Cold soups are chilled thoroughly before serving and garnished with finely diced vegetables or a little cream.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.2.3] A garnish must be edible, suit the soup and be small. Accompaniments, such as brown bread, are served alongside.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.b] A roux is a cooked mixture of equal amounts of fat and flour (by weight), and the amount of liquid added decides the thickness of the sauce. Changing the liquid, not the roux, gives different sauces.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.0] 1. Melt the fat. 2. Stir in an equal amount of flour and cook gently, briefly, to remove the raw flour taste. 3. Add the liquid gradually, stirring or whisking so that the starch disperses without lumps.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.1] 4. Bring the sauce to the boil, then simmer it to cook the starch fully. 5. Season.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.2] A roux thickens because the starch granules absorb liquid and swell as the sauce heats, a process called gelatinisation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.3] A white roux is cooked only briefly, until pale. It thickens most strongly and is used for white, parsley, cheese, mushroom and béchamel sauces.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.4] A brown roux is cooked longer until golden or brown. It thickens less and is used for brown and stewing sauces.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.5] The same fat and flour give thin pouring, thicker coating and very thick binding sauces by using progressively less liquid: more for a pouring sauce, less for a coating sauce, least for a binding sauce (panada).
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.6] Béchamel is a white sauce made by infusing milk with flavourings such as onion, bay leaf and cloves. Panada is a thick, roux-based paste used to bind ingredients such as croquettes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.3.7] Roux-based sauces accepted as examples are white, parsley, béchamel, cheese, mushroom, curry, stewing, panada and brown sauce.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.b] Besides roux-based sauces, sauces are classified by what thickens or makes them, or by how they are served. Examiners ask for a class with an example, so learn one or two for each.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.0] Roux-based sauces: white, parsley, cheese, mushroom, béchamel, curry and stewing sauce.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.1] Egg-based sauces: custard sets because egg proteins coagulate in hot milk. Mayonnaise is an emulsion of oil in egg yolk, and hollandaise is an emulsion of butter in egg yolk with lemon juice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.2] Sweet sauces: chocolate, toffee, caramel and butterscotch, served with desserts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.3] Fruit sauces: apple sauce, orange sauce and coulis, a thin purée of raw or cooked fruit, often sieved.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.4] Cold sauces and dressings: mint sauce and French dressing, which are served cold.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.5] Miscellaneous sauces: bread sauce, thickened with breadcrumbs.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.4.6] Uses: white sauce with fish, cheese sauce with cauliflower, apple sauce with pork, mint sauce with lamb, custard with apple tart and French dressing on salad.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.5.b] Convenience soups are ready-made soups that save preparation time. They come in five forms, and each is stored and prepared differently.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.5.0] Dehydrated or packaged soups are dried mixes reconstituted with boiling water or milk. They have a long shelf life.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.5.1] Cook-chill soups are sold chilled and heated before eating. They have a shorter shelf life than canned soups.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.5.2] Canned soups are sterilised in sealed tins, which gives a long shelf life, and they only need heating.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.5.3] Frozen soups are stored at −18 °C and heated after thawing. They keep their flavour well.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.5.4] Single-portion soups are sold in individual pots or cups that are heated or reconstituted for one serving.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.6.b] Convenience soups trade cost and quality for speed and ease. Weigh them against homemade soup when you evaluate them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.6.0] Advantages: little preparation time, simple skills needed, useful in an emergency, wide variety available, and easy to store. Dried and canned soups also have a long shelf life.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.6.1] Disadvantages: they can be expensive per portion, are often high in salt, and may contain artificial colours, flavours and additives.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.6.2] They may also have less fibre and flavour than homemade soup, and you have less choice over the ingredients used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.6.3] Adding fresh vegetables, herbs, milk or a topping to a convenience soup improves its flavour and adds nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.b] Labels let a consumer compare soups and choose for health or allergy needs. Labelling rules for food are set by Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.0] The label must give the name of the food, the ingredients list, and the net quantity in metric units.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.1] Ingredients are listed in descending order of weight at the time of manufacture, so the first ingredient is the one used in greatest amount.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.2] Allergenic ingredients, such as gluten, milk, eggs, celery and nuts, must be emphasised within the ingredients list so that people with allergies can spot them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.3] The nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt per 100 g or 100 ml.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.4] Cooking instructions, storage conditions and a use-by or best-before date are shown.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.5] The name and address of the food business operator are given, along with the country of origin where it is required.
[hom-cooking-methods-s4.7.6] Labels let the consumer compare soups by salt content, ingredients and allergens, and check that the soup suits their needs.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.b] Sugars are the sweet, water-loving carbohydrates, and these two properties explain why they are used so widely in cooking. Starch is the contrast: it is neither sweet nor soluble in cold water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.0] All sugars are sweet, but to different degrees. Fructose is sweeter than sucrose (table sugar), and lactose (milk sugar) is the least sweet of the common sugars.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.1] On a scale where sucrose scores 100, the 2017 marking scheme gives fructose 170 and lactose 15. Learn the order (fructose, then sucrose, then lactose) and use the figures only as that scale.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.2] Sweetness is why sugar is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.3] Sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve readily in water, and solubility increases with temperature, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.4] A concentrated sugar solution is a syrup. Syrups are used to sweeten drinks and sauces, to make icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.5] Starch is different: it is insoluble in cold water, so it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension (tiny solid particles spread through the liquid). It is also not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.b] Starch behaves differently from sugar: it is insoluble in cold water and must be heated with a liquid before it thickens anything. It also takes in moisture from the air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.0] Gelatinisation: when starch is heated in a liquid, the granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, thickening it into a sol (a colloidal solution, where tiny particles stay spread through the liquid).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.1] On cooling, a concentrated starch mixture may set into a gel (a solid-like network that holds the liquid), which is why a cooled custard or sauce can become firm.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.2] Gelatinised starch thickens white sauces, custard, soups, gravies, risotto and fillings for pies and flans.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.3] To prevent lumps, the granules must be separated before they swell. Mix the starch with a little cold liquid first (a cornflour paste), or coat it in fat as in a roux.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.4] A roux is a mixture of fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added. The fat separates the starch granules, and the starch in the flour gelatinises as the liquid is stirred in, giving a base for white sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.5] Starch is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump if stored in damp air. Store them in airtight containers.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.6] Sugar, especially brown sugar, is also hygroscopic, so it can turn hard or lumpy if left open in damp air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.b] How a carbohydrate food is cooked decides what happens to it. Dry heat browns it, while moist heat softens it and thickens liquids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.0] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch makes the granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs: the starch breaks down into brown dextrins. This gives a brown colour and a crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.1] Dry heat on sugar causes caramelisation and the Maillard reaction, which brown the surface of the food and give it flavour and a crisp texture.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.2] Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch causes gelatinisation: the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid, thickening sauces and puddings. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.3] Moist heat softens the cellulose in vegetable cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest. The vegetable keeps its fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.4] Sugar dissolves in the cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when fruit is stewed, so the syrup carries the flavour of the fruit. Sugar also helps the fruit keep its shape.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.b] Enzymes (proteins that speed up chemical reactions) break complex carbohydrates down in stages until they are small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.0] 1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins breaking starch down into smaller polysaccharides and maltose. This enzyme stops working in the stomach acid, so no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.1] 2. Pancreatic amylase, in pancreatic juice released into the small intestine, completes the breakdown of the remaining starch to maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.2] 3. Maltase, sucrase and lactase, made by the wall of the small intestine and found in the intestinal juice, finish the job. Maltase splits maltose into two glucose units, sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.3] Only monosaccharides (single sugar units: glucose, fructose and galactose) are small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.4] Humans have no enzyme that digests cellulose, so it passes through the intestine as dietary fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.b] Sugar does much more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each property has a use, and exam answers pair the two.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.0] Sweetness: sweetens cakes, desserts and drinks. Solubility: dissolves to make syrups and icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.1] Preservation: sugar draws water out of micro-organisms, so they cannot grow and spoilage is slowed in jam, jelly, chutney and crystallised fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.2] Fermentation: sugar is the food for yeast, which produces carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.3] Aeration (trapping air): creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures such as sponge cakes. Whisking traps air in egg white, and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable, which makes meringue.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.4] Maillard reaction: sugar reacts with amino acids (the units that make up protein) when heated, a non-enzymic browning that gives brown colour and flavour to roast meat, toast, roast potatoes and baked goods. Caramelisation is different because it involves sugar alone.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.5] Gel formation: sugar, acid and pectin together set jam into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.6] Caramelisation gives crème caramel and toffee, crystallisation gives confectionery, and inversion is used in jam and boiled sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.b] Caramelisation, the Maillard reaction and dextrinisation all brown food, so they are easy to confuse. What differs is what is being heated and with what.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.0] Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry until it melts and browns, so it needs no protein, as in caramel sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.1] The Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids together, heated dry, so it browns foods that contain both, such as toast and roast meat.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.2] Dextrinisation is starch, not sugar, broken into brown dextrins by dry heat, which is why toast browns even though bread is mostly starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.3] Gelatinisation is a different change again: starch heated in a liquid (moist heat) swells and thickens it, while inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by acid or enzyme.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.b] Hydrolysis is the reaction that splits big sugars into small ones, and it is the same reaction that happens in digestion. Inversion is the form of it that cooks use on sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: the bond between two units is split and water is added across it. It is the reverse of condensation (joining units with the loss of water).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.1] A disaccharide reacts with water, with enzymes or with heat and acid, and splits into its monosaccharide units. Sucrose gives glucose and fructose, lactose gives glucose and galactose, and maltose gives two glucose units.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.2] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. It produces invert sugar, a mixture of glucose and fructose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.3] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise than sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.4] This is why invert sugar is used in jam and boiled sweets, and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.b] Heating and cooling sugar and starch changes their colour, flavour and texture in three different ways. Each is wanted in some foods and avoided in others.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.0] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a distinctive, pleasant flavour. Overheating makes it dark and bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.1] Caramel is used in caramel sauce, crème caramel, toffee and brûlée.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.2] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution) and the mixture cools, sugar crystals form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.3] Controlled crystallisation gives the texture of fudge, fondant and sweets, while unwanted large crystals make jam grainy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.4] Crystallisation is prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (which causes inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.5] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains of glucose units called dextrins, which are brown. The 2005 scheme names the brown compound pyrodextrin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.6] Dextrinisation gives toast and the crust of baked bread their brown colour and flavour. Browned flour used for a roux thickens less, because its starch has already been broken into shorter chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.7] Dextrinisation is a property of starch, not sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.b] Jam sets because of pectin, a substance in fruit cell walls. Making jam depends on getting the pectin out of the fruit and then giving it the right conditions to set.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.0] Pectin is extracted by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft. Acid, such as lemon juice, helps release the pectin from the cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.1] Fruit high in pectin includes cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit (especially the peel), gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants. Strawberries are low in pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.2] Unripe fruit contains more pectin than ripe fruit, so under-ripe apples are a good source of pectin for jam.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.3] A gel forms only when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions and the mixture is boiled and then cooled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.4] During cooling, the pectin molecules link into a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.5] Too little pectin, as in strawberries, gives a runny jam. Add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit to fix this.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.6] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.b] This simple test shows that starch does not thicken a cold liquid but does thicken it when heated.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.0] Aim: to show the effect of heat on starch mixed with a liquid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.1] Method: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water and observe that it does not dissolve and does not thicken. Then heat the mixture while stirring and observe that it thickens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.2] Variable: temperature is the variable that changes (cold, then heated).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.3] Control and comparison: the unheated cornflour and cold water mixture is compared with the heated one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.4] Result: cold, the starch settles as a cloudy suspension and the mixture stays thin; heated, the granules swell, burst and absorb the water, and the mixture thickens into a sol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.b] Heating sugar on its own, with no water, shows caramelisation happening step by step.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.0] Aim: to observe how granulated sugar changes when it is heated on its own, with no water added.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.1] Method: place a measured quantity of granulated sugar in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat, stirring gently as it melts, and note the colour and smell at regular intervals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.2] Variables: heating time is the independent variable; colour, smell and taste are the dependent variables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.3] Control: a sample of the same sugar left unheated, for comparison.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.4] Safety: molten sugar is extremely hot and causes severe burns, so use heatproof utensils and keep hands and skin away from the pan.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.5] Expected results: the colour darkens gradually from pale to golden, then deep brown, and the caramel smell strengthens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.b] These two tests show a change in starch caused by dry heat and a change in sucrose caused by acid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.0] Dextrinisation, aim: to show that dry heat changes starch. Method: toast white bread, or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.1] Dextrinisation, result: the browned starch is darker and thickens less than unbrowned starch, because its chains are shorter. Safety: the pan is hot.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.2] Inversion, aim: to show that acid changes sucrose. Method: boil a sucrose solution with a little lemon juice and cool it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.3] Inversion, result: the invert sugar does not crystallise as readily and is sweeter.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.4] A Benedict's test shows the invert sugar: reducing sugars give an orange-red colour, while sucrose does not.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s3: 3. Choice of cooking method
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.b] Cooking methods fall into three groups according to what carries the heat to the food. The right choice depends on the food, the eater and the result you want.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.0] Moist methods use water, stock or steam as the medium. Dry methods use hot air or radiant heat (heat given off as rays from a hot element or flame) with little or no added liquid. Fat-based methods use fat or oil as the medium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.1] The food: tender cuts and delicate foods such as fish, eggs and vegetables suit quick methods, because they need little time to become tender. Tough, fibrous cuts need long, moist methods, which soften the fibres.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.2] Nutrition: steaming, stir-frying and grilling use little or no water and short times, so they conserve water-soluble vitamins. Boiling loses them into the cooking water unless that liquid is used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.3] Palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat): dry and fat-based methods brown and crisp the surface, adding colour and flavour, while moist methods give a soft, tender texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.4] Diet: steaming, poaching, boiling and grilling on a rack add little or no fat, which suits a diet that limits fat. Deep-fat frying adds fat and energy because the food absorbs fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.5] Consumer needs: an older person who has difficulty chewing or swallowing needs soft, moist, easily digested food, so a moist method is the better choice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.6] Time and equipment: a pressure cooker shortens cooking time, while stewing and braising are long, slow methods in a covered pan or oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.7] Cost: slow moist cooking makes an inexpensive cut of meat tender and palatable, so you do not have to buy an expensive cut.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.b] In moist methods the food is cooked in water, stock or steam. The temperature of the liquid is what separates them: boiling is hottest, simmering is just below it and poaching is lower again.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.0] Boiling: food is cooked in rapidly bubbling water at boiling point, 100 °C at sea level. It is quick and used for vegetables, pasta, rice, eggs and potatoes, but water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) into the water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.1] Simmering: liquid is kept just below boiling point, with small bubbles breaking the surface. It is gentler than boiling and is used for stocks, soups, stews and stewed fruit.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.2] Stewing: food is cooked slowly and gently in a small amount of liquid in a covered pan or casserole, on the hob or in the oven. It suits tough meat and vegetables, as in Irish stew, a traditional stew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.3] Stewing keeps nutrients because the liquid is served with the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.4] Steaming: food is cooked by the steam from boiling water without touching the water, in a steamer, on a plate over a pan or in a covered bowl in a pan. No fat is added, and nutrients and shape are kept. It suits fish, chicken, vegetables, rice and steamed puddings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.5] Poaching: food is cooked gently in liquid kept slightly below simmering point, about 85 °C, on the hob or in the oven. It suits delicate foods that would break up or toughen in fiercer heat, such as eggs, fish and fruit like pears.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.6] Braising: tough meat or vegetables are browned in fat, then cooked slowly with a little liquid in a covered dish, in the oven or on the hob. The long, slow, moist cooking tenderises tough cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.7] Pressure cooking: food is cooked in a sealed pan in which steam builds up pressure. This raises the temperature above 100 °C and shortens the cooking time. It is used for stocks, stews, tough meat and pulses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.b] Dry methods cook food with hot air or radiant heat rather than liquid. They brown the surface, which adds colour and flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.0] Roasting: meat, poultry, potatoes or vegetables are cooked uncovered in the oven in a roasting tin, usually with a little fat. The surface browns while the centre cooks by conduction and convection.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.1] Baking: food is cooked by the dry heat of an oven, mainly by convection (hot air circulating) and radiation. It is used for cakes, bread, pastry, fish, pasta and potato dishes, as with fish pie and lasagne.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.2] Grilling: food is cooked by radiant heat from an element or flame above or below it. The heat seals the surface, so it is a fast method. It suits tender cuts, steaks, chops, thin fish, sausages, rashers, tomatoes, mushrooms, peppers and kebabs.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.3] Barbecuing: this is grilling outdoors on a grid over burning charcoal or gas, using radiant heat, which gives a smoky flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.4] Grilling lowers the fat content of food, because fat drips away from it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.b] Grilling is fast and the heat is fierce, so food can burn outside while it is raw inside, or dry out. These rules prevent that.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.0] Preheat the grill to the required temperature so that the surface seals on contact.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.1] Oil the grid or the food to prevent sticking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.2] Use thin pieces, so that heat reaches the centre before the surface burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.3] Do not add salt before grilling, because salt draws moisture to the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.4] Turn the food with tongs rather than a fork, so that its juices stay inside instead of running out through holes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.5] Use the correct cooking time, since grilled food dries out and overcooks quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.b] Fat-based methods, usually called frying, cook food in hot fat or oil. They give a crisp, golden surface, but the amount of fat used varies a great deal from one method to another.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.0] Shallow frying: food is cooked in hot fat in a shallow pan, partly covered by the fat and turned once or twice, giving a crisp surface. It is used for eggs, fish, chops and pancakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.1] Deep-fat frying: food is fully immersed in hot oil in a deep pan or fryer, which seals the surface quickly. It is used for chips and fish in batter. The food absorbs some fat, so it is higher in fat and energy than grilled food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.2] Stir-frying: small, thin pieces are tossed and stirred constantly over high heat in a wok or large pan with a little oil. Because the cooking is so quick, it keeps colour, crispness and vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.3] Dry frying: food is cooked in a non-stick pan without added fat, using the fat already in foods such as bacon rashers and sausages.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.4] Pot roasting: meat is cooked in a little fat in a covered pan on the hob, so it cooks in its own juices and steam and stays moist. It is not done in the oven and not uncovered.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.b] A microwave oven is a fast method that suits reheating and cooking small amounts. It works differently from the other methods, so its advantages and limits are worth knowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.0] Microwave ovens cook and reheat quickly with little added fat and water, so vitamins are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.1] They do not brown food unless a grill or combination mode is used, so the surface stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.2] Containers must be microwave-safe, and metal must not be used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.b] Examiners often ask you to give dishes or foods for a method. These are the examples that are accepted.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.0] Baking: apple crumble, sponge, soda bread, fish pie, lasagne, pizza and muffins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.1] Grilling: kebabs, chicken satay, burgers, fish, rashers, sausages, tomatoes, mushrooms and bruschetta.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.2] Steaming: fish, chicken, rice, Christmas pudding (a traditional steamed pudding), fruit and vegetables. Roasting: meat, potatoes and vegetables.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.3] Frying has four types: dry, shallow, deep and stir-fry. The moist methods are boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.4] Classifying methods: moist covers boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking. Dry covers baking, grilling, roasting and barbecuing. Frying covers dry, shallow, deep and stir-frying.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.b] Steaming is a good choice for an older person because it is simple and gives soft food that is easy to eat. It also has weaknesses, so a full evaluation covers ease, nutrition and palatability.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.0] Ease: steaming needs little attention or equipment, a whole meal can cook in a tiered steamer, and it suits small portions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.1] Texture: the moist, soft food is easy to chew and swallow, which suits an older person with difficulty chewing or swallowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.2] Nutrition: steaming adds no fat, so it suits a diet that limits fat. Because the food does not sit in water, water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.3] Palatability: steamed food keeps its colour and moisture, but it can taste bland and does not brown. Overcooking spoils its texture and colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.4] Fish, chicken, rice and vegetables suit steaming; steamed puddings are a traditional example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.5] Herbs, lemon juice and a sauce add flavour without added salt, which suits a diet that limits salt.
