TARGET hom-cereals-grains-s4: 4. Preparation and cooking of cereals
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.b] Cereals are mostly starch, with some protein, sugar, cellulose and B group vitamins, and heat changes all of them. The way you heat the cereal, with moisture or without, decides which change is most obvious.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.0] Moist heat (boiling, simmering, steaming) makes starch granules absorb liquid, swell and burst. This is why the cereal softens and thickens the liquid around it.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.1] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting) dries the surface and breaks the starch down into dextrins, a change called dextrinisation. This forms a brown, crisp crust.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.2] In frying, the starch also absorbs fat, so the cooked cereal food takes up fat as well as browning.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.3] Dry heat also caramelises sugars in cereal products (heating sugar until it turns brown), adding colour and flavour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.4] Heat softens the cellulose (the fibre) in the bran and cell walls, so cooked cereals are easier to digest.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.5] Heat coagulates protein (it sets firm), which sets the structure of batters, doughs, baked goods and cooked cereals.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.6] Heat destroys some B group vitamins, and more are lost when cereals are boiled in a large volume of water that is then discarded, because the vitamins dissolve into it.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.7] Overall, the colour, flavour and texture of the cereal all change during cooking.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.b] Gelatinisation is what happens when starch is heated in a liquid: the granules absorb water, swell and burst, and the mixture thickens. It is how flour or cornflour thickens a sauce.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.0] 1. Starch granules absorb water from the liquid and swell.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.1] 2. Some starch (amylose) leaches out of the swollen granules into the liquid.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.2] 3. The liquid thickens as the swollen granules and amylose form a gel-like network that fills it.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.3] 4. On further heating the granules burst, and the starch is fully gelatinised.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.4] Heat is essential. Flour left cold in a liquid only forms a paste, so it does not thicken by absorbing liquid alone.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.5] Starch granules must be kept apart before heating, by stirring, by blending the flour with a little cold liquid first, or by rubbing fat in as in a roux. Otherwise they clump into lumps.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.6] Flour or cornflour must be cooked long enough to gelatinise fully, otherwise the sauce tastes floury.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.7] A roux is a cooked mixture of equal weights of fat and flour, used as the base of a sauce. The fat coats the starch granules and keeps the flour from forming lumps when the liquid is added.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.b] Rice shows clearly what moist heat does to a cereal, and the same ideas apply to pasta and oats. The method you choose also decides how many B vitamins you keep.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.0] Rinsing rice removes surface starch, so that the cooked grains stay separate instead of sticking together.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.1] Absorption method: rice is cooked in a measured amount of water, covered, until all of the water is absorbed, and it is left off the heat to finish.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.2] Boiling method: rice is cooked in excess water, which is drained off afterwards.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.3] Moist heat makes rice grains swell, burst and absorb liquid, and gelatinisation gives the cooked rice its soft texture.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.4] Fibre in the grain softens during cooking, making the rice easier to digest. Its colour also changes.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.5] B group vitamins leach (dissolve out) into the cooking water, so use only the water needed, cover the pan, do not overcook, and use the cooking liquid, for example in soups, where practical.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.6] Brown rice takes longer to cook than white rice because the intact bran layer slows water absorption into the grain.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.7] Pasta is boiled in plenty of boiling water until al dente (cooked but still slightly firm). Oats for porridge are cooked in milk or water and stirred as the starch gelatinises, which stops lumps forming.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.b] A raising agent produces or traps a gas (air, steam or carbon dioxide) that expands in the heat of baking. The protein and starch then set around the bubbles and hold the structure, giving a light, risen product.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.0] Air (a physical raising agent) is trapped by beating, whisking eggs, creaming fat and sugar, sieving flour, folding and rubbing in. It expands in the oven, as in sponge cakes, meringues and, slightly, shortcrust pastry.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.1] Steam: water in a mixture turns to steam in the hot oven and expands the product, as in choux pastry, puff and rough puff pastry, Yorkshire pudding and pancakes.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.2] Yeast (a biological raising agent) ferments sugars in warm, moist dough, producing carbon dioxide and alcohol and releasing energy. The gluten (a protein, not starch) traps the gas and the dough rises during proving.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.3] Yeast works best at about 35-40 degrees Celsius and is killed above about 55-60 degrees Celsius, so dough is proved in a warm place, not a hot one. Baking then kills the yeast.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.4] Bicarbonate of soda (bread soda) releases carbon dioxide when it reacts with an acid such as buttermilk, yoghurt, treacle or honey in the presence of moisture and heat. It is used in soda bread and scones.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.5] Baking powder contains bicarbonate of soda with an acid salt and a starch filler. The acid and alkali react when moistened and heated to release carbon dioxide, so it is used in cakes, muffins and scones.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.6] Self-raising flour is plain flour with baking powder already added.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.7] Raising agents for named products: bread uses yeast, bread soda, or air or baking powder; sponge cake uses air, self-raising flour or baking powder; muffins use baking powder or bread soda; lemon drizzle cake uses baking powder or air.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.b] A raising agent only works if the mixture and the oven let it do its job. Small errors in measuring or handling can leave a baked product flat or heavy.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.0] Measure accurately, because too much or too little raising agent gives a poor rise or a coarse texture.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.1] Use the correct oven temperature, so the gas expands and the protein and starch set at the right moment.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.2] Do not open the oven door early, because the cold air can make the risen mixture collapse before it has set.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.3] Handle the mixture correctly, for example folding gently so the trapped air is not knocked out.

[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.b] Sugars are the sweet, water-loving carbohydrates, and these two properties explain why they are used so widely in cooking. Starch is the contrast: it is neither sweet nor soluble in cold water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.0] All sugars are sweet, but to different degrees. Fructose is sweeter than sucrose (table sugar), and lactose (milk sugar) is the least sweet of the common sugars.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.1] On a scale where sucrose scores 100, the 2017 marking scheme gives fructose 170 and lactose 15. Learn the order (fructose, then sucrose, then lactose) and use the figures only as that scale.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.2] Sweetness is why sugar is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.3] Sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve readily in water, and solubility increases with temperature, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.4] A concentrated sugar solution is a syrup. Syrups are used to sweeten drinks and sauces, to make icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.5] Starch is different: it is insoluble in cold water, so it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension (tiny solid particles spread through the liquid). It is also not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.b] Starch behaves differently from sugar: it is insoluble in cold water and must be heated with a liquid before it thickens anything. It also takes in moisture from the air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.0] Gelatinisation: when starch is heated in a liquid, the granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, thickening it into a sol (a colloidal solution, where tiny particles stay spread through the liquid).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.1] On cooling, a concentrated starch mixture may set into a gel (a solid-like network that holds the liquid), which is why a cooled custard or sauce can become firm.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.2] Gelatinised starch thickens white sauces, custard, soups, gravies, risotto and fillings for pies and flans.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.3] To prevent lumps, the granules must be separated before they swell. Mix the starch with a little cold liquid first (a cornflour paste), or coat it in fat as in a roux.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.4] A roux is a mixture of fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added. The fat separates the starch granules, and the starch in the flour gelatinises as the liquid is stirred in, giving a base for white sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.5] Starch is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump if stored in damp air. Store them in airtight containers.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.6] Sugar, especially brown sugar, is also hygroscopic, so it can turn hard or lumpy if left open in damp air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.b] How a carbohydrate food is cooked decides what happens to it. Dry heat browns it, while moist heat softens it and thickens liquids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.0] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch makes the granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs: the starch breaks down into brown dextrins. This gives a brown colour and a crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.1] Dry heat on sugar causes caramelisation and the Maillard reaction, which brown the surface of the food and give it flavour and a crisp texture.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.2] Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch causes gelatinisation: the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid, thickening sauces and puddings. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.3] Moist heat softens the cellulose in vegetable cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest. The vegetable keeps its fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.4] Sugar dissolves in the cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when fruit is stewed, so the syrup carries the flavour of the fruit. Sugar also helps the fruit keep its shape.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.b] Enzymes (proteins that speed up chemical reactions) break complex carbohydrates down in stages until they are small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.0] 1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins breaking starch down into smaller polysaccharides and maltose. This enzyme stops working in the stomach acid, so no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.1] 2. Pancreatic amylase, in pancreatic juice released into the small intestine, completes the breakdown of the remaining starch to maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.2] 3. Maltase, sucrase and lactase, made by the wall of the small intestine and found in the intestinal juice, finish the job. Maltase splits maltose into two glucose units, sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.3] Only monosaccharides (single sugar units: glucose, fructose and galactose) are small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.4] Humans have no enzyme that digests cellulose, so it passes through the intestine as dietary fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.b] Sugar does much more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each property has a use, and exam answers pair the two.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.0] Sweetness: sweetens cakes, desserts and drinks. Solubility: dissolves to make syrups and icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.1] Preservation: sugar draws water out of micro-organisms, so they cannot grow and spoilage is slowed in jam, jelly, chutney and crystallised fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.2] Fermentation: sugar is the food for yeast, which produces carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.3] Aeration (trapping air): creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures such as sponge cakes. Whisking traps air in egg white, and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable, which makes meringue.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.4] Maillard reaction: sugar reacts with amino acids (the units that make up protein) when heated, a non-enzymic browning that gives brown colour and flavour to roast meat, toast, roast potatoes and baked goods. Caramelisation is different because it involves sugar alone.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.5] Gel formation: sugar, acid and pectin together set jam into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.6] Caramelisation gives crème caramel and toffee, crystallisation gives confectionery, and inversion is used in jam and boiled sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.b] Caramelisation, the Maillard reaction and dextrinisation all brown food, so they are easy to confuse. What differs is what is being heated and with what.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.0] Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry until it melts and browns, so it needs no protein, as in caramel sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.1] The Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids together, heated dry, so it browns foods that contain both, such as toast and roast meat.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.2] Dextrinisation is starch, not sugar, broken into brown dextrins by dry heat, which is why toast browns even though bread is mostly starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.3] Gelatinisation is a different change again: starch heated in a liquid (moist heat) swells and thickens it, while inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by acid or enzyme.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.b] Hydrolysis is the reaction that splits big sugars into small ones, and it is the same reaction that happens in digestion. Inversion is the form of it that cooks use on sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: the bond between two units is split and water is added across it. It is the reverse of condensation (joining units with the loss of water).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.1] A disaccharide reacts with water, with enzymes or with heat and acid, and splits into its monosaccharide units. Sucrose gives glucose and fructose, lactose gives glucose and galactose, and maltose gives two glucose units.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.2] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. It produces invert sugar, a mixture of glucose and fructose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.3] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise than sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.4] This is why invert sugar is used in jam and boiled sweets, and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.b] Heating and cooling sugar and starch changes their colour, flavour and texture in three different ways. Each is wanted in some foods and avoided in others.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.0] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a distinctive, pleasant flavour. Overheating makes it dark and bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.1] Caramel is used in caramel sauce, crème caramel, toffee and brûlée.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.2] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution) and the mixture cools, sugar crystals form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.3] Controlled crystallisation gives the texture of fudge, fondant and sweets, while unwanted large crystals make jam grainy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.4] Crystallisation is prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (which causes inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.5] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains of glucose units called dextrins, which are brown. The 2005 scheme names the brown compound pyrodextrin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.6] Dextrinisation gives toast and the crust of baked bread their brown colour and flavour. Browned flour used for a roux thickens less, because its starch has already been broken into shorter chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.7] Dextrinisation is a property of starch, not sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.b] Jam sets because of pectin, a substance in fruit cell walls. Making jam depends on getting the pectin out of the fruit and then giving it the right conditions to set.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.0] Pectin is extracted by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft. Acid, such as lemon juice, helps release the pectin from the cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.1] Fruit high in pectin includes cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit (especially the peel), gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants. Strawberries are low in pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.2] Unripe fruit contains more pectin than ripe fruit, so under-ripe apples are a good source of pectin for jam.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.3] A gel forms only when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions and the mixture is boiled and then cooled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.4] During cooling, the pectin molecules link into a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.5] Too little pectin, as in strawberries, gives a runny jam. Add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit to fix this.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.6] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.b] This simple test shows that starch does not thicken a cold liquid but does thicken it when heated.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.0] Aim: to show the effect of heat on starch mixed with a liquid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.1] Method: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water and observe that it does not dissolve and does not thicken. Then heat the mixture while stirring and observe that it thickens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.2] Variable: temperature is the variable that changes (cold, then heated).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.3] Control and comparison: the unheated cornflour and cold water mixture is compared with the heated one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.4] Result: cold, the starch settles as a cloudy suspension and the mixture stays thin; heated, the granules swell, burst and absorb the water, and the mixture thickens into a sol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.b] Heating sugar on its own, with no water, shows caramelisation happening step by step.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.0] Aim: to observe how granulated sugar changes when it is heated on its own, with no water added.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.1] Method: place a measured quantity of granulated sugar in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat, stirring gently as it melts, and note the colour and smell at regular intervals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.2] Variables: heating time is the independent variable; colour, smell and taste are the dependent variables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.3] Control: a sample of the same sugar left unheated, for comparison.

[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.b] These two tests show a change in starch caused by dry heat and a change in sucrose caused by acid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.0] Dextrinisation, aim: to show that dry heat changes starch. Method: toast white bread, or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.1] Dextrinisation, result: the browned starch is darker and thickens less than unbrowned starch, because its chains are shorter. Safety: the pan is hot.

CANDIDATES hom-cereals-grains-s3: 3. Milling and flour
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.b] Milling turns the whole wheat grain into flour by separating the bran, germ and endosperm and then grinding the endosperm finely. Each stage has a purpose, and an account of production should give the stages in order with the reason for each.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.0] 1. Intake and cleaning: the wheat is sampled, graded and may be blended to give the quality needed. It is then passed through sieves, air currents and magnets to remove stones, dust, chaff and metal, so that none of these end up in the flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.1] 2. Conditioning (tempering): a controlled amount of water is added and the grain is left to rest. This toughens the bran so it separates in large pieces rather than shattering into the flour, and it softens the endosperm (the starchy centre of the grain) ready for milling.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.2] 3. Break rolling: corrugated (grooved) rollers crack open the grain and release the endosperm, while the bran and germ stay in larger pieces because the bran is not crushed.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.3] 4. Sifting (plansifting): plansifters, which are stacks of sieves, separate the bran, germ and endosperm particles by size, so each stream can be dealt with separately.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.4] 5. Reduction rolling: smooth rollers grind the endosperm repeatedly, with a sieving step after each pass, until it becomes fine white flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.5] 6. Finishing: flour from different batches is blended for consistent quality, such as gluten strength, and may be treated with permitted improvers. It may be fortified (have extra nutrients added) and is tested for moisture, gluten strength and colour before packing.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.6] Extraction rate is the percentage of the original grain that ends up in the flour. White flour is about 75% and wholemeal flour is 100%, because it keeps all three parts of the grain. Wheatmeal falls between the two.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.7] Stone-ground flour is made by grinding the grain between millstones instead of rollers, and it is accepted as a type of flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.8] Diagram: a flow chart of the milling stages in order (intake and cleaning, conditioning, break rolling, sifting, reduction rolling, finishing), with the bran, germ and endosperm streams branching off at the sifting step.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.b] The difference between white and wholemeal flour comes down to how much of the grain is kept during milling. This changes the colour, nutrition, texture of baked foods and how long the flour keeps.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.0] White flour is made from the endosperm only, because the bran and germ are removed. It is lighter in colour and texture, and has about 75% extraction.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.1] White flour has less fibre and fewer B vitamins and minerals than wholemeal, because these are mostly lost with the bran and germ. It has a longer shelf life.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.2] Wholemeal flour is made from the whole grain, with 100% extraction, so it is brown and higher in fibre, B vitamins and minerals.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.3] Wholemeal flour makes denser baked products. It has a shorter shelf life because of the oil in the germ, which goes off over time.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.b] Flours differ in how much of the grain they contain, how much gluten-forming protein they have and whether anything has been added. This decides which job each one does best.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.0] Plain white (cream) flour has a medium gluten content. It is used for pastry, cakes (creamed or whisked), biscuits, sauces and roux, pizza, scones and batters.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.1] Self-raising flour is plain flour with a raising agent, usually baking powder, added, so it is not a different wheat. It is used for cakes, sponges, scones and biscuits.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.2] Strong flour is made from hard wheat and is high in gluten-forming protein. It is used for yeast breads, pizza dough and puff pastry because the gluten traps gas, such as the carbon dioxide produced by yeast, for a good rise.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.3] Wholemeal (wholewheat) flour is milled from the whole grain, so it contains bran, germ and endosperm. It is used for brown bread, scones, muffins and wholemeal pastry, and gives a denser texture because the bran cuts the gluten strands.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.4] Wheatmeal (brown) flour is partly refined, with part of the bran removed. It is used for brown bread and soda bread.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.5] Cornflour is a fine starch made from the endosperm of maize, not a wheat flour, so it does not contain gluten. It is used as a thickener for sauces, custards and gravy.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.6] Rice flour and other gluten-free flours (rice, maize and other blends) are used in baking for people with coeliac disease, who cannot eat gluten.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.b] Gluten is the protein network that gives bread and pastry their structure, and its development is controlled by the way flour is handled.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.0] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin in wheat flour are mixed with water.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.1] Gluten is elastic and stretchy: it traps gas and gives bread its structure.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.2] Kneading develops gluten, producing a strong, elastic dough, whereas rubbing in fat and mixing lightly limit gluten, giving tender pastry.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.3] Strong flour contains more gluten-forming protein than plain flour, so it gives a more elastic dough.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.b] Flour is the syllabus's example of a food that undergoes extensive processing. The grain is cleaned, separated, ground, blended, treated and packed, and the packaging and label then protect and inform the buyer.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.0] Flour is a processed food: milling turns the whole grain into a refined ingredient with a different nutrient profile.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.1] Packaging: flour is packed in strong, sealed, food-grade bags, paper or plastic, that protect it from moisture, insects, contamination and odours, and are easy to handle and store.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.2] Labelling: the label gives the name of the food (the type of flour), the ingredients list, the net quantity, the nutrition declaration, the best-before date, storage instructions and the name and address of the food business. It may also carry cooking or recipe guidance.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.3] Allergens: wheat and gluten must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold type, so people with allergies or coeliac disease can spot them. This is set by Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.4] Traceability: a batch (lot) code and date mark allow the manufacturer to trace the flour back through production, so it can be recalled if there is a problem.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.5] Fortified flour: any added nutrients are shown on the nutrition declaration.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s5: 5. Properties
[hom-proteins-s5.0.b] Cooking and food preparation change protein in two linked steps. First the folded protein chain unfolds (denaturation), then the unfolded chains join up and set (coagulation). Both are permanent, and together they explain most of what happens to eggs, meat, fish and milk.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.0] Denaturation is the irreversible unfolding of the secondary and tertiary structure of a protein (the folded shape of its chain), without breaking its peptide bonds (the links between amino acids), so the chain keeps its amino acids but changes shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.1] Denatured chains lose the original shape and functional properties of the protein, because the function of a protein depends on its shape.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.2] Denatured chains can then link to one another, and this linking is coagulation.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.3] Coagulation is the setting of denatured protein into a solid or semi-solid mass, which is why a runny raw egg becomes firm when cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.4] Denaturation changes the texture and appearance of a food, and denatured protein is more digestible, so cooked protein foods are easier to digest than raw ones.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.5] Denaturation can be caused by heat, mechanical action (such as whisking), chemicals (such as acid and salt) and enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.6] Egg white coagulates from about 60 degrees C, turning from transparent to opaque and firm; the yolk sets at about 68 degrees C. These are approximate temperatures because eggs set over a range.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.7] Fish flesh changes from translucent to opaque as its protein coagulates during cooking, which is how you can tell fish is cooked.
[hom-proteins-s5.0.8] Examples of coagulation in cookery: omelette, quiche, boiled egg, meringue, soufflé and lemon meringue pie.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.b] A foam is a mass of air bubbles held in a thin film. Egg white makes a good foam because its protein, albumen, can be stretched into a film around each bubble and then set by heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.0] Whisking or beating stretches and partly denatures the albumen chains, which unfold and arrange themselves around the air bubbles. This is mechanical action, not heat, that creates the foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.1] The protein film around each bubble holds the trapped air, forming a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.2] Gentle heat in cooking then coagulates the albumen around the bubbles, setting the foam permanently, which is why a baked meringue stays firm.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.3] Over-whisking denatures the protein too far and breaks the film, so the foam becomes grainy and collapses.
[hom-proteins-s5.1.4] Examples: meringue, pavlova, soufflé and whisked sponge cake.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.b] A gel is a liquid held in a soft solid. Some proteins, especially gelatine, can do this: they dissolve in hot liquid and set into a network as it cools.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.0] A gel forms when protein chains (gelatine) dissolved in a hot liquid cool and link into a network that traps the liquid, such as water.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.1] Gelatine is the main protein used to make gels in cookery.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.2] A firmer gel results from a higher concentration of gelatine, because more chains form a denser network.
[hom-proteins-s5.2.3] Examples: jelly, aspic, mousses and cheesecake.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.b] Dry heat cooks food without added liquid. It acts mainly on the surface protein, which is why roasted and grilled foods firm up and brown.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.0] Dry heat (roasting, baking, grilling and frying) denatures and coagulates the surface protein, causing shrinkage and firmness.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.1] Prolonged dry heat dries the muscle protein and makes meat tough and dry, so overcooked meat is chewy.
[hom-proteins-s5.3.2] Dry heat at high temperatures produces the Maillard reaction, which browns the surface of meat, bread and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.b] The golden-brown crust on bread and roast meat is not caused by sugar alone or by enzymes. It comes from protein and sugar reacting together under dry heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.0] The Maillard reaction is non-enzymic browning, which happens when the amino acids of protein react with a reducing sugar (a sugar such as glucose) on heating.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.1] It needs high heat and a dry surface with little moisture; it does not need enzymes.
[hom-proteins-s5.4.2] It produces brown colour and roasted, savoury flavour, as in a toasted bread crust, roast meat, grilled or toasted cheese and biscuits.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.b] Moist heat cooks food in liquid or steam at lower temperatures. For meat it does something dry heat cannot: it changes the tough connective tissue into a soft jelly.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.0] Moist heat (boiling, stewing, poaching and braising) converts the collagen (the tough protein in connective tissue) to gelatine, which tenderises meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.1] Collagen conversion needs long, slow cooking in moisture, so cheaper cuts of meat, which contain a lot of connective tissue, become tender.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.2] Prolonged high heat toughens muscle protein, so gentle moist heat is used for tender results.
[hom-proteins-s5.5.3] Heat breaks the weak bonds that hold the folded structure, which is why heat denatures protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.b] Whisking, beating and mincing physically stretch and break protein, so they change texture without any heat.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.0] Whisking, beating and mincing unfold protein chains; whisking egg white is the example that forms a foam.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.1] Kneading bread dough develops the gluten (the protein of wheat flour) into an elastic network that traps the carbon dioxide from yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.2] Beating meat with a mallet breaks up the muscle fibres and tenderises it.
[hom-proteins-s5.6.3] Excessive agitation denatures protein so far that it becomes tough or curdled.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.b] Acids and salt change protein without any heating. That is why a splash of lemon juice curdles milk and a marinade softens meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.0] Acids such as lemon juice and vinegar denature protein; lemon juice added to milk curdles it, which is also how milk sours.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.1] A marinade of acid, such as vinegar, wine or lemon, tenderises meat by denaturing its surface protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.2] Alkalis also denature protein and change its texture.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.3] Cheese-making uses acid to help set the milk protein, and salt to increase the firmness of the curd (the solid part of the milk), which controls the texture of the cheese.
[hom-proteins-s5.7.4] The acid in the stomach denatures food protein before enzymes hydrolyse (break) it, so the chains are easier for digestive enzymes to reach.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.b] Enzymes are biological catalysts, and some of them work on protein. Cooks use them to set milk for cheese and to soften tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.0] Rennin, traditionally from the stomach of young calves, coagulates casein (the main protein of milk) for cheese-making.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.1] Papain, from papaya, is a proteolytic enzyme (one that breaks down protein) that tenderises tough meat.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.2] Proteolytic enzymes hydrolyse peptide bonds, so too much enzyme action makes protein soft and mushy.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.3] Enzymes in the digestive tract hydrolyse protein to amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s5.8.4] Heat inactivates enzymes, which is why cooking stops enzymic tenderising.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.b] This investigation shows denaturation in practice by comparing egg white that is left alone, heated and whisked.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.0] Aim: to show how heat and mechanical action denature egg white protein.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.1] Independent variable: the treatment (heating in a water bath, or whisking); dependent variable: the change in appearance and texture of the white.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.2] Controlled variables: the same volume of egg white, the same starting temperature and the same type of dish.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.3] Control: place an untreated sample of egg white in a labelled dish for comparison.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.4] Method: heat a second sample gently in a water bath until it sets, then whisk a third sample until stiff.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.5] Safety: use fresh eggs, wash hands after handling raw egg, and do not taste raw egg white, because raw egg can carry bacteria.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.6] Expected result: the heated white turns opaque and firm, and the whisked white forms a stable foam; the untreated white stays clear and runny.
[hom-proteins-s5.9.7] Conclusion: heat and whisking both denature the protein of egg white and change its appearance and texture.
