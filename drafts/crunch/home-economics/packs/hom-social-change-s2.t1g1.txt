TARGET hom-social-change-s2: 2. Working hours, leisure time and conditions of work
[hom-social-change-s2.0.b] The standard working week has shortened over the last century, and regular overtime has become less common for many workers. This has changed how families divide their time, and the law now sets limits on how much an employee can be asked to work.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.0] Working hours fell as the standard working week was shortened and regular overtime (extra paid hours beyond the normal week) became less common for many workers.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.1] The Organisation of Working Time Act 1997 sets an average maximum working week of 48 hours, a minimum daily rest period, rest breaks during the working day, a weekly rest period, paid annual leave and public holiday entitlements.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.2] Because these limits are set by Irish law, an employee cannot lawfully be required to work beyond them, which protects workers' health and their time with their families.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.3] Part-time and flexible hours let some parents work around school hours and be at home earlier in the day. Other flexible arrangements include job-sharing, flexitime, term-time working, compressed hours, and remote and hybrid working.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.4] The Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 gives employees a right to request flexible and remote working, for example so they can care for a child or relative.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.5] Shift work and extended opening hours in retail and services mean that some family members work evenings or weekends, so families must plan time together.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.6] Shorter hours give families more time for shared meals, homework, household tasks and outings, and mean more time at home to care for children and for older relatives. They also bring a better work-life balance and less stress.
[hom-social-change-s2.0.7] Not every effect is positive: long commuting and irregular hours cut time together, and technology means work can follow people home, for example emails received out of hours.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.b] Shorter working hours and easier housework left families with more free time than earlier generations had. That free time changed what families do together, with benefits and some drawbacks.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.0] Paid annual leave gives employees a set number of holiday weeks each year, which made family holidays a regular feature of family life.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.1] Labour-saving appliances such as washing machines, dishwashers and microwaves, and convenience foods, cut the time spent on housework and cooking, releasing time for leisure.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.2] Weekends became the main block of free time for most workers and their families.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.3] Rising household incomes increased spending on leisure, including cinema, sport, holidays, travel and eating out.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.4] Home entertainment such as television, radio, computers, the internet, streaming and gaming changed how families spend evenings, often indoors.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.5] Community sport and clubs, such as GAA, soccer, swimming and dance, give children and parents structured leisure outside the home.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.6] Shared activities strengthen family relationships and communication, support children's physical, social and emotional development, and relaxation reduces stress.
[hom-social-change-s2.1.7] Leisure also has downsides: screen-based leisure can separate family members, and because leisure costs money, some families cannot afford it.
[hom-social-change-s2.2.b] As the economy became more productive, pay rose, and the law now protects the lowest earners. Higher pay changes what a family can afford.
[hom-social-change-s2.2.0] Real pay (pay after allowing for price rises) rose as productivity and living standards increased, giving families more disposable income to save, buy a home, and pay for education, holidays and leisure.
[hom-social-change-s2.2.1] The National Minimum Wage Act 2000 sets a legal minimum hourly rate of pay, protecting low-paid workers and their families.
[hom-social-change-s2.2.2] The Payment of Wages Act 1991 sets the rules on how wages must be paid and on what deductions an employer may make.
[hom-social-change-s2.2.3] Higher incomes let families move to larger or better housing, buy household goods and build savings, and financial security reduces stress and arguments in the home.
[hom-social-change-s2.2.4] Better pay and the cost of housing mean many families now need two incomes, which links to the growth in women's paid work.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.b] Employees can now take time off for children, illness and holidays without losing all of their income.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.0] Statutory paid annual leave means every employee is entitled to paid holidays, which is why family holidays became regular.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.1] Maternity leave and maternity benefit come under the Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004, so a mother can take time off for childbirth without losing all of her income.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.2] Paternity leave comes under the Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016 and lasts two weeks, so a father can be at home after the birth.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.3] Parental leave under the Parental Leave Act 1998 is unpaid time off to care for a child, while Parent's Leave and Benefit under the Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019 gives each parent leave with a benefit payment.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.4] Statutory sick leave under the Sick Leave Act 2022 lets employees take paid time off for illness: 3 days in 2023, 5 in 2024, 7 in 2025 and 10 days a year from 2026.
[hom-social-change-s2.3.5] Time off for children and illness lets parents care for their families without losing all their income.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.b] Alongside pay and leave, employees gained legal protection against unsafe workplaces and unfair treatment, and a way to bargain together.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.0] Employees are entitled to written terms of employment, so both sides know the job, pay and conditions.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.1] The Unfair Dismissals Acts protect employees from being dismissed without a fair reason, and redundancy payments compensate workers whose jobs are cut.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.2] The Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005 requires employers to provide a safe workplace and reduce the risk of workplace injury, and the Health and Safety Authority enforces it. Healthier, safer work lowers the risk of injury that would cut family income.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.3] Grievance and disciplinary procedures give employees a route to challenge poor treatment or unfair conditions, and complaints can be taken to the Workplace Relations Commission, which hears employment complaints.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.4] The Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act 1996 is a law that protects young workers.
[hom-social-change-s2.4.5] Trade unions negotiate pay and conditions on behalf of their members through collective agreements (agreements between unions and employers covering a group of workers). ICTU is the national body for Irish trade unions.
[hom-social-change-s2.5.b] Some of the biggest gains for families are not in the weekly pay packet but in the benefits that come with a job.
[hom-social-change-s2.5.0] Occupational pension schemes (pensions arranged through an employer) provide retirement income, reducing the need for adult children to support retired parents.
[hom-social-change-s2.5.1] The My Future Fund scheme began on 1 January 2026 and automatically enrols employees aged 23 to 60 who earn over EUR 20,000, so more workers build up a pension.
[hom-social-change-s2.5.2] In My Future Fund the employee, the employer and the State all contribute, which means the worker does not carry the whole cost.
[hom-social-change-s2.5.3] Other benefits of a job can include canteens, health insurance, bonuses, training and career breaks.
[hom-social-change-s2.6.b] Taken together, shorter hours, more leisure and better pay and conditions have changed family life, with benefits and strains.
[hom-social-change-s2.6.0] Higher incomes let families move to larger or better housing and buy household goods.
[hom-social-change-s2.6.1] Reduced hours and more leisure can strengthen family relationships through shared activities.
[hom-social-change-s2.6.2] Long hours or shift work can cause tiredness and strain, and reduce time spent with children.
[hom-social-change-s2.6.3] When both parents work full-time, there may be less time for family meals and supervision of children.
[hom-social-change-s2.7.b] Comparing family life a hundred years ago with today shows the changes in work directly. An interview with an older relative gives first-hand evidence.
[hom-social-change-s2.7.0] Interview an older relative about the hours worked each week, the holidays taken each year and the leisure activities they had as a young adult.
[hom-social-change-s2.7.1] Record the same three measures for a present-day family and note the differences in working hours, holidays and leisure.
[hom-social-change-s2.7.2] Compare the two answers to show how changes in work have altered who has time for leisure and when it is taken.

CANDIDATES hom-work-leisure-s2: 2. Changes in patterns of work
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.b] New technology changes how work is organised, how many workers are needed and which skills are in demand. Some effects are gains for workers and some are losses, and exam answers need both.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.0] Automation (machines and robots taking over repetitive production and processing tasks) means fewer workers are needed for the same output, because the machines do the routine work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.1] Increased output with fewer workers reduces working hours in some industries, which gives people more leisure time, but it also removes jobs at the lower-skilled end.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.2] Computers and electronic communication change office work. They create new skilled jobs to design, install, maintain and operate the technology, so jobs shift towards programming, supervising and maintaining.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.3] Electronic communication allows home working (teleworking), which suits some people. The downside is that it makes it harder to switch off from work, and it reduces face-to-face social contact at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.4] Technology also makes work safer and cleaner in some settings, because dangerous and dirty tasks are carried out by machines. This means fewer injuries and healthier workplaces.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.5] Workers whose skills become out of date, or who lose unskilled and manual jobs, need retraining. Companies can also relocate production to low-cost countries, which removes jobs at home.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.6] Technology has also changed office and service work: banking, shopping and booking online mean fewer counter and clerical jobs and more technical ones.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.b] Employment has moved from extracting and manufacturing towards services. To describe the shift you first need to know the three kinds of industry.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.0] Primary industry extracts resources from the land and sea: agriculture, fishing, forestry and mining or quarrying (such as peat).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.1] Secondary industry manufactures and processes primary raw materials into finished goods, for example food processing, clothing, engineering and construction.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.2] Tertiary (service) industry provides services such as tourism, leisure, healthcare, transport, media, retail, education, banking and finance, hospitality and IT. Note that tertiary means services, not third-level education.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.3] Automation and mechanisation reduced the number of manufacturing and farming jobs because machines do more of the work. Service industries expanded and absorbed many workers as incomes rose and demand for services grew.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.4] Companies relocated production to lower-cost countries, causing job losses in manufacturing in Ireland. Growth of tourism, finance and technology firms added service jobs.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.5] The effects on people were that manual and unskilled workers were displaced and rural areas lost jobs, while urban areas gained service jobs. Service work brought more part-time and shift work and a new demand for qualified workers.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.6] Setting: compare Ireland's current occupational structure with that of fifty years ago, noting the shift from farming, factory and other manual work to service and professional work. Describe the pattern without figures.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.b] As jobs have shifted towards services and technology, employers increasingly expect higher qualifications. Education has become the way into work and the way to move up within it.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.0] Service and technology jobs need literacy, numeracy, computing and communication skills, which are developed through education.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.1] Employers use qualifications to select between applicants, so a larger share of applicants now hold them. The Leaving Certificate is the minimum for many jobs, and many need a further education or higher qualification.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.2] Qualifications widen employment options and allow career advancement, because they show an employer what a worker can do.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.3] Early school leavers and unskilled workers find it difficult to acquire work, as many jobs require a minimum level of education. They are most at risk of unemployment and low pay.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.4] Continuing education, such as evening courses, distance learning and part-time courses, allows adults to gain qualifications, keep pace with technology, improve promotion prospects or change career.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.5] Upskilling programmes (courses that build on skills a worker already has) also let adults keep their skills up to date.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.6] Reskilling (training in new skills) allows workers whose jobs have disappeared to move into new occupations.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.b] Many more women, including married women and mothers, are now in paid work than in the middle of the twentieth century. The causes are social and economic, and each one needs a short reason.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.0] Women are better educated than in the past, and qualifications open access to professional and skilled work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.1] Equality legislation removed barriers: the Civil Service (Employment of Married Women) Act 1973 removed the marriage bar (the rule forcing married women to leave civil service jobs).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.2] The Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 first legislated for equal pay for equal work. Both Acts are now covered by the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.3] Income from paid work gives women financial independence, self-esteem, status and a stronger personal identity, as well as social contact outside the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.4] The growth of service industries and part-time work created many jobs that suited women's skills and flexible hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.5] Rising living, housing and child care costs, and the need for a second income, encourage women into paid employment. More marital breakdown and lone parenthood also make earning necessary.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.6] Flexible hours, job-sharing, term-time work, maternity and parental leave and career breaks make it possible to combine paid work with home and family responsibilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.7] More child care facilities, labour-saving appliances and smaller families free up women's time, and changing attitudes make work more accepted. The result is financial independence for women and more dual-earner families (both parents earning).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.b] Pay, safety and time off have all improved, which made paid work more attractive. These improvements also enabled women to take part in the workforce more fully.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.0] Better pay, including the introduction of the minimum wage, plus pension entitlement and paid holiday arrangements, make paid work more attractive.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.1] Statutory protections for workers (those set down in law) cover health and safety, pay and working time. Employment is protected by legislation, as are equal pay and equality law.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.2] Safer and cleaner workplaces, made possible by technology, reduce risk of injury and illness. Working hours are also shorter than in the past.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.3] Statutory leave includes maternity leave, parental leave and force majeure leave (urgent family reasons). Parental leave and career breaks let workers take time away for family reasons and return to their job.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.4] Facilities for working parents, such as crèches and breastfeeding breaks, make it easier to stay in work after having a child.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.5] Improved conditions have enabled many women to return to the workforce after raising children, because work no longer has to be given up for family life.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.b] Working time is now arranged in many ways other than the traditional full-time, fixed-hours week. These arrangements let workers fit paid work around family life.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.0] Flexitime: employees choose their start and finish times within agreed core hours, when everyone must be at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.1] Job-sharing: two people share one full-time post, dividing the hours, pay and duties between them.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.2] Part-time and term-time working: reduced hours, or work arranged around the school year, which suits parents of school-age children.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.3] Compressed or reduced working weeks, such as working longer days across fewer days, and teleworking: working from home or another remote location using electronic communication.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.4] Career breaks: an agreed period of unpaid leave, after which the worker returns to their job.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.5] Parental leave: under the Parental Leave Act 1998 (as amended), parents are entitled to unpaid leave to look after a young child, currently 26 weeks per child for a child under 12 (longer if the child has a disability or long-term illness).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.6] It is taken in one block or in agreed blocks, and it is not paid by the employer by right.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.7] Related leaves exist under other laws: maternity leave (Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004), paternity leave (Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016), parent's leave (Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019), carer's leave (Carer's Leave Act 2001) and force majeure leave.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.b] The law now gives employees a right to ask for flexible hours or to work remotely. The employer does not have to agree, but must handle the request fairly.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.0] The Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 provides these rights, and the flexible and remote working provisions commenced on 6 March 2024.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.1] Parents and carers of children up to 12 (or up to 16 if the child is disabled or long-term ill), and carers, can request flexible working. Any employee can request remote working.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.2] The employer must consider the request and reply in writing within set time limits, giving reasons for any refusal, so workers get a clear answer.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.3] The Act also provides paid domestic-violence leave (5 days, from November 2023) and unpaid medical care leave (from 3 July 2023).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.b] Taken together, the changes in technology, industry, education, women's work and working hours affect individuals and families in both helpful and difficult ways.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.0] Workers displaced from manufacturing may need retraining before they can find work in services, so the availability of work changes for individuals.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.1] Flexible working helps parents plan childcare and family life more smoothly. It means more time with children, less stress and an easier balance of work and family.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.2] Blurred boundaries between work and home can increase stress and make it harder to switch off.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.3] Flexible working can also mean lower income, slower promotion, a shortfall in pension and resentment from colleagues.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.4] Families in which both parents work gain income but may face role overload and role conflict, because family tasks still have to be done.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.5] Employers gain access to a wider, more skilled labour pool and can keep staff by offering flexible arrangements.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s5: 5. Social change
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.b] Societies never stand still. Social change is a significant alteration over time in the structures, institutions, relationships, values or behaviour of a society, in other words in the way a society is organised.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.0] In exam terms, social change is change in society caused by law reform, technology or major events. The internet and divorce legislation are good examples.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.1] Change can be gradual, such as the slow shift in attitudes to the role of women or to marriage over several decades, or sudden, such as a new law, a war or a pandemic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.2] Change can be planned, such as a law or a policy, or unplanned. It happens in every society, but the speed differs from one society to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.3] Cultural lag is the gap in time between the appearance of a new material invention and the adjustment of norms, values and institutions to it, because people and organisations take time to catch up. The concept was developed by the sociologist William Ogburn.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.b] Many of the biggest changes come from shifts in how people think and live. These social causes feed one another: more education leads to new attitudes, and new attitudes lead to new family patterns.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.0] Changing attitudes and values: there is more acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce, lone parenthood, same-sex relationships and diversity, less stigma attached to children born outside marriage, and the Church has less influence than before.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.1] Greater equality for women through better education, careers, financial independence and legal equality changes gender roles at home. The marriage bar, the rule forcing women to leave their jobs on marrying, ended in the civil service in 1973.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.2] Urbanisation is the movement of people from rural areas to towns and cities. It weakens close kin ties and extended family contact and brings new lifestyles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.3] Towns have better services and facilities, but also more pressure, traffic and anti-social behaviour. Rural depopulation means rural areas can lose services and older people can become isolated.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.4] Education: more people reach second and third level, which changes attitudes, expectations and the value placed on qualifications. Wider access also links to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.5] Demographic change means changes in the make-up of the population: falling birth rates, smaller families, later marriage and parenthood, longer life expectancy and an ageing population. These alter the structure of households and the demand for services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.6] Migration, the movement of people into and out of Ireland, brings new cultures, languages and religions. Cultural diffusion, the spread of ideas, fashions and practices between societies through travel, migration and the media, has a similar effect.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.7] Globalisation, the growth of economic and cultural links between countries, means events and ideas in one country affect others. Social movements and pressure groups also campaign for change, for example for equality, and gain support for their aims.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.b] Law reform both reflects change in attitudes and drives it, because a new law changes what people may do and what they come to expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.0] Divorce: the referendum was held in November 1995 and passed narrowly. The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 then set up the legal process. Say referendum 1995, Act 1996, not that divorce was available in 1995.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.1] Same-sex marriage was approved by referendum in 2015 and given effect by the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.2] The Civil Partnership Act 2010 provided civil partnership for same-sex couples. This was the earlier arrangement, because since 2015 same-sex couples can marry.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.3] Other legal changes that alter family life include maternity and paternity leave and equality legislation, which support parents at work and promote equal treatment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.b] How a society earns its living shapes family life, because work decides who is at home, how much money the household has and where people live.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.0] Industrialisation moved many workers from farms and rural trades into factories and towns. Service industries have since grown, and work patterns have changed to include flexible hours, job sharing and working from home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.1] Growth of female employment has produced dual-earner households. These have more income but more need for childcare, and can bring role overload and role conflict, which are effects, not causes.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.2] Unemployment, recession, emigration and returning emigrants change family life by splitting relatives apart and bringing them back. High housing costs delay home ownership, marriage and parenthood, while higher standards of living improve comfort.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.3] State welfare: improved social welfare, such as child benefit and pensions, reduces dependence on the family and eases financial strain.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.b] New technology changes daily life faster than norms and institutions can adjust, which is cultural lag in action. Its effects can be positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.0] Modern communications technology, including mobile phones, the internet and social media, changes how families communicate, how children learn and how people work, and it changes how people spend leisure time. Examples are children skilled in IT, video calls and apps.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.1] Positive effects include access to information and contact with distant relatives. Negative effects include addiction, cyber-bullying and less face-to-face time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.2] Labour-saving appliances free time and make shared housework easier, which supports more equal roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.3] Contraception and medical advances affect family size and life expectancy, because families can plan the number of children and people live longer.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.4] Transport shortens distances, so relatives and workers can live further apart and still keep in touch.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.b] Social change affects the family, institutions and everyday life, and its effects can be both positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.0] Changing gender roles: more women take part in paid employment, and household tasks are more often shared between partners, giving more equal roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.1] Smaller families: many couples have fewer children or have them later in life, and fewer marriages, and later ones, are taking place.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.2] Changing family structures: lone-parent, blended and cohabiting families have become more common, so family structures are more diverse, and the extended family has declined.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.3] An ageing population increases demand for health and care services and for support for older people, and the roles of older people change. More families use childcare.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.4] Changes in values, such as greater acceptance of diversity, change the norms people expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.5] Effects can be positive, such as more choice and freedom, or negative, such as stress and strain, role overload, loss of community and social problems. Rapid change can leave some groups behind, for example people whose skills are no longer needed in changing industries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.6] Change affects institutions: the Church loses influence, the State takes on more services, and schools adapt to diversity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.7] Social change can cause tension and conflict between groups that hold different values, for example older and younger generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.b] Not everyone welcomes change, and several forces can slow it down or stop it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.0] Vested interests are groups who benefit from the existing system, so they may oppose changes that would reduce their advantages.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.1] Tradition and religious belief can resist changes in norms and values that conflict with long-held teachings.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.2] Fear of the unknown and uncertainty about the consequences of change make people reluctant to accept it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.3] The cost of change, in money, time and disruption, can slow or prevent it, and generations may disagree about it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.b] Ireland has seen rapid change in recent decades, and each example below links a cause to an effect on family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.0] Divorce: Irish voters approved divorce by referendum in 1995, and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 provided for it. Marriage equality followed, with a referendum in 2015 and the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.1] Women in employment: the marriage bar in the civil service ended in 1973, and more married women have been in paid work since, which changed roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.2] Migration: the Celtic Tiger, a period of rapid economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s, brought increased immigration and with it new languages, cultures and religions.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.3] Emigration in earlier decades and in the 2008 recession years, and return migration, moved relatives apart and then back together, affecting families and communities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.4] Rural depopulation and urban growth shifted people from the countryside to towns, and the influence of the Church has fallen.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.b] Exam questions on social change usually ask for the impact on family life, and marks are given for each cause covered.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.0] Structure a discuss answer by cause (social, economic, technological) and then effect, with one Irish example for each point.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.1] Link each cause to a clear effect, for example urbanisation weakening extended family contact, or dual incomes increasing the need for childcare.
