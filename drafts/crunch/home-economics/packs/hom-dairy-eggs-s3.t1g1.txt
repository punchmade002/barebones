TARGET hom-dairy-eggs-s3: 3. Cream, butter and yoghurt
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.b] Cream is the fat-rich layer separated from milk. Because nearly all the fat of the milk ends up in it, its nutrition is very different from that of milk, and its types differ mainly in how much fat they hold.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.0] Cream is high in energy and contributes little protein or carbohydrate, because most of the protein and lactose (milk sugar) stay behind in the skimmed milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.1] Its fat is mostly saturated and contains cholesterol, so cream is not suited to low-fat, low-cholesterol or slimming diets and is best used in small amounts.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.2] The fat-soluble vitamins A and D are carried in the cream fat, because these vitamins dissolve in fat rather than water.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.3] Cream contains no vitamin C and no fibre.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.4] Double cream has the highest fat content (about 48%), whips to stiff peaks, pours thick and can be piped, so it suits fillings, toppings and rich sauces.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.5] Single cream has less fat (about 18%), does not whip, and is used to pour over desserts, in coffee and in sauces.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.6] Whipping cream (about 35 to 38% fat) is whipped to a foam by beating air into it, because the fat forms a network that traps the air.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.0.7] Sour cream is cultured with lactic acid bacteria, giving a tangy flavour used in dips, toppings and baked potatoes. Crème fraîche is another cultured cream.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.1.b] Cream has to be separated from the milk, made to a set fat level and heat treated so that it is safe and keeps. Take care not to mix up separating cream with churning butter.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.1.0] Cream is separated from milk by centrifugal separation (a spinning machine called a centrifuge), which spins the milk so the lighter fat rises and is drawn off. This separates cream from skimmed milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.1.1] The fat content of each cream is standardised (adjusted) to the level named on the label, so that single, whipping and double cream are always the same.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.1.2] The separated cream is then pasteurised or UHT treated, as for milk. UHT (long-life) cream keeps unopened without refrigeration.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.1.3] After heat treatment the cream is cooled, packaged and labelled.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.2.b] Cream is a perishable, high-fat food that absorbs smells easily, so choosing and storing it carefully protects its safety and flavour.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.2.0] Select cream with the fat content stated on the label, which shows whether it is single, double or whipping cream and so whether it will whip or pour.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.2.1] Check the use-by date and that the carton is intact and sealed.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.2.2] Refrigerate cream promptly and keep it in its sealed container, away from strong-smelling foods, which it would otherwise absorb.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.2.3] Once opened, use cream within the period stated on the label.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.2.4] Whipped cream does not keep and should be used soon after whipping.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.b] Butter is made from cream by agitating it until the fat separates out. Because it is almost all fat, its nutritional profile follows the cream's.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.0] Butter is made by churning (agitating) cream, which breaks down the membranes around the fat globules so the fat clumps together into butter grains.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.1] The liquid left after churning is buttermilk. The butter is then washed, salted if wanted, worked (kneaded) to remove excess water, then shaped, wrapped and labelled.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.2] Butter is about 80% fat (the EU minimum for the name butter) with a small amount of water, and its fat is mostly saturated.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.3] Butter is high in cholesterol and supplies vitamins A and D from its fat. It has no vitamin C or fibre and negligible protein.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.4] Salted butter has salt added for flavour and preservation, so unsalted butter suits those limiting salt.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.5] Spreadable butter is blended with a vegetable oil to soften it. Reduced-fat spreads contain less fat because they are blended with other fats and water, and are chosen to reduce saturated fat.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.3.6] A product with much less than 80% fat cannot legally be sold as butter, which is why lower-fat products are called reduced-fat spreads or dairy spreads.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.b] Butter is used for its flavour and for the way its fat behaves in cooking. It also picks up other smells easily, which shapes how it is stored.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.0] Spreading on bread and toast.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.1] Shortcrust and other pastry, where the fat is rubbed into the flour to make a crumbly (shortened) texture. Scones are made the same way.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.2] Creaming with sugar in cake making, where air is beaten into the fat to aerate the mixture.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.3] Frying and making sauces, such as white sauce or melted butter sauces, where it adds flavour. Butter burns at a lower heat than oil.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.4] Butter is refrigerated, kept covered, and stored away from strong-smelling foods to prevent it absorbing odours.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.4.5] Buy butter from a clean shop in intact packs, and use it before the use-by date. It can be frozen for long storage.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.b] Yoghurt is made from milk and so shares milk's nutrients, with the lactic acid making it easier to digest for many people.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.0] Yoghurt supplies HBV protein (high biological value, with all the essential amino acids) for growth and repair.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.1] Yoghurt supplies lactose as an energy source.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.2] Yoghurt is rich in calcium and phosphorus for bones and teeth.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.3] Yoghurt supplies vitamins A, D and B group vitamins.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.4] Yoghurt is about 87% water, with traces of minerals such as potassium.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.5] Full-fat yoghurt contains saturated fat, and yoghurt lacks vitamin C and is low in iron.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.6] Dietetic value: yoghurt suits all ages, is versatile, can be fortified, is inexpensive and is a convenient snack.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.5.7] Yoghurt is a cream substitute for those limiting cream's fat, and should be balanced with starch, fibre and iron foods, because it provides little of these.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.b] Yoghurt is milk fermented by lactic acid bacteria: the bacteria change lactose to lactic acid, which gives the sharp taste, sets the milk and preserves it. The stages must be learned in order, with their temperatures.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.0] 1. Milk is homogenised.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.1] 2. Milk is pasteurised at 80 to 90°C.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.2] 3. Milk is cooled to about 37°C, the temperature at which the culture can work.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.3] 4. A lactic culture such as Lactobacillus bulgaricus (usually with Streptococcus thermophilus) is added.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.4] 5. The mixture is incubated for 6 to 8 hours. Lactose changes to lactic acid, and the milk proteins coagulate (set) and thicken to form yoghurt.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.5] 6. The yoghurt is cooled to about 5°C to halt fermentation.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.6] 7. Sweeteners, fruit, nuts, colours, flavours or stabilisers may be added.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.6.7] 8. The yoghurt is weighed, packaged (in sealed plastic pots or cartons) and labelled.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.7.b] Yoghurt is sold in sealed packs with a legal label, and it comes in several types depending on how it is set and what is added.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.7.0] Yoghurt is packaged in sealed plastic pots with foil or plastic lids, or in cartons.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.7.1] The label gives the name and type, brand, ingredients, quantity, nutrition information, allergens (milk, and any nuts), use-by date and storage (refrigerate).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.7.2] The legal basis is Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.7.3] Set yoghurt is incubated in the pot and is firm. Stirred or drinking yoghurt is set in a vat, then stirred and poured into pots.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.7.4] Greek-style yoghurt is strained and so thicker. Plain (natural) yoghurt has no sugar added.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.8.b] Bio-yoghurt has an extra live culture added for the consumer's benefit. How and when it is added matters, because heat would kill it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.8.0] Bio-yoghurt contains an added live culture such as Lactobacillus acidophilus, and bifidobacteria.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.8.1] Acidophilus bacteria aid digestion, and help balance the bacteria in the gut.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.8.2] Probiotic yoghurts contain live bacteria intended to benefit the gut. They must be refrigerated to stay alive.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.8.3] The culture is added after pasteurisation, because heat would destroy it.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.b] Yoghurt is perishable and used in many ways, so choose and store it well and know a range of uses.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.0] Buy from a clean shop, and check the use-by date and that the lid is sealed and the pot undamaged.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.1] Check the ingredients list for sugar, fruit and possible allergens, and the origin of the product. Check the fat and sugar content on the label.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.2] Refrigerate yoghurt promptly at about 4°C, rotate stock so older packs are used first, and reseal with the original lid. Use it within the time on the label.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.3] Uses: eaten alone or as a drink, in desserts, salads and dressings, and smoothies.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.4] Uses: as a garnish or topping, on cereal, as a dip (such as tzatziki or raita), and in curries, marinades and baking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.9.5] Yoghurt may replace cream or mayonnaise in cooking as a lower-fat option, but it curdles if boiled.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.10.b] Consumers want healthier, more convenient and more specialised dairy foods, so dairy producers change their products and packs. Each product change below answers a reason such as health awareness, convenience or intolerance.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.10.0] Low-fat and skimmed products, including low-fat cheese and reduced-fat spreads, meet concern about obesity and heart disease.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.10.1] Fortified products, such as milk with added vitamins, calcium or omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s3.10.2] Functional yoghurts (foods with an added health benefit) with added probiotic cultures or plant stanol or sterol products.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s4: 4. Uses of micro-organisms in food production
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.b] Not all micro-organisms spoil food. Some are added on purpose, because their feeding changes the food in useful ways, and this process is called fermentation.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.0] Fermentation is the breakdown of organic substances, such as sugars, by bacteria or yeast, usually in the absence or shortage of oxygen. It is used to make many foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.1] The by-products of fermentation include carbon dioxide, energy, acids (such as lactic acid and acetic acid) and alcohol, which is why fermented foods taste and behave differently from the raw ingredients.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.2] In lactic acid fermentation, bacteria turn sugars such as lactose (the sugar in milk) into lactic acid. This is the basis of yoghurt and cheese.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.3] In alcoholic fermentation, yeast turns sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. This is the basis of bread, beer and wine.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.b] Milk contains the sugar lactose, which lactic acid bacteria feed on. Their acid changes the milk's flavour and texture, which is how milk becomes cheese or yoghurt.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.0] Starter cultures are lactic acid bacteria added to milk to begin fermentation. They ferment the lactose in milk into lactic acid, giving cheese and yoghurt their sour flavour and texture.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.1] In cheese-making, the lactic acid works together with rennet to curdle the milk and set the curd, so acid on its own is not enough. Ripening by further bacteria or moulds then develops the flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.2] Yoghurt is made by fermenting milk, typically with Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus. The lactose becomes lactic acid, so the milk thickens and tastes sour. Some yoghurts add other strains.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.3] Probiotic (functional) yoghurts and drinks contain live bacteria that may benefit the intestinal flora (the helpful bacteria living in the gut) when eaten, so they are examples of functional foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.b] Moulds are fungi that grow as fine threads. In a few foods a particular mould is added deliberately, and each gives the food its own flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.0] Blue-veined cheese: the mould Penicillium roqueforti grows through the cheese, producing blue-green veins and a sharp flavour. Cashel Blue, made in Co. Tipperary, is an Irish example; Roquefort and Stilton are others.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.1] Surface-ripened soft cheese: Penicillium camemberti grows on the outside and gives the white rind of Camembert and Brie.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.2] Soy sauce is produced by the mould Aspergillus oryzae, which breaks down the protein and starch in soya beans by fermentation, giving the sauce its savoury flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.b] Yeast is a single-celled fungus, not a bacterium. It ferments sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and different foods make use of different parts of that result.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.0] In bread-making (including sourdough), yeast ferments the sugars in the dough and produces carbon dioxide, which makes the dough rise. This is why yeast is called a biological raising agent.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.1] The alcohol produced in the dough evaporates during baking, so bread is not alcoholic.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.2] In brewing, yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (barley that has been germinated) to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide, giving beer.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.3] In wine-making, yeast ferments the sugars in grape juice into alcohol. Spirits are distilled from fermented liquids.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.4] Vinegar is made by acetic acid bacteria, such as Acetobacter, which oxidise (add oxygen to) alcohol to form acetic acid.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.b] Micro-organisms are also used in meat products and as foods in their own right. Some provide protein or vitamins rather than flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.0] Salami, pepperoni and chorizo are made by adding bacterial starter cultures to cured meat. The bacteria ferment its sugars to lactic acid, which flavours the product and helps to preserve it.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.1] Quorn is a meat substitute made from mycoprotein, which comes from the fungus Fusarium venenatum grown by fermentation in large fermenters. It is high in protein and fibre and low in saturated fat.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.2] Mycoprotein is a novel protein food, meaning a new kind of food produced by fermentation rather than from animals. It is from a fungus, not from yeast, bacteria or plants.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.3] Mushrooms and truffles are large fungi that are eaten directly as food.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.4] Yeast extracts and yeast-based food supplements are used as sources of B vitamins, and bacteria are also used in food supplements.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.b] Using micro-organisms on purpose gives foods qualities that the raw ingredients do not have.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.0] Fermentation adds flavour and changes texture, for example the sourness of yoghurt, the veins of blue cheese and the rise of bread dough.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.1] Fermented foods can have a longer keeping time, because acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid are produced, for example in salami and vinegar.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.2] Micro-organisms improve nutritional value, since yeast extracts supply B vitamins and mycoprotein supplies protein.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.3] They add variety to the diet, from cheeses, yoghurts and breads to meat substitutes such as Quorn.

CANDIDATES hom-dairy-eggs-s2: 2. Milk processing
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.0.b] Raw milk is a perfect home for bacteria, so it spoils within days and can carry disease-causing bacteria. Manufacturers heat-treat and package it so that it is safe and keeps longer.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.0.0] Raw milk contains water, lactose (milk sugar) and protein at a neutral pH, which is exactly what bacteria need to grow, so it spoils quickly. It can also carry pathogenic (disease-causing) bacteria such as tuberculosis bacteria, Salmonella, Listeria and E. coli.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.0.1] Heat treatment destroys pathogenic bacteria and most spoilage bacteria, which makes milk safe to drink and extends its shelf life.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.0.2] Refrigeration slows bacterial growth but does not destroy bacteria, because bacteria grow fastest in warm, moist conditions and only slow down in the cold. This is why heat treatment is needed as well.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.0.3] Every heat treatment causes some loss of heat-sensitive vitamins (thiamine B1, B12, folate and vitamin C). The more severe the heat, the greater the vitamin loss and the more the flavour changes, so the process chosen balances safety, shelf life and nutrient loss.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.b] Pasteurisation is the gentlest of the heat treatments. It makes milk safe to drink while leaving its taste, colour and most nutrients almost unchanged, but it does not make the milk sterile.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.0] Milk is heated to 72 to 75°C for 15 to 25 seconds, usually in a heat exchanger (a set of plates) as a continuous flow.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.1] It is then cooled rapidly to below 10°C and packaged. Rapid cooling stops any surviving bacteria from multiplying.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.2] Effect on safety: pathogenic bacteria and most spoilage bacteria are destroyed, but not all, and not bacterial spores (tough resting forms). The milk is therefore not sterile.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.3] Nutritive effect: a small amount of thiamine (B1) and vitamin C is lost, while taste, colour and the other nutrients are little changed.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.4] Because some bacteria survive, pasteurised milk keeps for only a few days. It must be kept refrigerated, and once opened it should be used within a few days. Its shelf life is short compared with UHT milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.1.5] Pasteurisation is also needed for milk that will be made into cream, yoghurt and cheese.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.2.b] Sterilisation uses stronger heat for longer than pasteurisation, and the heating is done inside the sealed container. The result is milk that keeps for months but tastes different.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.2.0] The milk is homogenised, filled into bottles, sealed and then heated in the sealed bottle to about 110 to 120°C for about 20 to 30 minutes (approximate figures), then cooled. This is a higher temperature for longer than in pasteurisation.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.2.1] Effect on safety: all bacteria and their spores are destroyed, so the milk is sterile and safer, and keeps for several months unopened without refrigeration. Once opened it must be refrigerated.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.2.2] Nutritive effect: more vitamin is lost than in pasteurisation, including thiamine, vitamin C and some other B vitamins, because the heating is long.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.2.3] Flavour is altered by the stronger heat, giving a creamy colour and a cooked, caramelised taste.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.b] UHT heats milk to a very high temperature for only a moment. Because the heating is so short, the milk is made sterile with less damage to flavour and vitamins than in-bottle sterilisation.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.0] Milk is heated to approximately 132°C for 1 to 3 seconds, by steam injection or in a heat exchanger, then cooled rapidly.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.1] It is then packed aseptically, which means into sterile, airtight cartons in sterile conditions, so that no bacteria can get back in.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.2] Effect: all bacteria are destroyed, giving commercially sterile milk (pathogenic and spoilage micro-organisms are both destroyed).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.3] Unopened UHT milk keeps for several months without refrigeration. Once opened it must be refrigerated and used within a few days.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.4] Nutritive effect: some loss of heat-sensitive B group vitamins and vitamin C. The loss is less than in in-bottle sterilisation because the heating time is so short.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.5] There may be a slight cooked flavour, which is the main disadvantage.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.3.6] Advantages: long unopened shelf life, no refrigeration needed in the shop or the cupboard, and convenience for travel, holidays and emergencies.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.4.b] Fat in raw milk floats to the top as cream. Homogenisation changes the texture and appearance of the milk, but it does not make the milk safe.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.4.0] Milk is forced at high pressure through a very small opening, which breaks the fat globules into much smaller particles that are distributed evenly through the milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.4.1] Even distribution stops the cream rising to the surface, so there is no cream line and the milk stays uniform.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.4.2] The milk looks whiter and is creamier and smoother in texture.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.4.3] Smaller fat globules are more easily digested.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.4.4] Homogenisation is not a heat treatment and does not separate the fat, so it does not destroy bacteria. In production it is usually carried out before pasteurisation or UHT treatment.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.b] Milk goes through a series of stages between the farm and the shop. In order, they make up the production profile of a processed food that has had its shelf life extended.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.0] 1. Milk is collected from farms in refrigerated tankers, then tested for quality and bacterial content on arrival, and cooled and stored.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.1] 2. Milk is separated where cream is required, and skimmed milk is produced.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.2] 3. The fat content is standardised (adjusted to a set level) to make whole, semi-skimmed or skimmed milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.3] 4. Vitamins or minerals are added if a fortified milk is being made.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.4] 5. Milk is homogenised and then heat-treated by pasteurisation, sterilisation or UHT, then cooled.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.5] 6. Milk is filled and packaged under hygienic conditions, so that it is not contaminated after heat treatment.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.6] 7. Milk is labelled, stored and distributed, with pasteurised milk kept chilled throughout.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.5.7] Diagram: draw a flow chart of milk from farm to shop, labelling collection, testing, separation and standardising, homogenisation, heat treatment, packaging, labelling and distribution.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.b] Packaging keeps heat-treated milk safe after processing, and the label gives the buyer the information they need and the law requires.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.0] Packaging includes glass bottles, plastic bottles, waxed or plastic-coated cardboard cartons, aseptic cartons for UHT milk, and metal cans for evaporated and condensed milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.1] Packaging protects milk from light and contamination, because light destroys riboflavin and vitamin A. It also holds the stated quantity, is hygienic and convenient, and is increasingly recyclable.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.2] Label: the name of the food and type of milk (whole, semi-skimmed, skimmed or fortified), the brand, the name and address of the producer, and the quantity.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.3] Label: nutritional information, including energy, protein, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars and salt.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.4] Label: a date stamp and storage instructions, and allergen information (milk). Pasteurised milk carries a use-by date, whereas unopened UHT milk carries a best-before date.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.6.5] Labels must comply with Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on food information to consumers, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.b] Milk is the syllabus's example of a food processed to extend its shelf life. Besides heat-treated liquid milk, other processed milks keep for months and are useful where fresh milk is not available.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.0] Evaporated milk is concentrated by removing water by heating. It is canned and sterilised, unsweetened, keeps for months and is used in cooking and drinks.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.1] Condensed milk is concentrated by removing water and is usually sweetened with added sugar. The high sugar concentration helps to preserve it, and it is thick and sweet, used in sweet dishes and confectionery.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.2] Dried milk is made into powder by removing all the water, by spray drying (milk sprayed as droplets into hot air) or roller drying (a film of milk dried on heated rollers), giving a long shelf life.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.3] Dried milk is reconstituted (mixed back up) with water and used in baking, infant formula and where fresh milk is not available.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.4] Processed milks have the convenience of long storage and can be used where fresh milk is not available.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s2.7.5] Pasteurisation, sterilisation, UHT, evaporating, condensing and drying all count as processes that extend shelf life. If one is chosen for an answer, give the method, the effect and the labelling.

CANDIDATES hom-dairy-eggs-s1: 1. Milk: nutritional significance and contribution to the diet
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.b] Milk is a complete food for young mammals, meaning it supports a growing baby animal, and a useful source of most nutrients for people. It is widely available, so it is a staple of the Irish diet.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.0] Water makes up about 87% of milk, so a glass of milk contributes to hydration as well as supplying nutrients.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.1] Protein makes up about 3.5% of whole milk. It is mainly casein, with whey proteins making up the rest, and it is high biological value (HBV) protein, which means it contains all the essential amino acids (the ones the body cannot make).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.2] Protein from milk is used for growth and repair, and for making hormones and enzymes.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.3] Fat in whole milk is about 3.5 to 4%, mostly saturated (fat that is solid at room temperature and raises cholesterol). It is held in small globules, so it is easily digested, and cholesterol is present.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.4] The fat carries the fat-soluble vitamins A and D, so these are highest in whole milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.5] Lactose is the milk sugar, a disaccharide (a double sugar) at about 4 to 5%. It is the only significant carbohydrate in milk, it supplies energy, and it aids the absorption of calcium. Milk contains almost no starch.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.6] Minerals: calcium and phosphorus are present in useful amounts for bones and teeth, with traces of magnesium, potassium and sodium. Milk is low in iron.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.7] Vitamins: A and D, and the B group vitamins, including riboflavin (B2) and B12. Skimmed milk keeps the B vitamins but loses A and D with the fat.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.8] Milk supplies energy from lactose and fat, so the more fat a milk contains, the more energy it gives.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.b] Milk is a good source of protein and calcium, but it is not a complete diet for an adult. Knowing what it lacks explains why it must be balanced with other foods.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.0] Milk contains no vitamin C, so it cannot be the only source of this vitamin.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.1] Milk contains no fibre, so it does not help bowel movement the way cereals, fruit and vegetables do.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.2] Milk contains very little starch, so its carbohydrate is almost all lactose.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.3] Milk is low in iron, so it should not be relied on to prevent iron deficiency.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.b] Nutritive value means the nutrients a food contains and what each one does in the body. A good answer names the nutrient, its function and the milk fact.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.0] Protein (HBV) is for growth and repair, and fat and lactose give energy.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.1] Calcium and phosphorus build and maintain bones and teeth, and vitamins A, D and the B group support the body's functioning, while water gives hydration.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.2] Milk lacks vitamin C, fibre and iron, so a full answer also states what it does not provide.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.b] Dietetic value means how milk suits different people and different diets. Each point is strongest when it is given with its reason.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.0] Protein supports growth and repair in children, adolescents and pregnant women, who need extra for their body's needs.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.1] Calcium and phosphorus support bone and tooth development and maintenance, and are needed by older people and nursing mothers.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.2] Lactose and fat give energy for an active lifestyle.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.3] Milk is easily digested, so it suits invalid and elderly diets, and convalescents.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.4] Low-fat milks suit low-cholesterol and weight-watching diets, because less fat means less saturated fat and energy.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.5] Milk is versatile, used in drinks, cereals, sauces, soups, puddings and baking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.6] Milk is inexpensive, widely available and quick to use with no preparation, and nothing is wasted.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.7] Milk can be fortified (have nutrients added) to add nutrients that are otherwise low.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.b] Milk has real strengths, but there are situations where it is not suitable. State these limits as well as the benefits.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.0] Milk is not suitable as the only food, because it has no vitamin C or fibre and little iron.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.1] Whole milk is high in saturated fat, so it is not ideal for people cutting cholesterol or calories.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.2] Lactose intolerance means a person lacks the enzyme lactase and cannot digest lactose, the sugar in milk. Lactose-free milk exists for them.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.3] Milk allergy is a different condition, a reaction to the milk protein.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.5.b] Irish healthy eating guidance treats milk and other dairy foods as a food group that should be eaten every day.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.5.0] The Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and the Irish Food Pyramid place milk in the dairy food group (the milk, yoghurt and cheese shelf), which should be included every day.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.5.1] Three servings a day are recommended for most adults and five a day for 9 to 18 year olds, because of the calcium and protein needed for growth and bone development.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.5.2] Lower-fat choices are advised, and cheese in small amounts.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.5.3] Three servings of milk, cheese and yoghurt are recommended because of the HBV protein, calcium, vitamins A, D and some B, versatility and low cost.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.b] Questions on a teenager's diet ask for several different, practical ways to include dairy, each with a nutritional reason.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.0] Milk can be used on cereal or porridge, in smoothies and milky drinks, in soups, sauces and batters, and in puddings and baking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.1] Yoghurt can be eaten as a snack, on cereal, in smoothies, or used as a dip or dressing.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.2] Cheese can go in sandwiches, salads, pasta, pizza and soups, or on potatoes.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.3] Cream can be used in soups, mash, sauces and desserts.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.b] Young children are growing quickly, so milk matters more to them than to most adults. The type of milk and what it is eaten with both matter.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.0] Protein is for growth and repair, calcium and phosphorus with vitamin D build developing bones and teeth, and B vitamins and water are also supplied.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.1] Young children need the energy and fat-soluble vitamins A and D that whole milk supplies.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.2] Reduced-fat and skimmed milks have less energy and fewer fat-soluble vitamins, so they are less suitable as a young child's main milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.3] Safefood advises full-fat milk for ages 1 to 2, and skimmed milk is not suitable as a main drink under 5.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.4] The HSE advises that cow's milk is not given as the main drink to babies under 12 months, only from 12 months as whole pasteurised milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.5] Milk is low in iron and has no vitamin C, so iron-rich foods such as meat, fish, eggs or fortified cereals should be given alongside it, with foods supplying vitamin C and fibre. Too much milk can crowd out other foods.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.6] Milk can be used in young children's diets in cereals, sauces, soups and puddings to add protein and calcium, and it can be fortified.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.b] Milk is sold in several types that differ mainly in how much fat they contain, and so in energy and fat-soluble vitamins. Fortified milk differs by what has been added.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.0] Whole milk is full fat, about 3.5 to 4% fat, with the most energy and vitamin A of the common types, and is the most popular type.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.1] Semi-skimmed or low-fat milk is a reduced-fat milk, with part of the fat removed. Compared with whole milk it has less energy, fat, saturated fat and vitamin A.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.2] Semi-skimmed milk has slightly more protein, carbohydrate and calcium per 100 ml, so it is the milk type higher in calcium, protein and carbohydrate than whole milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.3] Skimmed milk has 0.1 to 0.4% fat. Most of the fat is removed along with vitamins A and D, so it suits low-fat and low-cholesterol diets, while the protein, lactose and calcium remain.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.4] Fortified milk has nutrients added to whole, low-fat or skimmed milk, such as vitamins A and D, folic acid, calcium, protein or omega-3 fatty acids. Fortification adds nutrients that are low or were lost in processing, so it can restore vitamins A and D to low-fat milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.5] Semi-skimmed and skimmed milk are not fortified unless the label says so.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.6] Other types to name are long-life (UHT), sterilised, evaporated, condensed and dried milk, which are heat-treated or concentrated, and lactose-free milk. Goat's milk and soya (plant) milk are alternatives.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.9.b] Nutrition labels give values per 100 ml, so different milks can be compared like with like. Use only the figures you are given and link each to a function.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.9.0] Energy and fat content: a lower-energy, lower-fat milk suits a slimming or low-cholesterol diet, for weight control and heart health.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.9.1] Protein: needed for growth and repair, so compare grams per 100 ml for growing teenagers.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.9.2] Calcium: compare the amount per 100 ml, because it is needed for bones and teeth.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.9.3] Vitamin B12: needed for healthy red blood cells, for making use of folate and fatty acids in metabolism, and for the myelin sheath of nerves.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.9.4] A fat-free milk compared with whole milk has lower energy and fat, similar protein and vitamin B12, and more calcium, which is the basis for recommending it to a teenager.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s6: 6. Fats and oils as a food commodity
[hom-lipids-s6.0.b] Fats and oils are the most concentrated fat foods in the diet. Knowing what they supply, and what they leave out, explains how they should be used and chosen.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.0] Fats and oils are almost pure fat, supplying 82–99.9% fat. Butter and margarine are about four-fifths fat or more and oils are almost pure fat, so the balance is mostly water in butter, margarine and spreads, and water varies inversely with fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.1] They supply energy at 37 kJ or 9 kcal per gram, which is more than twice the energy in a gram of protein or carbohydrate, so a small amount of fat adds a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.2] They supply fat-soluble vitamins (those that dissolve in fat, not water) and fatty acids. Butter has vitamins A and D naturally, vegetable oils supply vitamin E, and essential fatty acids (ones the body cannot make) come from plant oils.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.3] Margarine and spreads are usually fortified with vitamins A and D, which are not naturally present in plant oils, so they match the vitamins that butter provides.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.4] They supply little or no protein, carbohydrate, fibre or minerals. Butter and margarine contain traces of minerals such as calcium and sodium (from added salt).
[hom-lipids-s6.0.5] Spreads contain water as well as fat, so a lower-fat spread contains more water than a butter or margarine of the same type.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.6] The type of fat depends on its source. Animal fats (butter, lard, dripping) are saturated and raise LDL (the 'bad' cholesterol), olive oil is monounsaturated, and plant and nut oils are polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.7] Spreads and oils are eaten daily but in small amounts, yet much of the fat in the diet is hidden in processed, convenience and takeaway foods, meat products and dairy, so total fat intake is greater than the visible fats suggest.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.8] In the Food Pyramid, fats, spreads and oils are used sparingly, choosing mono- or polyunsaturated types.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.b] The three main commodities start from different raw materials. Butter comes from milk, oils from plants, and margarine from plant oils that are then hardened.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.0] Butter is made by churning cream. The fat droplets clump together, the buttermilk is drained off, and the butter is worked and salted or left unsalted.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.1] In the EU, butter is at least 80% and under 90% milk fat, with at most 16% water. It is an animal, saturated fat and a water-in-oil emulsion (tiny water droplets spread through the fat).
[hom-lipids-s6.1.2] Oils are pressed or extracted from seeds, nuts and fruits such as olives, rapeseed and sunflower. They are then refined (cleaned, bleached and deodorised) to remove impurities, giving a bland oil with a higher smoke point (the temperature at which it starts to give off smoke).
[hom-lipids-s6.1.3] Extra virgin olive oil is made from olives by mechanical pressing without chemical refining, so it keeps its flavour. It is used for dressing and gentle cooking.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.4] Margarine and spreads are made from vegetable oils, and sometimes fish oils. Spreads are lower in fat than margarine and butter and contain more water.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.5] The types on sale are butter, margarine, low-fat spreads, functional dairy spreads (cholesterol-lowering), blended spreads (butter with vegetable oil), oils (olive, rapeseed, sunflower, vegetable), lard, suet, dripping and cooking fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.b] The right fat depends on what it is for, what the label says and your health needs. Reading the label first makes each choice easier.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.0] Read the label for the type of fat, ingredients, energy per 100 g, total fat, saturates, salt, additives and allergens (milk, soya).
[hom-lipids-s6.2.1] The ingredients list shows the largest ingredient first, so a spread listing water or vegetable oil first contains mainly those.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.2] Choose by health: more mono- and polyunsaturated fat and less saturated fat. Olive or rapeseed oil are healthier choices, and low-fat or functional spreads suit people watching weight or cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.3] Choose spreads with few hydrogenated fats in the ingredients list when reducing trans fat (a fat formed during hydrogenation that is harmful to the heart) in the diet, and avoid partially hydrogenated fats.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.4] Choose by use. Oils have higher smoke points than butter, so oils suit frying and butter suits spreading and baking.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.5] Hard margarine or block fat suits pastry and creaming because it is plastic (firm yet workable) at room temperature. Low-fat spreads are unsuitable for cooking and baking because of their high water content.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.6] Other factors are cost, taste, dietary needs (dairy-free, vegan, allergy), brand, packaging, shelf life, organic, and whether it is Irish (the Bord Bia quality mark).
[hom-lipids-s6.3.b] Fats spoil in two ways, which is why storage advice differs. Air, light and heat make fats go rancid, while fat also soaks up smells from other foods.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.0] Rancidity (the off smell and taste of spoiled fat) is the main storage problem. Oxidative rancidity is caused by air, light and heat, and hydrolytic rancidity by water breaking down the fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.1] Store fats and oils in dark or opaque, airtight containers in a cool place, away from light and heat, to limit oxidative rancidity. Buy sensible quantities and use them before the date.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.2] Store butter and spreads in the refrigerator, with a cover or in the original wrapper or tub, because fats absorb strong odours such as onion and fish. Freeze butter for longer storage.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.3] Store nuts and seeds in airtight containers in a cool place, because their oils go rancid quickly.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.4] Oily fish and fatty meat are stored in the fridge and used promptly, or frozen.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.5] Keep used frying oil covered and strain it, so food particles do not speed up its breakdown. Do not top up old oil, and discard it when it is dark, smokes at a low temperature or foams.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.6] Foods that contain fat, such as pastries, cakes, biscuits, crisps, nuts and whole-grain flours, are kept in airtight tins or packs.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.b] How fat is handled in the kitchen changes both the energy in a dish and how it turns out. Some methods add almost no fat, while others depend on it for texture.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.0] Methods that add little or no fat are grilling, steaming, poaching, microwaving and baking on a rack. Trimming visible fat from meat before cooking reduces the fat in the finished dish, as does removing skin, using a non-stick pan and cutting the fat in recipes.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.1] Frying adds fat and therefore energy, because the food absorbs fat. Shallow frying uses a little fat and transfers heat evenly to food, giving it a golden colour.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.2] Deep-fat frying in a deep pan at the correct, controlled temperature limits the amount of oil that food absorbs. Do not overheat the oil, and drain the food on absorbent paper.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.3] Creaming butter or margarine with sugar traps air in the mixture, giving cakes a light texture.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.4] Rubbing fat into flour makes shortcrust pastry. The fat coats the flour proteins and limits gluten, which shortens the pastry. Fat is layered in flaky and puff pastry.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.5] Fat is also used for frying and roasting (heat transfer and browning), greasing tins, emulsions such as mayonnaise, hollandaise and dressings, roux and sauces, and for flavour, richness and moistness.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.6] Fat from roasted meat collects in the tin and can be strained and used as dripping for frying.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.b] Heat changes fat, and not always for the better. Knowing what happens when fat is overheated, and how manufacturers process fats, explains the safety rules and the labels.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.0] Fat melts when heated, but overheating breaks it down (its smoke point is reached and acrolein, an irritant, forms). Repeated heating lowers the smoke point and produces off flavours.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.1] Fat that reaches flash point (the temperature at which it ignites) can catch fire, so never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.2] Processing includes refining, hydrogenation (turning oil into fat), blending, emulsifying, and adding vitamins and colour. Hydrogenation can form trans fatty acids, and the EU limit is 2 g per 100 g of fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.b] Margarine is made by turning liquid vegetable oil into a spreadable solid fat and mixing it with a water phase. The stages must be written in order.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.0] 1. Extract the oil from seeds such as sunflower, rapeseed, soya or palm, then refine it to remove impurities.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.1] 2. Hydrogenate it. Hydrogen gas is passed through the heated oil in the presence of a nickel catalyst (a substance that speeds up the reaction), which adds hydrogen at the double bonds and makes the liquid oil a semi-solid fat. The catalyst is then removed.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.2] 3. Blend the hydrogenated fat with other oils to give the texture (plasticity) and spreadability needed.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.3] 4. Prepare the water phase from water or skimmed milk, salt, colour (carotene), flavour and vitamins A and D. Lecithin is added as an emulsifier, which keeps the fat and water from separating.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.4] 5. Emulsify by mixing the fat and water phases, with churning, to form a stable water-in-oil emulsion.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.5] 6. Chill rapidly in a votator, a scraped-surface heat exchanger, while working the mixture so that a plastic, spreadable fat forms.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.6] 7. Weigh the margarine and pack it in foil-wrapped blocks or plastic tubs. An airtight, light-proof pack protects the fat from oxidation and absorbed smells.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.b] The label tells shoppers what is in the pack and how to store it, and the law sets out what it must show.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.0] The label gives the name and type (margarine or spread), and lists the ingredients in descending order by weight with allergens such as milk and soya highlighted.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.1] It also gives a nutrition declaration per 100 g (energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt), the net quantity, a date mark (best before) and storage instructions, the manufacturer's name and address, and the country of origin and batch (lot) code.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.2] In Ireland, food labelling follows Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, with the FSAI giving guidance on food safety and labelling.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.b] Low-fat spreads and functional spreads are different products for different needs. One lowers the fat in the diet, and the other lowers blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.0] Low-fat spreads have about half the fat of butter and more water, so they are lower in energy and suit a calorie-controlled diet.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.1] Low-fat spreads are made from vegetable oils, water and milk proteins, and are low in saturated fat and high in unsaturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.2] Low-fat spreads are suitable for spreading on bread but unsuitable for frying or baking because of their high water content.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.3] Functional dairy spreads contain added plant stanol or sterol esters, which block the absorption of cholesterol in the small intestine so that more is excreted. Examples are Benecol (plant stanol ester) and Flora pro.activ (plant sterol).
[hom-lipids-s6.8.4] Functional dairy spreads are made from vegetable oil, buttermilk, plant stanol or sterol ester, water and salt. They contain no hydrogenated fat and virtually no trans fatty acids.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.5] The cholesterol-lowering effect depends on eating the spread regularly as part of a healthy diet, and it lowers blood (LDL) cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.b] Data questions give the nutrition figures for several spreads and ask you to judge them. The method is to compare the figures, match them to dietary advice and give a verdict.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.0] Compare the energy value per 100 g or per serving of each spread.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.1] Compare total fat, saturated fat, monounsaturated fat and polyunsaturated fat, and the salt content (and omega-3 where it is given) for each spread.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.2] Lower energy and lower total fat suit weight control, and lower saturated fat reduces the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s6.9.3] Higher monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fat, and a lower saturated fat content, suit a diet aimed at protecting the heart. Lower salt suits blood pressure.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.4] Check the water content and whether the spread is suitable for frying or baking.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.5] Compare butter, a spread and a light spread, and state the fat content differences. A lower fat content follows from a higher water content.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.6] Reach a verdict for each product, such as suiting weight reduction or CHD risk reduction, and give a reason for each judgement that refers to the figures.
