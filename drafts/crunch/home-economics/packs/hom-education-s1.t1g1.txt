TARGET hom-education-s1: 1. The purpose of education
[hom-education-s1.0.b] Socialisation is the process by which a person learns the norms (accepted rules of behaviour), values, beliefs, language and behaviour of their society so that they can take part in it. The family is the first agent of socialisation, and school is a second, major one.
[hom-education-s1.0.0] Formal socialisation is the planned, stated part of school: the curriculum, timetable, school rules, uniform, codes of conduct, sanctions and rewards. It teaches punctuality, respect for authority, following instructions and responsibility, because pupils must keep to these every day.
[hom-education-s1.0.1] Informal socialisation is the unplanned part, called the hidden curriculum (what pupils pick up without being taught it directly): peer groups, playground rules, friendships, school routines, the school's ethos (its character and values) and the example set by teachers.
[hom-education-s1.0.2] The hidden curriculum teaches sharing, taking turns, cooperation, teamwork and how to get on with others. Remember that it is not the written curriculum: rules and timetable are formal, while peer groups and playground life are informal.
[hom-education-s1.0.3] School is an early and long experience of a group outside the family, so children learn to share, take turns and cooperate with others who are not relatives.
[hom-education-s1.0.4] Education transmits (passes on) the culture of a society, including its language (English and Irish), customs, history, shared values, and the rights and duties of citizens.
[hom-education-s1.0.5] School brings together children from different backgrounds, which helps them fit into wider society.
[hom-education-s1.0.6] Through school, pupils learn to accept responsibility, follow rules and respect the rights of others.
[hom-education-s1.1.b] Physical development is how the body grows stronger, more skilled and healthier. School supports it through activity, practical work and teaching about health.
[hom-education-s1.1.0] Physical education, games, sport and dance develop gross motor skills (large body movements) such as running, jumping, balance and co-ordination, as well as strength, fitness and general body development.
[hom-education-s1.1.1] Practical subjects such as Home Economics, Art, Construction Studies, Engineering and Science develop fine motor skills (small, precise hand movements), manual dexterity and hand-eye coordination. In early years, play with building blocks, jigsaws, drawing and writing does the same.
[hom-education-s1.1.2] Regular physical activity strengthens muscles and bones and builds cardiovascular fitness (a healthy heart and lungs).
[hom-education-s1.1.3] Lessons on nutrition, hygiene, safety and health (in SPHE, Home Economics and PE) give pupils the knowledge to look after their physical health.
[hom-education-s1.1.4] Physical education and school sport can establish exercise habits that a person keeps into adult life, so the benefit lasts beyond school.
[hom-education-s1.2.b] Emotional development is learning to understand and manage feelings and to get on with other people. School gives children many situations that call for this.
[hom-education-s1.2.0] Starting school moves a child towards emotional independence from parents, because they meet other adults and peers outside the home, cope with separation and learn to depend on themselves.
[hom-education-s1.2.1] Group work and class discussion develop empathy, the ability to understand and respond to the feelings of others, and help pupils express their own feelings appropriately.
[hom-education-s1.2.2] Education helps a young person form healthy relationships and friendships.
[hom-education-s1.2.3] Coping with success and failure in tests, sport, performances and tasks builds resilience (the ability to recover from setbacks), confidence, self-esteem and maturity.
[hom-education-s1.2.4] Praise and encouragement from teachers strengthen self-worth.
[hom-education-s1.2.5] The Social, Personal and Health Education (SPHE) programme teaches emotional literacy (recognising and naming feelings), relationships, coping skills and personal safety. It continues into senior cycle as a non-exam programme.
[hom-education-s1.2.6] Guidance counsellors and pastoral care (support for pupils' wellbeing) give extra help.
[hom-education-s1.3.b] Moral development is learning the difference between right and wrong and acting on it. School teaches this through its rules, its subjects and the people in it.
[hom-education-s1.3.0] The ethos of a school sets out its values and the behaviour it expects from pupils.
[hom-education-s1.3.1] School rules, sanctions and rewards teach pupils to distinguish right from wrong, fairness and accountability for their actions, because actions have consequences they must accept.
[hom-education-s1.3.2] Religious education and SPHE address moral questions such as honesty, fairness, respect and tolerance, and subjects such as History and English raise the same questions.
[hom-education-s1.3.3] Moral development is also shaped by teachers as role models and by the example of the peer group.
[hom-education-s1.3.4] Pupils learn to respect the rights, property and opinions of others and to accept responsibility, so becoming responsible citizens.
[hom-education-s1.4.b] Intellectual development is the growth of knowledge and thinking skills. A school's curriculum and assessment are built to promote it.
[hom-education-s1.4.0] A broad curriculum develops literacy, numeracy, languages, scientific reasoning, creativity and artistic skills, and builds knowledge across many subjects.
[hom-education-s1.4.1] Subjects that require analysis, argument and problem solving, and research, develop critical thinking and the ability to apply knowledge.
[hom-education-s1.4.2] Concentration and memory are trained through homework, revision and classroom tasks, because they require pupils to practise focusing and recalling.
[hom-education-s1.4.3] Examinations and continuous assessment (work marked throughout the year) measure learning and progress, and give motivation, goals and a sense of achievement.
[hom-education-s1.4.4] Competition in class, sport, quizzes and extra-curricular activities challenges students to extend their abilities.
[hom-education-s1.4.5] Education also builds curiosity and a habit of lifelong learning.
[hom-education-s1.5.b] Most people spend much of their lives working, and school is where the groundwork is laid. Education gives skills, qualifications and habits that employers look for.
[hom-education-s1.5.0] Literacy, numeracy, communication and computer or ICT skills are basic skills that employers expect in almost every job.
[hom-education-s1.5.1] Qualifications such as Junior Cycle and the Leaving Certificate, and Post Leaving Certificate, further education and third-level awards, show employers what a person has learned and open doors to jobs and higher-paid work.
[hom-education-s1.5.2] Higher levels of education are linked with better employment prospects.
[hom-education-s1.5.3] Punctuality, good attendance, respect for authority, reliability, trustworthiness, responsibility, self-discipline and teamwork are taught through daily school routines and group tasks. Social skills are learned by working with others.
[hom-education-s1.5.4] Work experience (in Transition Year, the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP) and the Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA)), career guidance and subjects chosen for a career help students connect school with the world of work.
[hom-education-s1.5.5] Vocational and practical subjects such as Business Studies teach skills used directly in employment.
[hom-education-s1.5.6] Education matters more as industry changes, because technology and a service-based economy raise the education required for employment. It also prepares people to retrain throughout their working life.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s3: 3. Primary and secondary social groups, kinship, socialisation
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.b] A social group is two or more people who interact regularly and share a sense of belonging, such as a purpose, an identity or a place. Groups differ in how close and how formal their relationships are, and a crowd of strangers is not a group.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.0] A primary group is small, intimate and face-to-face, with long-lasting relationships; examples are the family and close friends.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.1] Primary groups meet emotional needs and give a sense of belonging, so they shape a person's personality, values and sense of identity. They are also where primary socialisation mainly happens.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.2] A secondary group is larger and more formal, with relationships that are impersonal and often short-term or limited to a role; examples are a school class, a workplace, a trade union, a sports club and a political party.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.3] Secondary groups are organised around a specific goal, such as completing a course or producing goods. Members often join by choice and leave when the purpose is finished.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.4] To compare them: primary groups are small, close, lasting and personal, while secondary groups are larger, formal, often temporary and purpose-based. A person belongs to both types at once, and some groups, such as a small close-knit club, sit between the two.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.5] An in-group is a group with which a person identifies and feels loyalty; an out-group is a group that members see as different from or opposed to their own.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.6] A reference group is a group whose norms and values (the standards of behaviour it expects) a person uses to judge their own behaviour, even if they are not a member of it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.7] A peer group is made up of people of similar age and status. It is important in adolescence and can act as a primary or a secondary agent of socialisation, depending on how close its members are.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.b] Kinship is the network of family ties between people who are related by blood, marriage or adoption. It is a relationship, not a type of household, so it includes relatives who live apart.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.0] Kinship means family ties: relationships based on blood (common ancestry or lineage), marriage or adoption.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.1] Consanguineal kin are related by blood, for example parents, children, siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.2] Affinal kin are related through marriage, for example a spouse, a spouse's parents, who are in-laws, and step-relatives. The terms consanguineal and affinal are optional; blood and marriage are enough.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.3] A nuclear family is parents and their dependent children living together as one household.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.4] An extended family includes kin beyond the nuclear family, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who live together or keep in close contact.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.5] The family of orientation is the family a person is born into; the family of procreation is the family a person forms through their own marriage or partnership and children.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.6] Kinship is found in every society, which is part of why the family is called universal.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.7] Descent, the line through which kinship is traced, can run through the mother's side, the father's side or both; most modern Western societies trace both sides.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.b] Kin networks matter because relatives are often the first people to turn to when help is needed. They have also changed as society has changed.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.0] Kin networks provide practical and emotional support, including childcare, financial help, care for older members and a sense of identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.1] Kinship obligations include mutual support and, in many societies, inheritance rights between relatives.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.2] Kin networks have become looser as families have become smaller and more mobile, but relatives keep in contact through phones and social media.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.b] Socialisation is the lifelong process by which people learn the norms, values, behaviour, roles and skills of their society and so become members of it. Without it a child could not use language or form relationships.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.0] Socialisation is lifelong, so it continues in adolescence and adulthood as well as in childhood.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.1] Primary socialisation takes place in early childhood, mainly within the family, where children learn language, toilet training, manners, self-control, right from wrong and a first sense of identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.2] Secondary socialisation takes place later in wider settings such as school, the peer group, the workplace, religious institutions and the media. There people learn the roles and norms of that setting, such as punctuality, rules, teamwork and respect for authority.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.3] Through socialisation the culture of a society is passed from one generation to the next, which maintains continuity over time. It also produces conformity and social order and lets individuals take part in society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.4] People learn through observation, imitation, rewards and punishments (sanctions), role models and direct instruction. In the family, children learn through observation, imitation and consistent discipline.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.5] Anticipatory socialisation is learning the norms of a role or group that a person hopes to join, such as a student learning workplace norms before starting an apprenticeship.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.6] Resocialisation is learning new norms after a major change in life, such as entering the army or prison, where old habits are replaced.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.b] Agents of socialisation are the people and institutions that pass on norms and values. Each one teaches something different.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.0] The main agents are the family, school, peer group, mass media, religion and the workplace.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.1] School socialises formally through the curriculum and informally through the hidden curriculum, the unwritten lessons taught by its rules and routines.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.2] The peer group becomes an important agent in adolescence, when young people test independence and learn the norms of their age group.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.3] Mass media, including television, the internet and social media, transmit norms and values and shape consumer habits.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.b] Monogamy is one form of marriage; the other forms are classed as polygamy. It describes how many partners a person has at one time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.0] Monogamy is marriage, or a partnership, with one partner at a time, traditionally one husband and one wife.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.1] Monogamy is the norm in the Western world, and it is the only form of marriage recognised in Ireland. It is not the only form worldwide.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.2] Polygamy is marriage to more than one spouse at the same time; polygyny is one man with several wives, and polyandry is one woman with several husbands. Both are found in some other societies.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.3] Polygamy is illegal in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.4] Bigamy is entering a marriage while still legally married to another person, without the death or divorce of the first spouse. It is an offence in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.5] Serial monogamy is a succession of monogamous marriages, one after another, following divorce or the death of a spouse.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.6] Spouses in a monogamous marriage are expected to remain faithful and loyal to each other, live together, and support one another and their children financially.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.7] Civil and religious marriages are both legally recognised in Ireland, and both are monogamous. Same-sex marriage is also lawful in Ireland and is monogamous, being a marriage of two people.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s4: 4. Social institutions
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.b] Every society has basic needs, such as raising children, passing on knowledge and organising work. A social institution is the lasting, organised way a society meets one of them, which is why institutions matter for understanding family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.0] A social institution is an established, organised set of norms (accepted rules of behaviour), roles and values built around a basic need of society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.1] The five main social institutions are the family, education, religion, the economy (work) and government (the State). Health and the media are sometimes added to this list.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.2] An institution is not a building or a single organisation. A school is an organisation within the institution of education, so the school down the road is part of the institution, not the institution itself.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.3] Institutions last across generations, while individual groups come and go. Because they carry norms and values, pass on culture and give structure to social life, each one is an agent of socialisation (a means by which people learn how to behave in society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.4] Institutions are linked, so what happens in one affects the others. For example, the economy affects family life, and education prepares people for work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.5] Functionalism is the view that each institution performs functions that help keep society stable. This view describes what institutions do; it does not label any institution good or bad.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.6] A manifest function is an intended and recognised result of an institution, for example the school's function of educating young people.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.7] A latent function is an unintended and often hidden result, for example a school providing supervised care for children while parents are at work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.b] The family is the first and most basic institution, because it is where every person's care and learning begins. Over time other institutions take over or add to some of its work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.0] The family reproduces the population and provides for the care of children, older people and dependants (people who rely on others for support).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.1] The family socialises children, passing on language, norms, values and skills, so that children learn how to take part in society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.2] The family is the basic economic unit of consumption (using goods and services) and provides economic support through shared income and household work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.3] The family provides emotional security and a sense of belonging and identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.4] Other institutions adopt or supplement family functions. Schools take on much of children's education, the State provides welfare and health services, employers provide income, and religious bodies provide moral guidance.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.5] Examples of supplementing a family function: parenting courses support the emotional function, schooling from preschool to third level supports the educational function, welfare payments support the economic function, and medical services support the physical function.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.b] Education is how a society deliberately passes on what it knows, and it also shapes children's behaviour and future work. It works both in the classroom and in the everyday life of the school.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.0] Education transmits knowledge, skills and the culture of a society from one generation to the next. It does this formally through the curriculum (the subjects taught), from primary to third level.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.1] It also teaches informally through the hidden curriculum, the unwritten lessons of school life such as rules, punctuality, respect for authority, sharing and teamwork.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.2] Education socialises children, reinforcing the behaviour and values learned at home. It promotes social cohesion by teaching shared history, language and civic values, such as the Irish language and Irish history in Irish schools.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.3] Education prepares people for work through literacy and numeracy, qualifications, specialist skills and career guidance. Work experience comes through Transition Year, the Leaving Certificate Applied, the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme and college.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.4] Education sorts and allocates people to positions in the economy by means of qualifications. It can support social mobility (moving to a higher social position) by opening access to skilled and professional occupations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.5] In Ireland, education runs through primary, post-primary, further and higher education. The Leaving Certificate is the state examination at the end of second level, and most students use it for entry to third-level courses.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.b] Education is meant to give everyone a fair chance, but a family's circumstances can affect how far a child gets. Supports exist to narrow that gap.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.0] Socio-economic status (a family's income, occupation and social position) affects chances in education. Higher-status families often have more educational opportunities and go on to third level more often.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.1] Costs of books, uniforms and equipment can exclude low-income families, because they cannot afford what a child needs to take part fully.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.2] Grants, book rental schemes and allowances help families meet these costs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.3] DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) supports aim to reduce the gap for pupils at risk of being left behind.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.b] Religion is the institution that answers questions about meaning and right and wrong, and it gives people a community to belong to. Describe it neutrally: Ireland has many faiths and people of no religion.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.0] Religion provides a system of beliefs and values that gives meaning to life and explains events that cannot be explained by science alone.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.1] Religion provides moral codes that guide behaviour and reinforce many of a society's norms and values, for example rules against stealing or killing.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.2] Religious rituals mark key stages of life, such as birth, first communion or confirmation, marriage and death.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.3] Religious institutions provide community networks and social support to their members, so people feel they belong.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.4] Churches and other religious bodies have supported education, health care and charity. In Ireland many primary schools still have a religious patron.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.5] The influence of religion on Irish society has changed, with declining influence of the Church, more civil ceremonies and a more secular society (one not guided by religion).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.b] The economy is the institution that decides how a society produces and shares what people need. Work is how most families connect to it, which is why economic change reaches family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.0] The economic institution organises the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.1] Employment is the main way the economy allocates status and income to individuals and families, and income decides a family's standard of living.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.2] Economic institutions include businesses, banks, markets and the systems of trade through which goods move between producers and consumers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.3] Work is where people take on adult roles and learn workplace norms (the accepted behaviour at work).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.4] Economic institutions shape family life, for example by determining whether one or both parents must work outside the home, and so hours of work and childcare needs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.5] Economic changes affect family income and roles. Unemployment lowers living standards and strains relationships, while growth of service industries creates jobs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.6] Families are consumers, buying goods and services with the income that work provides.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.b] Government is the institution with the power to make rules for everyone and to provide services that individuals cannot organise alone. Its structure spreads that power so no single body holds all of it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.0] The political institution, the State, maintains order, makes laws, collects taxes and provides public services, including social welfare, health, education, housing and protection of the vulnerable.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.1] Government is typically divided into three branches: the legislature, which makes laws; the executive, which carries them out; and the judiciary (the courts), which interprets and applies them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.2] This division is called the separation of powers, and it prevents any one branch from holding unchecked authority.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.3] In Ireland the Oireachtas, the national parliament, consists of the President, Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann. The Government is the executive, and the courts are the judiciary.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.4] Local authorities provide local services such as housing, planning and libraries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.5] Citizens take part in government by voting in elections and referendums, electing representatives to the Dáil in general elections.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.6] The State supports the family in each function: welfare payments for the economic function, health services for the physical function, and schooling for the educational function.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.7] Child protection is part of this support. The State's agency for children is Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, which has exercised the Child Care Act 1991 functions since 2014.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.b] Health services and the media are often added to the five main institutions because they also shape everyday life. Treat them as additions, since the list of institutions is not fixed.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.0] The health institution promotes health and provides care and treatment for illness and injury.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.1] In Ireland the Health Service Executive (HSE) provides public health care.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.2] The media, including newspapers, radio, television and online platforms, inform the public and entertain it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.3] The media also shape public opinion and attitudes and transmit norms and values, which makes them both an institution and an agent of socialisation.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.4] The legal system (courts and law) is part of government, so it needs no separate institution.
