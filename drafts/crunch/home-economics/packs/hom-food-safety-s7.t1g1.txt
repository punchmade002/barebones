TARGET hom-food-safety-s7: 7. HACCP and ISO 9000
[hom-food-safety-s7.0.b] Rather than waiting to test finished food, a food business can look for trouble at every step and stop it early. That is the idea behind HACCP, and it comes with a small set of terms that exams ask you to define.
[hom-food-safety-s7.0.0] HACCP stands for Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Point. It is a preventive system, which means it identifies and controls hazards at each stage from raw material to consumer, before they can reach the consumer, rather than relying on testing the finished product.
[hom-food-safety-s7.0.1] A hazard is anything that could contaminate food or harm the consumer. A control measure is an action that prevents, removes or reduces a hazard, such as thorough cooking, which destroys surviving bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s7.0.2] A critical control point (CCP) is a step at which a control measure is essential and can be monitored, for example the cooking step. A critical limit is the value that separates safe from unsafe, for example a minimum core temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s7.0.3] Hazard analysis identifies the hazards at each stage of food processing and assesses how likely and how serious each one is, so effort goes where the risk is greatest.
[hom-food-safety-s7.0.4] HACCP is used by caterers, restaurants, food manufacturers and food businesses generally.
[hom-food-safety-s7.1.b] Hazards fall into three groups depending on what the contaminant is. Naming the type, with an example, helps you decide what control measure fits.
[hom-food-safety-s7.1.0] Biological hazards are micro-organisms, such as food poisoning bacteria, moulds and viruses, which can multiply in food and make people ill.
[hom-food-safety-s7.1.1] Chemical hazards include pesticide residues, cleaning chemicals, metal residues and allergens, which can get into food during growing, cleaning or processing.
[hom-food-safety-s7.1.2] Physical hazards are foreign bodies such as glass, metal, bone, stones, pests and hair, which can injure the consumer or signal poor hygiene.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.b] The seven principles are the steps a business follows to build a HACCP system. They run in a logical order: find the hazards, pick the key steps, set limits, check them, fix problems, prove it works and write it all down.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.0] Principle 1: carry out a hazard analysis at each stage of the process.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.1] Principle 2: identify the critical control points, the steps where control is essential.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.2] Principle 3: set a critical limit for each critical control point, such as a minimum cooking temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.3] Principle 4: set up monitoring procedures to check each critical control point, so you know the limit is being met.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.4] Principle 5: set corrective actions to take when monitoring shows that a critical limit has not been met.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.5] Principle 6: set up verification procedures to confirm that the system is working.
[hom-food-safety-s7.2.6] Principle 7: keep records and documentation of the system and its results, which gives written evidence of what was done.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.b] A HACCP answer follows the food from the shop to the plate, giving a hazard and its control at each stage. The first three stages happen before any cooking starts.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.0] Purchase hazards: damaged goods, bacterial or chemical contamination, and pests.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.1] Purchase controls: buy from a reputable shop, and check packaging, use-by dates, temperatures and any signs of pest damage, so unsafe food is never brought home. Keep raw and cooked foods separate in the trolley, and choose foods within date and with quality assurance marks.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.2] Transport hazard: bacteria multiply when food warms up during transport and delays, because warmth lets them grow quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.3] Transport control: carry perishable food in cool bags or refrigerated containers, cover it, minimise the time before it is stored, and store it promptly.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.4] Storage hazards: bacterial multiplication and cross-contamination (bacteria passing from one food to another) of food.
[hom-food-safety-s7.3.5] Storage controls: chill perishable food below 5°C, freeze food at −18°C, and keep raw food separate from cooked food, with raw below cooked and covered. Check vegetables for mould, and use clean sealed containers.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.b] Preparation and cooking are where food is handled most and where bacteria can be destroyed. Cooking is the critical control point, because it is the step that kills bacteria already on the food.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.0] Preparation hazards: contamination of food, multiplication of bacteria and cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.1] Preparation controls: wash hands with antibacterial soap and hot water before and after handling food, and handle food as little as possible. Keep perishables chilled until needed, and clean equipment in hot water and detergent.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.2] Use colour-coded boards and separate utensils for raw and cooked meat, and keep raw meat away from cooked meat and ready-to-eat foods, so bacteria from raw meat cannot reach food that will not be cooked again.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.3] Thaw frozen meat or poultry fully, preferably in the fridge on the bottom shelf, before cooking it. Thawing fully matters because a frozen centre may stay too cool to cook through, and the bottom shelf stops drips reaching other food.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.4] Cooking hazard: bacteria that survive cooking, which can cause food poisoning.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.5] Cooking control: cook thoroughly, turning or stirring the food regularly so that it cooks evenly, and use a thermometer to check.
[hom-food-safety-s7.4.6] Poultry must reach a core temperature (the temperature at the centre) of at least 72°C to destroy bacteria. In the exam example this is the critical limit, and the current FSAI guidance of 75°C is also correct.
[hom-food-safety-s7.5.b] Safe cooking can be undone if food is left standing or reheated badly. The last two stages deal with bacteria that survived cooking and the food that is left over.
[hom-food-safety-s7.5.0] Serving hazards: surviving bacteria multiplying in food that is held too long, and cross-contamination from utensils.
[hom-food-safety-s7.5.1] Serving controls: serve cooked food straight after cooking, on a clean plate, with clean utensils.
[hom-food-safety-s7.5.2] Hold cooked food above 63°C, because bacteria multiply more slowly when it is kept hot, and do not hold it for more than about 90 minutes.
[hom-food-safety-s7.5.3] Reheating hazard: food that has been cooled and stored can have bacteria that multiply during storage, or survive.
[hom-food-safety-s7.5.4] Reheating control: cool leftovers quickly, cover them and refrigerate. Then reheat food quickly to above 100°C throughout, and reheat it only once. Current guidance of piping hot, about 70 to 75°C at the core, is also correct.
[hom-food-safety-s7.6.b] Exams can ask for the HACCP stages of a dish other than a hot main course, such as a barbecue. The stages are the same, with a few extra controls suited to outdoor cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s7.6.0] Cook poultry and sausages right through, with no pink or red juices, because pink juices show the centre has not reached a safe temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s7.6.1] Do not use the same plate or tongs for raw and cooked food, so raw-meat bacteria cannot pass onto food that is ready to eat.
[hom-food-safety-s7.6.2] Keep raw meat separate from salads, which will not be cooked, and keep perishable salads and coleslaw in the fridge until needed.
[hom-food-safety-s7.6.3] A marinade used on raw meat must be brought to the boil before it is used as a sauce, otherwise it carries raw-meat bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.b] HACCP helps both the consumer and the business. It makes food safer and gives the business proof that it took proper care.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.0] HACCP identifies and eliminates or reduces hazards, so food is safer and outbreaks are prevented.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.1] It focuses workers on safety at every step of food preparation.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.2] It meets legal requirements and provides written records as evidence of due care.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.3] Records help a business plan for future safety, time and cost, with less waste and fewer recalls.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.4] It increases consumer confidence, and the system should be reviewed and evaluated regularly.
[hom-food-safety-s7.7.5] In a catering business, HACCP helps prevent food poisoning outbreaks by identifying hazards from purchase to serving, controlling them at critical points and monitoring to prevent contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s7.8.b] HACCP is not the only system a food business may use. ISO 9000 is a separate, broader approach to managing quality.
[hom-food-safety-s7.8.0] ISO is the International Organization for Standardization, which publishes internationally agreed standards. In Ireland the National Standards Authority of Ireland (NSAI) is the national standards body.
[hom-food-safety-s7.8.1] ISO 9000 is a family of standards for quality management systems. A quality management system uses documented procedures, records and regular internal review to ensure that products meet customer requirements.
[hom-food-safety-s7.8.2] Continual improvement of the system is a central principle of ISO 9000.
[hom-food-safety-s7.8.3] ISO 9001 is the standard in the family against which organisations are formally audited and certified. Certification by an independent body shows customers that the business has a consistent quality system.
[hom-food-safety-s7.8.4] HACCP is a food safety system that controls hazards, whereas ISO 9000 is a general quality management system for any business and covers safety only indirectly. A food business can use both.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s2: 2. Food poisoning bacteria
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.b] Most bacteria in food are harmless or even useful, so only a few cause illness. Food poisoning is illness caused by eating food contaminated with pathogenic (disease-causing) bacteria or their toxins.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.0] Six environmental factors affect bacterial growth: food, moisture, warmth, oxygen requirement, pH and time. Protein-rich, moist foods are high risk because they give bacteria what they need to grow.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.1] Most food poisoning bacteria favour a near-neutral pH, neither strongly acid nor strongly alkaline.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.2] Under good conditions a bacterium divides by binary fission (splitting in two) about every 20 minutes, so numbers rise very quickly and a few bacteria can become millions within hours.
[hom-food-safety-s2.0.3] The danger zone, about 5 to 63 °C, is the range in which food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest. Below 5 °C growth slows down, and above 63 °C most bacteria stop multiplying and thorough cooking destroys them.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.b] Micro-organisms are grouped by the temperature at which they grow best. This explains why the danger zone sits around body temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.0] Psychrophiles grow best at about −5 to 20 °C, in cold conditions such as refrigerated food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.1] Mesophiles grow best at about 20 to 45 °C. Most food poisoning bacteria are mesophiles, which is why the danger zone is close to body temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.2] Salmonella has an optimum (best) growth temperature of about 37 °C, which is normal body temperature.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.3] Thermophiles grow best at about 45 to 75 °C, in hot environments.
[hom-food-safety-s2.1.4] The danger zone, about 5 to 63 °C, is the range in which mesophilic food poisoning bacteria multiply rapidly.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.b] Micro-organisms also differ in their need for oxygen. This decides which foods and storage conditions suit each one.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.0] Aerobic micro-organisms require oxygen to survive and grow; moulds are aerobic.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.1] Anaerobic micro-organisms thrive without oxygen, so sealed foods suit them; Clostridium botulinum is an example.
[hom-food-safety-s2.2.2] Facultative micro-organisms adapt to either aerobic or anaerobic conditions, so they can grow with or without oxygen; Salmonella, Staphylococcus aureus and yeast are facultative.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.b] Salmonella is a common infectious food poisoning bacterium, meaning the illness comes from eating the living bacteria themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.0] Structure: a rod-shaped, Gram-negative bacterium that does not form spores (tough resting cells). It reproduces by binary fission.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.1] Habitat and sources: the intestines of humans, animals and birds; faeces; polluted water; unwashed hands, which carry it onto food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.2] High-risk foods: raw or undercooked poultry, meat, fish and eggs; unpasteurised milk and dairy products.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.3] Growth: needs food, moisture and time, and favours a near-neutral pH. It is a mesophile with an optimum of about 37 °C, and it is facultative.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.4] Incubation period: usually about 12 to 36 hours after eating contaminated food, with a wider range of 12 to 72 hours.
[hom-food-safety-s2.3.5] Symptoms: diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain and fever.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.b] Staphylococcus aureus is a common toxic food poisoning bacterium. It makes a poison in the food as it grows, and the poison causes the illness.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.0] Structure: a Gram-positive, spherical cell (a coccus) that forms grape-like clusters; it does not form spores.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.1] Habitat and sources: the skin, nose and throat of humans and their hair; infected cuts and boils. It spreads to food by handling.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.2] High-risk foods: cooked meats, poultry, ham, egg products, cream-filled cakes, custard and egg or potato salads made with mayonnaise.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.3] Growth: a mesophile that grows at about 37 °C and is facultative. It tolerates salt and sugar better than most bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.4] Toxin: it produces an enterotoxin (a toxin that acts on the gut) in food. The toxin is heat-stable, so it is not destroyed by normal cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.5] Incubation period: short, usually about 2 to 4 hours, within a wider range of about 1 to 6 hours.
[hom-food-safety-s2.4.6] Symptoms: sudden nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhoea.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.b] Clostridium botulinum is a toxic food poisoning bacterium that produces a very potent neurotoxin (a poison that attacks the nerves). It is rare but dangerous.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.0] Structure: a rod-shaped, Gram-positive, spore-forming bacterium that is an obligate anaerobe, meaning it can only grow where there is no oxygen.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.1] Habitat and sources: soil and water, from which its spores reach vegetables and other foods.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.2] High-risk foods: home-canned low-acid vegetables, badly processed canned or vacuum-packed foods, cured meats, and honey, which must not be given to babies under one year.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.3] Growth: it multiplies in sealed, low-acid foods where no oxygen is present, because these are the conditions it needs.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.4] Toxin: a neurotoxin that blocks nerve signals to muscles, causing paralysis. The toxin is destroyed by boiling for about 10 minutes, but the spores survive ordinary cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.5] Incubation period: typically 12 to 36 hours after eating contaminated food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.5.6] Symptoms: double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty swallowing and speaking, muscle weakness, paralysis and breathing difficulties; it can be fatal.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.b] A question may ask for any common strain, so it helps to know a few more with one typical high-risk food each.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.0] Listeria is infectious. It is found in pâté, coleslaw, soft cheese and smoked fish, and it grows at fridge temperature. It is serious for pregnant women, newborns and the elderly.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.1] E. coli is infectious. High-risk foods are undercooked minced meat, polluted water and unpasteurised milk.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.2] Campylobacter is infectious. High-risk foods are raw poultry and unpasteurised milk.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.3] Bacillus cereus is linked to cooked rice and reheated rice.
[hom-food-safety-s2.6.4] Clostridium perfringens is toxic. High-risk foods are stews, gravies and large cooked joints that cool slowly.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.b] Food poisoning falls into two types, depending on whether the harm comes from the bacteria or from their toxin. Incubation period is the time between eating contaminated food and the appearance of the first symptoms.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.0] Infectious food poisoning: caused by eating food containing large numbers of living pathogenic bacteria, which multiply in the gut.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.1] Infectious food poisoning: the bacteria release endotoxins, which are made inside their cells and released when the cells die.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.2] Infectious food poisoning: endotoxins and bacteria are easily destroyed by proper cooking and reheating, so the risk comes from undercooked food. Incubation is usually more than 12 hours; examples are Salmonella, E. coli, Campylobacter and Listeria.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.3] Toxic food poisoning: caused by eating food that already contains exotoxin, a poison produced by bacteria as they grow in the food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.4] Toxic food poisoning: exotoxins are hard to destroy and may need boiling for 30 minutes, so cooking may not make the food safe. Symptoms appear quickly, often within 2 hours; examples are Staphylococcus aureus, Clostridium botulinum and Clostridium perfringens.
[hom-food-safety-s2.7.5] In one line each: infectious means the bacteria themselves are eaten, onset is slower and cooking destroys them; toxic means the toxin is eaten, onset is fast and the toxin survives cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.b] Outbreaks usually trace back to a few handling failures that let bacteria reach food or multiply in it. Each one links to the growth conditions bacteria need.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.0] Undercooked poultry or minced meat allows surviving bacteria to cause illness.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.1] Cooked food left at room temperature allows bacteria to multiply in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.2] Cross-contamination from raw meat or poultry transfers bacteria to food that is eaten without cooking.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.3] Food handlers with poor personal hygiene or illness, such as not washing hands after using the toilet, transfer bacteria to food.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.4] Contaminated water or unpasteurised dairy products introduce bacteria before any cooking takes place.
[hom-food-safety-s2.8.5] Slow cooling of large batches, inadequate reheating and preparing food too far in advance also give bacteria time to grow or survive.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s6: 6. Safe food preparation
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.b] Almost every rule for handling food comes from two facts about food poisoning bacteria: they multiply fastest in warm food, and they travel easily from one food to another. Knowing these makes the individual rules easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.0] Food poisoning bacteria multiply fastest in the danger zone, 5°C to 63°C. This is why safe preparation keeps food cold, hot, or moving quickly through that range.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.1] Cross-contamination is the transfer of bacteria from raw food to cooked or ready-to-eat food, directly or by hands, chopping boards, cloths, utensils or meat juices. This is why raw and cooked foods are kept apart.
[hom-food-safety-s6.0.2] Freezing does not kill bacteria, it only stops them multiplying, and thawing lets them start multiplying again. So frozen food must be handled with care once it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.b] Storage aims to keep food cold or frozen so bacteria cannot multiply, and to stop bacteria passing from one food to another. Short questions usually ask for three to six storage guidelines.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.0] Cover all food and store it in sealed containers, which prevents drying out, odour transfer and cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.1] Keep perishable food (food that spoils quickly) in the fridge at 5°C or below, which slows the growth of bacteria. Check the fridge and freezer temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.2] Store raw meat, poultry and fish on the lowest shelf, on a plate and loosely covered with the shop wrapping removed, so that juices cannot drip onto cooked or ready-to-eat food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.3] Keep raw and cooked foods apart, covered or in sealed containers, so bacteria cannot pass between them.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.4] Cool cooked food quickly before refrigerating or freezing it, and do not overpack the fridge, so that cold air can circulate around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.5] Check the use-by or best-before date of each food and follow the storage instructions on its label. Use-by is a safety date for highly perishable food, while best-before is a quality date.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.6] Remove fruit and vegetables from plastic wrapping, which encourages mould, and check them regularly for mould.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.7] Store dry foods in a cool, dry, well-ventilated cupboard, in vermin-proof containers, and keep storage areas clean.
[hom-food-safety-s6.1.8] Keep perishable food out of the fridge for the shortest possible time while it is being prepared, because food left in warm air soon enters the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.b] Freezing keeps food safe for a long time, but only if it is frozen and thawed properly. Bacteria are not killed by freezing, so the care taken when thawing matters.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.0] Freeze food quickly at −25°C, then store it at −18°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.1] Defrost frozen food completely before cooking, preferably in the fridge on the bottom shelf, so that it thaws slowly and any drips cannot reach other food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.2.2] Never refreeze food that has thawed, because bacteria multiply while it thaws.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.b] Cooking and reheating must heat food right through to kill bacteria, and hot food must then stay hot or be eaten promptly. Otherwise bacteria multiply again in the danger zone.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.0] Cook food thoroughly, using the right time and temperature, and turn or stir it so that heat reaches the centre. Cook meat, poultry and minced meat right through, to a core temperature of at least 72°C for poultry.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.1] Reheat food quickly until it is above 100°C throughout, and reheat it only once.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.2] High-risk foods such as stews and gravies should be brought to boiling when reheated, because their moist, protein-rich nature suits bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.3] Cover food when reheating. In a microwave, stir or turn the food and allow standing time, so that heat reaches all parts evenly, and follow the appliance's instructions.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.4] Serve cooked food immediately. If holding it hot, keep it above 63°C, because bacteria multiply rapidly between 5°C and 63°C.
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.5] Eat food that has been held hot within about 90 minutes (1.5 hours).
[hom-food-safety-s6.3.6] Do not keep food in the danger zone. Cool leftovers quickly, refrigerate them and use them soon.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.b] People carry food poisoning bacteria on their hands, hair, skin and clothes, so the person preparing food is a major source of contamination. Examiners usually ask for four to six personal hygiene points.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.0] Wash hands in hot water with liquid soap, and a nailbrush for the nails, before handling food, because hands carry food poisoning bacteria. Dry them on a clean towel or paper towel.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.1] Wash hands after the toilet, sneezing or coughing, changing nappies, handling pets, handling rubbish and handling raw meat.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.2] Keep nails short and clean and remove jewellery before preparing food, because dirt and bacteria collect under nails and around jewellery.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.3] Tie hair back or cover it. Wear a clean apron or clothing, and change it if it becomes soiled.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.4] Cover cuts, boils and sores with a brightly coloured waterproof plaster, so that infection cannot pass to food and the plaster can be seen if it falls into the food.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.5] Do not cough or sneeze over food, and do not taste food with your fingers or lick your fingers.
[hom-food-safety-s6.4.6] Do not prepare or serve food for others while suffering from diarrhoea or vomiting, because the illness can pass to the people eating it.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.b] A clean kitchen with sensible habits stops bacteria building up on surfaces and equipment and passing to food. Four kitchen hygiene practices is a common exam question.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.0] Clean and disinfect work surfaces often, and wash equipment in hot water and detergent. Clean as you go.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.1] Use colour-coded chopping boards and separate utensils for raw and cooked foods, to prevent cross-contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.2] Never prepare raw and cooked foods on the same surface.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.3] Handle food as little as possible during preparation, and keep it covered, because every touch can pass on bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.4] Floors, walls and work surfaces should be easy to clean, and the kitchen should have good lighting.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.5] Effective ventilation reduces condensation, which encourages the growth of micro-organisms.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.6] The kitchen should have a clean water supply and no access for pets or pests.
[hom-food-safety-s6.5.7] Keep a covered waste bin and empty it regularly, and change clean cloths and dishtowels often.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s3: 3. Micro-organisms and the control of microbial spoilage
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.b] Spoilage is any change that makes food unfit or unpleasant to eat, such as an off smell, taste, colour or texture. Several causes are at work, but micro-organisms are the main one.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.0] Micro-organisms (bacteria, moulds and yeasts) are the main cause of spoilage, and they are the cause this subsection deals with.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.1] Enzyme action (the food's own chemicals continuing to work) causes changes such as browning and over-ripening.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.2] Physical damage and bruising break the surface of food, which lets other spoilage in.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.3] Moisture loss makes food shrink, wrinkle or harden, for example a dried-out piece of fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.4] Rancidity is the going-off of fats, which gives them an unpleasant smell and taste.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.5] Chemical contamination, for example by pesticides, antibiotics or metals, spoils food and can make it unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s3.0.6] Spoilage micro-organisms mainly make food unpleasant, so spoiled food is not always unsafe. Pathogens (disease-causing micro-organisms) can make food unsafe even when it looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.b] Bacteria are the micro-organisms that spoil most protein and moist foods, and they change food in several ways.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.0] Putrefaction: bacteria break down protein in meat, fish and eggs, producing foul odours and a slimy surface.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.1] Souring: lactic acid bacteria ferment the lactose (milk sugar) in milk, producing lactic acid that curdles the milk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.2] Bacteria that survive in food can produce toxins (poisons), which can make food unsafe even when the food looks and smells normal.
[hom-food-safety-s3.1.3] The foods at greatest risk are high-moisture, high-protein foods such as meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk and cooked rice, because bacteria find both water and nutrients in them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.b] Moulds and yeasts spoil foods that bacteria find harder to use, especially acidic and sugary ones.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.0] Moulds grow visibly on food as fluffy, powdery or coloured patches, for example on bread, cheese, fruit and jam, and some produce mycotoxins (poisons made by moulds) that are harmful if eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.1] Mould spores (tiny reproductive cells) spread through the air, which is how they land on food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.2] Yeasts ferment the sugars in fruit juices, jams, syrups and fruit, producing carbon dioxide, alcohol and off-flavours, and sometimes bubbling or swollen containers.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.3] Mould and yeast growth is mostly on the surface of food, where oxygen is available, but moulds also grow through open-textured foods such as bread.
[hom-food-safety-s3.2.4] Acidic, sugary foods favour moulds and yeasts because they tolerate acid better than most bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.b] Micro-organisms spoil food only when the conditions they need are present. Remove or change one of them and growth slows or stops.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.0] Food: micro-organisms need nutrients, particularly protein and carbohydrate, as a source of energy and for building new cells.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.1] Moisture: their chemical reactions and nutrient absorption take place in solution, so dry foods support little growth and high-moisture foods are high risk.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.2] Warmth: most spoilage and poisoning micro-organisms multiply fastest in the danger zone (5 to 63 °C), while cold slows growth, freezing stops it without killing them, and heat kills them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.3] Oxygen: aerobic micro-organisms such as moulds need oxygen, while anaerobic bacteria grow in sealed packs from which oxygen is absent, and facultative ones can adapt to either.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.4] pH (how acid or alkaline food is): most bacteria prefer a near-neutral pH, whereas moulds and yeasts tolerate acid. Lactic acid bacteria are an exception because they tolerate acid, but strongly acidic foods are generally safer.
[hom-food-safety-s3.3.5] Time: micro-organisms need time to multiply, and some bacteria can double in number in about 20 minutes under ideal conditions (binary fission, where one cell divides into two).
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.b] Control of spoilage works either by destroying micro-organisms, keeping them away from food, or restricting one of the conditions they need. The first two principles deal with the micro-organisms themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.0] Heat treatments kill micro-organisms: cooking, pasteurisation, which uses a lower temperature for a set time, and sterilisation, which destroys all micro-organisms and their spores. Canning and bottling also use heat.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.1] Irradiation destroys micro-organisms and parasites in food by exposing it to controlled radiation, without making the food radioactive.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.2] Irradiation can extend shelf life and slow ripening and sprouting in fruit and vegetables, with little effect on colour, flavour or texture.
[hom-food-safety-s3.4.3] Preventing micro-organisms reaching the food in the first place uses hygiene, clean equipment, covering food, sealed packaging, sterile containers and separating raw and cooked food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.b] The other principles leave micro-organisms alive but take away something they need. Most foods use more than one, and applying each to a named food is the usual exam task.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.0] Low temperature: refrigeration slows the growth of micro-organisms, and freezing inactivates them without destroying them, so growth resumes on thawing.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.1] Removing moisture: drying, salting, sugaring and concentrating reduce the water available to micro-organisms. Dissolved salt or sugar draws water out of microbial cells by osmosis, which dehydrates them.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.2] Acidity: pickling in vinegar, or adding lemon juice, lowers the pH to a level that most bacteria cannot tolerate. Chutney and fermentation acids work the same way.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.3] Removing oxygen: vacuum packing and canning exclude air, preventing growth of aerobic micro-organisms, and modified-atmosphere packs replace the air around the food.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.4] Preservatives: chemicals such as sulphur dioxide, benzoates and nitrites inhibit the growth of micro-organisms. Natural preservatives are salt, sugar, vinegar, alcohol and smoke.
[hom-food-safety-s3.5.5] Combining principles: jam, for example, uses heat, sugar, acid and sealing together, so several principles protect the same food.
