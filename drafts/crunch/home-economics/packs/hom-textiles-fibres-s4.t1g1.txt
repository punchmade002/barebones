TARGET hom-textiles-fibres-s4: 4. Fabric profiles
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.b] A profile follows one named fabric from its fibre to its end use, with the evidence used to identify it. Examiners ask for it under set headings, so knowing the structure lets you collect marks heading by heading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.0] Name the fabric and its fibres first, for example woven cotton or knitted polyester, because older marking schemes gave a mark for the name alone.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.1] Fibre production: how the fibre is obtained from its raw material, or made from a polymer (a long chain of repeating units).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.2] Yarn production: how the fibres become a yarn, either by spinning staple fibres (short fibres) or by twisting filaments (continuous fibres).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.3] A yarn is fibres or filaments twisted together, and a fabric is made by weaving or knitting yarns, so keep these three terms apart.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.4] Properties: give two or three properties, each linked to the fibre structure or the yarn, so the property has a reason behind it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.5] Identification: give the burning test result and the microscopic appearance of the fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.6] Uses: give suitable household or clothing uses, each with a reason based on the properties.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.0.7] Other headings seen in past questions are fabric construction technique and fibre composition, so answer exactly the headings the question lists.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.b] Cotton is the usual natural fabric to profile. Its production is a sequence of stages, and each stage has its own job, so learn them in order.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.0] 1. Harvest: the bolls (seed pods) on the cotton plant open when the plant matures, and the fibres are picked by hand or machine.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.1] 2. Ginning: machines separate the fibres from the seeds and remove large impurities, so that only clean fibre goes on to be made into yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.2] 3. Baling and grading: the fibres are baled and graded by length, fineness and colour, so that similar fibres are used together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.3] 4. Cleaning and blending: fibres are cleaned and opened, and mixed so that the yarn is uniform.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.4] 5. Carding: fibres are combed into a loose web and drawn into a continuous sliver (a loose rope of fibres).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.5] 6. Combing (optional): combing before spinning removes short fibres, which gives a finer, smoother yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.1.6] 7. Spinning: the sliver is twisted into yarn, because the twist is what holds the short fibres together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.b] Cotton's properties come from its fibre structure, and the same structure shows up in the burning test and under the microscope.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.0] Properties: cotton is absorbent, strong and stronger when wet, cool and comfortable to wear, washable and dyes well, which is why it suits everyday clothing and household textiles.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.1] Weaknesses: cotton creases easily unless treated, so it often needs ironing; it also shrinks, burns easily, may mildew and has little elasticity.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.2] Burning test: cotton burns quickly with a steady yellow flame, keeps burning when removed from the flame, smells of burning paper and leaves a soft grey ash.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.3] Microscope: each fibre is a flat, twisted ribbon with natural twists (convolutions), and a central canal (lumen) is visible.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.2.4] Uses: shirts, jeans, towels, bed linen, curtains and T-shirts, where absorbency and washability matter.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.b] A profile of any natural fabric is accepted, so you can write about wool, linen or silk instead of cotton. Each needs only a few points under each heading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.0] Wool production: shearing (clipping the fleece from the sheep), grading, scouring (washing out grease and dirt), carding and spinning.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.1] Wool properties: warm, resilient and absorbent, but it shrinks and felts, so it needs careful washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.2] Wool burning test: burns slowly with a smell of burnt hair or feathers and leaves a black, crushable ash.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.3] Linen production: it comes from the flax stem, which is retted, scutched, combed and spun.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.4] Linen properties and test: strong, absorbent and creases easily, so it often needs ironing; it burns quickly with a paper smell, like cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.5] Silk production: it comes from the cocoon, with the continuous filaments reeled off.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.3.6] Silk properties and test: smooth, so it drapes well; it burns slowly with a burnt-hair smell and leaves a crushable black bead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.b] Manufactured fibres are made from chemicals rather than harvested. Nylon is the usual example, and its production turns small molecules into a long chain, then into filaments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.0] 1. Polymer: two chemicals (monomers), joined in equal proportions and heated, link up to form a long nylon polymer.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.1] 2. Extrusion: the molten polymer is forced through a spinneret, a plate with many fine holes, to form filaments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.2] 3. Cooling: the filaments solidify and harden as they cool.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.3] 4. Drawing: the filaments are stretched to line up their molecules, which increases their strength.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.4] 5. Winding: the filaments are wound on bobbins, ready to be made into yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.4.5] Yarn production: the continuous filaments are twisted together into a filament yarn, which can be textured to add bulk.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.b] Nylon's long, aligned molecules and smooth, non-absorbent filaments explain both its properties and what it does in a flame.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.0] Properties: nylon is very strong, elastic, light, abrasion resistant (it resists rubbing and wear), low in absorbency and quick drying, and it resists creasing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.1] Weaknesses: nylon builds up static, may feel hot to wear because it absorbs so little moisture, and is flammable, melting when it burns.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.2] Burning test: nylon melts and shrinks away from the flame, forms a hard bead and gives off a chemical smell.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.3] Microscope: nylon appears as smooth, even, round rod-like filaments with no scales or twists.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.5.4] Uses: hosiery, swimwear, lingerie, jackets and carpets, such as in tights and swimsuits, where strength, stretch and quick drying matter.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.b] Polyester and viscose are also accepted as the manufactured fabric in a profile, and they are made in different ways.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.0] Polyester production: a viscous liquid is extruded through a spinneret and solidifies in cold air.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.1] Polyester properties: strong, crease resistant and non-absorbent, so it dries quickly.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.2] Polyester burning test: it burns and melts, with a sweetish smell, and leaves a hard bead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.3] Viscose production: cellulose is treated and dissolved, then forced through a spinneret into a bath.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.6.4] Viscose properties and test: it is weak when wet, so it needs gentle handling, and it burns like cotton because both are cellulose.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.b] A blend combines two or more fibres in one yarn, or different yarns in the warp and weft, to get the best properties of each. This is different from joining two separate fabrics, because the fibres are combined in the yarn or in warp and weft.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.0] Examples of blends are polyester-cotton, wool-nylon, cotton-Lycra and linen-cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.1] Production: each fibre is produced separately, with cotton harvested, ginned and cleaned and polyester extruded and cut into staple (short) lengths.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.2] Yarn production: the staple fibres are blended before or during carding, then drawn and spun into one yarn.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.3] A blend can also be made by using one fibre yarn for the warp and another for the weft.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.4] Properties: cotton gives absorbency and comfort, while polyester gives strength, crease resistance and quick drying.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.5] Compared with pure cotton, the blend dries faster, is easier to iron and is cheaper; in the same way, linen-cotton is cheaper than pure linen.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.6] Burning test: the results are mixed, because the blend burns like cotton and melts like polyester, so the test is indicative only.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.7] Microscope: both fibre types are visible in the same sample, with cotton's twisted ribbon beside polyester's smooth filaments.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.7.8] Uses: shirts, school uniforms, bed linen and curtains, because easy care and comfort both matter there.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.b] Modification changes a yarn or filament to improve the properties of the fabric made from it. Filament modification changes a continuous filament yarn so that it is bulkier, softer or stretchier, without changing the polymer. Examiners ask for two examples, so know several and what each achieves.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.0] Modification examples in marking schemes include blending, crimping, twisting, mercerising, brushing, flame or stain resistance and water repellence.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.1] Crimping and texturising: the filaments are crimped, looped or curled, trapping air so the yarn is bulkier, warmer and softer, as in textured (bulked) polyester.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.2] Air-jet texturing: compressed air loops the filaments, giving bulk without mechanical crimping.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.3] Heat setting: a textured yarn is heated under tension so that it keeps its crimp or loops in the finished fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.4] Twisting: more twist gives a stronger, smoother yarn, and ply yarns are made by twisting yarns together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.5] Mercerising: cotton under tension is treated with caustic soda, which gives extra lustre, strength, absorbency and a better affinity for dye.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.6] Brushing raises the surface of the fabric, trapping air and giving a warmer, softer fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.8.7] Textured or slub yarns have uneven thick and thin sections, or loops added to the yarn, to create surface interest in a fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.b] Fibre identification is an investigation in which a known control sample is compared with an unknown sample. Two tests are used together because one alone can mislead.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.0] Aim: to identify an unknown fabric sample from its burning behaviour and its microscopic appearance.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.1] Burning method: hold a small fibre sample with tongs or forceps, remove any finish where possible, and bring it slowly towards a flame held over a non-flammable surface.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.2] Observations: record how the fibre behaves as it approaches the flame, as it burns and when removed from the flame, whether it melts, the smell and the appearance of the ash or residue.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.3] Control: so the unknown has something to match, test a known sample of cotton, wool or nylon under the same conditions, and compare the results, so the unknown has something to be matched against.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.4] Microscope: mount a few fibres on a slide, in water, examine them under magnification and compare their shape (twist, scales, rod or ribbon) with reference images.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.5] Safety: ventilate the room, keep water and a fire blanket nearby, use small samples, tie back hair and do not breathe in fumes from the burning sample.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.6] Limits: finishes and dyes change the results, and blends give mixed results, so use both tests together.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s4.9.7] Conclusion: the fibre is identified when both the burning result and the microscopic appearance match a known fibre.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s3: 3. Fibre classification
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.b] A fibre is a hair-like strand, the raw material that is spun into yarn and then made into fabric. Fibres are grouped by where their raw material comes from and how the fibre is formed, which gives three classes: natural, regenerated and synthetic.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.0] Staple fibres are short, such as cotton, wool and linen, so they must be twisted together to make a yarn. Filaments are continuous and very long, such as silk and all man-made fibres as they are extruded (pushed out).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.1] Natural fibres are obtained directly from plants or animals and need only cleaning and processing before they are spun into yarn, which is why they are the simplest class.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.2] Natural fibres of plant origin are made of cellulose (the plant material that forms cell walls), and are called cellulosic: cotton, linen, and also hemp and jute. Natural fibres of animal origin are made of protein: wool and silk.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.3] Regenerated fibres are made by dissolving a natural polymer (a long chain molecule), usually cellulose from wood pulp, and forcing the solution through a spinneret (a plate with fine holes) so that it reforms as a fibre. Examples are viscose (rayon), acetate and triacetate.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.4] Synthetic fibres are made entirely from chemicals: small molecules called monomers, derived from coal or petroleum, are built into polymers. Examples are nylon (polyamide), polyester, acrylic and elastane (Lycra).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.5] Regenerated and synthetic fibres together are called man-made (manufactured) fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.0.6] Diagram: a classification tree branching from textile fibres into natural (plant and animal), regenerated and synthetic, with one named example at the end of each branch.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.b] Each natural fibre comes from one plant or animal source, and that source decides what the fibre is made of and what it is used for. Learn one source, one use and one property for each of the four main fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.0] Cotton: a cellulose fibre from the seed hairs of the cotton plant (the boll); used for shirts, jeans, T-shirts, towels, bed linen and curtains. It is absorbent and cool to wear.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.1] Linen: a cellulose fibre from the stem of the flax plant; used for tea towels, tablecloths, summer clothing and bed linen. It is strong and absorbent, so it suits cloths that must dry dishes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.2] Wool: a protein fibre from the fleece of sheep; used for jumpers, knitwear, tweed, suits, blankets and carpets. It is warm and resilient (springs back into shape).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.3] Silk: a protein filament from the cocoon of the silkworm (the caterpillar of the silk moth, fed on mulberry leaves); used for scarves, ties, formal and evening wear, lingerie and luxury bedding. It is smooth, drapes well and is absorbent.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.4] Cotton and linen absorb moisture readily and are cool to wear; wool and silk are warm because they trap air in their structure.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.1.5] Natural fibres are popular for household textiles because they are absorbent, comfortable, durable, washable, and accept dyes and finishes well. They come from renewable sources, and many people simply prefer natural fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.b] Regenerated fibres start from cellulose that already exists in a plant. The cellulose is dissolved and re-formed as a long filament, which is why they are called regenerated.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.0] Viscose (rayon): cellulose from wood pulp is treated with chemicals, dissolved and forced through a spinneret into a chemical bath, where the fibre reforms. Rayon and viscose are the same fibre family, not two different fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.1] Viscose is absorbent, soft, drapes well and dyes well, but it is weak when wet, so it needs gentle handling. It is used for dresses, blouses, linings and soft furnishings.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.2] Acetate: cellulose is chemically combined with acetic acid, dissolved and extruded to form a fibre with a silky, lustrous appearance. Triacetate is a related fibre.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.3] Acetate drapes well, so it is used for linings, blouses and evening wear. It is also used for ties.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.4] Lyocell is a newer regenerated cellulose fibre made by dissolving wood pulp in a solvent; it is absorbent and soft, much like other cellulose fibres such as cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.2.5] Regenerated fibres start from a plant-based raw material, wood pulp cellulose, so they are classed apart from synthetics, which start from coal or petroleum chemicals.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.3.b] Synthetic fibres are built from chemicals rather than grown, so their properties can be designed. Each one has a typical strength that decides where it is used.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.3.0] Nylon (polyamide): made from chemicals derived from coal or petroleum; very strong, elastic, light, abrasion resistant and low in absorbency. Used for hosiery, swimwear, jackets, carpets and ropes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.3.1] Polyester: made from petroleum-based chemicals; strong, crease resistant and quick drying. Used for shirts, fleece, curtains and bed linen, and often blended with cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.3.2] Acrylic: made from a polymer of acrylonitrile; it resembles wool, being soft, warm and light, so it is used for knitwear, socks and blankets.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.3.3] Elastane is a stretch fibre; Lycra is a brand name for it. It is usually blended in small amounts with other fibres, for swimwear, sportswear and hosiery.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.3.4] Synthetic fibres can be produced in a range of thicknesses and lustres by changing the spinneret holes and the amount of drawing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.b] Both are man-made and both are extruded through a spinneret, but they begin with different raw materials and behave differently. Compare them point by point: source, process, moisture, heat and an example.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.0] Source: regenerated fibres begin with a natural polymer, cellulose from wood pulp; synthetic fibres begin with small molecules, called monomers, made from coal or petroleum.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.1] Process: regenerated fibres are dissolved and reformed from an existing natural polymer; synthetic fibres are formed by linking monomers into a polymer, then extruding it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.2] Moisture: regenerated fibres such as viscose absorb moisture like cotton and drape well; synthetic fibres such as polyester absorb little moisture, so they dry quickly and resist creasing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.3] Heat: nylon and polyester melt at high temperatures, because they are plastic-like polymers, whereas viscose chars and burns like cotton.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.4] For example, regenerated fibres include viscose and acetate; synthetic fibres include nylon, polyester and acrylic.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.4.5] Example in use: a viscose blouse is more absorbent and comfortable next to the skin; a polyester blouse resists creasing and dries more quickly.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.5.b] Regenerated and synthetic fibres are made in the same basic way: a thick liquid is pushed through tiny holes and hardened into long filaments. The steps below apply to both.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.5.0] 1. A thick liquid, either a molten polymer or a polymer solution, is forced through a spinneret, a metal plate with many fine holes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.5.1] 2. The emerging streams harden: by cooling in air (melt spinning), by setting in a chemical bath (wet spinning), or by warm air evaporating the solvent (dry spinning).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.5.2] 3. The filaments are drawn, or stretched, so that their molecules line up, which makes them stronger.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s3.5.3] Diagram: a spinneret cross-section showing polymer pumped through holes, forming filaments that are drawn onto a winding bobbin.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s1: 1. Textiles as a household resource
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.b] A textile is a material made from fibres or yarns (the threads spun from fibres) that has been woven, knitted or otherwise made into fabric. Textiles are a household resource, like food or fuel: they are bought, used, cared for and eventually replaced.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.0] Textiles serve household purposes, such as bedding, curtains, carpets, towels and upholstery, and clothing purposes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.1] Because textiles cost money and need care, choosing well saves money and effort over the life of the item.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.2] Most household textiles do several jobs at once. A curtain, for example, gives privacy, decoration and some warmth.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.b] Textiles are used throughout the home for practical and decorative purposes, often several at once. Examiners often ask for a list of functions, so learn each one with its example item.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.0] Warmth and insulation: blankets, duvets, throws and thick curtains trap still air within their fibres and layers, reducing heat loss from the body.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.1] Sound absorption (sound insulation): carpets, rugs, curtains and upholstery absorb sound and reduce echo in a room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.2] Privacy and light control: curtains, net curtains and blinds screen the room from view and control the light entering it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.3] Comfort: cushions, mattress covers, bed linen and upholstery give softness and support.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.4] Protection: oven gloves and aprons protect the user from heat and spills, carpets protect floors from wear, and mattress protectors protect the mattress.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.5] Absorbency: towels, tea towels, dishcloths and bath mats take up water from the body, from dishes or from the floor, which makes washing and drying easier.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.6] Decoration and aesthetics: curtains, cushions, wall hangings, bedspreads and rugs add colour, pattern and texture to a room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.7] Hygiene: washable textiles can be laundered to remove dirt and micro-organisms. Safety is a further function, because fire-resistant finishes make textiles safer.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.b] A textile is chosen by weighing the properties of the fabric against the job it has to do, the money available and the household's preferences. Examiners often ask for three or four of these criteria, each with an explanation.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.0] Fitness for purpose: the fabric's properties must suit its job, for example a towel needs absorbency and a curtain needs to drape well and resist fading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.1] Suitability in use: the fabric must withstand the wear, washing and conditions of the room it is used in, such as a kitchen, a living room or a child's bedroom. Purpose is the job; use is the wear and conditions, and both come under fitness.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.2] Cost: the price must fit the budget and the expected life of the item, so a durable fabric can be better value. Wool and silk cost more than most other fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.3] Personal choice: likes and dislikes, colour and style, comfort, a preference for natural or easy-care synthetic fabrics, and current trends.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.4] Aesthetic appeal: appearance, drape, weight, texture, lustre, colour and pattern, because the textile must look right in the room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.5] Care and cleaning: whether the fabric is washable or dry clean, how much ironing it needs, and its fade resistance, stain resistance and shrinkage, which affect how practical it is for the household.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.6] Safety: a flame-retardant finish or fire-resistance label is wanted, and a loose or pile surface is avoided because it burns more easily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.7] In a longer Higher Level answer, durability (how long the fabric lasts), finishes (treatments added to improve a fabric) and sustainability (the environmental effect of the fabric) are also accepted, with one developed line on each.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.b] Each property below describes how a fabric behaves in use. Define each in plain words and give an example fabric, since examiners ask what a property means and which fabric has it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.0] Absorbent: the fabric takes in and holds moisture. Cotton and linen are absorbent, which makes them suitable for towels, and an absorbent fabric also takes up dye easily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.1] Flammable: the fabric ignites and burns readily when exposed to a flame, creating a fire risk. Cotton burns easily, so flammability is not wanted in household textiles.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.2] Crease-resistant: the fabric resists wrinkling in wear and washing and returns towards its smooth shape, so less ironing is needed. Polyester and wool resist creasing, but cotton and linen crease unless finished.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.3] Colourfast: the colour resists running in water and fading in light, so it stays bright after washing, because the dye is fixed firmly to the fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.4] Durable (hard wearing): the fabric resists abrasion, tearing and wear over a long period of use.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.5] Drape: the way a fabric hangs in folds. A good drape suits curtains and loose clothing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.6] Other properties examiners accept as desirable are warm, strong, washable, easy to dye, shrink resistant, stain resistant, quick drying and resilient (springs back into shape).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.7] Static: synthetic fibres build up static electricity, so the fabric clings, and fabric conditioner reduces this. Wool can shrink in hot washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.b] Choosing a fabric means matching its properties to the job of the item. When you recommend a fabric for a stated item, give the fabric and a reason from its properties.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.0] Cotton towels: absorbent, strong when wet and able to withstand hot washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.1] Linen tea towels: absorbent, strong and quick drying, but they crease easily unless treated.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.2] Polyester curtains: drape well, resist fading, wash easily, dry quickly and resist creasing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.3] Cotton curtains: easy to launder and dye, with a crisp appearance and strong when wet, but they shrink if not pre-shrunk.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.4] Wool blankets: warm, insulating, absorbent and elastic (resilient), but they can shrink and felt (the fibres mat together) if washed at high temperatures or carelessly.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.5] Carpets in nylon or wool: strong, hard wearing and resistant to abrasion from foot traffic.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.6] Bed linen in cotton or polyester-cotton: washable, strong and easy to iron.

CANDIDATES hom-clothing-selection-s1: 1. Textile care
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.b] A garment's care label is not arbitrary: a fabric's properties come from its fibres, its yarn and its construction, and these decide how it reacts to water, heat, friction and detergent. Knowing the fibre family tells you how to wash, dry and iron it safely.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.0] Natural cellulose fibres (cotton, linen) absorb water readily, swell when wet and are strong when wet, so they can be washed hot and often. They also crease easily and tend to shrink on first washing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.1] Protein fibres (wool, silk) absorb moisture but are damaged by alkaline detergents and high temperatures, so they need a mild or wool-specific detergent, a low temperature and gentle action. Wool is washed cool, about 30 °C, as the label states.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.2] Wool can felt or shrink when agitated in hot water, which is why hot, rough washing ruins it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.3] Manufactured fibres such as polyester and nylon absorb very little water, dry quickly, keep their shape and resist creasing. They can attract static, and they melt or glaze under a hot iron or high tumble-dry heat.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.4] Blended fabrics combine the care needs of both fibres, so the more sensitive (most delicate) fibre sets the washing temperature and ironing heat. For example, a wool/polyester mixture is washed and ironed as wool.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.0.5] Fabric construction matters too: a tightly woven fabric resists water and wind more than a loosely knitted one, because the gaps between yarns are smaller. Knitted fabric stretches and can sag or shrink.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.b] Shrinkage is a permanent reduction in a fabric's length or width after laundering. It happens in three different ways, and the cause decides how to prevent it.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.0] Relaxation shrinkage happens because fibres stretched during spinning, weaving and finishing relax back to their natural length in water. It is most marked in cotton, linen and viscose.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.1] Felting shrinkage happens to wool when the scales on its fibres interlock under heat, moisture and agitation. The fabric thickens and shrinks, and this cannot be reversed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.2] Heat shrinkage occurs when a fabric is washed or tumble dried hotter than it can tolerate, especially in knitted and thermoplastic fabrics (those that soften with heat).
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.3] Percentage shrinkage = (original length − new length) ÷ original length × 100.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.4] Prevention: follow the care label, wash at the stated temperature and avoid hot tumble drying, because heat is a common cause.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.1.5] Pre-shrunk fabric or a shrink-resistant finish reduces shrinkage in garments that will be laundered often. Washing a test sample of fabric before cutting out a garment shows how much it will shrink.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.b] Water repellence is how well a fabric resists wetting. It matters for rainwear and for choosing the right fabric for a garment.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.0] Three things give a fabric water repellence: a close weave or tight knit, so the gaps between yarns are too small for water to penetrate; a fibre that absorbs little water; and a water-repellent finish.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.1] Fibres that do not absorb water, such as polyester and nylon, shed water more readily than cotton or linen. Cotton and wool absorb water, so they are not naturally repellent.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.2] A water-repellent finish coats the surface of the fabric with a chemical so that water forms beads that roll off.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.3] Washing and wear reduce a finish, so a repellent garment may need re-proofing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.4] Water-repellent (showerproof) fabric resists wetting but will let water through in heavy or prolonged rain.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.5] A waterproof fabric (coated or laminated, with sealed seams) stops water passing through entirely.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.2.6] Breathable fabrics let perspiration vapour escape while keeping rain out, so the wearer stays dry inside as well as outside.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.b] Crease recovery is how well a fabric springs back to its smooth state after being folded or crushed. It decides how much ironing a garment needs.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.0] A crease forms when fibres are bent and the bonds between them shift, holding the fabric in the folded position.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.1] Cotton, linen and viscose have poor recovery, so they crease easily and need ironing. In cotton and linen, cellulose chains form hydrogen bonds that break and re-form when the fabric is wet and dried.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.2] Wool recovers well because its fibres are elastic, and creases hang out or can be relaxed by steaming.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.3] Polyester and nylon have good crease recovery because their fibres spring back to shape once the folding force is removed.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.4] Crease-resistant finishes, such as resin finishes on cotton or linen, improve recovery and cut down ironing. Blending with polyester also helps.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.3.5] Care link: damp garments can be shaken and hung to reduce creases, and ironing temperature must match the fibre.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.b] Care labels use a fixed set of symbols so that anyone can read how to launder a garment safely, whatever language the label is in. Reading the label before buying and before washing helps you avoid shrinkage, colour loss and damage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.0] The symbols are international (standard ISO 3758; GINETEX is the European association behind them) and appear in a fixed order: washing, bleaching, drying, ironing, then professional care.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.1] Wash: a washtub means the garment may be machine washed, and the number or dots in the tub give the maximum water temperature. Bars under the tub mean gentler action.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.2] A hand in the tub means hand wash only, usually at a low temperature with gentle action. A cross over the tub means do not wash in water.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.3] Bleach: a triangle means bleach may be used; a crossed-out triangle means do not bleach.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.4] Drying: a square containing a circle means tumble drying is allowed, with dots showing the heat setting; a crossed tumble-dry symbol means do not tumble dry.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.5] A square with a horizontal line means dry flat; a square with one vertical line means line dry. Drip dry means hang the wet garment to drip without wringing.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.6] Iron: one dot means a cool (low) iron, two dots a warm or medium iron and three dots a hot iron; a crossed iron means do not iron.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.7] Dry cleaning: a circle shows dry cleaning is allowed, and the letters inside tell the cleaner which solvent process to use. A crossed circle means do not dry clean.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.4.8] Diagram: draw and label the washtub (with 30 or 40 or dots), hand wash, triangle with a cross, tumble dry, dry flat, iron with two dots, and circle (dry clean).
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.b] A modern laundry detergent contains several components, each with a specific job in removing soil from the fabric. Choosing a detergent to match the fabric depends on knowing what each does.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.0] Surfactants reduce the surface tension of water, so the water can wet the fibres thoroughly. One end of a surfactant molecule attracts water and the other attracts oil and grease, so they lift oily and greasy soil away and break it into small particles.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.1] Surfactants hold the loosened soil suspended in the wash water, so it is rinsed away and does not redeposit on the fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.2] Enzymes (in biological detergents) are proteins that act as biological catalysts, speeding up the breakdown of stains: protease breaks down protein stains such as blood and egg, amylase breaks down starch stains and lipase breaks down fat stains.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.3] Enzymes work best in warm, not hot, water and are damaged or destroyed by very high temperatures.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.4] Fluorescent whitening agents (optical brighteners) absorb ultraviolet light and re-emit it as blue-white light, which makes white and light fabrics look brighter and whiter.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.5] Fluorescent agents do not clean or remove stains; they only change how the fabric reflects light.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.5.6] Match the detergent to the fabric: use a mild detergent for wool and silk. A biological detergent is not for wool or silk, because enzymes can attack their protein fibres.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.b] Simple tests let you compare how different fabrics behave, using their general properties of shrinkage, water repellence and crease recovery. They only give useful results if the test is fair.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.0] Shrinkage test: mark and measure a square on each sample, wash and dry under identical conditions, remeasure and calculate the percentage shrinkage.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.1] Water-repellence test: place drops of water on each sample (flat or on a slope) and time or observe whether the drops bead and roll off, or soak in and darken the fabric.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.2] Crease-recovery test: crumple equal-sized samples in a closed fist for one minute, release, and compare how smooth each is after a set time.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.3] Fair test: use equal-sized samples, the same conditions and the same timing, so that the only variable is the fabric. Name the fibre or fabric of each sample.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.4] Repeat the test on several samples of each fabric to give reliable results, note any variation, and record the results in a table.
[hom-clothing-selection-s1.6.5] Draw a conclusion on how suitable each fabric is for a garment, using your results.
