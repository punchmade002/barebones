MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-housing-s1: 1. Housing choices
[hom-housing-s1.0.b] A household chooses a home by weighing what it needs and can afford against what is available in the place it wants to live. No single factor decides alone, so a good answer shows how several of them pull against each other.
[hom-housing-s1.0.0] Socio-economic factors include income (which decides whether a household can afford a deposit or rent), employment, household size and the stage of the family life cycle, such as a young couple, young children, teenagers, an empty nest or an older couple whose children have left home.
[hom-housing-s1.0.1] Each stage of the family life cycle needs a different amount of space, which is why a growing family and an older couple choose differently.
[hom-housing-s1.0.2] Cost decides the size, type and location of home a household can afford. The deposit and running costs matter as much as the purchase price or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.0.3] Legal and lender fees, stamp duty, repayments, insurance, maintenance, heating and travel to work must all be counted, not just the headline price. Parental help with a deposit is also an economic factor.
[hom-housing-s1.0.4] Special needs, such as those of older people or people with a disability, may call for single-storey living, step-free access or a stairlift, because stairs and steps can make a home unusable.
[hom-housing-s1.1.b] Once a household knows what it needs and can afford, it looks at where homes are, what kinds exist and whether any are on offer.
[hom-housing-s1.1.0] Location is shaped by proximity to work, schools, shops, childcare, public transport, health services and family or friends. Urban areas offer services and jobs, while rural areas offer space and quiet. Safety, noise and risks such as flooding or pollution also count.
[hom-housing-s1.1.1] Personal preference covers layout, aspect (the direction the home faces), view, garden size and whether the household wants to own or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.1.2] Type and size of home: a household chooses between a bungalow, two-storey, detached, semi-detached, terraced, townhouse, duplex, apartment or bedsit. It also weighs room for the family, a possible attic conversion or extension, parking, space to work from home, and energy efficiency, shown by the BER.
[hom-housing-s1.1.3] Availability, meaning the supply of suitable homes at a given time and place, limits choice. In some urban and commuter areas private supply is short, which raises prices and rents, and social housing waiting lists are long because demand exceeds supply.
[hom-housing-s1.1.4] Setting: a case study of a student, a young couple or a retired couple, asking which of these factors matters most to them. For example, a student may rank low cost and closeness to college first, while a retired couple may rank step-free access and nearby health services first.
[hom-housing-s1.2.b] The way new homes are planned and built changes over time. Examiners want you to recognise the current trends and say what each means for households.
[hom-housing-s1.2.0] Mixed estates plan detached, semi-detached, terraced and apartment or duplex units together, so that different household types live in the same community.
[hom-housing-s1.2.1] New developments include shared open space and play areas, and in larger estates a crèche or shops, so families have facilities on their doorstep. Many estates and complexes have a gated or controlled entrance.
[hom-housing-s1.2.2] Refurbishment and inner-city redevelopment bring vacant, derelict and older buildings back into residential use. This includes the restoration of period homes, attic conversions and extensions, which add space without using new land.
[hom-housing-s1.2.3] Apartment living and higher-density development grow in cities and towns, using less land per household. Townhouses are built on small urban sites for the same reason.
[hom-housing-s1.2.4] An eco-house (an energy-efficient, environmentally friendly or passive home) is an emerging trend. They have high insulation and renewable heating, and may use timber frame or other modern methods of construction, so they cost less to heat.
[hom-housing-s1.2.5] Housing development follows employment. Demand rises where jobs grow, which pulls people towards commuter towns and suburbs around cities, and commuting distance becomes a cost to the household. Older marking schemes also name satellite towns and one-off rural houses.
[hom-housing-s1.2.6] Working from home creates demand for a study or home-office space, which affects the size and layout of home a household looks for.
[hom-housing-s1.3.b] Housing policy is the government's plan for how enough suitable homes are provided and who is helped. The current national plan is Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025 to 2030, published by the Government in November 2025. It rests on activating supply and supporting people.
[hom-housing-s1.3.0] Increasing the supply of new homes, with investment in the infrastructure (water, wastewater and roads) that housing needs, because homes cannot be built where these services are missing.
[hom-housing-s1.3.1] Social housing is built by local authorities and approved housing bodies, and social and affordable housing is delivered by these together with voluntary and co-operative housing organisations.
[hom-housing-s1.3.2] Affordable homeownership supports include the Starter Homes programme, and cost rental homes are let at rents based on the cost of providing them.
[hom-housing-s1.3.3] Measures to reduce and prevent homelessness include the Housing First programme, and rented accommodation must be registered, so that rented homes are on a register.
[hom-housing-s1.3.4] National rent controls protect tenants, and modern methods of construction (factory-built components) speed up building.
[hom-housing-s1.3.5] Sustainable communities are a policy aim: new housing should be supported by shops, schools, transport, parks and other infrastructure and services. Higher-density living and increased housing supply in urban areas aim to make more homes available at affordable cost.
[hom-housing-s1.3.6] Energy-efficient homes are promoted through the Building Regulations, BER ratings and SEAI grants for upgrading existing homes.
[hom-housing-s1.3.7] Accommodation for Travellers is planned by each local authority under a Traveller Accommodation Programme, required by the Housing (Traveller Accommodation) Act 1998.
[hom-housing-s1.3.8] Policy affects a household's choice because it changes what is built and where, what supports and grants are open to a buyer or tenant, and how much a tenant is protected.
[hom-housing-s1.4.b] Buying makes the household an owner-occupier. It gives control and a possible asset, but it demands money up front and a long commitment.
[hom-housing-s1.4.0] Advantage: a home may rise in value, so it can act as an investment, and ownership builds equity (the part of the home's value the owner owns).
[hom-housing-s1.4.1] Advantage: security of tenure, meaning the right to stay in the home, because an owner cannot be asked to leave by a landlord.
[hom-housing-s1.4.2] Advantage: freedom to choose the design and type of home, to alter and decorate it, and to decide whether to share it with others.
[hom-housing-s1.4.3] Advantage: mortgage repayments (the regular payments on a loan taken to buy the home) may cost less than rent over the long term, and grants or other supports may apply to some purchases.
[hom-housing-s1.4.4] Disadvantage: a large deposit and a long financial commitment are needed before the purchase can be completed.
[hom-housing-s1.4.5] Disadvantage: legal, lending, valuation and survey fees and stamp duty add to the initial cost on top of the price.
[hom-housing-s1.4.6] Disadvantage: the owner pays for all maintenance, repairs and insurance, and property tax where it applies, because there is no landlord to carry them.
[hom-housing-s1.4.7] Disadvantage: the value may fall, leaving the owner owing more than the home is worth, and buying ties the household to one location, so moving is slow and expensive.
[hom-housing-s1.5.b] A tenant pays a landlord (the owner who lets the home) for the right to live in a property, under a tenancy. Renting asks for little money at the start but gives no ownership.
[hom-housing-s1.5.0] Advantage: little capital is needed, usually only a deposit and rent paid in advance.
[hom-housing-s1.5.1] Advantage: mobility, so a household can move when work or family circumstances change.
[hom-housing-s1.5.2] Advantage: the landlord is normally responsible for structural repairs and maintenance, so the tenant has no repair bills.
[hom-housing-s1.5.3] Advantage: a wider choice of location and accommodation, from apartments and houses to shared houses and digs.
[hom-housing-s1.5.4] Advantage: tenants have legal protections, including notice rules before a tenancy can be ended.
[hom-housing-s1.5.5] Disadvantage: rent paid gives no ownership or return, often described as dead money. The rent can also be as high as a mortgage repayment on a similar home, and there is no long-term security if the landlord sells.
[hom-housing-s1.5.6] Disadvantage: an agency fee, a deposit and rent in advance add to the initial cost.
[hom-housing-s1.5.7] Disadvantage: the tenant must insure the contents of the home.
[hom-housing-s1.5.8] Disadvantage: facilities may be poor or shared, and the tenant has no say over changes to the property.
[hom-housing-s1.6.b] The private rented sector is made up of homes let by private landlords rather than by the State. Its standards and registration are regulated to protect tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.0] Private rented housing (houses, apartments, flats and rooms) is provided by individual landlords and businesses, mainly in cities and larger towns. Rent depends on demand and supply, location, size, quality and amenities, and quality varies.
[hom-housing-s1.6.1] Landlords must register tenancies with the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), which helps the state track rented housing and its standards. The RTB also deals with disputes between landlords and tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.2] Rented homes must meet the minimum standards in the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019, enforced by local authorities, who inspect rented homes. The property must be structurally sound and in good repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.3] Every room must have adequate ventilation and lighting, hot and cold water must be provided, and there must be heating and fire safety. Gas and electricity supplies must be safe.
[hom-housing-s1.6.4] Bathrooms, showers and other sanitary facilities must be in good working order and kept in a proper state of repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.5] Private renting is popular with students, young workers and people who are saving for a deposit to buy a home. It gives flexibility to people in temporary work or who want to move, and the State helps some tenants with rent through social housing supports.
