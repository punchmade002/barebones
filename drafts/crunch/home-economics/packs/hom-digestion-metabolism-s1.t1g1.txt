TARGET hom-digestion-metabolism-s1: 1. Digestion and absorption of protein
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.b] Digestion is the breakdown of large food molecules into small soluble ones that can pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood. It is chemical digestion, carried out by enzymes through a reaction called hydrolysis.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.0] Enzymes are proteins that act as biological catalysts, which means they speed up digestion without being used up.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.1] Each enzyme has an active site (the part where the food molecule binds) whose shape fits one type of substrate (the molecule the enzyme acts on), so an enzyme acts on only one kind of molecule.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.2] Enzymes lower the activation energy of a reaction (the energy needed to get it started), so digestion proceeds at body temperature, about 37 °C.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.3] Each digestive enzyme has an optimum pH, the acidity at which it works best: pepsin works in strong acid, while pancreatic and intestinal enzymes work in the alkaline conditions of the small intestine.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.4] Hydrolysis breaks a chemical bond by adding water: a hydrogen atom attaches to one part of the molecule and a hydroxyl group (–OH) to the other.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.5] Protein is a chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds, so proteases (also called proteinases), the enzymes that digest protein, work by hydrolysing these bonds.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.6] As protein is digested the chains get shorter in this order: protein, then peptones (shorter chains), then peptides (shorter again), then amino acids. Only amino acids, and the very smallest peptides, are small enough to absorb.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.0.7] Diagram: two amino acids joined by a peptide bond, with the water molecule added during hydrolysis labelled.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.1.b] Examiners ask you to fill in this table, so learn each row as a set of five linked words. The substrate is the molecule the enzyme acts on and the product is what it is turned into.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.1.0] Stomach: gastric juice; pepsin; protein; peptones.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.1.1] Stomach of infants: gastric juice; rennin; caseinogen; casein.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.1.2] Pancreas (its enzyme acts in the duodenum): pancreatic juice; trypsin (a proteinase); peptones; peptides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.1.3] Small intestine: intestinal juice; peptidases (also called erepsin); peptides; amino acids.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.b] Protein digestion starts in the stomach, not the mouth. The stomach uses acid to prepare the protein and then an enzyme to start splitting it.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.0] In the mouth no protein is digested, because saliva contains no protein-digesting enzyme. Chewing breaks food into pieces and so increases its surface area, but this is mechanical only.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.1] Gastric glands in the stomach wall secrete gastric juice, which contains hydrochloric acid and the inactive enzyme pepsinogen.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.2] Hydrochloric acid unfolds (denatures) the protein, exposing its peptide bonds to enzyme action. Acid also kills most bacteria in food.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.3] Hydrochloric acid converts pepsinogen into the active enzyme pepsin, and also gives the acid pH of about 2 that pepsin works best at.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.4] Pepsin hydrolyses protein into peptones, which are shorter chains than the original protein.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.5] Protein-digesting enzymes are themselves proteins, so pepsinogen is made inactive and activated only in the stomach. This is why it does not digest the cells that make it.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.6] Rennin, found in the stomach of infants, clots milk by changing the soluble milk protein caseinogen into insoluble casein (curds), so milk stays in the stomach long enough for pepsin to act on it.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.2.7] The stomach churns food into chyme, a semi-liquid mixture that passes slowly into the duodenum (the first part of the small intestine).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.b] Peptones leaving the stomach are still too large to absorb. In the small intestine two more enzymes, one from the pancreas and one from the intestinal wall, finish the job.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.0] The pancreas is a gland, not part of the alimentary canal (the tube food passes through), so food never enters it. It secretes pancreatic juice into the duodenum through the pancreatic duct.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.1] Pancreatic juice contains trypsin, which is made in inactive form as trypsinogen so that it cannot digest the cells that make it.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.2] Enterokinase, an enzyme from the lining of the small intestine found in the intestinal juice, converts trypsinogen into active trypsin.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.3] Pancreatic juice is alkaline because it also contains alkaline salts, so it neutralises the acid chyme and gives trypsin the working pH it needs.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.4] Trypsin hydrolyses peptones into shorter peptides, which are still too large to be absorbed.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.5] Intestinal juice is secreted by glands in the wall of the small intestine and contains peptidases (erepsin).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.3.6] Peptidases hydrolyse peptides into amino acids, the main form in which protein is absorbed. Protein digestion is now complete.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.b] Trace the journey of protein from mouth to liver in order, naming the organ, secretion, enzyme, substrate and product at each stage, then how the products are absorbed.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.0] Mouth: no protein is digested, because saliva contains no protein-digesting enzyme.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.1] Stomach: pepsin acts on the protein (and rennin in infants), and chyme passes into the duodenum.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.2] Duodenum: trypsin and then peptidases complete digestion, and pancreatic juice enters through the pancreatic duct.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.3] Villi of the small intestine: amino acids are absorbed into the capillaries (the tiniest blood vessels) inside each villus.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.4] The capillaries join to form the hepatic portal vein, which carries the absorbed amino acids from the small intestine to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.5] Liver: processes the amino acids before they enter the general circulation.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.4.6] Diagram: the digestive system, labelled mouth, oesophagus, stomach, liver, pancreas, small intestine, large intestine and hepatic portal vein, with where protein digestion begins and ends marked.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.b] Absorption is the passage of digested nutrients through the wall of the small intestine into the blood or lymph. The small intestine is built to make this fast.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.0] The small intestine is long, and its lining is folded into villi, finger-like projections whose cells have microvilli (tiny folds on their surface), so the surface area for absorption is very large.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.1] Each villus has a thin one-cell-thick wall, so nutrients have only a short distance to cross, a capillary network, and a central lacteal (a lymph vessel).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.2] Amino acids are absorbed mainly by active transport, which uses energy and carrier proteins and is coupled with the uptake of sodium ions.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.3] Amino acids move through the cells lining each villus into the capillary network inside it. They do not enter the lacteal, which is the route for fat.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.4] Very small peptides (two or three amino acids) may be absorbed too. They are broken down into amino acids inside the cells.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.5.5] Diagram: cross-section of a villus showing the capillary network, lacteal, epithelial wall (the one-cell-thick lining) and microvilli.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.b] Once absorbed, amino acids are used where the body needs them. The body has no store of amino acids, so any not needed are broken down in the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.0] Body cells take up amino acids from the blood and use them to build body protein for growth and repair, and to make enzymes, hormones and antibodies.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.1] The liver makes plasma proteins, such as albumin, from amino acids and passes the rest on in the blood.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.2] Amino acids cannot be stored, so surplus amino acids are broken down in the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.3] Deamination is the removal of the amino group (–NH₂) from an amino acid. In the liver it is converted to ammonia (a nitrogen-containing compound formed when the amino group is removed from an amino acid), and then to urea because ammonia is toxic.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.4] Urea is carried in the blood to the kidneys, filtered out and excreted in urine.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s1.6.5] The remaining carbon part, with its carboxyl group (–COOH), is oxidised to release heat and energy, or converted into glucose or fat. This is why protein is an energy source when eaten in excess.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s1: 1. Composition and amino acids
[hom-proteins-s1.0.b] Protein is built from a small set of chemical elements, and one of them, nitrogen, is found in no other nutrient. This is what makes protein different from carbohydrate and fat.
[hom-proteins-s1.0.0] Every protein contains carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Some proteins also contain small amounts of sulphur, phosphorus and iron.
[hom-proteins-s1.0.1] Protein is the only nutrient that contains nitrogen. Carbohydrate and fat contain only carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, so they contain none. Nitrogen is the element essential for growth.
[hom-proteins-s1.0.2] These elements are arranged into basic units called amino acids. A protein is a large molecule made of many amino acids joined together.
[hom-proteins-s1.0.3] Nitrogen sits in the amino group (the –NH₂ part) of every amino acid, so the nitrogen in a food comes from its protein.
[hom-proteins-s1.0.4] Sulphur is found in only some amino acids, such as cysteine and methionine, which is why only some proteins contain it.
[hom-proteins-s1.1.b] Every amino acid has the same central carbon atom bonded to four groups. Only the side group differs between amino acids, and that difference is what makes each one unique.
[hom-proteins-s1.1.0] An amino group, –NH₂, which carries the nitrogen. It is basic (alkaline).
[hom-proteins-s1.1.1] A carboxyl group, –COOH, which is the acidic part of the amino acid.
[hom-proteins-s1.1.2] A hydrogen atom.
[hom-proteins-s1.1.3] A variable side group, written R. It differs between amino acids and gives each one its identity. R is a group of atoms, not an element.
[hom-proteins-s1.1.4] The body builds its proteins from about 20 different amino acids. Different proteins are different sequences of the same 20, much as different words are made from the same letters.
[hom-proteins-s1.1.5] Diagram: draw a central carbon bonded to H, R, –COOH and –NH₂. Label each group, naming the amino group (–NH₂), the carboxyl group (–COOH) and R as the side group that varies.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.b] The body can make some amino acids itself but not others. Those it cannot make have to arrive in food, which is why the idea matters for diet.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.0] Essential (indispensable) amino acids cannot be made by the body, or not in adequate amounts, so they must be supplied by the diet. Lysine is an example.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.1] Eight amino acids are essential for adults: lysine, leucine, isoleucine, valine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine and tryptophan.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.2] Children also need histidine and arginine, which makes ten essential amino acids for children. Give the figures of eight for adults and ten for children; if a question gives nine, accept it, since some sources count histidine as essential for adults too.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.3] Non-essential amino acids can be made by the body from other amino acids and nitrogen-containing compounds, so food does not need to supply them.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.4] Examples of non-essential amino acids: alanine, asparagine, aspartic acid, cysteine, glutamic acid, glutamine, glycine, proline, serine and tyrosine.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.5] Non-essential does not mean unimportant. It means a dietary supply is not normally required, because the body can make them.
[hom-proteins-s1.2.6] A protein's biological value (its quality as a source of amino acids) depends on whether it supplies all the essential amino acids in the right proportions.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.b] Amino acids become proteins by being joined end to end. The link between two amino acids is called a peptide bond.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.0] A peptide bond forms when the carboxyl group (–COOH) of one amino acid reacts with the amino group (–NH₂) of another. It is not formed between the R groups.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.1] A molecule of water is released in this reaction, so peptide bond formation is a condensation reaction.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.2] The link formed is –CO–NH–.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.3] Two amino acids joined by one peptide bond form a dipeptide. A short chain of amino acids is a peptide, and a long chain is a polypeptide.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.4] A protein is made of one or more polypeptide chains.
[hom-proteins-s1.3.5] Diagram: draw two amino acids side by side, then joined. Label the –CO–NH– link and the molecule of water released.
[hom-proteins-s1.4.b] Hydrolysis is the opposite of the reaction that builds protein. It is how long protein chains are broken back into their amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s1.4.0] Hydrolysis breaks a peptide bond by adding a molecule of water, which reverses the condensation reaction.
[hom-proteins-s1.4.1] Repeated hydrolysis splits a polypeptide into smaller peptides and finally into free amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s1.4.2] In the body the enzymes pepsin (in the stomach) and trypsin (in the small intestine) do this in digestion. Acid and heat can also hydrolyse protein.
