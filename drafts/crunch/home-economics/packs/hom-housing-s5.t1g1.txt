MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-housing-s5: 5. Location, planning and professional services
[hom-housing-s5.0.b] Where a home is built shapes the household's daily life and costs for years. The choice depends on how suitable the site is, what the household needs and what the site costs. A local authority is the county or city council, and its development plan sets how land in the area may be used.
[hom-housing-s5.0.0] Urban or rural setting: urban sites give shops, transport, services and jobs, but cost more and offer less space. Rural sites give space, quiet and a lower land price, but may need a private water supply and a wastewater treatment system, and mean longer travel.
[hom-housing-s5.0.1] Proximity to family, work, schools, childcare, shops, health services and public transport affects the daily time and cost of travel, and the ease of family life.
[hom-housing-s5.0.2] Ground conditions: a stable soil structure, good slope and drainage and freedom from flood risk are checked by a site survey before building, because a poor site can damage the house later.
[hom-housing-s5.0.3] Shelter from wind matters, and so does the absence of risks nearby such as pollution, pylons, factories or landfill.
[hom-housing-s5.0.4] Aspect (the direction the site faces) and orientation of the house determine how much daylight and solar heat it receives. They also affect privacy and views.
[hom-housing-s5.0.5] Services available: electricity, water, sewerage, gas, broadband and refuse collection, plus the cost of connecting them, which adds to the real price of a site.
[hom-housing-s5.0.6] Budget and value: the price of the site and the cost of connecting services limit the location a household can afford. Future developments in the area affect the prospects for the value of the property.
[hom-housing-s5.0.7] Building and planning regulations, including the development plan, can restrict where and how a house may be built. In a case study, apply all these factors to the needs of the family described to choose the location, house design, interior and services.
[hom-housing-s5.1.b] Once a site is chosen, the style of house must suit the family, the site and the budget. A house that suits one family can be a poor fit for another.
[hom-housing-s5.1.0] House style is chosen by comparing traditional and modern designs, and bungalow and two-storey layouts, against the family's budget.
[hom-housing-s5.1.1] The size, stage (age group) and lifestyle of the family determine the number of bedrooms, the amount of living space and the need for storage, because a growing family needs more of each.
[hom-housing-s5.1.2] Special needs, such as a wheelchair user, may require a single-storey house, step-free access and wider doors so that the person can move around independently.
[hom-housing-s5.1.3] Potential to convert the attic, extend the house or add a garage must be checked against planning rules for the area, so that future changes are allowed.
[hom-housing-s5.1.4] Personal taste and the style of neighbouring houses determine whether a design fits its surroundings.
[hom-housing-s5.2.b] There are two kinds of permission, and they differ in how much detail the applicant must supply and in what each one settles. Outline permission is a first check on the idea. Full permission is the approval needed to build.
[hom-housing-s5.2.0] Planning permission from the local authority is required for most building work unless the development is exempt under the planning regulations, for example some small extensions.
[hom-housing-s5.2.1] Outline permission establishes whether the planning authority agrees in principle to a development, before detailed plans are prepared. It is not permission to build.
[hom-housing-s5.2.2] An outline application needs only a site map showing the location and layout of the proposed house, with basic details of the proposal, not detailed house plans. This saves the cost of drawing full plans for a site that may be refused.
[hom-housing-s5.2.3] Full permission requires detailed plans and drawings, the site layout, house plans, elevations and finishes, details of water supply and drainage, and the materials to be used in construction.
[hom-housing-s5.2.4] Full permission also requires the appropriate fee, copies of the public notices (newspaper and site notice) and the position of the site notice.
[hom-housing-s5.2.5] A person who is granted outline permission must still get full permission (detailed approval) before building.
[hom-housing-s5.3.b] The procedure for full permission is set in the Planning and Development Act 2024. It follows a fixed order so that the public can see and comment on a proposal before the council decides. Learn the order of the steps.
[hom-housing-s5.3.0] 1. Optional first step: hold a pre-planning meeting with the planning authority to discuss the proposal before applying, and prepare drawings with an architect.
[hom-housing-s5.3.1] 2. Publish a notice of intention to apply in a local newspaper, and erect a site notice on the proposed site, so that neighbours and the public are told.
[hom-housing-s5.3.2] 3. Lodge the application with the local authority: the application form, the site maps, the house plans and drawings, the materials, a copy of the notices and the fee. The authority acknowledges receipt.
[hom-housing-s5.3.3] 4. The application is placed on the planning register, where members of the public can inspect it and lodge a written submission or objection.
[hom-housing-s5.3.4] 5. Planning officials inspect the site and examine the plans against the development plan before a decision is made. The authority may ask for further information.
[hom-housing-s5.3.5] 6. The authority grants permission, often with conditions, or refuses it. If permission is refused, the applicant must be given the reasons.
[hom-housing-s5.3.6] 7. Appeals against the decision of the planning authority are made to An Coimisiún Pleanála, which replaced An Bord Pleanála in June 2025. An applicant who is refused, or a person who objected, may appeal. If nobody appeals, permission is confirmed.
[hom-housing-s5.3.7] Diagram: a flowchart of the sequence from the pre-planning meeting and the notices through to the decision and the appeal route.
[hom-housing-s5.4.b] Planning control is the system that decides what may be built and where. Without it, anyone could build anything anywhere, so permission is needed for most building work.
[hom-housing-s5.4.0] Developments must fit in with the local development plan for the area, which is how the council plans land use.
[hom-housing-s5.4.1] Houses must not be built so that they overlook neighbours, which protects their privacy.
[hom-housing-s5.4.2] Industrial factories must not be built in residential areas, so that homes are kept away from noise and pollution.
[hom-housing-s5.4.3] Scenic areas, tourist attractions and old buildings are protected, so scenic areas have restrictions on house building.
[hom-housing-s5.4.4] New houses must conform to the style of others in the area, and property values are protected.
[hom-housing-s5.4.5] Services and infrastructure are planned, so that roads, water and sewerage can cope with new development.
[hom-housing-s5.5.b] Building a house involves legal, technical and practical work that most families cannot do themselves. Each professional has a separate role, and exam marks go to the specific role, not to a vague statement that they help with the house.
[hom-housing-s5.5.0] Architect: advises on the site and design, prepares plans and drawings from the family's brief and budget, prepares and submits the planning application, advises on builders and materials, and may supervise construction.
[hom-housing-s5.5.1] Engineer (structural or site engineer): advises on the structure and foundations, supervises structural work and solves building problems.
[hom-housing-s5.5.2] Surveyor: surveys the site and building, checks boundaries and defects, identifies potential problem areas and recommends how to deal with them before they affect the building. A quantity surveyor estimates and controls costs, and a surveyor may draw up a snag list of faults before final payment.
[hom-housing-s5.5.3] Solicitor: handles the legal side, including contracts, checking title deeds, rights of way (checked with the Land Registry), registering ownership, legal advice on building and planning regulations and loan paperwork.
[hom-housing-s5.5.4] Builder: agrees the price and completion date in a contract, prepares the site, manages subcontractors such as electricians, plumbers, plasterers and tilers, follows the drawings and rectifies faults.
[hom-housing-s5.5.5] Other services include interior designers, landscape architects, electricians, plumbers and BER assessors. An interior designer brings expertise and access to suppliers, but at a cost.
[hom-housing-s5.5.6] A book of house plans is a catalogue of ready-drawn designs. It is cheaper than commissioning an architect, but the plans are not tailored to the site or the family, must still be submitted for planning permission and often need an architect or engineer to adapt them to the site.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s3: 3. Housing styles
[hom-housing-s3.0.b] Early nineteenth-century housing in Ireland reflected the materials available locally and the wealth of each household. In the countryside the size and roof of a home told you whether the family were labourers, farmers or landowners.
[hom-housing-s3.0.0] Labourers and small farmers lived in single-storey thatched cottages, usually of two rooms (a kitchen and a bedroom), with solid stone or mud walls, which were cheap and came from the local area.
[hom-housing-s3.0.1] Cottages had a half-door and a small number of small windows. The half-door opened into the kitchen and could be closed at the bottom to keep animals out and let in light and air.
[hom-housing-s3.0.2] Thatch was made of reeds, straw or hay, and an open hearth in the main room was used for both cooking and heating, so the kitchen was the centre of family life.
[hom-housing-s3.0.3] Some cottages had a loft directly under the thatch, reached by a ladder, where children slept. This used the roof space as extra sleeping room without adding to the building.
[hom-housing-s3.0.4] Prosperous farmers lived in two-storey, slate-roofed houses with several bedrooms and a parlour (a sitting room kept for visitors), showing their greater wealth.
[hom-housing-s3.0.5] Wealthy landowners lived in large estate houses, with servants' quarters generally in the basement.
[hom-housing-s3.0.6] Diagram: a thatched cottage in cross-section, showing the thatched roof, the loft reached by ladder, the open hearth and the half-door.
[hom-housing-s3.1.b] Towns developed their own styles of housing, and the gap between rich and poor was very visible in the streets. Keep these urban types separate from the rural ones, because examiners do.
[hom-housing-s3.1.0] Georgian terraces in towns (rows of joined houses, for example in Georgian Dublin such as Merrion Square) were two, three or four storeys high, with basements, high ceilings and prominent doors, and were built for the better-off.
[hom-housing-s3.1.1] The middle classes lived in small terraced houses in the suburbs, away from the crowded town centre.
[hom-housing-s3.1.2] Shopkeepers often lived above their shops, which kept them close to their business.
[hom-housing-s3.1.3] Workers lived in small single-storey cottages or, in the towns, in crowded tenements (large buildings divided into rooms let to many families), where space and sanitation were poor.
[hom-housing-s3.2.b] Better transport in the later nineteenth century changed what houses were built from and how they looked, and it changed who lived where in towns.
[hom-housing-s3.2.0] Improved transport brought a wider variety of building materials, because heavy goods such as slate could now be moved cheaply over long distances.
[hom-housing-s3.2.1] Houses became more ornate, with Gothic and Tudor styles.
[hom-housing-s3.2.2] Two-storey, slate-roofed stone buildings became the most common rural dwelling by the end of the century.
[hom-housing-s3.2.3] As wealthier families moved to suburban terraced houses, many large Georgian houses in towns were divided and let to poorer families, which added to overcrowding in town centres.
[hom-housing-s3.3.b] The twentieth century brought new house types and new materials, and for the first time governments played a part in providing and improving homes.
[hom-housing-s3.3.0] Detached houses (standing alone) and semi-detached houses (joined to one neighbour) appeared, along with council housing (homes built by the local authority) and new housing estates.
[hom-housing-s3.3.1] Brick with plaster walls replaced stone, and tiles replaced slates; two-storey houses were built where land was expensive, because building up used less land.
[hom-housing-s3.3.2] Rural electrification in the mid-twentieth century changed rural homes, bringing electric light and power to the countryside.
[hom-housing-s3.3.3] Government housing grants funded improvements to rural houses.
[hom-housing-s3.3.4] The slate-roofed bungalow gradually replaced the thatched cottage in rural Ireland.
[hom-housing-s3.4.b] Later in the twentieth century, housing was built in larger numbers and to higher standards, with new materials that made homes warmer and easier to maintain.
[hom-housing-s3.4.0] Private estates were built to a common design, while individual private houses varied between classical and modern designs.
[hom-housing-s3.4.1] High-rise blocks were built in some cities, for example the Ballymun flats in Dublin in the 1960s.
[hom-housing-s3.4.2] The Building Regulations (legal minimum standards for construction) greatly improved housing standards.
[hom-housing-s3.4.3] New materials and methods such as double glazing, PVC and fibreglass came into use, which cut heat loss and reduced maintenance.
[hom-housing-s3.4.4] Indoor sanitation improved, so that homes had proper indoor toilets and bathrooms.
[hom-housing-s3.5.b] Today's housing is shaped by the cost of land, changing households and concern for the environment, so there is a much wider mix of types than in earlier centuries.
[hom-housing-s3.5.0] Twenty-first-century estates usually consist of one style but include single-storey detached, dormer and two-storey semi-detached houses, which gives a mix of homes for different households.
[hom-housing-s3.5.1] Apartments, townhouses and mixed-density schemes (a mix of house and apartment types on one site) are built, including gated complexes.
[hom-housing-s3.5.2] Old dilapidated buildings and thatched cottages are restored, and custom-designed houses are built on private sites.
[hom-housing-s3.5.3] Attic space is converted into rooms with Velux roof windows, and extensions, conservatories and sunroom extensions are added to many houses to give extra living space.
[hom-housing-s3.5.4] Modern homes include home-office space and open-plan living (a kitchen, dining and living area in one space).
[hom-housing-s3.5.5] Attention is paid to insulation, glazing, security, accessibility and renewable energy, reflecting greater environmental awareness.
[hom-housing-s3.6.b] Each change in style across the centuries had causes, and naming them turns a list of periods into a developed answer.
[hom-housing-s3.6.0] Materials and transport: better transport brought slate, brick and tiles to places that once used only local materials.
[hom-housing-s3.6.1] Rising incomes let more families afford larger and better-built homes.
[hom-housing-s3.6.2] Population moved from the country to towns, which led to terraces, tenements, suburbs and later apartments.
[hom-housing-s3.6.3] Government housing policy and grants, and building regulations, led to council housing, rural improvements and higher standards.
[hom-housing-s3.6.4] Family size and land values: smaller families and expensive land led to semi-detached houses, townhouses and apartments.
[hom-housing-s3.6.5] Environmental awareness led to a focus on insulation, glazing and renewable energy.
[hom-housing-s3.7.b] Different styles suit different households, so each is popular for a reason. Tying each style to the type of household it suits is what examiners reward.
[hom-housing-s3.7.0] Bungalows are single-storey, so they suit older people, people with mobility needs and rural sites, but they need a large site. The dormer bungalow adds bedrooms in the roof space.
[hom-housing-s3.7.1] Two-storey detached houses suit large families and sites with space for a garden, and give privacy, but they cost more to buy and to heat.
[hom-housing-s3.7.2] Semi-detached and terraced houses make efficient use of land and cost less; a terraced house loses less heat than a detached house because it shares walls with its neighbours. The drawbacks are less privacy, noise from neighbours and a small garden.
[hom-housing-s3.7.3] Apartments and duplexes save space and maintenance and are usually in secure complexes with communal space, so they suit first-time buyers, singles, downsizers and city living. The drawbacks are limited space, no private garden and management fees.
[hom-housing-s3.7.4] Townhouses are multi-storey homes built on small urban sites, with limited outdoor space.
[hom-housing-s3.7.5] Traditional, neo-Georgian and restored period houses offer proportion and character, while modern minimalist homes are bright and easy to care for.
[hom-housing-s3.7.6] Custom-designed one-off houses, timber-frame and energy-efficient or passive houses are other styles, and old cottages are kept for holiday and local use in tourist areas.
[hom-housing-s3.8.b] A home has to work for the people living in it, so the make-up and needs of the household strongly affect the style chosen.
[hom-housing-s3.8.0] Family size and stage, and the ages of children, decide how many bedrooms and how much space are needed, so a growing family may choose a two-storey house.
[hom-housing-s3.8.1] Special needs or disability may call for a single-storey home, such as a bungalow, with no stairs.
[hom-housing-s3.8.2] Some households want a garden or a home office, which rules out styles with no private outdoor space.
[hom-housing-s3.8.3] Wanting to be near family and community, and the sense of belonging this gives, can affect where and what a household chooses.
[hom-housing-s3.9.b] Culture shapes what people like and what a place looks like, so it affects both what is built and what people choose to buy.
[hom-housing-s3.9.0] Personal taste, aesthetics and fashion influence choice, for example a preference for a traditional or a modern minimalist look.
[hom-housing-s3.9.1] Regional building traditions and local materials, such as stone or slate, shape the look of rural houses in each area.
[hom-housing-s3.9.2] Respect for heritage and the character of the area leads many people to restore older houses or build in keeping with their surroundings.
[hom-housing-s3.10.b] Money is often the deciding factor, because it limits what a household can buy or build and what it can afford to run.
[hom-housing-s3.10.0] Income and the money available decide what a household can afford, so a first-time buyer may choose an apartment or a terraced house.
[hom-housing-s3.10.1] The price of land affects the cost of any style, which is why cheaper land allows larger, detached houses.
[hom-housing-s3.10.2] The cost of building and the running costs, such as heating, matter because a large detached house costs more to heat than a terraced one.
[hom-housing-s3.10.3] Resale value and investment potential matter because a home is usually a household's largest purchase.
[hom-housing-s3.10.4] A household also decides whether to build, buy new or buy second-hand.
[hom-housing-s3.11.b] The site and its surroundings affect how comfortable a home is and what may be built on it.
[hom-housing-s3.11.0] Aspect (the direction a house faces) matters because south-facing rooms gain daylight and solar heat.
[hom-housing-s3.11.1] Shelter from wind and rain, and exposure to them, affect comfort and heating costs.
[hom-housing-s3.11.2] The slope of the site affects what style can be built on it.
[hom-housing-s3.11.3] Energy efficiency through insulation and glazing lowers heating costs and heat loss.
[hom-housing-s3.11.4] The style must fit the surrounding landscape and neighbouring buildings, and planning rules control scale and materials in many areas.
[hom-housing-s3.11.5] Setting: a school visit to houses from different periods, or photographs of house styles, can be used to discuss their suitability to their surroundings.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s4: 4. Housing provision
[hom-housing-s4.0.b] A home that suits one household can be unworkable for another. Each group needs its own mix of space, location, access and services, and in an exam you should say who each requirement serves and why.
[hom-housing-s4.0.0] Families need enough bedrooms for every member, adequate storage, a well-equipped, ventilated and well-lit kitchen, and a living room for relaxing and watching television, so that everyone has space for their own needs.
[hom-housing-s4.0.1] A larger family also needs more than one bathroom, and a hot-press (an airing cupboard for the hot water tank and linen) helps with laundry.
[hom-housing-s4.0.2] Families with young children need a safe, enclosed back garden so the children can play outdoors without danger, and safe stairs. Teenagers need space to study, and families with a car or sports equipment may need a garage or shed.
[hom-housing-s4.0.3] Families also need to be close to schools, childcare, shops, parks and public transport, which cuts the time and cost of daily journeys for school-going children and working parents.
[hom-housing-s4.0.4] Single people usually need smaller, affordable accommodation, such as an apartment, bedsit, small townhouse or a room in a shared house, because they have one income. It should be close to work, college or public transport, with privacy, security and space for study.
[hom-housing-s4.1.b] For these groups the home itself must be adapted, because stairs, steps and standard fittings can make an ordinary house unsafe or unusable.
[hom-housing-s4.1.0] Older people need single-storey living living, or a ground-floor bedroom and bathroom, or a stairlift where there are stairs, because climbing stairs becomes difficult. They need a level or ramped entrance and non-slip floors with safe lighting to prevent falls.
[hom-housing-s4.1.1] Older people also need a home that is warm, well insulated and easy to heat, an alarm or emergency call system, and nearness to shops, health services, public transport and community and family support. Sheltered housing (accommodation designed for older people) is one option.
[hom-housing-s4.1.2] People with disabilities need adjustments that depend on the disability. For mobility: ramps for wheelchair or walking-aid users, wide doorways and hallways, lowered worktops, lowered switches and sockets at reachable heights, a raised toilet with grab rails, an accessible bathroom or shower, and a lift or stairlift.
[hom-housing-s4.1.3] For a hearing disability, flashing lights are linked to the doorbell and alarms, so the person is alerted without hearing a sound. For a sight disability, audible or tactile alarms and good lighting help, and communication or alarm systems are needed for emergencies.
[hom-housing-s4.2.b] Homeless people first need somewhere safe to stay tonight, and the aim is then a permanent home with support.
[hom-housing-s4.2.0] Homeless people need emergency shelter in hostels or emergency bed and breakfast accommodation, ideally regular rather than a nightly queue for a bed, with washing and laundry facilities, privacy and dignity, a safe place to store possessions and a communal space (kitchen or day room) to reduce isolation.
[hom-housing-s4.2.1] For homeless people the next step is rent-assisted private accommodation or local authority housing, and then a permanent home with support.
[hom-housing-s4.2.2] Serviced halting sites (sites with water and other services) exist for Travellers who live in mobile homes or caravans.
[hom-housing-s4.3.b] The State and voluntary bodies provide homes for people who cannot afford to buy or rent privately. Several routes exist, and each works differently.
[hom-housing-s4.3.0] Local authority housing is rented housing for households on a lower income who cannot afford a home. It is allocated by need through a social housing assessment and a waiting list, with priority given to households unable to house themselves.
[hom-housing-s4.3.1] Local authorities consider household size, income, current accommodation, age and disability. Families, people with disabilities and older people are often given priority, so single people can wait longer.
[hom-housing-s4.3.2] Voluntary housing is provided by approved housing bodies (non-profit voluntary organisations), often for specific groups such as older people, people with disabilities or homeless people. The tenant's landlord is the approved housing body.
[hom-housing-s4.3.3] Co-operative housing is built by a group of members who pool the costs of the site, builder and solicitor, share costs and manage the housing on non-profit principles. A local authority may provide a low-cost site for a co-operative whose members are on its waiting list.
[hom-housing-s4.3.4] The Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) is a local authority support for private rented housing: the local authority pays the private landlord and the tenant pays a contribution. The Rental Accommodation Scheme (RAS) is where the local authority arranges long-term private rented homes for households with a long-term need.
[hom-housing-s4.3.5] Cost rental means homes let at a rent based on cost rather than profit, for households above social housing income limits. Do not confuse it with social housing (let at subsidised rent) or with affordable housing (sold below market price).
[hom-housing-s4.3.6] Shared equity and affordable purchase: under the First Home scheme the State and participating lenders take an equity share in a new home, which the buyer can buy out later. Local authorities also sell affordable homes below market price.
[hom-housing-s4.3.7] Adaptation grants help older people and people with disabilities to adapt a home so they can stay in it, and specific provision exists for homeless people, older people and Travellers. Demand for social housing exceeds supply, so applicants can wait for a number of years for a home.
[hom-housing-s4.4.b] Distribution is about where homes are and in what tenure (the way a home is held: owned, rented privately or rented from a social landlord). It is separate from whether those homes meet need.
[hom-housing-s4.4.0] By tenure, homes are owner-occupied (with a mortgage or owned outright), privately rented or socially rented from a local authority or approved housing body. Owner-occupation is the largest tenure in Ireland, followed by private renting and social housing.
[hom-housing-s4.4.1] By sector, private housing is built by developers and sold or let on the open market, while social housing is built and allocated by local authorities and approved housing bodies for people who cannot afford a home.
[hom-housing-s4.4.2] By location, urban areas have higher density, more apartments and greater demand, while rural areas have one-off houses and bungalows and, in some places, vacant homes.
[hom-housing-s4.4.3] Strong demand in cities and commuter areas pushes up prices and rents, while some rural areas have vacant housing but fewer jobs and services, so empty homes do not solve the shortage where people want to live.
[hom-housing-s4.5.b] A home can exist and still be a poor place to live. Quality is judged by structure and repair, insulation and heating, damp and ventilation, lighting, space for the household, safety and facilities.
[hom-housing-s4.5.0] A Building Energy Rating (BER) certificate rates a home's energy efficiency from A, the most efficient, to G, the least efficient, so buyers and tenants can compare running costs.
[hom-housing-s4.5.1] Older housing stock is often poorly insulated and costly to heat, which can cause fuel poverty (being unable to afford to heat the home adequately). New homes must meet the Building Regulations.
[hom-housing-s4.5.2] Damp, poor ventilation and inadequate lighting reduce the quality of accommodation and can affect health. Cold and overcrowding also harm health, and school and work performance.
[hom-housing-s4.5.3] Rented housing must meet legal minimum standards under the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019, which local authorities enforce.
[hom-housing-s4.6.b] Buying and renting both cost money, but at different times and with different returns. Comparing them means looking at initial costs, ongoing costs and what you are left with.
[hom-housing-s4.6.0] Buying has initial costs: a deposit, legal fees, lender, valuation and survey fees, stamp duty on the purchase, and furnishing.
[hom-housing-s4.6.1] Buying has ongoing costs: mortgage repayments, mortgage protection and home insurance, property tax, maintenance and repairs, and service charges for apartments. It builds equity (the part of the home you own), may rise in value and gives security.
[hom-housing-s4.6.2] Renting has initial costs of a deposit (a month's rent is the usual) and rent in advance, and an agency fee where an agent is used. Ongoing costs are rent, contents insurance and utilities.
[hom-housing-s4.6.3] The tenant has no repair costs but builds no equity, so rent is sometimes called dead money. Renting needs less upfront capital and gives mobility, with lower upfront costs than buying.
[hom-housing-s4.6.4] In high-demand urban areas, rent can be as high as, or higher than, mortgage repayments on a similar home, so renting for years can cost more than buying, but buying requires a deposit that many people cannot save.
[hom-housing-s4.6.5] Rural homes are often cheaper to buy than urban ones, but residents may have to travel further to reach schools, shops and jobs.
[hom-housing-s4.7.b] Adequacy asks whether the housing on offer matches what households need. It goes beyond counting dwellings.
[hom-housing-s4.7.0] Adequacy means housing must be enough in number, affordable, secure, suitable, accessible, in good condition and near employment, transport, schools, healthcare and shops, for each type of household.
[hom-housing-s4.7.1] Strengths include a wide variety of house types and tenures, social housing through local authorities, approved housing bodies and co-operatives, cost rental, rent supports (HAP and RAS), supports for first-time buyers, adaptation grants, and better standards through the Building Regulations and the BER.
[hom-housing-s4.7.2] Weaknesses include high prices and rents in cities, waiting lists for social housing, homelessness, poor access for some people with disabilities and older people, poor stock in places, vacant property, and uneven distribution between urban and rural areas.
[hom-housing-s4.7.3] Emergency accommodation does not offer the stability of a permanent home, and long stays in it are a weakness.
[hom-housing-s4.7.4] Low-income families, lone parents, single people (lower priority for social housing), homeless people, Travellers, older people, people with disabilities, students and new arrivals (refugees and asylum seekers) may not get housing suited to their needs.
[hom-housing-s4.7.5] Local authority building, voluntary and co-operative schemes increase supply, and adaptation grants help particular groups to stay in their homes.
[hom-housing-s4.7.6] Renovating vacant property and planning mixed, serviced developments are further ways to improve adequacy, along with more social, affordable and cost rental homes and planning for need.
[hom-housing-s4.8.b] A house is only as useful as what is around it. Amenities and services meet daily needs, and an estate without them isolates its residents.
[hom-housing-s4.8.0] Schools, childcare and playgrounds serve children and reduce long journeys for families.
[hom-housing-s4.8.1] Shops, post offices, banks, health services, pharmacies and community centres provide necessities and social contact, which builds a sense of community.
[hom-housing-s4.8.2] Public transport gives access to employment and services, so a development without transport isolates its residents.
[hom-housing-s4.8.3] Parks, sports grounds and play areas promote exercise, leisure and community interaction.
[hom-housing-s4.8.4] Street lighting improves safety, and regular refuse collection keeps the area hygienic.
[hom-housing-s4.8.5] Accessible healthcare, pharmacies and services for older people or people with disabilities support independence.
[hom-housing-s4.8.6] Setting: a group housing study of a local area such as a townland, village or housing estate, recording which amenities are present and which are missing.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s1: 1. Housing choices
[hom-housing-s1.0.b] A household chooses a home by weighing what it needs and can afford against what is available in the place it wants to live. No single factor decides alone, so a good answer shows how several of them pull against each other.
[hom-housing-s1.0.0] Socio-economic factors include income (which decides whether a household can afford a deposit or rent), employment, household size and the stage of the family life cycle, such as a young couple, young children, teenagers, an empty nest or an older couple whose children have left home.
[hom-housing-s1.0.1] Each stage of the family life cycle needs a different amount of space, which is why a growing family and an older couple choose differently.
[hom-housing-s1.0.2] Cost decides the size, type and location of home a household can afford. The deposit and running costs matter as much as the purchase price or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.0.3] Legal and lender fees, stamp duty, repayments, insurance, maintenance, heating and travel to work must all be counted, not just the headline price. Parental help with a deposit is also an economic factor.
[hom-housing-s1.0.4] Special needs, such as those of older people or people with a disability, may call for single-storey living, step-free access or a stairlift, because stairs and steps can make a home unusable.
[hom-housing-s1.1.b] Once a household knows what it needs and can afford, it looks at where homes are, what kinds exist and whether any are on offer.
[hom-housing-s1.1.0] Location is shaped by proximity to work, schools, shops, childcare, public transport, health services and family or friends. Urban areas offer services and jobs, while rural areas offer space and quiet. Safety, noise and risks such as flooding or pollution also count.
[hom-housing-s1.1.1] Personal preference covers layout, aspect (the direction the home faces), view, garden size and whether the household wants to own or rent.
[hom-housing-s1.1.2] Type and size of home: a household chooses between a bungalow, two-storey, detached, semi-detached, terraced, townhouse, duplex, apartment or bedsit. It also weighs room for the family, a possible attic conversion or extension, parking, space to work from home, and energy efficiency, shown by the BER.
[hom-housing-s1.1.3] Availability, meaning the supply of suitable homes at a given time and place, limits choice. In some urban and commuter areas private supply is short, which raises prices and rents, and social housing waiting lists are long because demand exceeds supply.
[hom-housing-s1.1.4] Setting: a case study of a student, a young couple or a retired couple, asking which of these factors matters most to them. For example, a student may rank low cost and closeness to college first, while a retired couple may rank step-free access and nearby health services first.
[hom-housing-s1.2.b] The way new homes are planned and built changes over time. Examiners want you to recognise the current trends and say what each means for households.
[hom-housing-s1.2.0] Mixed estates plan detached, semi-detached, terraced and apartment or duplex units together, so that different household types live in the same community.
[hom-housing-s1.2.1] New developments include shared open space and play areas, and in larger estates a crèche or shops, so families have facilities on their doorstep. Many estates and complexes have a gated or controlled entrance.
[hom-housing-s1.2.2] Refurbishment and inner-city redevelopment bring vacant, derelict and older buildings back into residential use. This includes the restoration of period homes, attic conversions and extensions, which add space without using new land.
[hom-housing-s1.2.3] Apartment living and higher-density development grow in cities and towns, using less land per household. Townhouses are built on small urban sites for the same reason.
[hom-housing-s1.2.4] An eco-house (an energy-efficient, environmentally friendly or passive home) is an emerging trend. They have high insulation and renewable heating, and may use timber frame or other modern methods of construction, so they cost less to heat.
[hom-housing-s1.2.5] Housing development follows employment. Demand rises where jobs grow, which pulls people towards commuter towns and suburbs around cities, and commuting distance becomes a cost to the household. Older marking schemes also name satellite towns and one-off rural houses.
[hom-housing-s1.2.6] Working from home creates demand for a study or home-office space, which affects the size and layout of home a household looks for.
[hom-housing-s1.3.b] Housing policy is the government's plan for how enough suitable homes are provided and who is helped. The current national plan is Delivering Homes, Building Communities 2025 to 2030, published by the Government in November 2025. It rests on activating supply and supporting people.
[hom-housing-s1.3.0] Increasing the supply of new homes, with investment in the infrastructure (water, wastewater and roads) that housing needs, because homes cannot be built where these services are missing.
[hom-housing-s1.3.1] Social housing is built by local authorities and approved housing bodies, and social and affordable housing is delivered by these together with voluntary and co-operative housing organisations.
[hom-housing-s1.3.2] Affordable homeownership supports include the Starter Homes programme, and cost rental homes are let at rents based on the cost of providing them.
[hom-housing-s1.3.3] Measures to reduce and prevent homelessness include the Housing First programme, and rented accommodation must be registered, so that rented homes are on a register.
[hom-housing-s1.3.4] National rent controls protect tenants, and modern methods of construction (factory-built components) speed up building.
[hom-housing-s1.3.5] Sustainable communities are a policy aim: new housing should be supported by shops, schools, transport, parks and other infrastructure and services. Higher-density living and increased housing supply in urban areas aim to make more homes available at affordable cost.
[hom-housing-s1.3.6] Energy-efficient homes are promoted through the Building Regulations, BER ratings and SEAI grants for upgrading existing homes.
[hom-housing-s1.3.7] Accommodation for Travellers is planned by each local authority under a Traveller Accommodation Programme, required by the Housing (Traveller Accommodation) Act 1998.
[hom-housing-s1.3.8] Policy affects a household's choice because it changes what is built and where, what supports and grants are open to a buyer or tenant, and how much a tenant is protected.
[hom-housing-s1.4.b] Buying makes the household an owner-occupier. It gives control and a possible asset, but it demands money up front and a long commitment.
[hom-housing-s1.4.0] Advantage: a home may rise in value, so it can act as an investment, and ownership builds equity (the part of the home's value the owner owns).
[hom-housing-s1.4.1] Advantage: security of tenure, meaning the right to stay in the home, because an owner cannot be asked to leave by a landlord.
[hom-housing-s1.4.2] Advantage: freedom to choose the design and type of home, to alter and decorate it, and to decide whether to share it with others.
[hom-housing-s1.4.3] Advantage: mortgage repayments (the regular payments on a loan taken to buy the home) may cost less than rent over the long term, and grants or other supports may apply to some purchases.
[hom-housing-s1.4.4] Disadvantage: a large deposit and a long financial commitment are needed before the purchase can be completed.
[hom-housing-s1.4.5] Disadvantage: legal, lending, valuation and survey fees and stamp duty add to the initial cost on top of the price.
[hom-housing-s1.4.6] Disadvantage: the owner pays for all maintenance, repairs and insurance, and property tax where it applies, because there is no landlord to carry them.
[hom-housing-s1.4.7] Disadvantage: the value may fall, leaving the owner owing more than the home is worth, and buying ties the household to one location, so moving is slow and expensive.
[hom-housing-s1.5.b] A tenant pays a landlord (the owner who lets the home) for the right to live in a property, under a tenancy. Renting asks for little money at the start but gives no ownership.
[hom-housing-s1.5.0] Advantage: little capital is needed, usually only a deposit and rent paid in advance.
[hom-housing-s1.5.1] Advantage: mobility, so a household can move when work or family circumstances change.
[hom-housing-s1.5.2] Advantage: the landlord is normally responsible for structural repairs and maintenance, so the tenant has no repair bills.
[hom-housing-s1.5.3] Advantage: a wider choice of location and accommodation, from apartments and houses to shared houses and digs.
[hom-housing-s1.5.4] Advantage: tenants have legal protections, including notice rules before a tenancy can be ended.
[hom-housing-s1.5.5] Disadvantage: rent paid gives no ownership or return, often described as dead money. The rent can also be as high as a mortgage repayment on a similar home, and there is no long-term security if the landlord sells.
[hom-housing-s1.5.6] Disadvantage: an agency fee, a deposit and rent in advance add to the initial cost.
[hom-housing-s1.5.7] Disadvantage: the tenant must insure the contents of the home.
[hom-housing-s1.5.8] Disadvantage: facilities may be poor or shared, and the tenant has no say over changes to the property.
[hom-housing-s1.6.b] The private rented sector is made up of homes let by private landlords rather than by the State. Its standards and registration are regulated to protect tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.0] Private rented housing (houses, apartments, flats and rooms) is provided by individual landlords and businesses, mainly in cities and larger towns. Rent depends on demand and supply, location, size, quality and amenities, and quality varies.
[hom-housing-s1.6.1] Landlords must register tenancies with the Residential Tenancies Board (RTB), which helps the state track rented housing and its standards. The RTB also deals with disputes between landlords and tenants.
[hom-housing-s1.6.2] Rented homes must meet the minimum standards in the Housing (Standards for Rented Houses) Regulations 2019, enforced by local authorities, who inspect rented homes. The property must be structurally sound and in good repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.3] Every room must have adequate ventilation and lighting, hot and cold water must be provided, and there must be heating and fire safety. Gas and electricity supplies must be safe.
[hom-housing-s1.6.4] Bathrooms, showers and other sanitary facilities must be in good working order and kept in a proper state of repair.
[hom-housing-s1.6.5] Private renting is popular with students, young workers and people who are saving for a deposit to buy a home. It gives flexibility to people in temporary work or who want to move, and the State helps some tenants with rent through social housing supports.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s6: 6. House design and building standards
[hom-housing-s6.0.b] A house is lived in for decades, so its design has to look good, work with the site and sun, and cause as little harm to the environment as possible. These factors shape the plan before any room is drawn.
[hom-housing-s6.0.0] Aesthetic factors: the elements of design (colour, pattern, shape, texture and line) and principles such as proportion, balance and harmony are used to create an attractive home that does not date quickly, so it stays pleasant and keeps its appeal.
[hom-housing-s6.0.1] Aspect (the direction a room faces): a south-facing living room gains the most daylight and solar heat, so living rooms go on the south side while bedrooms and utility rooms are placed according to their use and the sun's path.
[hom-housing-s6.0.2] Passive solar design uses the layout, orientation and glazing (glass areas) of the house to capture and retain heat without mechanical systems, which cuts heating costs because the sun does part of the work.
[hom-housing-s6.0.3] Environmental factors include using durable, sustainable and locally sourced materials, and minimising the energy used in making them and in running the house, so the home has a smaller impact over its life.
[hom-housing-s6.0.4] The house should fit its surroundings, with its scale and materials suiting the landscape and neighbouring buildings, so it does not look out of place.
[hom-housing-s6.0.5] Site slope and drainage affect the layout of the house, the foundations and the position of the garden, because a sloping or wet site needs different foundations and the water has to be drained away from the building.
[hom-housing-s6.1.b] A home must suit the people living in it now and the people they will become. Planning for both avoids costly alterations later.
[hom-housing-s6.1.0] The number and ages of family members determine the number of bedrooms, the bathrooms and the amount of living space, because a growing family needs more room than a couple.
[hom-housing-s6.1.1] Storage for clothing, sports and garden equipment must be provided in proportion to the size of the family, so that belongings do not clutter the living areas.
[hom-housing-s6.1.2] Teenagers need study space, and a home office may be needed where family members work from home, so a quiet room or corner is planned in.
[hom-housing-s6.1.3] Young children need safe design, such as a layout that allows supervision, so adults can see and reach them easily.
[hom-housing-s6.1.4] Room use should be planned so that each room suits its function, such as a utility room for washing and drying, which keeps laundry out of the kitchen.
[hom-housing-s6.1.5] Future needs are planned for by allowing space to extend, convert the attic or add a ground-floor bedroom, and by flexible rooms, for example a playroom that becomes a study as children grow.
[hom-housing-s6.1.6] Accessibility for ageing or for people with disabilities requires wide doors, level access and an accessible bathroom, so the home stays usable if someone's mobility changes.
[hom-housing-s6.1.7] To evaluate a house plan for a stated family, look at the relationship of kitchen, living and bedrooms, use of space, safety and supervision for children, storage, light and aspect, flexibility, accessibility and cost. Then name a strength, a weakness and a modification with a reason.
[hom-housing-s6.2.b] A well-designed house keeps its heat in and uses fuel efficiently, which saves money and makes the home comfortable. The Building Energy Rating (BER) is the label that shows how well a home does this, much like the energy label on an appliance.
[hom-housing-s6.2.0] Insulation in the roof, walls (including cavity walls) or floor reduces heat loss, and double or triple glazing reduces heat loss through windows; draught proofing and a compact shape also cut the heat that escapes.
[hom-housing-s6.2.1] Heating systems can be made efficient with zoned heating (separate areas heated separately), thermostats and timers, or geothermal heating, so heat is supplied only where and when it is needed.
[hom-housing-s6.2.2] Renewable energy such as solar panels or a heat pump, efficient LED lighting and efficient appliances also cut fuel use, and passive solar gain heats the home for free.
[hom-housing-s6.2.3] A BER certificate rates a home's energy performance on a scale from A, the most efficient, to G, the least efficient. The very top band is now A0, a home that uses no fossil fuel for heating.
[hom-housing-s6.2.4] A registered BER assessor inspects the home and calculates the rating, measuring the area of the rooms and windows, the thickness of the walls, the levels of insulation, the heating system and the floor and wall types. The assessor then issues an advisory report on improvements.
[hom-housing-s6.2.5] A BER certificate is required by law when a home is built, sold or rented, and the rating must be shown in every advertisement for sale or rent, so buyers and tenants can compare running costs. It lasts ten years unless the home is upgraded.
[hom-housing-s6.2.6] Insulation, efficient heating and passive solar design all improve the BER rating and reduce running costs, and a better BER also means a more comfortable home and often a higher value.
[hom-housing-s6.3.b] Ergonomics means designing a space to suit the way people move and work in it, so everyday tasks are easy and safe. Technology adds ways to save energy and labour and to protect the home.
[hom-housing-s6.3.0] Ergonomics is the design of a space so that it suits how people move and work; in a kitchen, the sink, cooker and fridge are placed at the three points of an imaginary triangle (the work triangle), so the cook does not walk far between them.
[hom-housing-s6.3.1] Circulation space must allow people to move safely between work surfaces and to open doors and drawers without obstruction, because cramped layouts cause accidents.
[hom-housing-s6.3.2] Switches and sockets are placed at heights that can be reached comfortably, including by children and people with limited mobility, and counter heights and storage within easy reach follow the same idea. Stairs should be safe and the layout accessible.
[hom-housing-s6.3.3] Sensor lights and LED lamps reduce energy use in the home, because LEDs use little electricity and sensor lights switch on only when someone is there.
[hom-housing-s6.3.4] Zoned heating with programmers and smart thermostats, underfloor heating, solar thermal and solar photovoltaic panels, heat pumps and heat-recovery ventilation save energy, while centralised vacuum systems and smart home controls save labour.
[hom-housing-s6.3.5] Phone-linked alarm systems and electronic gates and garage doors add security to the home, because the owner can monitor and control access from anywhere.
[hom-housing-s6.3.6] Modern methods of construction, such as factory-built timber frame or panels, are a technological development in how the house itself is built.
[hom-housing-s6.4.b] The price of a house is more than the cost of building it. Costs fall into what you pay at the start and what you keep paying to maintain and run the home.
[hom-housing-s6.4.0] Initial costs include the site and servicing, the builder, materials, professional fees (architect, engineer), legal fees, planning and lender costs, mortgage costs, stamp duty on a purchase, and furnishing.
[hom-housing-s6.4.1] Maintenance costs include cleaning, decorating, roof and gutter repair, servicing of the heating system and replacing worn parts, and they recur throughout the life of the house.
[hom-housing-s6.4.2] Durable, low-maintenance materials such as brick and uPVC windows reduce long-term repair and decoration costs, because they need less upkeep.
[hom-housing-s6.4.3] Energy-efficient design lowers running costs over the life of the house, even where it adds to the initial cost, so spending more at the start can save money overall.
[hom-housing-s6.4.4] A cheap build can cost more over time if it has poor insulation, weak materials or a costly heating system, because repairs and heating bills add up.
[hom-housing-s6.5.b] Buildings must be safe, durable and energy efficient, so the State sets legal standards and checks them, and builders can offer warranties. Each method has a name, someone who runs it and a job to do.
[hom-housing-s6.5.0] The Building Regulations are legal standards for the design and construction of buildings, made under the Building Control Act 1990. They cover safety, materials and construction, including structure, fire safety, resistance to moisture, sound, ventilation, hygiene, drainage, heat-producing appliances, stairs and guards, insulation, heating, lighting and waste.
[hom-housing-s6.5.1] Technical guidance is issued in parts: Part L covers conservation of fuel and energy and Part M covers access and use for people with disabilities.
[hom-housing-s6.5.2] Local authorities, acting as building control authorities, enforce the Building Regulations through building control and inspect building work.
[hom-housing-s6.5.3] Under the Building Control (Amendment) Regulations 2014, new building works must be notified to the building control authority with a commencement notice, with certificates of compliance on design, and an assigned certifier and a builder who undertake to inspect and build in line with the regulations.
[hom-housing-s6.5.4] On completion, a certificate of compliance on completion, signed by the builder and the assigned certifier, is lodged with the building control authority and recorded on a public register before the building is opened or occupied. The aim is to hold designers, builders and certifiers responsible for compliance.
[hom-housing-s6.5.5] A structural defects warranty such as HomeBond's covers the cost of repairing major structural defects in a new home for ten years from completion.
[hom-housing-s6.5.6] HomeBond is a company whose registered builders' homes are inspected during construction. Lenders often require such a warranty, or equivalent latent defects insurance, before lending on a new home.
[hom-housing-s6.5.7] SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland) home energy grants support the cost of insulation, heating upgrades such as heat pumps, and solar panels in existing homes, with the aim of improving energy efficiency.
[hom-housing-s6.5.8] Standards matter for safety, comfort, durability and energy saving, a lender may require them, and they protect resale value.
