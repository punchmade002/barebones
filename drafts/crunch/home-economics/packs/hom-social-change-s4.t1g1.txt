[hom-social-change-s4.0.b] A few generations ago, marriage was expected of everyone and was the only accepted basis for living together and raising a family. Pressure from society, family and the Church has fallen, so people now treat marriage as a choice.
[hom-social-change-s4.0.0] Couples now tend to marry later, often after completing their education and establishing a career. Some do not marry at all, because social, family and Church pressure to marry young has fallen.
[hom-social-change-s4.0.1] Cohabitation, living together without being married, has become common and is widely accepted, because marriage is no longer seen as the only acceptable basis for a couple's life together.
[hom-social-change-s4.0.2] Marriage is no longer seen as the only acceptable basis for a family, so many children are born to parents who are not married.
[hom-social-change-s4.0.3] Legislation recognises cohabiting couples through the Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010. Cohabitation still gives fewer legal rights than marriage.
[hom-social-change-s4.0.4] Children born outside marriage are no longer treated differently in law, under the Status of Children Act 1987. In this way the law reflected, and helped to change, attitudes.
[hom-social-change-s4.0.5] The 2015 marriage referendum allowed same-sex couples to marry, under the Marriage Act 2015, reflecting wider acceptance of different types of family.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.b] Divorce only became possible in Ireland after the 1995 referendum. Attitudes changed, and the law followed, so married couples now have two legal routes when a marriage breaks down.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.0] Divorce became legal in Ireland after a referendum in 1995 (carried narrowly, after an earlier referendum in 1986 had rejected divorce). The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 sets out the conditions for granting one.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.1] Under that Act, a divorce may be granted only where three conditions are met. First, the spouses have lived apart for two of the previous three years, a period set by the Family Law Act 2019, in operation from 1 December 2019.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.2] Second, there is no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, meaning the couple are unlikely to get back together.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.3] Third, proper provision has been or will be made for the spouses and dependent children, so that nobody is left without support.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.4] Judicial separation, provided for under the Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989, as amended, allows spouses to live apart under a court order that deals with maintenance and property.
[hom-social-change-s4.1.5] Judicial separation does not end the marriage, so neither spouse can remarry. Divorce does end the marriage.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.b] Changed attitudes to marriage, cohabitation, separation and divorce have changed what families look like and how they feel to live in.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.0] There are more lone-parent, blended (reconstituted, where partners bring children from earlier relationships) and cohabiting families, so family structures show more variety.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.1] Families are smaller and first births come later, because couples marry later and plan when to have children.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.2] There is more family breakdown, but also greater freedom to leave an unhappy or abusive marriage.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.3] Children may live in more than one household when parents separate.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.4] Family relationships are now chosen rather than expected, because people are less bound by duty to follow one set pattern.
[hom-social-change-s4.2.5] There is less stigma (social disapproval) for lone parents than there once was.
[hom-social-change-s4.3.b] Parenting has moved from strict authority towards partnership with children, and from mothers alone towards both parents and wider carers.
[hom-social-change-s4.3.0] Parenting has become more child-centred, with greater emphasis on children's views, rights and wellbeing, open communication and negotiated rules instead of strict obedience.
[hom-social-change-s4.3.1] Children's rights are recognised in the Constitution, through the Thirty-first Amendment (Children) 2012, which inserted Article 42A and was signed into law in 2015.
[hom-social-change-s4.3.2] Physical punishment of children by parents is prohibited by Irish law: the Children First Act 2015 removed the defence of reasonable chastisement, so smacking is no longer lawful discipline.

[hom-social-change-s4.3.4] Smaller families are now more common, as contraception and family planning let couples choose the number and timing of their children. The cost of rearing children and women's work also play a part, and each child gets more attention and money.
[hom-social-change-s4.3.5] Mothers and fathers increasingly share childcare, and fathers are more involved in caring and housework. Paternity leave (the Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016, two weeks) recognises the father's role.
[hom-social-change-s4.3.6] Parental leave (the Parental Leave Act 1998) allows parents to take unpaid time off work to care for children. Paid Parent's Leave and Benefit (the Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019) recognises both parents' role, and some men are the main carers (house husbands).
[hom-social-change-s4.3.7] Grandparents often take on regular childcare, particularly where both parents work outside the home. Paid childcare (childminders, creches and after-school services) and pre-school education are also widely used.
[hom-social-change-s4.4.b] A more open and shared style of parenting brings benefits, but it also puts new pressures on parents and changes the way children fit into the family.
[hom-social-change-s4.4.0] Relationships are closer and more open, and decisions are more equal, because children's views are heard.
[hom-social-change-s4.4.1] Children have greater independence, because they are given more say and more responsibility.
[hom-social-change-s4.4.2] Working parents feel more pressure and guilt, a state sometimes called time poverty (never having enough time for everything).
[hom-social-change-s4.4.3] Childcare costs money, which adds to the strain on family budgets.
[hom-social-change-s4.4.4] There is less respect for traditional authority, because rules are negotiated rather than simply obeyed.
[hom-social-change-s4.4.5] Children may take on more responsibility for chores, or for the care of younger siblings.

[hom-social-change-s4.5.0] Traditional roles assigned the male as breadwinner (earner) and head of the household, and the female as homemaker and carer, with responsibilities kept largely separate. Children obeyed and helped, and older children minded younger ones.
[hom-social-change-s4.5.1] Egalitarian roles (equal, shared roles) are now more common. Partners share earning, finances, housework, childcare, discipline and decisions, and both partners often work, which is the dual-earner family.
[hom-social-change-s4.5.2] Men take a greater part in housework and childcare, while women take a greater part in paid work and decision-making.
[hom-social-change-s4.5.3] A man taking on the role of house husband or primary carer, and a woman as main earner, are now socially accepted.
[hom-social-change-s4.5.4] The decline in the influence of the Church lessened expectations about marriage, sexuality and parental roles.
[hom-social-change-s4.5.5] Household tasks are shared with children, and older children may look after younger siblings.
[hom-social-change-s4.5.6] Family diversity means that nuclear, extended, lone-parent, blended, cohabiting and same-sex-parent families are all recognised as families.
[hom-social-change-s4.6.b] Shared roles bring clear gains, but they also create new tensions that families have to manage.
[hom-social-change-s4.6.0] Relationships between partners are more equal, because tasks and decisions are shared.
[hom-social-change-s4.6.1] Families have more income when both partners work.
[hom-social-change-s4.6.2] Women gain greater independence, because earning their own money reduces reliance on a partner.
[hom-social-change-s4.6.3] Role overload and role conflict (the strain of balancing work and home duties), time pressure and stress can follow when both partners work and also care for the family.

[hom-social-change-s4.6.5] Some men feel a loss of status, because the breadwinner role is no longer theirs alone.

[hom-social-change-s4.7.1] Contraception and family planning allowed couples to control family size and plan or delay childbearing.

[hom-social-change-s4.8.4] Cyberbullying and online safety are concerns for families.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s4: 4. When a marriage breaks down
[hom-marriage-s4.0.b] Counselling is the first option for a couple in difficulty. It helps partners talk through their problems while there is still a chance to put things right, so that separation or divorce does not become necessary.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.0] Counselling aims to help a couple resolve their problems before they become grounds for separation or divorce, which is why it is the first step rather than the last.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.1] The service is confidential, so partners can speak openly. They may attend together, separately or, where relevant, with their children.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.2] Several visits may be needed before the couple reach a resolution, because problems built up over time are rarely settled in one session.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.3] The counsellor facilitates the discussion and may offer advice, but does not decide for the couple. The couple work out their own way forward.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.4] Counsellors may refer a couple to a specialist counsellor for a particular problem, such as addiction or difficulties in sexual relations.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.5] Marriage counselling is provided by Accord and by other independent counselling services in Ireland.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.b] Mediation is an alternative to court for a couple who have already decided to separate or divorce. Instead of a judge deciding the outcome, the couple work out the terms themselves with help.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.0] The Family Mediation Service of the Legal Aid Board offers a confidential service to couples who have decided to separate or divorce. It was established in 1986, is State-funded and is free, whereas private mediators charge.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.1] An impartial, trained mediator meets the couple together and helps them negotiate their own agreement. The mediator does not take sides or decide for them.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.2] The issues negotiated include parenting arrangements, custody and access to children, finances and maintenance, and the family home and other property.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.3] Mediation keeps children out of conflict, encourages communication between the partners, and helps them accept the separation.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.4] Mediation is less costly, less adversarial and quicker than court proceedings, which pit the spouses against each other.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.5] The outcome is a written agreement that each partner can bring to their solicitor to be made legally binding.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.b] Legal separation is a legally binding agreement under which spouses live apart but remain married. It lets a couple settle practical arrangements without going to court.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.0] The spouses agree to live apart and set out the terms in a deed of separation, a legally binding written agreement.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.1] Typical terms are living apart, care and access arrangements for the children, maintenance, and what happens to the family home.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.2] A solicitor draws up the deed, and each spouse should have their own solicitor. A mediator may help them reach the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.3] Legal separation can be done without going to court if both spouses agree to the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.4] The spouses are still married, so neither can remarry. Only a divorce or a nullity decree allows that.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.5] Where the spouses cannot agree, a court can grant a judicial separation. This is a court order, so it is different from a deed of separation, which the couple agree themselves.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.b] Nullity and annulment both declare that a marriage was never valid, but only a legal nullity decree has effect in the eyes of the State. Do not confuse either with divorce, which ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.0] Legal nullity is a declaration by a court (the Circuit Court or High Court) that the marriage was never valid in law. The parties are then free to marry other people.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.1] Couples married in a civil or State-recognised religious ceremony need a legal nullity decree before either spouse can remarry.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.2] A marriage may be void, for example where one party is already married or in a civil partnership, the parties are within the prohibited degrees of relationship, or the formalities were not followed.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.3] Nullity may also be granted for a lack of capacity or free consent, or where a marriage is voidable, for example because one spouse cannot sustain a normal marital relationship, including inability to consummate.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.4] Church annulment is granted by a Church marriage tribunal, which studies the marriage and declares it invalid in the eyes of the Church.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.5] A church annulment has no legal effect in the State, so the couple remain legally married. A civil nullity decree or a divorce is still needed before either can remarry civilly.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.6] Nullity says the marriage never validly existed. Divorce ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.b] Divorce is a court decree that legally ends a valid marriage. It was introduced in Ireland by the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996, after the 1995 referendum.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.0] The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 was amended by the Family Law Act 2019, which shortened the living-apart period from four of the previous five years to two of the previous three, and came into force on 1 December 2019.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.1] First condition: the spouses must have lived apart for at least two of the previous three years. The couple can live apart under the same roof, and they do not have to be legally separated first.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.2] Second condition: there must be no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, meaning no realistic chance of the spouses getting back together.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.3] Third condition: proper provision has been or will be made for each spouse and for any dependent children, so that nobody is left without support.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.4] The couple must also have a link to Ireland: either living here permanently, or one of them having lived here for at least one year before applying.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.5] When granting a divorce, the court can make orders on custody and access, maintenance, the family home and other property, and succession rights, which extinguish a spouse's right to inherit from the other.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.6] A decree of divorce is final, and each spouse is then free to remarry.

CANDIDATES hom-family-caring-s1: 1. Roles and responsibilities in the family
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.b] A family is not the same from one decade to the next. It passes through stages, and each stage changes who does what and how heavy the workload is. A role is the pattern of behaviour expected of a person in a position such as parent or child, and a responsibility is a duty that goes with that role.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.0] 1. Couple (establishment) stage: partners set up a shared home and divide household tasks, often according to paid work, time and skills.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.1] 2. Childbearing (expanding) stage: parents take on the provider, nurturing, educating and socialising roles for the first time, and the workload is at its peak.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.2] 3. Child-rearing stage (children of school age and adolescents): parents keep these roles while children take on more household tasks and independent responsibilities, and parents move from direct control towards guidance.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.3] 4. Launching stage: grown children leave home, so parents' roles change from daily care to advice and support at a distance, and the workload eases.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.4] 5. Later life stage (a retired couple, or one partner alone): older members may move from giving care to needing it, and adult children often take on practical, financial and emotional support for them.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.5] Roles are not fixed. They are shaped by the stage the family is in, by each member's age, health, paid work and skills, and by social attitudes. Textbooks label the stages slightly differently, so describe each one as well as naming it.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.b] Parents carry a bundle of different roles at once. These roles can be shared between two parents or carried by one, and they matter because a parent who fills them well, with firm and fair explained rules, gives children the best start.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.0] Provider: supplying food, clothing, shelter and money for the family's needs.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.1] Nurturer: giving love, affection and emotional security, so that children feel safe and valued.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.2] Educator: teaching skills, language, values and good habits through daily routines and example.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.3] Socialiser: introducing children to the norms, beliefs and behaviour expected in family and community, so they learn how to get on with others.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.4] Protector: ensuring safety, supervising children and making decisions in their best interests.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.5] Manager: planning the household budget, meals, schedules and maintenance of the home.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.6] Disciplinarian: setting boundaries and consequences that are explained and applied consistently, because children cope better with rules they understand.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.b] Adolescents are not just receivers of care. As they grow, they take on real roles and responsibilities that prepare them for adult life, and these are best treated as growing responsibility rather than just chores.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.0] Contributing to the household: helping with cooking, cleaning, shopping and minding younger siblings, so the workload is shared and parents have more time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.1] Managing their own schoolwork, timekeeping and personal belongings, which builds the self-discipline they will need as adults.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.2] Developing independence: making decisions about friends, leisure and part-time work within agreed limits, so freedom grows alongside trust.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.3] Respecting family rules and the privacy of others in shared space, so that everyone in the home can live together comfortably.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.4] Sharing in family decisions, such as holidays or how to spend leisure time, which gives them a voice in family life.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.5] Some adolescents earn money through part-time work, or give informal care to a sibling or grandparent.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.b] Who does what in a family has long depended on whether a person is male or female. These expectations have changed a great deal, but they have not disappeared.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.0] Traditional segregated roles (separate, with each partner's tasks kept apart) assigned men as breadwinners and women as homemakers and primary carers of children. The father earned and the mother ran the home, in contrast to joint roles, where partners share tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.1] Where women work outside the home, they often still carry the larger share of domestic work and childcare, a pattern called the double shift, or second shift (a paid job followed by unpaid work at home).
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.2] Men's roles have expanded to include direct childcare, cooking and shared decision-making, and some fathers take the primary caring role.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.3] Gender stereotypes can limit children's roles and experience, for example expecting girls to help in the kitchen and boys to do outdoor jobs.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.4] Sharing household tasks equally reduces overload and resentment, and gives children a model of shared responsibility.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.5] Adult daughters often take on more of the care of ageing parents than sons do.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.b] Family roles have shifted a lot in recent times because society, money and technology have all changed. Each factor below matters because it changes who earns, who cares and who does the housework.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.0] Women's participation in paid employment has increased, so many households depend on two earners and domestic roles have had to be shared. Greater gender equality and changed attitudes support this.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.1] Lone-parent families have increased, so one adult often carries the provider, carer and household roles alone.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.2] Smaller families, by choice or because of later marriage and childbearing, mean each child receives more parental time and money.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.3] Greater acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce and blended families has created new parenting roles, such as step-parent.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.4] Weaker extended-family and community support means parents have fewer relatives and neighbours to share childcare and other tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.5] Longer life expectancy means some families care for parents or grandparents while still raising children, a pattern called the sandwich generation (adults caught between caring for children and ageing parents at once).
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.6] Rising housing, childcare and education costs make two incomes necessary for many households, shifting the provider role to both partners. Unemployment can also reverse traditional provider and carer roles.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.7] Longer working hours and commuting reduce the time available for family meals, shared activities and housework. Labour-saving appliances cut housework time so tasks can be divided differently, while mobile phones and the internet allow remote working and contact but also blur work and home time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.b] Role conflict arises when the demands of two or more roles held by one person cannot be met at the same time. It is common in modern families, where one adult may be a worker, parent, partner and carer all at once.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.0] Work-family conflict: a parent is needed at work during the hours a child is ill or has a school event.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.1] Role overload: one person carries too many responsibilities, such as employee, parent, carer and homemaker, for the time available.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.2] Conflict between partners over who does which tasks, or whose career takes priority.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.3] Intergenerational conflict: an adult may have to care for an older parent while still raising children at home.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.4] Consequences include fatigue, stress, less quality time with the family, reduced quality of parenting, tension between partners and guilt.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.5] Reducing conflict: explicit agreement on the division of tasks, flexible working arrangements, shared decision-making and help from extended family or services, such as childcare.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.b] One way to see how roles have changed is a short case study or interview with an older person, asking about family life fifty to one hundred years ago. A planned set of questions gives you something to compare with family life today.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.0] 1. Decide who to interview, such as a grandparent or an older neighbour, and agree a time, so they are ready to talk.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.1] 2. Ask who earned the money in the family and who cared for the children, to show the provider and carer roles at that time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.2] 3. Ask who did the housework and what tasks children were expected to do, to show how household work was divided.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.3] 4. Ask about family size, since the number of children affects how much care and work each person carried.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.4] 5. Record the answers accurately in your own notes, and write up only what the person told you, with no invented findings.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.5] 6. Compare the answers with family life today, looking for the kinds of change expected: more women in paid work, smaller families, shared tasks, labour-saving appliances and more mobile adolescents.

CANDIDATES hom-family-society-s2: 2. The historical development of the Irish family
[hom-family-society-s2.0.b] The Irish family has changed a great deal since the start of the twentieth century. It helps to think of the change in three phases, with the 1960s as the turning point.
[hom-family-society-s2.0.0] The first phase is the traditional rural family, lasting until about 1960, when most families lived on and worked farms.
[hom-family-society-s2.0.1] The second phase, about 1960 to 1990, was a period of industrialisation (growth of factories and industry) and rising living standards, which moved families towards the nuclear form (parents and children only).
[hom-family-society-s2.0.2] The third phase is the modern family, from the 1990s onward, shaped by legal change, dual incomes and greater diversity of family types.
[hom-family-society-s2.0.3] In an exam you should be able to give four or five contrasts between past and present: structure and size, authority and roles, work, marriage and relationships, and living standards and State supports.
[hom-family-society-s2.0.4] Summary of past versus present: extended family to nuclear, blended and lone-parent; agriculture to industry and services; high child mortality to improved life expectancy; patriarchal to egalitarian; large to smaller families through family planning; changing roles of men and women; more education; a higher standard of living.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.b] Until the 1960s the Irish family was largely rural, agricultural and organised around the farm as an economic unit. Most features of family life, from size to authority, followed from this.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.0] The extended family (grandparents, unmarried aunts and uncles and children) was the most common type, living in one household or close by, especially in rural areas.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.1] Agriculture provided most employment, so the farm was the family's economic unit: all members worked together on it and shared its income. Farming became less labour-intensive from the 1940s.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.2] Families were large, and high child mortality meant many children did not reach adulthood.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.3] Authority was patriarchal, meaning power was held by the father: he decided matters and controlled the money and land. Discipline of children was authoritarian (strict, with obedience expected).
[hom-family-society-s2.1.4] Gender roles were strictly segregated, with men working outside the home and women responsible for housework and the children. The marriage bar required women in the civil service and public sector to leave their jobs on marrying, until it was lifted in 1973.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.5] The Catholic Church had a strong influence on family life, marriage and morality.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.6] Marriages were often arranged or matched and tied to land and inheritance, so marriage often came late and many people never married, particularly in rural areas. Emigration also took many adult children away.
[hom-family-society-s2.1.7] Rural electrification by the ESB, from the 1940s, eased the workload of mothers by bringing electric appliances and lighting to farm homes. The general standard of living also improved in the 1950s.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.b] Industrial and economic change weakened the rural extended family and moved the family towards the nuclear form. Education, jobs and family planning all changed what family life looked like.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.0] Free second-level education was announced in 1966 and introduced in 1967, so more children stayed in school for longer and were nurtured and educated for longer.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.1] Industrialisation and the growth of services drew people from farms to towns and cities, which loosened links with the extended family. The nuclear family became more common and families became more mobile.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.2] Couples married younger and increasingly married for love rather than for land or money, because more people now earned wages away from the farm.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.3] More women entered paid employment, so both parents could work outside the home, which reduced the domestic role of women.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.4] Wages rose and living standards improved, so families could afford more goods and better housing.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.5] The Health (Family Planning) Act 1979 made contraceptives available on prescription for bona fide family planning (in practice, married couples). Amendments in 1985, 1992 and 1993 widened access, and family size fell as couples chose to have fewer children.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.6] Emigration continued in the 1980s, and many families were separated when adult children left to find work.
[hom-family-society-s2.2.7] The Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989 allowed spouses to separate under a court order.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.b] Changes in the law made separation, divorce and other partnerships legally recognised, which altered family structure.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.0] The Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989 provided for judicial separation, allowing spouses to live apart under a court order on property and maintenance.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.1] Divorce was approved by referendum in November 1995, by a narrow majority, which removed the constitutional ban on divorce.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.2] The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 set out the conditions for divorce and divorce was available from 1997. The spouses must have lived apart for a set period, there must be no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, and proper provision must be made for spouses and dependent children.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.3] The Family Law Act 2019 amended the living-apart condition to two of the previous three years. More lone-parent and blended families followed once divorce was possible.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.4] Civil partnership was provided for by the Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010, in force from 1 January 2011. It gave same-sex couples legal recognition and rights similar to those of marriage.
[hom-family-society-s2.3.5] Same-sex marriage was approved by referendum on 22 May 2015 and made law by the Marriage Act 2015, extending marriage to same-sex couples. Same-sex parent families are a recognised family structure.
[hom-family-society-s2.4.b] Economic growth, legal change and the recession after 2008 reshaped family roles and living arrangements. Parents today share roles more equally, with more State support.
[hom-family-society-s2.4.0] Dual-income families became common, with both parents working, and families became smaller. Parents shared decisions and household tasks more equally, so relationships became democratic or egalitarian (shared power and roles).
[hom-family-society-s2.4.1] Some fathers became the main carers at home, a change from the strictly segregated roles of the past.
[hom-family-society-s2.4.2] Standards of living are higher, and State supports for families include welfare, childcare schemes, free pre-school and medical cards.
[hom-family-society-s2.4.3] Lone-parent and blended families grew as separation and divorce became legal options, and same-sex parent families are recognised.
[hom-family-society-s2.4.4] Religious influence has declined and Ireland has become more multi-denominational and multicultural. Couples have children later and have fewer of them, and cohabitation and births outside marriage are common.
[hom-family-society-s2.4.5] Gender equality has advanced through women's education and employment, and the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015 prohibit discrimination in employment.
[hom-family-society-s2.5.b] Two working parents, economic shocks and families spread across countries brought new pressures, and new ways of staying in touch.
[hom-family-society-s2.5.0] Demand for childcare rose as both parents worked, so families relied on creches, childminders and grandparents.
[hom-family-society-s2.5.1] Role overload arose when parents cared for their own children and elderly parents at the same time, which makes it hard to meet every demand.
[hom-family-society-s2.5.2] The recession after 2008 brought unemployment (even among highly qualified parents), mortgage arrears and emigration, and some fathers became the main carers at home.
[hom-family-society-s2.5.3] Family members often live abroad, and mobile phones, email, video calls and social media keep relatives in contact across distances.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s5: 5. Social change
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.b] Societies never stand still. Social change is a significant alteration over time in the structures, institutions, relationships, values or behaviour of a society, in other words in the way a society is organised.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.0] In exam terms, social change is change in society caused by law reform, technology or major events. The internet and divorce legislation are good examples.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.1] Change can be gradual, such as the slow shift in attitudes to the role of women or to marriage over several decades, or sudden, such as a new law, a war or a pandemic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.2] Change can be planned, such as a law or a policy, or unplanned. It happens in every society, but the speed differs from one society to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.3] Cultural lag is the gap in time between the appearance of a new material invention and the adjustment of norms, values and institutions to it, because people and organisations take time to catch up. The concept was developed by the sociologist William Ogburn.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.b] Many of the biggest changes come from shifts in how people think and live. These social causes feed one another: more education leads to new attitudes, and new attitudes lead to new family patterns.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.0] Changing attitudes and values: there is more acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce, lone parenthood, same-sex relationships and diversity, less stigma attached to children born outside marriage, and the Church has less influence than before.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.1] Greater equality for women through better education, careers, financial independence and legal equality changes gender roles at home. The marriage bar, the rule forcing women to leave their jobs on marrying, ended in the civil service in 1973.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.2] Urbanisation is the movement of people from rural areas to towns and cities. It weakens close kin ties and extended family contact and brings new lifestyles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.3] Towns have better services and facilities, but also more pressure, traffic and anti-social behaviour. Rural depopulation means rural areas can lose services and older people can become isolated.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.4] Education: more people reach second and third level, which changes attitudes, expectations and the value placed on qualifications. Wider access also links to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.5] Demographic change means changes in the make-up of the population: falling birth rates, smaller families, later marriage and parenthood, longer life expectancy and an ageing population. These alter the structure of households and the demand for services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.6] Migration, the movement of people into and out of Ireland, brings new cultures, languages and religions. Cultural diffusion, the spread of ideas, fashions and practices between societies through travel, migration and the media, has a similar effect.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.7] Globalisation, the growth of economic and cultural links between countries, means events and ideas in one country affect others. Social movements and pressure groups also campaign for change, for example for equality, and gain support for their aims.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.b] Law reform both reflects change in attitudes and drives it, because a new law changes what people may do and what they come to expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.0] Divorce: the referendum was held in November 1995 and passed narrowly. The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 then set up the legal process. Say referendum 1995, Act 1996, not that divorce was available in 1995.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.1] Same-sex marriage was approved by referendum in 2015 and given effect by the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.2] The Civil Partnership Act 2010 provided civil partnership for same-sex couples. This was the earlier arrangement, because since 2015 same-sex couples can marry.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.3] Other legal changes that alter family life include maternity and paternity leave and equality legislation, which support parents at work and promote equal treatment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.b] How a society earns its living shapes family life, because work decides who is at home, how much money the household has and where people live.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.0] Industrialisation moved many workers from farms and rural trades into factories and towns. Service industries have since grown, and work patterns have changed to include flexible hours, job sharing and working from home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.1] Growth of female employment has produced dual-earner households. These have more income but more need for childcare, and can bring role overload and role conflict, which are effects, not causes.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.2] Unemployment, recession, emigration and returning emigrants change family life by splitting relatives apart and bringing them back. High housing costs delay home ownership, marriage and parenthood, while higher standards of living improve comfort.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.3] State welfare: improved social welfare, such as child benefit and pensions, reduces dependence on the family and eases financial strain.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.b] New technology changes daily life faster than norms and institutions can adjust, which is cultural lag in action. Its effects can be positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.0] Modern communications technology, including mobile phones, the internet and social media, changes how families communicate, how children learn and how people work, and it changes how people spend leisure time. Examples are children skilled in IT, video calls and apps.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.1] Positive effects include access to information and contact with distant relatives. Negative effects include addiction, cyber-bullying and less face-to-face time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.2] Labour-saving appliances free time and make shared housework easier, which supports more equal roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.3] Contraception and medical advances affect family size and life expectancy, because families can plan the number of children and people live longer.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.4] Transport shortens distances, so relatives and workers can live further apart and still keep in touch.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.b] Social change affects the family, institutions and everyday life, and its effects can be both positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.0] Changing gender roles: more women take part in paid employment, and household tasks are more often shared between partners, giving more equal roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.1] Smaller families: many couples have fewer children or have them later in life, and fewer marriages, and later ones, are taking place.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.2] Changing family structures: lone-parent, blended and cohabiting families have become more common, so family structures are more diverse, and the extended family has declined.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.3] An ageing population increases demand for health and care services and for support for older people, and the roles of older people change. More families use childcare.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.4] Changes in values, such as greater acceptance of diversity, change the norms people expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.5] Effects can be positive, such as more choice and freedom, or negative, such as stress and strain, role overload, loss of community and social problems. Rapid change can leave some groups behind, for example people whose skills are no longer needed in changing industries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.6] Change affects institutions: the Church loses influence, the State takes on more services, and schools adapt to diversity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.7] Social change can cause tension and conflict between groups that hold different values, for example older and younger generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.b] Not everyone welcomes change, and several forces can slow it down or stop it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.0] Vested interests are groups who benefit from the existing system, so they may oppose changes that would reduce their advantages.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.1] Tradition and religious belief can resist changes in norms and values that conflict with long-held teachings.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.2] Fear of the unknown and uncertainty about the consequences of change make people reluctant to accept it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.3] The cost of change, in money, time and disruption, can slow or prevent it, and generations may disagree about it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.b] Ireland has seen rapid change in recent decades, and each example below links a cause to an effect on family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.0] Divorce: Irish voters approved divorce by referendum in 1995, and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 provided for it. Marriage equality followed, with a referendum in 2015 and the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.1] Women in employment: the marriage bar in the civil service ended in 1973, and more married women have been in paid work since, which changed roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.2] Migration: the Celtic Tiger, a period of rapid economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s, brought increased immigration and with it new languages, cultures and religions.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.3] Emigration in earlier decades and in the 2008 recession years, and return migration, moved relatives apart and then back together, affecting families and communities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.4] Rural depopulation and urban growth shifted people from the countryside to towns, and the influence of the Church has fallen.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.b] Exam questions on social change usually ask for the impact on family life, and marks are given for each cause covered.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.0] Structure a discuss answer by cause (social, economic, technological) and then effect, with one Irish example for each point.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.1] Link each cause to a clear effect, for example urbanisation weakening extended family contact, or dual incomes increasing the need for childcare.
