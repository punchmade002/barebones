TARGET hom-housing-s6: 6. House design and building standards
[hom-housing-s6.0.b] A house is lived in for decades, so its design has to look good, work with the site and sun, and cause as little harm to the environment as possible. These factors shape the plan before any room is drawn.
[hom-housing-s6.0.0] Aesthetic factors: the elements of design (colour, pattern, shape, texture and line) and principles such as proportion, balance and harmony are used to create an attractive home that does not date quickly, so it stays pleasant and keeps its appeal.
[hom-housing-s6.0.1] Aspect (the direction a room faces): a south-facing living room gains the most daylight and solar heat, so living rooms go on the south side while bedrooms and utility rooms are placed according to their use and the sun's path.
[hom-housing-s6.0.2] Passive solar design uses the layout, orientation and glazing (glass areas) of the house to capture and retain heat without mechanical systems, which cuts heating costs because the sun does part of the work.
[hom-housing-s6.0.3] Environmental factors include using durable, sustainable and locally sourced materials, and minimising the energy used in making them and in running the house, so the home has a smaller impact over its life.
[hom-housing-s6.0.4] The house should fit its surroundings, with its scale and materials suiting the landscape and neighbouring buildings, so it does not look out of place.
[hom-housing-s6.0.5] Site slope and drainage affect the layout of the house, the foundations and the position of the garden, because a sloping or wet site needs different foundations and the water has to be drained away from the building.
[hom-housing-s6.1.b] A home must suit the people living in it now and the people they will become. Planning for both avoids costly alterations later.
[hom-housing-s6.1.0] The number and ages of family members determine the number of bedrooms, the bathrooms and the amount of living space, because a growing family needs more room than a couple.
[hom-housing-s6.1.1] Storage for clothing, sports and garden equipment must be provided in proportion to the size of the family, so that belongings do not clutter the living areas.
[hom-housing-s6.1.2] Teenagers need study space, and a home office may be needed where family members work from home, so a quiet room or corner is planned in.
[hom-housing-s6.1.3] Young children need safe design, such as a layout that allows supervision, so adults can see and reach them easily.
[hom-housing-s6.1.4] Room use should be planned so that each room suits its function, such as a utility room for washing and drying, which keeps laundry out of the kitchen.
[hom-housing-s6.1.5] Future needs are planned for by allowing space to extend, convert the attic or add a ground-floor bedroom, and by flexible rooms, for example a playroom that becomes a study as children grow.
[hom-housing-s6.1.6] Accessibility for ageing or for people with disabilities requires wide doors, level access and an accessible bathroom, so the home stays usable if someone's mobility changes.
[hom-housing-s6.1.7] To evaluate a house plan for a stated family, look at the relationship of kitchen, living and bedrooms, use of space, safety and supervision for children, storage, light and aspect, flexibility, accessibility and cost. Then name a strength, a weakness and a modification with a reason.
[hom-housing-s6.2.b] A well-designed house keeps its heat in and uses fuel efficiently, which saves money and makes the home comfortable. The Building Energy Rating (BER) is the label that shows how well a home does this, much like the energy label on an appliance.
[hom-housing-s6.2.0] Insulation in the roof, walls (including cavity walls) or floor reduces heat loss, and double or triple glazing reduces heat loss through windows; draught proofing and a compact shape also cut the heat that escapes.
[hom-housing-s6.2.1] Heating systems can be made efficient with zoned heating (separate areas heated separately), thermostats and timers, or geothermal heating, so heat is supplied only where and when it is needed.
[hom-housing-s6.2.2] Renewable energy such as solar panels or a heat pump, efficient LED lighting and efficient appliances also cut fuel use, and passive solar gain heats the home for free.
[hom-housing-s6.2.3] A BER certificate rates a home's energy performance on a scale from A, the most efficient, to G, the least efficient. The very top band is now A0, a home that uses no fossil fuel for heating.
[hom-housing-s6.2.4] A registered BER assessor inspects the home and calculates the rating, measuring the area of the rooms and windows, the thickness of the walls, the levels of insulation, the heating system and the floor and wall types. The assessor then issues an advisory report on improvements.
[hom-housing-s6.2.5] A BER certificate is required by law when a home is built, sold or rented, and the rating must be shown in every advertisement for sale or rent, so buyers and tenants can compare running costs. It lasts ten years unless the home is upgraded.
[hom-housing-s6.2.6] Insulation, efficient heating and passive solar design all improve the BER rating and reduce running costs, and a better BER also means a more comfortable home and often a higher value.
[hom-housing-s6.3.b] Ergonomics means designing a space to suit the way people move and work in it, so everyday tasks are easy and safe. Technology adds ways to save energy and labour and to protect the home.
[hom-housing-s6.3.0] Ergonomics is the design of a space so that it suits how people move and work; in a kitchen, the sink, cooker and fridge are placed at the three points of an imaginary triangle (the work triangle), so the cook does not walk far between them.
[hom-housing-s6.3.1] Circulation space must allow people to move safely between work surfaces and to open doors and drawers without obstruction, because cramped layouts cause accidents.
[hom-housing-s6.3.2] Switches and sockets are placed at heights that can be reached comfortably, including by children and people with limited mobility, and counter heights and storage within easy reach follow the same idea. Stairs should be safe and the layout accessible.
[hom-housing-s6.3.3] Sensor lights and LED lamps reduce energy use in the home, because LEDs use little electricity and sensor lights switch on only when someone is there.
[hom-housing-s6.3.4] Zoned heating with programmers and smart thermostats, underfloor heating, solar thermal and solar photovoltaic panels, heat pumps and heat-recovery ventilation save energy, while centralised vacuum systems and smart home controls save labour.
[hom-housing-s6.3.5] Phone-linked alarm systems and electronic gates and garage doors add security to the home, because the owner can monitor and control access from anywhere.
[hom-housing-s6.3.6] Modern methods of construction, such as factory-built timber frame or panels, are a technological development in how the house itself is built.
[hom-housing-s6.4.b] The price of a house is more than the cost of building it. Costs fall into what you pay at the start and what you keep paying to maintain and run the home.
[hom-housing-s6.4.0] Initial costs include the site and servicing, the builder, materials, professional fees (architect, engineer), legal fees, planning and lender costs, mortgage costs, stamp duty on a purchase, and furnishing.
[hom-housing-s6.4.1] Maintenance costs include cleaning, decorating, roof and gutter repair, servicing of the heating system and replacing worn parts, and they recur throughout the life of the house.
[hom-housing-s6.4.2] Durable, low-maintenance materials such as brick and uPVC windows reduce long-term repair and decoration costs, because they need less upkeep.
[hom-housing-s6.4.3] Energy-efficient design lowers running costs over the life of the house, even where it adds to the initial cost, so spending more at the start can save money overall.
[hom-housing-s6.4.4] A cheap build can cost more over time if it has poor insulation, weak materials or a costly heating system, because repairs and heating bills add up.
[hom-housing-s6.5.b] Buildings must be safe, durable and energy efficient, so the State sets legal standards and checks them, and builders can offer warranties. Each method has a name, someone who runs it and a job to do.
[hom-housing-s6.5.0] The Building Regulations are legal standards for the design and construction of buildings, made under the Building Control Act 1990. They cover safety, materials and construction, including structure, fire safety, resistance to moisture, sound, ventilation, hygiene, drainage, heat-producing appliances, stairs and guards, insulation, heating, lighting and waste.
[hom-housing-s6.5.1] Technical guidance is issued in parts: Part L covers conservation of fuel and energy and Part M covers access and use for people with disabilities.
[hom-housing-s6.5.2] Local authorities, acting as building control authorities, enforce the Building Regulations through building control and inspect building work.
[hom-housing-s6.5.3] Under the Building Control (Amendment) Regulations 2014, new building works must be notified to the building control authority with a commencement notice, with certificates of compliance on design, and an assigned certifier and a builder who undertake to inspect and build in line with the regulations.
[hom-housing-s6.5.4] On completion, a certificate of compliance on completion, signed by the builder and the assigned certifier, is lodged with the building control authority and recorded on a public register before the building is opened or occupied. The aim is to hold designers, builders and certifiers responsible for compliance.
[hom-housing-s6.5.5] A structural defects warranty such as HomeBond's covers the cost of repairing major structural defects in a new home for ten years from completion.
[hom-housing-s6.5.6] HomeBond is a company whose registered builders' homes are inspected during construction. Lenders often require such a warranty, or equivalent latent defects insurance, before lending on a new home.
[hom-housing-s6.5.7] SEAI (Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland) home energy grants support the cost of insulation, heating upgrades such as heat pumps, and solar panels in existing homes, with the aim of improving energy efficiency.
[hom-housing-s6.5.8] Standards matter for safety, comfort, durability and energy saving, a lender may require them, and they protect resale value.

CANDIDATES hom-home-services-s2: 2. Emissions, energy inefficiency and energy efficiency
[hom-home-services-s2.0.b] Heat is the largest energy use in a home, so wasted heat costs money and adds to emissions. Heat moves from warm to cool by conduction, convection and radiation, and in a house most is lost through the fabric (roof, walls, windows, doors, floors) and through air leakage.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.0] Conduction: heat passes through solid walls, roof, floor and glazing from the warm inside to the cold outside, so any uninsulated surface is a route for loss.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.1] Convection: warm air rises and escapes through gaps, while cold air enters at lower levels, creating draughts.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.2] Air leakage: gaps around doors, windows, floorboards, letterboxes and chimneys let warm air out and draw cold air in.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.3] Heat rises, so an uninsulated roof or attic is one of the largest routes for heat loss in a typical house.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.4] Single glazing loses heat much faster than an insulated wall; double glazing traps a layer of still air between two panes, which slows heat transfer.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.5] Thermal bridges are parts of the structure, such as lintels and junctions between walls and floors, where heat escapes faster than through the surrounding fabric.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.6] Uninsulated hot water cylinders and pipes lose heat continuously to the surrounding air, even when no water is drawn off.
[hom-home-services-s2.0.7] Heating that runs with no timer, room thermostat or radiator valves heats rooms that are not in use, so energy is paid for but wasted.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.b] Beyond the building fabric, energy is wasted by how heating, hot water, electrical appliances and cooking are used. Each habit below is a fault you would record in a home survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.0] Heating: an old or unserviced boiler, no timer, room thermostat or thermostatic radiator valves, rooms heated when not in use, a thermostat set too high, an open fire losing heat up the chimney, and radiators blocked by furniture or curtains.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.1] Hot water: an uninsulated cylinder and pipes, an immersion (electric heating element in the cylinder) left on, long hot showers or baths, water heated at the wrong time, and dripping hot taps.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.2] Electricity and appliances: old filament bulbs, lights left on, appliances left on standby, half loads in washing machines and dishwashers, old or low-rated appliances, a tumble dryer used instead of line-drying, and a fridge door left open.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.3] Cooking: using more water or heat than needed, no lids on pans, a large pan on a small ring (or the reverse), opening the oven door, preheating too long, and cooking one dish in a large oven.
[hom-home-services-s2.1.4] Cooking: using an oven where a microwave, toaster or slow cooker would do, and boiling too much water in the kettle.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.b] Burning a carbon fuel (gas, oil, coal, turf or wood) in boilers, stoves and fires gives off gases and particles. What comes out depends on how completely the fuel burns.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.0] Complete combustion happens with plenty of air and a well-adjusted burner, and gives carbon dioxide and water vapour. For methane: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.1] Incomplete combustion, caused by too little air, a blocked flue or a dirty or faulty burner, also produces carbon monoxide (CO) and soot or smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.2] Carbon monoxide is a colourless, odourless and poisonous gas that binds to haemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells), so less oxygen is carried around the body. Effects include headache, dizziness, nausea and death.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.3] Carbon monoxide is prevented by servicing appliances, ensuring ventilation, keeping flues clear and fitting a CO alarm, because you cannot smell or see the gas.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.4] Water vapour is also given off when fuels burn, and it can cause condensation in the home.
[hom-home-services-s2.2.5] Diagram: a flow chart from fuel burned in the boiler or stove to the pollutants released (CO₂, CO, SO₂, nitrogen oxides, particulates) and their environmental effects.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.b] Each gas given off by burning fuels does its own kind of damage. Match each gas to its effect and do not swap them.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.0] Carbon dioxide is a greenhouse gas that builds up in the atmosphere and traps heat, contributing to global warming and climate change: rising temperatures and sea levels, extreme weather, and changes to crops and species.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.1] Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) comes from the sulphur in coal, oil and turf. It dissolves in rainwater to form acid rain, which damages trees and crops, acidifies lakes and kills fish, corrodes metal and wears limestone buildings and statues. It also irritates the lungs.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.2] Nitrogen oxides form when the air is heated in a burner or engine at high temperatures. They contribute to acid rain and smog and irritate the lungs.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.3] Smoke and particles come from coal, turf, wood and badly adjusted oil burners. They cause smog, dirty buildings and breathing problems such as asthma and bronchitis; smokeless fuel and efficient stoves reduce them.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.4] Per unit of heat, coal and turf give the most carbon dioxide and smoke, oil gives less, and natural gas gives the least. Electricity gives no emissions in the house but may do at the power station.
[hom-home-services-s2.3.5] Wood is a renewable fuel, but burning it still releases carbon dioxide and, if the stove is poor, smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.b] Aim: to identify where energy is wasted in a home and to recommend changes that improve efficiency.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.0] Step 1: walk through the house with a checklist and note draughts, uninsulated attic space, single glazing, missing draught seals and any unlagged cylinder or pipes.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.1] Step 2: check for draughts by holding the back of the hand or a smoke pencil near gaps, noting where air movement shows leakage.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.2] Step 3: record the heating controls: whether there is a timer, a room thermostat and radiator valves, and the thermostat setting in each room.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.3] Step 4: record the room temperatures at the same time on two occasions, and the hours the heating runs each day.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.4] Step 5: identify how hot water is heated (boiler, immersion or electric shower), how long the immersion runs, and whether the cylinder is insulated.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.5] Step 6: observe cooking practices, such as opening the oven door often, preheating for longer than needed, using large pans on small rings, leaving pans uncovered and boiling more water than needed in the kettle.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.6] Step 7: compare meter readings or fuel deliveries over a set period, and note appliances left on standby.
[hom-home-services-s2.4.7] Safety: keep naked flames away from gas and oil appliances and do not use a lit taper to check for leaks from fuel pipes.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.b] The cheapest way to cut energy use is to stop heat leaving the house. These measures target the fabric and air leaks found in a survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.0] Insulation: quilt laid between the joists in the attic, cavity wall insulation, internal or external wall insulation, and floor insulation.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.1] Glazing: double or triple glazing, with lined curtains closed at night, and low-emissivity coatings on glass.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.2] Draught-proofing of doors, windows, letterboxes and floorboards with seals and brushes, which stops warm air escaping and cold air entering.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.3] Ventilation balanced with insulation: an airtight fabric needs a controlled ventilation system so that moisture and stale air are removed without wasting heat.
[hom-home-services-s2.5.4] Improvements are ranked by cost and effect: draught-proofing and attic insulation are cheap and effective, while a heat pump or external wall insulation costs much more. SEAI grants help with costs.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.b] Heating and hot water are the biggest energy users, so controlling when and where heat is made saves the most. Cleaner heating also cuts emissions.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.0] Heating controls: a programmer or timer, a room thermostat, thermostatic radiator valves on each radiator, and a cylinder thermostat with a time switch for hot water, so heat is only produced when and where it is needed. Zoning (heating separate areas independently) and closing doors also help.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.1] Efficient boilers: a condensing boiler recovers heat from its flue gases, and an annual service keeps the burner adjusted so combustion stays complete.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.2] Heating systems: a stove in place of an open fire, and background heating where it suits.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.3] Hot water: lagging the cylinder and pipes with jackets, using the immersion only when required on a timer, fixing dripping taps, and setting the cylinder thermostat correctly.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.4] Choosing a shower over a bath saves water only if the shower is short, because a long or power shower can use as much as a bath.
[hom-home-services-s2.6.5] Renewable heat: solar thermal collectors, a heat pump or a well-managed wood-burning stove with a flue and an air supply controlled for complete combustion.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.b] Everyday electricity and cooking habits waste energy too, and changing them costs little. Each fix answers a fault from the home survey.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.0] Lighting and appliances: LED lamps, switching off unused lights, sensors and timers, and appliances with the highest rating on the energy label.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.1] Laundry and standby: full loads on washing machines and dishwashers, eco programmes, switching off standby, and line-drying clothes instead of using a tumble dryer.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.2] Cooking: using lids, matching pan size to the ring, boiling only the water needed, and filling the oven when it is on.
[hom-home-services-s2.7.3] Cooking: using the microwave, a pressure cooker or a slow cooker for small or long cooking, avoiding opening the oven door, and cooking several items together.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.b] Emissions fall when less fuel is burned and when what is burned burns cleanly. These steps answer the "way to cut it" part of an emissions question.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.0] Burn less fuel overall, through insulation, controls and efficient appliances, because less fuel burned means fewer pollutants.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.1] Change from coal or oil to gas or smokeless fuel, and choose a renewable electricity supplier, because these produce less carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and smoke.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.2] Service boilers so combustion stays complete, keep flues clear, and fit a CO alarm, which warns of carbon monoxide that you cannot smell.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.3] Do not burn rubbish, and use smokeless fuel and efficient stoves to reduce smoke and particles.
[hom-home-services-s2.8.4] Renewable options such as solar thermal and PV (photovoltaic panels that make electricity), and heat pumps, burn no fuel in the home.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.b] It is far cheaper to build efficiency in than to add it later. A new house can be designed to lose little heat and to gain free heat from the sun.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.0] A compact shape with few corners has less outside wall area through which heat escapes.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.1] South-facing living rooms with large windows gain free heat from the sun (passive solar gain), while small north windows lose little heat.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.2] High levels of insulation in the attic, walls and floor, with double or triple glazing, and airtight construction with controlled ventilation.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.3] A porch or lobby cuts draughts when the door is opened, and orientation and shelter from wind cut heat loss.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.4] Zoned heating with controls, and an efficient or renewable heating system.
[hom-home-services-s2.9.5] Rainwater use, and LED and sensor lighting.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.b] A BER is a certificate that rates a dwelling's energy performance, like the energy label on appliances. It tells owners and buyers how efficient a home is.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.0] The BER assessment is carried out by an assessor registered with SEAI, and SEAI runs the system.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.1] It rates a dwelling on a scale from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient). From 24 May 2026 the scale is A0 and A to G, and the sub-bands such as B1 to B3 were removed.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.2] A valid BER is required when a home is sold or rented, it must appear in adverts, and it has been required since 2009. BERs issued before 24 May 2026 stay valid for sale or rent for ten years from issue.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.3] It shows the energy efficiency of a house, including windows, walls, insulation and heating, and where improvements would have most effect.
[hom-home-services-s2.10.4] The EU energy label for appliances and bulbs was rescaled to A to G (appliances in March 2021, lamps in September 2021), so few products now reach A.

CANDIDATES hom-home-services-s6: 6. Insulation
[hom-home-services-s6.0.b] A warm house loses heat to the cold outside all the time. Insulation does not make heat; it slows the escape of heat, so less fuel is needed to keep the house warm.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.0] Heat leaves a house in four ways: by conduction through solid parts such as walls and glass, by convection (warm air rising and leaking out), by radiation (heat given off as rays), and by air leakage through gaps. Insulation reduces these losses, so less fuel is needed.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.1] Insulating materials are poor conductors of heat. Still air is a very poor conductor, so poor conductors such as still air, polystyrene, fibreglass, mineral wool, sheep's wool, cellulose and foam trap air in small pockets, which reduces heat transfer by conduction.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.2] Fibre and foam insulation holds air in small cells that are too small for convection currents to form. Because the air cannot circulate, it cannot carry heat by convection, which is why a big open gap is not insulation.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.3] Reflective foil and low-emissivity (low-E) coatings (thin coatings that give off little heat) reflect radiant heat back, so they reduce heat loss by radiation.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.4] Draught proofing stops heat leaving in moving air, because it seals the gaps around doors, windows and floorboards where air leaks.
[hom-home-services-s6.0.5] Insulation also keeps a house cool in hot weather, because it slows heat moving in as well as out.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.b] Warm air rises, so the roof is a main route of heat loss. Insulating the attic is the cheapest and most effective place to start.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.0] Fibre blanket or quilt: strips of mineral or glass fibre, or wool, are laid between and over the ceiling joists (the beams that carry the ceiling).
[hom-home-services-s6.1.1] Loose fill: pellets of polystyrene or vermiculite are poured between the joists.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.2] Sprayed or foam insulation: foam is applied between the roof rafters (the sloping beams that hold the roof), where it expands and sets into a solid layer.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.3] Blown fibre: cellulose or mineral fibre is blown into the roof space to cover it evenly.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.4] Rigid boards are fitted between the rafters in a converted attic, where the roof space is used as a room.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.5] Once the attic floor is insulated, the attic becomes colder, so lag the cold water tank and attic pipes to stop them freezing. Insulate and draught-proof the hatch, and keep the eaves ventilated.
[hom-home-services-s6.1.6] Diagram: cross-section of a roof showing the joists, the ceiling below and an insulation layer laid between and over the joists, with the roof space above.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.b] Walls are a large surface through which heat escapes. The method depends on whether the wall has a cavity (a gap between two layers of wall) or is solid.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.0] Cavity wall insulation: a cavity wall has an outer and an inner leaf (layer) with a gap between them. The cavity is filled with polystyrene beads, foam or mineral wool blown in through small holes, and even an unfilled cavity holds some still air.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.1] Internal dry lining: insulated plasterboard is fixed to the inside face of the wall, which adds insulation on the room side. It reduces room size and takes time to fit, but needs no scaffolding.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.2] External insulation: boards of polystyrene or mineral wool are fixed to the outside of the wall and finished with reinforced, waterproof render. This keeps the wall itself warm and avoids thermal bridges (cold spots where heat escapes), but needs planning checks.
[hom-home-services-s6.2.3] Solid walls have no cavity, so they can only be insulated from the inside (dry lining) or the outside (external insulation).
[hom-home-services-s6.2.4] Diagram: cross-section of a cavity wall showing the outer leaf, the insulation in the cavity, the inner leaf and an internal dry lining.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.b] Heat also escapes through floors, glass, gaps around doors and bare pipes. Each has its own simple method.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.0] Floors: seal the gaps between floorboards, add insulation between the joists on suspended floors, and lay a carpet with underlay. Rigid insulation goes under a new concrete floor. All of these reduce heat loss through the floor.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.1] Double glazing: two panes are separated by a sealed gap of air or argon (a gas used instead of air in some units). The trapped gas is a poor conductor and cannot circulate, so conduction and convection across the gap are reduced.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.2] Triple glazing adds a third pane and a second sealed gap for further insulation.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.3] Low-E glazing: a thin coating on the pane reflects radiant heat back into the room.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.4] Secondary glazing (an extra window fitted inside the existing one), heavy lined curtains and blinds close to the glass, and shutters trap a layer of still air against cold glass.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.5] Doors and gaps: draught excluders, brush strips on doors, a letterbox flap, keyhole covers and sealing strips around windows and floorboards stop air leaking in and out. A porch adds a buffer of still air.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.6] Lagging: an insulating jacket or factory-fitted foam on the hot-water cylinder and sleeves on exposed pipes save heat and help prevent pipes freezing.
[hom-home-services-s6.3.7] Insulation reduces condensation, because warm inner surfaces stay above the temperature at which moisture condenses. However, an airtight, well-insulated house needs controlled ventilation.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.b] Examiners often ask for the benefits of insulation. Each one follows from less heat escaping.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.0] Lower heating bills, because less heat escapes and the heating runs for shorter periods.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.1] Comfort: a steadier indoor temperature with no cold surfaces and fewer draughts.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.2] Less noise, because insulation absorbs sound from outside and between rooms.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.3] Less fuel burned, so lower carbon dioxide emissions and less use of fossil fuel, which makes a more sustainable house.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.4] Less condensation and damp, because inner wall and window surfaces stay warm. This means healthier air and less mould.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.5] A better Building Energy Rating (BER), which rates a dwelling's energy performance on a scale from A (most efficient) to G (least efficient) and is run by SEAI. A better BER can also raise the value of the property.
[hom-home-services-s6.4.6] SEAI grants may help with the cost of insulating.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.b] You may be asked to assess the insulation in your own home and suggest improvements. The method is to inspect, rank the problems and justify each recommendation.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.0] Inspect the attic for the depth and coverage of insulation. Check whether joists are left uncovered and whether the tank and pipes are lagged.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.1] Check doors and windows for draughts by feeling for cold air around the frames on a windy day, and note the type of glazing.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.2] Find out the wall type (cavity or solid) and check whether the hot-water cylinder has a jacket.
[hom-home-services-s6.5.3] Note cold rooms, cold surfaces and any signs of condensation or damp.

[hom-home-interior-s1.0.b] The elements of design are the visual building blocks of a room, the things you can actually see and touch when you walk in. The marking schemes list colour, line, shape, pattern and texture, and form (the 3-D version of shape) may be added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.0] You should be able to name three elements and explain two of them, each with an example of how it is used in a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.1] Each element does a job: line leads the eye, shape and form give a room its feel, colour sets mood, pattern adds interest and texture adds warmth or a clean look.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.b] Line and shape are the structure of a room. They decide whether it feels tall or wide, soft or crisp, before any colour is added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.0] Line is the path the eye follows; horizontal lines make a room feel wider and calmer, while vertical lines make it feel taller.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.1] Lines come from skirting boards, picture rails, curtain edges, cabinet edges and the joints between tiles or floorboards, so a room always contains them.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.2] Shape is the two-dimensional (flat) outline of an object or area; form is its three-dimensional volume, such as the bulk of a sofa.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.3] Rounded shapes look soft and informal, while angular shapes look crisp and formal.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.b] Colour is the element with the biggest effect on how a room feels. To use it well you need the colour wheel vocabulary and to know how warm and cool colours behave.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.0] Primary colours are red, yellow and blue; they cannot be made by mixing other colours.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.1] Secondary colours are orange, green and violet, each made by mixing two primaries.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.2] Tertiary colours are made by mixing a primary with a neighbouring secondary colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.3] Warm colours (red, orange and yellow) advance visually, which means they seem to come towards you, so a room feels cosy and smaller. Cool colours (blue, green and violet) recede, so a room feels spacious and calm.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.4] Pastels are pale, soft colours that feel restful, and neutrals are colours such as white and cream that go with almost anything.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.5] Pale colours reflect more light and make a room appear larger; dark colours absorb light and make a room appear smaller and more intimate.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.6] Pale ceilings look higher than dark ones.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.b] Changing how light or dark a colour is gives a range from one hue (a pure colour such as blue). Choosing which colours sit together then gives a colour scheme.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.0] A tint is a colour with white added, a tone has grey added, and a shade has black added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.1] A monochromatic scheme uses tints, tones and shades of a single colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.2] A complementary (contrasting) scheme pairs colours opposite each other on the colour wheel, such as blue and orange, for contrast.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.3] A harmonious (analogous) scheme uses colours that sit next to each other on the wheel, such as green, blue and violet, for a restful effect.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.4] An accent colour is a strong colour used in small amounts, such as cushions or a single feature wall, to draw the eye.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.b] Colour can do four jobs in a room, and the marking schemes list all four. Which colours you choose also depends on the way the room faces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.0] Colour can link rooms, so that a repeated colour makes one space flow into the next.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.1] Colour can alter the apparent proportions of a room, for example pale colours to make it look larger and dark colours to make it feel smaller and cosier.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.2] Colour can highlight a room's good features, or disguise poor ones.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.3] Colour can create atmosphere, such as pastels for a restful bedroom or warm colours for a cosy sitting room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.4] Aspect (the direction a room faces) matters: a north-facing room receives little daylight, so warm colours suit it, while cool colours suit a south-facing room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.b] Pattern adds variety and interest, but the amount and scale you use decide whether a room feels lively or fussy.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.0] Pattern is a repeated or decorative design, such as stripes, checks, florals or geometric motifs.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.1] Large-scale or bold patterns dominate a room and make it feel busy and can shrink it; small-scale patterns recede and create a calmer effect.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.2] Several patterns can work together if they are linked by a shared colour, texture or motif, which ties the room together.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.3] Too much pattern makes a room fussy, while too little makes it bland.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.b] Texture is how a surface feels, or looks as if it feels. It affects both the comfort of a room and how easy it is to keep clean.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.0] Texture is the surface quality of a material, perceived by touch and by the way light falls on it.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.1] Smooth textures such as glass, mirror, glazed tile, paint and polished steel look cool and are easy to keep hygienic, so they suit kitchens and bathrooms.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.2] Rough or soft textures such as carpet, upholstery, brick and textured wallpaper or paint add warmth and comfort to a sitting room or bedroom.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.3] Matt surfaces absorb light and hide surface flaws, while gloss surfaces reflect light and show marks.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.4] Contrasting textures, such as smooth glass beside rough wool, add interest without adding colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.b] The principles of design are the rules for putting the elements together. The four the marking schemes use are proportion, balance, emphasis and rhythm.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.0] Proportion is the size relationship between the parts of a room, such as furniture, windows, doors and wall areas, and between those parts and the room itself.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.1] Scale is the size of one object relative to the room around it; oversized furniture in a small room makes the room look crowded.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.2] Proportion guides furniture choice: a large sofa suits a large room, while a small room suits low, slim pieces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.b] Balance is about sharing visual weight around a room so that it feels settled rather than lopsided.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.0] Balance creates harmony, so that no single feature dominates and colour, pattern and texture are shared out evenly.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.1] Formal balance is symmetrical, with matching items placed either side of a central point, such as a pair of lamps beside a fireplace.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.2] Informal balance places items of different shapes but similar visual weight on either side of a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.b] Emphasis gives the eye somewhere to go first. That place is called the focal point, and it stops a room looking like a jumble of equal features.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.0] Emphasis creates a focal point: the feature the eye goes to first, such as a fireplace, a window view, a feature wall or a large painting.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.1] A focal point can be created with colour, lighting, a strong pattern or the position of furniture.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.2] A room works best with one focal point; two or more features competing for attention make the room feel disjointed.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.b] Rhythm is what makes the eye travel smoothly round a room instead of stopping and starting.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.0] Rhythm is created by repeating a colour, shape, style, line, pattern or texture so that the eye moves easily around the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.1] Repeating a cushion colour on the sofa and in a rug, for example, links the two areas and carries the eye across the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.b] Evaluate a room by naming each element used, then checking whether each principle is achieved and where it fails. Designing one follows a sensible order, starting from what the room is for.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.0] Start from the room's function, such as a living room for relaxing and watching television, and choose a colour scheme that suits that function.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.1] Place the focal point first, then arrange seating and storage so that the focal point is visible from the main entrance.

[hom-home-interior-s1.12.b] Aesthetic factors are about how a room looks and who decides that. They explain why two families can furnish the same room very differently.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.0] Aesthetic factors are the occupants' personal taste, current trends, the style of the house, and the function and décor of the room, which together set the look of a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.1] Trends change quickly, so a room decorated to the latest fashion can date within a few years; neutral base colours with accessories that are easy to change avoid this.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.2] Trends such as open-plan living, neutral schemes and natural materials show how fashion shapes interior design.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.b] A room can look beautiful and still be unpleasant to live in. Comfort factors make sure it works for the people using it.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.0] Comfort depends on heating, lighting and ventilation, so a room needs to be warm, well lit and able to be aired.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.1] Seating and work surfaces should be comfortable and at suitable heights for the people using them.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.2] Comfort also depends on the room's function (working versus relaxing), its aspect, the space available and the layout of the room.

[hom-home-interior-s1.13.4] Quiet and noisy zones should be separated, so that a family member working or studying is not disturbed by people watching television.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.b] Ergonomics is about the fit between people and the layout of a room, so that they can move, reach and work with least effort and strain. It is not the same as how the room looks.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.0] Ergonomics is the design of a room so that its layout suits how people move, reach and work.

[hom-home-interior-s1.14.2] Work zones group related tasks: preparation, cooking, washing up and storage, arranged in the order they are carried out.

[hom-home-interior-s1.14.4] Storage and work surfaces should sit at heights that avoid bending and stretching, with frequently used items between shoulder and knee height.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.5] Work surfaces with few joints and crevices are quicker to clean and resist the build-up of food and dirt.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.6] Sockets and switches should be easy to reach.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.7] Diagram: a kitchen plan showing the work triangle, with sink, cooker and fridge labelled.

[hom-home-interior-s1.15.1] Young children need safe, easy-to-clean surfaces, rounded furniture edges and a play area that can be supervised from the kitchen.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.2] Teenagers may need a study area with good lighting and a door that gives privacy.

CANDIDATES hom-housing-s3: 3. Housing styles
[hom-housing-s3.0.b] Early nineteenth-century housing in Ireland reflected the materials available locally and the wealth of each household. In the countryside the size and roof of a home told you whether the family were labourers, farmers or landowners.
[hom-housing-s3.0.0] Labourers and small farmers lived in single-storey thatched cottages, usually of two rooms (a kitchen and a bedroom), with solid stone or mud walls, which were cheap and came from the local area.
[hom-housing-s3.0.1] Cottages had a half-door and a small number of small windows. The half-door opened into the kitchen and could be closed at the bottom to keep animals out and let in light and air.
[hom-housing-s3.0.2] Thatch was made of reeds, straw or hay, and an open hearth in the main room was used for both cooking and heating, so the kitchen was the centre of family life.
[hom-housing-s3.0.3] Some cottages had a loft directly under the thatch, reached by a ladder, where children slept. This used the roof space as extra sleeping room without adding to the building.
[hom-housing-s3.0.4] Prosperous farmers lived in two-storey, slate-roofed houses with several bedrooms and a parlour (a sitting room kept for visitors), showing their greater wealth.
[hom-housing-s3.0.5] Wealthy landowners lived in large estate houses, with servants' quarters generally in the basement.
[hom-housing-s3.0.6] Diagram: a thatched cottage in cross-section, showing the thatched roof, the loft reached by ladder, the open hearth and the half-door.
[hom-housing-s3.1.b] Towns developed their own styles of housing, and the gap between rich and poor was very visible in the streets. Keep these urban types separate from the rural ones, because examiners do.
[hom-housing-s3.1.0] Georgian terraces in towns (rows of joined houses, for example in Georgian Dublin such as Merrion Square) were two, three or four storeys high, with basements, high ceilings and prominent doors, and were built for the better-off.
[hom-housing-s3.1.1] The middle classes lived in small terraced houses in the suburbs, away from the crowded town centre.
[hom-housing-s3.1.2] Shopkeepers often lived above their shops, which kept them close to their business.
[hom-housing-s3.1.3] Workers lived in small single-storey cottages or, in the towns, in crowded tenements (large buildings divided into rooms let to many families), where space and sanitation were poor.
[hom-housing-s3.2.b] Better transport in the later nineteenth century changed what houses were built from and how they looked, and it changed who lived where in towns.
[hom-housing-s3.2.0] Improved transport brought a wider variety of building materials, because heavy goods such as slate could now be moved cheaply over long distances.
[hom-housing-s3.2.1] Houses became more ornate, with Gothic and Tudor styles.
[hom-housing-s3.2.2] Two-storey, slate-roofed stone buildings became the most common rural dwelling by the end of the century.
[hom-housing-s3.2.3] As wealthier families moved to suburban terraced houses, many large Georgian houses in towns were divided and let to poorer families, which added to overcrowding in town centres.
[hom-housing-s3.3.b] The twentieth century brought new house types and new materials, and for the first time governments played a part in providing and improving homes.
[hom-housing-s3.3.0] Detached houses (standing alone) and semi-detached houses (joined to one neighbour) appeared, along with council housing (homes built by the local authority) and new housing estates.
[hom-housing-s3.3.1] Brick with plaster walls replaced stone, and tiles replaced slates; two-storey houses were built where land was expensive, because building up used less land.
[hom-housing-s3.3.2] Rural electrification in the mid-twentieth century changed rural homes, bringing electric light and power to the countryside.
[hom-housing-s3.3.3] Government housing grants funded improvements to rural houses.
[hom-housing-s3.3.4] The slate-roofed bungalow gradually replaced the thatched cottage in rural Ireland.
[hom-housing-s3.4.b] Later in the twentieth century, housing was built in larger numbers and to higher standards, with new materials that made homes warmer and easier to maintain.
[hom-housing-s3.4.0] Private estates were built to a common design, while individual private houses varied between classical and modern designs.
[hom-housing-s3.4.1] High-rise blocks were built in some cities, for example the Ballymun flats in Dublin in the 1960s.
[hom-housing-s3.4.2] The Building Regulations (legal minimum standards for construction) greatly improved housing standards.
[hom-housing-s3.4.3] New materials and methods such as double glazing, PVC and fibreglass came into use, which cut heat loss and reduced maintenance.
[hom-housing-s3.4.4] Indoor sanitation improved, so that homes had proper indoor toilets and bathrooms.
[hom-housing-s3.5.b] Today's housing is shaped by the cost of land, changing households and concern for the environment, so there is a much wider mix of types than in earlier centuries.
[hom-housing-s3.5.0] Twenty-first-century estates usually consist of one style but include single-storey detached, dormer and two-storey semi-detached houses, which gives a mix of homes for different households.
[hom-housing-s3.5.1] Apartments, townhouses and mixed-density schemes (a mix of house and apartment types on one site) are built, including gated complexes.
[hom-housing-s3.5.2] Old dilapidated buildings and thatched cottages are restored, and custom-designed houses are built on private sites.
[hom-housing-s3.5.3] Attic space is converted into rooms with Velux roof windows, and extensions, conservatories and sunroom extensions are added to many houses to give extra living space.
[hom-housing-s3.5.4] Modern homes include home-office space and open-plan living (a kitchen, dining and living area in one space).
[hom-housing-s3.5.5] Attention is paid to insulation, glazing, security, accessibility and renewable energy, reflecting greater environmental awareness.
[hom-housing-s3.6.b] Each change in style across the centuries had causes, and naming them turns a list of periods into a developed answer.
[hom-housing-s3.6.0] Materials and transport: better transport brought slate, brick and tiles to places that once used only local materials.
[hom-housing-s3.6.1] Rising incomes let more families afford larger and better-built homes.
[hom-housing-s3.6.2] Population moved from the country to towns, which led to terraces, tenements, suburbs and later apartments.
[hom-housing-s3.6.3] Government housing policy and grants, and building regulations, led to council housing, rural improvements and higher standards.
[hom-housing-s3.6.4] Family size and land values: smaller families and expensive land led to semi-detached houses, townhouses and apartments.
[hom-housing-s3.6.5] Environmental awareness led to a focus on insulation, glazing and renewable energy.
[hom-housing-s3.7.b] Different styles suit different households, so each is popular for a reason. Tying each style to the type of household it suits is what examiners reward.
[hom-housing-s3.7.0] Bungalows are single-storey, so they suit older people, people with mobility needs and rural sites, but they need a large site. The dormer bungalow adds bedrooms in the roof space.
[hom-housing-s3.7.1] Two-storey detached houses suit large families and sites with space for a garden, and give privacy, but they cost more to buy and to heat.
[hom-housing-s3.7.2] Semi-detached and terraced houses make efficient use of land and cost less; a terraced house loses less heat than a detached house because it shares walls with its neighbours. The drawbacks are less privacy, noise from neighbours and a small garden.
[hom-housing-s3.7.3] Apartments and duplexes save space and maintenance and are usually in secure complexes with communal space, so they suit first-time buyers, singles, downsizers and city living. The drawbacks are limited space, no private garden and management fees.
[hom-housing-s3.7.4] Townhouses are multi-storey homes built on small urban sites, with limited outdoor space.
[hom-housing-s3.7.5] Traditional, neo-Georgian and restored period houses offer proportion and character, while modern minimalist homes are bright and easy to care for.
[hom-housing-s3.7.6] Custom-designed one-off houses, timber-frame and energy-efficient or passive houses are other styles, and old cottages are kept for holiday and local use in tourist areas.
[hom-housing-s3.8.b] A home has to work for the people living in it, so the make-up and needs of the household strongly affect the style chosen.
[hom-housing-s3.8.0] Family size and stage, and the ages of children, decide how many bedrooms and how much space are needed, so a growing family may choose a two-storey house.
[hom-housing-s3.8.1] Special needs or disability may call for a single-storey home, such as a bungalow, with no stairs.
[hom-housing-s3.8.2] Some households want a garden or a home office, which rules out styles with no private outdoor space.
[hom-housing-s3.8.3] Wanting to be near family and community, and the sense of belonging this gives, can affect where and what a household chooses.
[hom-housing-s3.9.b] Culture shapes what people like and what a place looks like, so it affects both what is built and what people choose to buy.
[hom-housing-s3.9.0] Personal taste, aesthetics and fashion influence choice, for example a preference for a traditional or a modern minimalist look.
[hom-housing-s3.9.1] Regional building traditions and local materials, such as stone or slate, shape the look of rural houses in each area.
[hom-housing-s3.9.2] Respect for heritage and the character of the area leads many people to restore older houses or build in keeping with their surroundings.
[hom-housing-s3.10.b] Money is often the deciding factor, because it limits what a household can buy or build and what it can afford to run.
[hom-housing-s3.10.0] Income and the money available decide what a household can afford, so a first-time buyer may choose an apartment or a terraced house.
[hom-housing-s3.10.1] The price of land affects the cost of any style, which is why cheaper land allows larger, detached houses.
[hom-housing-s3.10.2] The cost of building and the running costs, such as heating, matter because a large detached house costs more to heat than a terraced one.
[hom-housing-s3.10.3] Resale value and investment potential matter because a home is usually a household's largest purchase.
[hom-housing-s3.10.4] A household also decides whether to build, buy new or buy second-hand.
[hom-housing-s3.11.b] The site and its surroundings affect how comfortable a home is and what may be built on it.
[hom-housing-s3.11.0] Aspect (the direction a house faces) matters because south-facing rooms gain daylight and solar heat.
[hom-housing-s3.11.1] Shelter from wind and rain, and exposure to them, affect comfort and heating costs.
[hom-housing-s3.11.2] The slope of the site affects what style can be built on it.
