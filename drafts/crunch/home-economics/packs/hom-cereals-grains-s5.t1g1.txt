TARGET hom-cereals-grains-s5: 5. Two types of pastry
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.b] Pastries are grouped by how the fat is combined with the flour and how the pastry rises, and this decides the texture of the finished pastry. Knowing the groups lets you name a type, say how it differs from another, and match each to a dish.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.0] Short pastries (shortcrust, sweet shortcrust, rich shortcrust, biscuit and cheese pastry): the fat is rubbed into the flour so that it coats the flour particles and limits gluten (the stretchy protein in flour), giving a crumbly texture.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.1] Flaked or laminated pastries (rough puff, flaky and puff): pieces or layers of fat are folded into the dough, so layers of dough and fat separate and rise, giving a flaky texture.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.2] Choux pastry: a cooked paste of water, fat and flour, with beaten egg added. It contains no rubbed-in fat and is raised by steam into a hollow shell, which is why it suits eclairs and profiteroles.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.3] Filo pastry: very thin sheets of dough layered with fat or oil, which become crisp when baked, as in samosas and strudel.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.4] Suet pastry is a type of its own: the fat is shredded suet, not rubbed in, and it is made with self-raising flour then steamed or baked, as in a steamed pudding. It can also be boiled.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.5] Matching dishes: shortcrust for apple tart, quiche and (as rich shortcrust) mince pies; rough puff or puff for sausage rolls, vol-au-vents and pie toppings; choux for eclairs and profiteroles; filo for samosas and strudel; suet for steamed pudding.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.0.6] Two types differ in their ingredients, proportions, method, characteristics or appearance. For example, shortcrust has the fat rubbed in while rough puff has the fat left in pieces and folded in, and rough puff uses a higher proportion of fat.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.b] Shortcrust is the most common short pastry and forms the base for sweet and savoury tarts and pies. Rubbing the fat into the flour coats the flour with fat, which limits gluten and makes the pastry short, meaning crumbly.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.0] Ingredients: plain flour, a pinch of salt, cold fat (butter, margarine or lard, or a mix) in roughly half the weight of the flour, and cold water to bind; sugar is added for sweet shortcrust.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.1] Step 1: sift the flour and salt into a bowl, which aerates the flour (puts air into it) and removes lumps.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.2] Step 2: rub the cold fat into the flour with the fingertips, lifting the mixture to add air, until it resembles fine breadcrumbs. Cold fingertips keep the fat from melting, and the fat coating the flour is what limits gluten.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.3] Step 3: add cold water gradually, mixing with a round-bladed knife until the dough just comes together. Too much liquid makes the pastry tough.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.4] Step 4: bring the dough together lightly, wrap and chill it so the gluten relaxes and the fat firms. Heavy handling would develop the gluten and toughen the pastry.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.5] Step 5: roll lightly on a floured surface, line the tin, and chill again before baking so the pastry does not shrink.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.6] Step 6: bake in a preheated hot oven (about 200 degrees Celsius, gas 6) until golden, then reduce the heat if the pastry is browning too quickly. Blind bake a case (bake it empty) when the filling is not cooked, such as quiche, to prevent a soggy base.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.1.7] Presentation: sweet shortcrust is dusted with icing sugar or glazed with beaten egg or milk; savoury shortcrust is used for quiche and pies, with the edges crimped or fluted. Used for tarts, pies and mince pies, the pastry should be neat, evenly browned, crisp and not shrunk.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.b] Rough puff is a puff pastry in which the fat is left in pieces rather than rubbed in, and the dough is folded in several turns to create layers. It uses a high proportion of hard fat (about three-quarters of the weight of the flour), so it is richer and lighter than shortcrust.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.0] Ingredients: plain flour, a pinch of salt, hard fat (butter or block margarine), and cold water to bind; a little lemon juice is optional.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.1] Step 1: cut the cold fat into cubes or irregular pieces, leave them visible and mix them lightly with the flour, binding the dough with cold water. The lumps of fat must stay, because they make the layers later.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.2] Step 2: roll the dough into a long rectangle, fold the bottom third up and the top third down, then turn the dough a quarter turn.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.3] Step 3: repeat the roll, fold and turn several times, resting and chilling between turns so the fat stays firm and the layers stay distinct.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.4] Step 4: roll to the required thickness and chill before cutting so that the edges stay sharp.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.5] Step 5: bake in a very hot, preheated oven (about 220 degrees Celsius, gas 7 to 8) and do not open the door early.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.6] Why it rises: the water in the dough and fat turns to steam and pushes the layers apart, then the fat melts into the dough and the layers set, so the pastry rises and becomes flaky and light.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.2.7] Presentation: sausage rolls, vol-au-vents, pie lids and toppings, and Eccles cakes. Glaze with egg wash, keeping it off the cut edges so the layers can rise; the layers should be even and golden, with sharp edges.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.b] Light, crisp pastry depends on keeping the fat solid, handling the dough as little as possible and not building up gluten. These guidelines apply to both types and explain why a pastry turns out tough, shrunk, soggy or heavy.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.0] Keep the ingredients, hands, utensils and kitchen cool so that the fat does not melt before baking.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.1] Weigh ingredients accurately so that the flour-to-fat ratio is correct.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.2] Sieve the flour to introduce air, and rub in lightly with the fingertips.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.3] Add cold liquid gradually; too much liquid makes the pastry sticky or tough and increases shrinkage.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.4] Avoid overhandling: working the dough develops gluten, which makes pastry tough, so handle it as little as possible.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.5] Rest and chill pastry before rolling and baking so that the gluten relaxes and the pastry does not shrink.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.6] Roll lightly in one direction and evenly, and avoid stretching the dough, which causes shrinkage.
[hom-cereals-grains-s5.3.7] Bake in a preheated hot oven, on a hot baking tray, and use blind baking for fillings that are not baked, such as quiche, which keeps the base crisp.

CANDIDATES hom-cereals-grains-s3: 3. Milling and flour
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.b] Milling turns the whole wheat grain into flour by separating the bran, germ and endosperm and then grinding the endosperm finely. Each stage has a purpose, and an account of production should give the stages in order with the reason for each.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.0] 1. Intake and cleaning: the wheat is sampled, graded and may be blended to give the quality needed. It is then passed through sieves, air currents and magnets to remove stones, dust, chaff and metal, so that none of these end up in the flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.1] 2. Conditioning (tempering): a controlled amount of water is added and the grain is left to rest. This toughens the bran so it separates in large pieces rather than shattering into the flour, and it softens the endosperm (the starchy centre of the grain) ready for milling.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.2] 3. Break rolling: corrugated (grooved) rollers crack open the grain and release the endosperm, while the bran and germ stay in larger pieces because the bran is not crushed.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.3] 4. Sifting (plansifting): plansifters, which are stacks of sieves, separate the bran, germ and endosperm particles by size, so each stream can be dealt with separately.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.4] 5. Reduction rolling: smooth rollers grind the endosperm repeatedly, with a sieving step after each pass, until it becomes fine white flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.5] 6. Finishing: flour from different batches is blended for consistent quality, such as gluten strength, and may be treated with permitted improvers. It may be fortified (have extra nutrients added) and is tested for moisture, gluten strength and colour before packing.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.6] Extraction rate is the percentage of the original grain that ends up in the flour. White flour is about 75% and wholemeal flour is 100%, because it keeps all three parts of the grain. Wheatmeal falls between the two.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.7] Stone-ground flour is made by grinding the grain between millstones instead of rollers, and it is accepted as a type of flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.8] Diagram: a flow chart of the milling stages in order (intake and cleaning, conditioning, break rolling, sifting, reduction rolling, finishing), with the bran, germ and endosperm streams branching off at the sifting step.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.b] The difference between white and wholemeal flour comes down to how much of the grain is kept during milling. This changes the colour, nutrition, texture of baked foods and how long the flour keeps.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.0] White flour is made from the endosperm only, because the bran and germ are removed. It is lighter in colour and texture, and has about 75% extraction.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.1] White flour has less fibre and fewer B vitamins and minerals than wholemeal, because these are mostly lost with the bran and germ. It has a longer shelf life.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.2] Wholemeal flour is made from the whole grain, with 100% extraction, so it is brown and higher in fibre, B vitamins and minerals.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.3] Wholemeal flour makes denser baked products. It has a shorter shelf life because of the oil in the germ, which goes off over time.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.b] Flours differ in how much of the grain they contain, how much gluten-forming protein they have and whether anything has been added. This decides which job each one does best.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.0] Plain white (cream) flour has a medium gluten content. It is used for pastry, cakes (creamed or whisked), biscuits, sauces and roux, pizza, scones and batters.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.1] Self-raising flour is plain flour with a raising agent, usually baking powder, added, so it is not a different wheat. It is used for cakes, sponges, scones and biscuits.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.2] Strong flour is made from hard wheat and is high in gluten-forming protein. It is used for yeast breads, pizza dough and puff pastry because the gluten traps gas, such as the carbon dioxide produced by yeast, for a good rise.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.3] Wholemeal (wholewheat) flour is milled from the whole grain, so it contains bran, germ and endosperm. It is used for brown bread, scones, muffins and wholemeal pastry, and gives a denser texture because the bran cuts the gluten strands.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.4] Wheatmeal (brown) flour is partly refined, with part of the bran removed. It is used for brown bread and soda bread.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.5] Cornflour is a fine starch made from the endosperm of maize, not a wheat flour, so it does not contain gluten. It is used as a thickener for sauces, custards and gravy.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.6] Rice flour and other gluten-free flours (rice, maize and other blends) are used in baking for people with coeliac disease, who cannot eat gluten.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.b] Gluten is the protein network that gives bread and pastry their structure, and its development is controlled by the way flour is handled.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.0] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin in wheat flour are mixed with water.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.1] Gluten is elastic and stretchy: it traps gas and gives bread its structure.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.2] Kneading develops gluten, producing a strong, elastic dough, whereas rubbing in fat and mixing lightly limit gluten, giving tender pastry.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.3] Strong flour contains more gluten-forming protein than plain flour, so it gives a more elastic dough.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.b] Flour is the syllabus's example of a food that undergoes extensive processing. The grain is cleaned, separated, ground, blended, treated and packed, and the packaging and label then protect and inform the buyer.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.0] Flour is a processed food: milling turns the whole grain into a refined ingredient with a different nutrient profile.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.1] Packaging: flour is packed in strong, sealed, food-grade bags, paper or plastic, that protect it from moisture, insects, contamination and odours, and are easy to handle and store.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.2] Labelling: the label gives the name of the food (the type of flour), the ingredients list, the net quantity, the nutrition declaration, the best-before date, storage instructions and the name and address of the food business. It may also carry cooking or recipe guidance.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.3] Allergens: wheat and gluten must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold type, so people with allergies or coeliac disease can spot them. This is set by Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.4] Traceability: a batch (lot) code and date mark allow the manufacturer to trace the flour back through production, so it can be recalled if there is a problem.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.5] Fortified flour: any added nutrients are shown on the nutrition declaration.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.6] Labelling also lets shoppers make a safe choice, compare products and follow the storage guidance.

CANDIDATES hom-cereals-grains-s4: 4. Preparation and cooking of cereals
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.b] Cereals are mostly starch, with some protein, sugar, cellulose and B group vitamins, and heat changes all of them. The way you heat the cereal, with moisture or without, decides which change is most obvious.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.0] Moist heat (boiling, simmering, steaming) makes starch granules absorb liquid, swell and burst. This is why the cereal softens and thickens the liquid around it.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.1] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting) dries the surface and breaks the starch down into dextrins, a change called dextrinisation. This forms a brown, crisp crust.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.2] In frying, the starch also absorbs fat, so the cooked cereal food takes up fat as well as browning.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.3] Dry heat also caramelises sugars in cereal products (heating sugar until it turns brown), adding colour and flavour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.4] Heat softens the cellulose (the fibre) in the bran and cell walls, so cooked cereals are easier to digest.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.5] Heat coagulates protein (it sets firm), which sets the structure of batters, doughs, baked goods and cooked cereals.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.6] Heat destroys some B group vitamins, and more are lost when cereals are boiled in a large volume of water that is then discarded, because the vitamins dissolve into it.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.0.7] Overall, the colour, flavour and texture of the cereal all change during cooking.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.b] Gelatinisation is what happens when starch is heated in a liquid: the granules absorb water, swell and burst, and the mixture thickens. It is how flour or cornflour thickens a sauce.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.0] 1. Starch granules absorb water from the liquid and swell.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.1] 2. Some starch (amylose) leaches out of the swollen granules into the liquid.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.2] 3. The liquid thickens as the swollen granules and amylose form a gel-like network that fills it.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.3] 4. On further heating the granules burst, and the starch is fully gelatinised.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.4] Heat is essential. Flour left cold in a liquid only forms a paste, so it does not thicken by absorbing liquid alone.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.5] Starch granules must be kept apart before heating, by stirring, by blending the flour with a little cold liquid first, or by rubbing fat in as in a roux. Otherwise they clump into lumps.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.6] Flour or cornflour must be cooked long enough to gelatinise fully, otherwise the sauce tastes floury.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.1.7] A roux is a cooked mixture of equal weights of fat and flour, used as the base of a sauce. The fat coats the starch granules and keeps the flour from forming lumps when the liquid is added.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.b] Rice shows clearly what moist heat does to a cereal, and the same ideas apply to pasta and oats. The method you choose also decides how many B vitamins you keep.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.0] Rinsing rice removes surface starch, so that the cooked grains stay separate instead of sticking together.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.1] Absorption method: rice is cooked in a measured amount of water, covered, until all of the water is absorbed, and it is left off the heat to finish.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.2] Boiling method: rice is cooked in excess water, which is drained off afterwards.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.3] Moist heat makes rice grains swell, burst and absorb liquid, and gelatinisation gives the cooked rice its soft texture.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.4] Fibre in the grain softens during cooking, making the rice easier to digest. Its colour also changes.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.5] B group vitamins leach (dissolve out) into the cooking water, so use only the water needed, cover the pan, do not overcook, and use the cooking liquid, for example in soups, where practical.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.6] Brown rice takes longer to cook than white rice because the intact bran layer slows water absorption into the grain.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.2.7] Pasta is boiled in plenty of boiling water until al dente (cooked but still slightly firm). Oats for porridge are cooked in milk or water and stirred as the starch gelatinises, which stops lumps forming.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.b] A raising agent produces or traps a gas (air, steam or carbon dioxide) that expands in the heat of baking. The protein and starch then set around the bubbles and hold the structure, giving a light, risen product.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.0] Air (a physical raising agent) is trapped by beating, whisking eggs, creaming fat and sugar, sieving flour, folding and rubbing in. It expands in the oven, as in sponge cakes, meringues and, slightly, shortcrust pastry.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.1] Steam: water in a mixture turns to steam in the hot oven and expands the product, as in choux pastry, puff and rough puff pastry, Yorkshire pudding and pancakes.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.2] Yeast (a biological raising agent) ferments sugars in warm, moist dough, producing carbon dioxide and alcohol and releasing energy. The gluten (a protein, not starch) traps the gas and the dough rises during proving.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.3] Yeast works best at about 35-40 degrees Celsius and is killed above about 55-60 degrees Celsius, so dough is proved in a warm place, not a hot one. Baking then kills the yeast.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.4] Bicarbonate of soda (bread soda) releases carbon dioxide when it reacts with an acid such as buttermilk, yoghurt, treacle or honey in the presence of moisture and heat. It is used in soda bread and scones.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.5] Baking powder contains bicarbonate of soda with an acid salt and a starch filler. The acid and alkali react when moistened and heated to release carbon dioxide, so it is used in cakes, muffins and scones.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.6] Self-raising flour is plain flour with baking powder already added.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.3.7] Raising agents for named products: bread uses yeast, bread soda, or air or baking powder; sponge cake uses air, self-raising flour or baking powder; muffins use baking powder or bread soda; lemon drizzle cake uses baking powder or air.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.b] A raising agent only works if the mixture and the oven let it do its job. Small errors in measuring or handling can leave a baked product flat or heavy.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.0] Measure accurately, because too much or too little raising agent gives a poor rise or a coarse texture.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.1] Use the correct oven temperature, so the gas expands and the protein and starch set at the right moment.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.2] Do not open the oven door early, because the cold air can make the risen mixture collapse before it has set.
[hom-cereals-grains-s4.4.3] Handle the mixture correctly, for example folding gently so the trapped air is not knocked out.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-lipids-s4.0.b] As a fat is heated it passes through three different stages, each at a higher temperature than the last: it melts, then it smokes, then it can burst into flame. Knowing these temperatures explains how fats are chosen and used in cooking.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.0] Melting point is the temperature at which a solid fat turns to liquid. Fats are mixtures of triglycerides, so they melt over a range rather than at one sharp temperature, and solid fats melt at about 30–40 °C. Oils are already liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.1] Fats with a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points, because their kinked chains do not pack tightly. Fats with more saturated fatty acids, whose chains are straight and pack closely, have higher melting points.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.2] Smoke point is the temperature at which an overheated lipid begins to decompose and gives off a continuous blue haze. Smoke points are about 200 °C for solid fats and about 250 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.3] On decomposition the fat splits into glycerol and fatty acids, and the glycerol breaks down further to release acrolein, which has an acrid, irritating smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.4] Free fatty acids, impurities and food particles left in the oil lower its smoke point, and repeated use builds these up, so used or dirty fat smokes at a lower temperature. This is why frying fat is strained and replaced.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.5] A fat with a high smoke point suits frying, while butter, with a lower smoke point, burns.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.6] Flash point is the temperature at which the vapour from an overheated lipid ignites spontaneously and bursts into flame. Flash points are about 310 °C for solid fats and about 325 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.7] A fat that reaches its flash point is a serious fire hazard. Never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended, never heat fat until it smokes, and smother a fat fire with a fire blanket or lid, never water.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.b] This practical compares how common fats and oils behave on heating, and links the results to their fatty acids and to impurities. Care is needed because the fats become very hot.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.0] Aim: to compare the melting, smoke and flash points of common fats and oils, for example butter, lard, olive oil and sunflower oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.1] 1. Use a small amount of each fat or oil, so any smoke or fire is limited.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.2] 2. Heat each gently, preferably on a hot plate rather than a flame, which is safer for a class demonstration, and read the temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.3] 3. Record the order in which the fats melt, and which fats smoke first. Do not heat beyond the smoke point.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.4] Conclusion: link the order of melting points to how saturated the fatty acids are, and the point at which smoke appears to the fat's impurities.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.b] Oil and water do not mix normally, but they can be forced to, and in some foods they stay mixed. This is the basis of mayonnaise, butter, milk and many sauces.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.0] An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix (immiscible), such as oil and water, with one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.1] Vigorous shaking, whisking or blending forces the liquids together by breaking one liquid into droplets that spread through the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.2] A temporary emulsion, such as French dressing (oil and vinegar), separates again on standing because nothing keeps the droplets apart.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.3] A permanent (stable) emulsion, such as mayonnaise, hollandaise sauce, ice cream, cream, milk or butter, stays mixed because an emulsifying agent coats the droplets.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.4] An emulsifier has a hydrophilic (water-attracting) end and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) end, which sit at the boundary between the oil and the water.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.5] In an oil-in-water emulsion, oil droplets are dispersed in water, as in mayonnaise, milk and cream.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.6] In a water-in-oil emulsion, water droplets are dispersed in fat, as in butter, margarine and some spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.b] Making mayonnaise and a French dressing side by side shows the difference between a temporary and a permanent emulsion.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.0] Aim: to show emulsion formation and the effect of an emulsifier, using mayonnaise (egg yolk, oil, and vinegar or lemon juice) and a French dressing (oil and vinegar).
[hom-lipids-s4.3.1] 1. Make the mayonnaise by adding the oil to the egg yolk slowly while whisking, so the droplets are small and evenly spread.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.2] 2. Shake the oil and vinegar together for the French dressing, and leave both to stand.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.3] Result: the French dressing separates, but the mayonnaise stays stable because the egg yolk is the emulsifier. Comparing with and without an emulsifier shows this.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.b] Two kinds of additive help keep an emulsion together, and they do different jobs. An emulsifier makes the emulsion, while a stabiliser helps it stay that way.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.0] An emulsifying agent is a molecule with a hydrophilic (water-loving) end and a hydrophobic (water-hating, fat-loving) end. The hydrophilic end attaches to the water and the hydrophobic end to the oil, so the droplets are coated and cannot join together.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.1] Emulsifying agents include lecithin (found in egg yolk and soya; the food additive E322), mustard, and mono- and diglycerides such as glycerol monostearate (GMS; mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids are E471).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.2] The function of an emulsifier is to reduce the tension between oil and water so they mix, and to make and keep the emulsion stable.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.3] Stabilisers do not form the emulsion. They thicken the water phase and stop droplets joining together (coalescing).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.4] Examples of stabilisers are starch, pectin, gelatine and gums such as xanthan gum and guar gum. They are used in dressings, sauces, ice cream and low-fat spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.b] Some fats can be spread or shaped without being runny or rock hard. This property, called plasticity, is why butter and margarine behave so differently from oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.0] Plasticity is the ability of a fat to be shaped, spread or worked (creamed, rubbed in) at a given temperature without being fully liquid or fully hard.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.1] It is due to a mixture of solid and liquid fat within the fat: the solid fat forms a network that holds the liquid fat. The proportion of saturated fatty acids only decides how much of the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.2] Butter, lard, margarine and shortening are plastic over a range of temperatures. Oils are not plastic, a very unsaturated fat is too liquid to hold its shape, and a very saturated fat is hard and brittle when cold.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.3] Plastic fats are valued in pastry and cake making because they can be creamed, which traps air, or rubbed into flour and layered. Plasticity also gives butter and margarine their spreadability.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.b] Liquid oils can be turned into solid fats by changing their fatty acids. This process, hydrogenation, is how margarine is made from vegetable oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.0] Hydrogenation is the addition of hydrogen to the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, using a nickel catalyst under heat and pressure.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.1] It converts unsaturated fatty acids to saturated ones, so a liquid oil becomes a semi-solid or solid fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.2] It is used to make margarine and spreads, hard margarines, shortenings and baking fats from liquid vegetable oils. The fats are solid, plastic and more stable against oxidative rancidity.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.3] Partial hydrogenation forms trans fatty acids, so the process is now controlled. Full hydrogenation gives a hard saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.b] Rancidity is the spoilage of fats and oils, which gives them an unpleasant taste and smell. It happens in two different ways, with different causes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.0] Oxidative rancidity: oxygen from the air reacts at the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, which also breaks the chain. This produces aldehydes and ketones, which cause the rancid smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.1] Light, heat and traces of metal ions speed up oxidative rancidity. It affects polyunsaturated oils most because they have the most double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.2] Hydrolytic rancidity: water, together with the enzyme lipase from the food itself or from micro-organisms such as moulds and bacteria, breaks fats down into fatty acids and glycerol. The free fatty acids, for example butyric acid in butter, give the smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.3] Hydrolytic rancidity can occur in a freezer, because lipase is not destroyed by freezing. Blanching vegetables before freezing destroys these enzymes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.4] Fats are protected from rancidity when stored in airtight, opaque containers in a cool, dark place, kept dry, bought in small amounts and used before the date. Refrigerating or freezing, using less-unsaturated fats, adding antioxidants and not reusing frying oil repeatedly also help.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.b] Antioxidants are the main chemical defence against oxidative rancidity. They are added to fats and oils so that they keep their flavour for longer.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.0] An antioxidant is a substance that slows oxidation. It delays oxidative rancidity by reacting with oxygen or free radicals before they attack the double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin E (tocopherols, E306), present in some vegetable oils, and vitamin C (ascorbic acid, E300).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.2] Synthetic antioxidants added to fats include butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, E320) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, E321).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.3] Antioxidants are added to oils, margarines, spreads, snack foods and fried foods to keep their flavour and extend their shelf life. They appear in the ingredients list by name or E number under the additives rules (Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008).
