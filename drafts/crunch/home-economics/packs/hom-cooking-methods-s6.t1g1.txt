TARGET hom-cooking-methods-s6: 6. Recipe balance, presentation and dishes for different needs
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.b] A recipe is balanced when a dish or meal supplies the right proportions of nutrients for the people who will eat it. The Department of Health Healthy Eating Guidelines and the Food Pyramid are the checks a cook uses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.0] A balanced main meal usually includes a protein food, a starchy food and vegetables, so that protein, carbohydrate, vitamins, minerals and fibre are all present, with little added fat, sugar and salt.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.1] On the Food Pyramid, vegetables, salad and fruit form the base, because they should be eaten most. Next come wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice, then milk, yogurt and cheese.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.2] Higher up are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, which are used sparingly. Foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt sit at the top and are not needed for health.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.3] Wholegrain or wholemeal versions of starchy foods, and pulses, add fibre, B vitamins and minerals to a dish, so they improve balance.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.4] Oily fish, lean meat, pulses and low-fat dairy supply protein with less saturated fat than fatty cuts of meat and full-fat dairy.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.5] Fresh, frozen or tinned fruit and vegetables in a main course or dessert supply vitamin C, folate and fibre, and extra vegetables add vitamins and minerals. Using less fat, sugar and salt also improves balance.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.0.6] Balance also covers colour, texture, flavour and cost, so that a meal is appealing as well as affordable.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.b] Modifying (adapting) a recipe means changing its ingredients, quantity or method so that it suits the people eating it. There are six main reasons.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.0] To make a dish more nutritious, by adding fibre or reducing fat, sugar or salt, in line with healthy-eating guidelines.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.1] To cater for a person on a special diet, such as a coeliac (someone who cannot eat gluten) who needs gluten-free flour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.2] To increase or decrease the number of portions, because standard recipes are written for a set number of servings, usually about four adults.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.3] To add variety to a basic recipe, for example by adding nuts to a vegetable stir-fry.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.4] To economise, by substituting an expensive ingredient with a cheaper one, for example margarine for butter in a sponge.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.1.5] To suit the likes and dislikes of those eating, for example by leaving out an ingredient that a family member avoids.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.b] Making a dish healthier usually means swapping a few ingredients or changing the cooking method. Each change below targets one nutrient.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.0] Reduce fat by grilling, baking, steaming or poaching instead of frying, which adds fat. Also trim visible fat from meat, skim fat from stews, and use a vegetable oil or low-fat spread instead of butter.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.1] Reduce salt by omitting added salt and stock cubes, which are high in salt. Flavour with herbs, spices, garlic or lemon juice instead, and choose reduced-salt products.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.2] Reduce sugar by using less of it, by adding fruit that gives natural sweetness, and by avoiding sugary toppings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.2.3] Increase fibre by replacing white flour with wholemeal flour, using brown rice and pasta, and adding oats, pulses and vegetables. Leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, since the skin is a source of fibre.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.b] Other changes alter who the dish suits or how many it feeds. After any change, the result still has to taste right.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.0] Gluten-free cooking replaces wheat flour with a gluten-free flour, such as rice or maize flour, and checks that other ingredients are gluten-free.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.1] Vegetarian versions replace meat with pulses, tofu, eggs or mushrooms, while keeping a source of iron and protein in the dish, because meat is normally where those come from.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.2] Scale a recipe by multiplying or dividing every ingredient by the same factor, so the proportions stay right. Then adjust the tin or dish size and the cooking time, since larger quantities usually take longer, and check the seasoning.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.3] Adapt flavour and texture by substituting herbs and spices, or by using a different fruit or vegetable in season.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.3.4] Taste and adjust the seasoning after modifying a recipe, because reduced salt or sugar changes the flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.b] Aesthetic awareness is a general reaction to how food looks, smells and feels, not a formal test. Sight, smell and texture help a cook judge food before it is bought and while it is prepared.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.0] Sight judges appearance, colour, size, shape and surface. Colour and appearance show freshness or ripeness, for example a firm, bright, unbruised apple.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.1] Certain colours are linked to flavours, such as yellow with lemon, and people expect particular appearances: mould is accepted on Stilton cheese but not on bread.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.2] Smell detects aroma through receptor cells in the nasal cavity (the nose), which tell us whether food is fresh or rancid (gone off, as fat does).
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.3] Texture is judged by mouth feel and by sight, and a variety of textures, such as a crisp salad served with a soft dish, makes a meal more interesting.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.4.4] The shape and size of cut pieces affect appearance; uniform cuts look neater and cook evenly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.b] Food that looks and smells good is more appealing and is eaten with more enjoyment. It also helps appetite, which matters especially for children, older people and the ill, and dishes are judged on presentation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.0] Cut food evenly so it looks neat and cooks evenly. Avoid overcooking, which dulls colour and softens texture; green vegetables cooked briefly in fast-boiling water keep their colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.1] A garnish adds colour and contrast, for example parsley on soup, parmesan shavings or grated cheese on pasta, or icing sugar on a sponge or cake.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.2] A garnish should be edible, in keeping with the dish and small enough not to overpower it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.3] Hot food should be served hot and cold food cold, on warmed plates for hot food and chilled plates for cold food, so the dish stays at its best temperature.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.4] Choose contrasting colours on the plate, such as a red pepper beside green beans, and vary shape and texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.5.5] Arrange food with height and balance, keep the rim of the plate clean and portion each item in proportion to the dish. A neat table setting also adds to the effect.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.b] The same recipe is not right for everyone. The number of people, their ages and activity levels, and the budget all shape what is cooked.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.0] For one person, plan a single-serving dish such as a filled jacket potato, or cook a batch that can be frozen in single portions, which avoids waste.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.1] Someone eating alone, such as an older person or a student, should still make sure the meal supplies enough nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.2] For a family group, plan meals to suit the ages, tastes, activity levels and budget of the family, use lower-cost cuts and ingredients, and involve the family in choices.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.3] A one-pot or batch dish, such as a casserole, feeds several people at low cost with little washing-up.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.4] Cook a dish in advance and reheat it on a busy day, so that the meal is ready at a convenient time.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.5] Young children need small, soft pieces that are easy to chew, with little added salt and sugar.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.6] Teenagers and active adults need larger portions and energy-rich starchy foods, and teenagers need extra iron and calcium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.6.7] Older people often need smaller, nutrient-dense portions (a lot of nutrition in a small amount), with soft textures if chewing is difficult.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.b] A modified diet changes the choice of food and the cooking methods, but the meal must still be balanced and attractive. For each diet, the cook changes particular ingredients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.0] Coeliac: wheat, barley and rye contain gluten, so gluten-free flours and starches, such as rice and maize, replace them in bread, pastry, sauces and coatings, and labels are checked. Oats must be labelled gluten-free.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.1] Diabetic: carbohydrate portions are controlled and added sugar is reduced, with wholegrain starches and high-fibre vegetables preferred and less fat used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.2] Low-fat or heart-healthy: lean meat, oily fish and low-fat dairy are used, with grilling, steaming and baking because they need little added fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.3] Low-salt: no added salt or stock cubes, processed meats and salted snacks are avoided, and dishes are flavoured with herbs and spices.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.4] Vegetarian and vegan: pulses, tofu, nuts and seeds replace meat, and eggs and dairy foods do so for vegetarians only. Include sources of iron and vitamin B12, use plant-based milks, and check for hidden animal ingredients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.5] Lactose intolerance: lactose-free milk or a fortified plant alternative replaces milk, and labels are checked for hidden milk ingredients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s6.7.6] High-fibre: wholemeal flour, pulses, vegetables and fruit are used.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s4: 4. Dietary guidelines
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.b] Dietary guidelines are official advice, based on scientific evidence, on the types and amounts of food that keep a population healthy. They turn nutrition science into practical advice for the general healthy population, and are revised as evidence changes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.0] Evidence comes from nutrition research, national food consumption surveys (which record what people actually eat) and health data on diet-related diseases.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.1] Expert committees review this evidence and turn it into practical advice on foods rather than nutrients, setting the types of food to eat and the amounts to limit. The guidelines are reviewed as new evidence appears.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.2] The Department of Health publishes the healthy eating guidelines for Ireland and the Food Pyramid, which is one of the tools used to explain them (the current pyramid is called 'Healthy Food for Life', 2016).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.3] The HSE supports the guidelines, and the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) and safefood are other Irish bodies in this field.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.4] The guidelines are written for the general healthy population, so they are not a substitute for advice for individuals with special medical needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.5] The aims are to make sure people get enough nutrients, to prevent deficiency, and to reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, some cancers and osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.6] One consistent message also helps consumers, schools and food planners, because everyone is working from the same advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.b] The Irish guidelines can be given as about eight principles. Together they say which foods to eat most, which to eat in moderation and which to keep small.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.0] Eat plenty of vegetables, salad and fruit: about five to seven portions a day, spread across meals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.1] Base meals on starchy foods, choosing wholegrain bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.2] Include protein from lean meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans, lentils and nuts, with fish eaten regularly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.3] Choose milk, yoghurt and cheese in lower-fat forms, as they supply calcium and HBV protein (high biological value protein, which contains all the essential amino acids).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.4] Use fats and oils sparingly, and prefer unsaturated oils such as rapeseed and olive oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.5] Limit foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, which are not needed for health and should be occasional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.6] Drink water as the main drink, and keep alcohol within recommended limits.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.7] Eat regular meals and prepare food with methods that add little fat, such as steaming, grilling and baking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.b] Each guideline is linked to a health benefit or a health risk. Examiners want the guideline and its reason together.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.0] Fruit and vegetables supply fibre, vitamins, minerals and antioxidants (substances that protect cells from damage), which protect against chronic disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.1] Wholegrain starchy foods give more fibre and nutrients than refined white flour products, and promote bowel health because fibre helps prevent constipation and bowel disorders.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.2] Saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.3] Excess salt is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of stroke and heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.4] Sugar adds energy without nutrients, contributes to obesity, and causes tooth decay.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.b] An RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) is a planning value: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of nearly all healthy people in a group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.0] An RDA is set for each nutrient and differs by age, sex and life stage, such as growth, pregnancy, lactation (breastfeeding) or adolescence.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.1] RDAs are used to plan menus and to check that a diet supplies enough of each nutrient over the day, by comparing the day's nutrient total with the RDA. They also guide food-labelling and supplement standards and institutions such as schools and hospitals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.2] An RDA is a guide for a group; it is not a precise target for an individual.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.3] Intake far above the RDA does not give extra benefit and can be harmful for some nutrients, so supplements should be used with care.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.4] Nutrients that are often low in Irish diets, such as iron and calcium, are the ones to check against the RDA when planning a menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.b] A food composition table is a reference book of food values. It lets you turn a menu into numbers, so you can check it against the RDAs and the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.0] A food composition table gives the nutrient and energy content of a food, usually per 100 g of edible food. Irish tables are produced from national food analysis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.1] Tables state whether values are for raw or cooked food, so the matching version must be used because cooking changes the nutrient content.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.2] Method: for each food, work out the grams eaten, divide by 100, then multiply by the value per 100 g.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.3] Example (sample data): 150 g of a food with 3 g fibre per 100 g supplies 150 / 100 x 3 = 4.5 g fibre.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.4] Add the results for all foods in the menu to give the day's total for each nutrient and for energy, then compare the total with the RDA. Where a total is low or high, adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.b] A computer nutrition program does the same job as a food composition table, but automatically. It is quick to use, though it is only as good as the information put in.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.0] Nutrition programs contain a built-in food composition database; the user enters each food and the quantity eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.1] The program totals the energy and nutrients for the day and compares them with the guidelines or RDAs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.2] Check that the portion size entered matches the food as eaten, and that the correct cooking method is selected, because the results depend on correct portions and the right cooking state.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.3] A program shows which nutrients are low or high, which helps the planner adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.4] Advantages are that it is fast and accurate and holds many foods and recipes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.b] The Food Pyramid turns the healthy eating guidelines into the types and relative amounts of food needed each day. Foods with similar nutrients are grouped on shelves, with the base shelf eaten most and the top shelf least.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.0] The current Irish pyramid has six shelves. At the base are vegetables, salad and fruit, with wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice above them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.1] Next come milk, yogurt and cheese, then meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (including lean choices), then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, to be used sparingly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.2] The top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt. These are not needed for health, have no recommended servings, and should be occasional. Water is the main drink.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.3] Servings: five to seven servings of vegetables, salad and fruit a day, three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese (five for ages 9 to 18), and three to five servings of wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.4] The number and size of servings from the other shelves are adjusted for age, sex, health, activity level and life stage, for example children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.5] To plan meals, take most foods from the large lower shelves, some from the middle and very little from the top, and vary the foods within each shelf. Include vegetables or fruit at every meal, and choose wholemeal and lower-fat versions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.6] Spread the choices across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks so the day covers every shelf and matches the person's portions. This supports energy balance and prevents both deficiency and excess.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.7] The pyramid guides what to eat and roughly how much, not an exact diet, and it does not replace advice for medical conditions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.8] Diagram: draw the Food Pyramid, label each shelf with the main foods on it, and mark the base shelf and the top shelf.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.b] A serving (portion) is a standard measure of food used to plan and count intake against the pyramid. It makes diets easy to compare, because everyone counts in the same units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.0] A portion is a standard amount of food used for planning, counting and comparing diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.1] One piece of fruit, such as an apple, or one serving of vegetables counts as one portion of fruit or vegetables.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.2] One glass of milk counts as one portion of milk, yoghurt or cheese in the dairy group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.3] Portion size should be adjusted to the person's age and activity: teenagers and active adults need larger portions from the starchy foods shelf than older, less active adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.4] Serving sizes and numbers rise for growing teenagers and active people and fall for older, less active adults. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra servings of some groups.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.b] Food supplements are concentrates of important nutrients. They can fill a gap in a diet, but they cannot do everything that food does.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.0] Food supplements are concentrates of nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, omega-3 fatty acids (polyunsaturated fatty acids) and amino acids, sold in tablet, capsule or powder form.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.1] They may be used to fill gaps where diet is restricted, where allergies or intolerances exclude foods, or where a person has special needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.2] Folic acid prevents neural tube defects (defects of the baby's brain and spine) in a developing baby when taken before conception and in early pregnancy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.3] Iron supplements can treat anaemia caused by low iron intake, under medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.4] Older people may absorb nutrients less efficiently, so they may need supplements, particularly vitamin D.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.5] Their popularity is growing for reasons such as busy lifestyles, poor diets, media and advertising, a wish to prevent illness, special needs (vegans, restricted diets, illness), sports performance and convenience.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.6] Supplements cannot replace a varied diet, because food supplies fibre and other nutrients that supplements do not contain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.7] High doses of some vitamins and minerals can be harmful, especially fat-soluble vitamins, so supplements should not be taken above the recommended levels. They can also cost a lot, so take medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.b] Healthy eating is only part of staying healthy. The lifestyle guidelines cover activity, mental wellbeing and habits that harm the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.0] Regular physical activity: build activity into daily life, such as brisk walking, cycling, sport or active housework, and include muscle-strengthening and weight-bearing exercise (which loads the bones, such as walking or running).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.1] Physical activity improves cardiovascular fitness, maintains muscle and bone, supports weight control and mental wellbeing, and may raise protective HDL cholesterol (the protective form of cholesterol).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.2] Mental health: get adequate sleep and rest, keep a realistic workload, and use stress management, relaxation and social contact.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.3] Avoid tobacco and second-hand smoke, which damage the lungs and blood vessels and greatly raise the risk of cancer and coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.4] Limit alcohol: it supplies empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrients), harms the liver and judgement, and must never be used to manage stress.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.5] Drink plenty of water and keep a healthy body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.6] Regular health checks and good personal hygiene complete the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.7] Together these habits reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension (high blood pressure) and heart disease.

CANDIDATES hom-meal-planning-s4: 4. Modified diets
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.b] Coronary heart disease (CHD) is the narrowing of the coronary arteries, the vessels feeding the heart muscle, by fatty deposits. The aim of the CHD diet is to lower LDL cholesterol and blood pressure and to reduce the strain on the heart and arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.0] Replace saturated animal fats with mono- and polyunsaturated oils such as olive, rapeseed and sunflower oil, because saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol, the type that clogs arteries. Use low-cholesterol products where possible.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.1] Choose low-fat dairy products and lean meat, and trim visible fat from meat before cooking. Eat more fish and skinless chicken in place of fatty red meat.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.2] Eat oily fish such as salmon, mackerel and sardines, which supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.3] Increase soluble fibre from oats, beans, lentils, fruit and vegetables, which helps lower LDL cholesterol. Eat more fruit and vegetables in general.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.4] Reduce salt, which helps keep blood pressure down, and use herbs, spices and lemon juice for flavour.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.5] Reduce refined carbohydrate and sugar, such as white bread, sugary foods and sugary drinks, and reduce alcohol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.6] Use low-fat cooking methods: grill, bake, steam or boil food rather than frying it, avoid creamy sauces, and skim fat from stock and gravy.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.7] A sample day's menu: porridge with low-fat milk and fruit; a wholegrain sandwich with chicken and salad; grilled oily fish with vegetables and potatoes; low-fat yoghurt or fruit.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.b] Cholesterol is the substance behind the CHD diet. The body needs some of it, but the type and amount in the blood decide whether it helps or harms the arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.0] Cholesterol is a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell, made mainly by the liver and also obtained from animal foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.1] Cholesterol forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed for some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.2] Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is associated with saturated fat and deposits cholesterol in artery walls, hardening them. This is why LDL is called the harmful cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.3] High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is associated with unsaturated fat and carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.4] Lifestyle changes that reduce CHD risk include maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, avoiding excess alcohol, exercising regularly and reducing stress.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.b] Some foods are designed to do more than feed you. These functional foods can be added to a CHD menu to help protect the heart.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.0] A functional food has health benefits beyond its basic nutritive value, so it does more than supply energy and nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.1] Plant sterol and stanol spreads, yoghurts, cheese spreads and drinks reduce cholesterol absorption and so lower LDL cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.2] Omega-3 enriched eggs and milk supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.3] Prebiotic and probiotic milk and yoghurt improve digestion.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.4] Fortified foods have vitamins and minerals added to them, so they supply nutrients the diet may otherwise lack.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.b] A lacto-vegetarian eats no meat, poultry, fish or eggs, but includes milk and dairy products. Without meat, careful choices are needed to get enough protein, iron, calcium and vitamins.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.0] Protein comes from milk, cheese and yoghurt (high biological value protein), and from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products such as tofu and textured vegetable protein (TVP), and mycoprotein. Combining plant proteins across the day gives a good range of amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.1] Calcium comes from milk, yoghurt and cheese, and from green leafy vegetables, sesame seeds and fortified soya products. Vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus help calcium absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.2] Iron comes from green leafy vegetables, pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit and fortified cereals; vitamin C from fruit and vegetables at the same meal improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.3] Vitamin B12 comes from dairy products, vitamin B group from wholegrains, and vitamin D from fortified spreads and cereals and from sunlight. Omega-3 fats come from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.4] Some cheeses are made with animal rennet, and some mycoprotein products contain egg white, so labels must be checked.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.5] Eggs are excluded, so the diet relies on dairy and pulses for protein and on fortified foods for nutrients that eggs would normally supply.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.6] Other types of vegetarian: lacto-ovo includes eggs and dairy, ovo includes eggs, and pesco includes fish. Some sources also allow eggs in a lacto-vegetarian diet, but the strict definition is dairy yes, eggs no.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.7] People become vegetarian for religious, cultural, moral, health, economic or environmental reasons. A sample day: orange juice, porridge with milk and wholemeal toast; vegetable soup, brown bread, yoghurt and fruit; vegetarian lasagne or a lentil dish with green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.b] A vegan diet excludes all animal-derived foods, including meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and honey. Because dairy and eggs are gone too, a vegan must plan protein, calcium, iron and vitamin B12 from plant and fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.0] Protein comes from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products, vegan mycoprotein, seitan and tempeh; eating pulses with grains or nuts over the day provides all the essential amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.1] Vitamin B12 has no reliable plant source, so it must come from fortified foods such as soya milk, cereals and yeast extract, or from a supplement.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.2] Calcium comes from fortified soya milk and flour, calcium-set tofu, sesame seeds in tahini, nuts, vegan cheese, and broccoli, spinach and other dark green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.3] Iron comes from pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit, dark green vegetables and fortified cereals; vitamin C from peppers, tomatoes, berries and citrus improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.4] Vitamin D comes from fortified foods and sunlight, and omega-3 fats from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.5] Hidden animal ingredients can turn up in stock cubes, some spreads, some bread and sweets (gelatine) and in cheese made with rennet, so choose labelled vegan products and check for gelatine, milk powder, egg, rennet or honey.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.6] A vegan menu example uses vegan mycoprotein mince, lentils or beans, vegetables and fruit, which excludes animal products and still supplies protein, iron, calcium and fortified B12.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.b] Coeliac disease is a lifelong intolerance to gluten, the protein found in wheat, barley and rye. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.0] Gluten damages the villi, the finger-like lining of the small intestine, so food is not properly absorbed and deficiencies of iron, calcium and folate can develop.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.1] Avoid bread, cakes, biscuits, pastries, pizzas and pies made with wheat, ordinary pasta and couscous, seitan, and processed foods such as sausages, some soups, sauces and gravies. Wheat, barley, rye and malt are all sources of gluten.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.2] Build meals around naturally gluten-free staples such as rice, corn (maize), potatoes, buckwheat, soya products and lentils, plus meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fruit and vegetables. Gluten-free versions of bread, pasta and flour are also available.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.3] Check ingredient lists for hidden gluten in stock, sauces, malt products and processed meats, and look for the gluten-free symbol or declaration on products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.4] A gluten-free claim means the food as sold contains no more than 20 mg of gluten per kilogram (Regulation (EU) No 828/2014, FSAI guidance). Wheat and oats used must still appear in the ingredients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.5] Avoid cross-contamination in the kitchen, for example by using a separate toaster and utensils, so crumbs of gluten do not reach safe food. Oats are naturally gluten-free but often contaminated, so use only oats labelled gluten-free.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.6] Gluten-free breads and cereals are often low in fibre, so choose brown rice, beans and other high-fibre gluten-free foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.7] Replace the iron, calcium and folate normally supplied by wheat products with other naturally gluten-free or fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.b] The diabetic diet aims to keep blood glucose stable, maintain a healthy weight and reduce the risk of heart disease. It does not mean no carbohydrate; it means carbohydrate that is low-GI, regular and controlled.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.0] Regular meals and snacks at set times prevent large swings in blood glucose; skipping meals is avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.1] Low-glycaemic-index (GI) carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, oats, pasta and pulses release glucose slowly, so blood glucose rises gradually. High-fibre foods and plenty of vegetables also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.2] Refined sugar, sugar-sweetened soft drinks, sweets and sugar-laden processed foods are avoided or limited, and low-calorie sweeteners can replace sugar.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.3] Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and low-sugar fruits are chosen, as high-sugar fruit should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.4] Saturated fat and salt are reduced, and unsaturated oils are used instead, because diabetes raises the risk of heart disease. Keeping a healthy weight and exercising also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.5] Insulin dose is matched to the carbohydrate eaten in people using insulin, so meal timing and portions must be consistent.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.b] People on modified diets need suitable foods that are easy to find and easy to use. Manufacturers and retailers both respond to this.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.0] Manufacturers produce specialised ranges such as gluten-free, diabetic, vegetarian and coronary heart disease products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.1] Manufacturers give detailed labels and packet recipes, and offer smaller portion sizes for people who need them.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.2] Retailers provide specialist shops or sections, designated shelves and wider ranges of special-diet foods, so customers can find suitable products quickly.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.3] Retailers run in-store cookery demonstrations to show customers how to use special-diet products, and provide information and websites.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.b] Labels let people on special diets check quickly whether a food is suitable. What the label shows, and how far it can be trusted, is what exams focus on.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.0] The name of the food and ingredients must be listed in descending order of weight, so the main ingredients are easy to identify.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.1] Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, allergens must be highlighted in the ingredient list.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.2] Nutrition information shows the energy, fat, saturated fat, sugar, salt and fibre content of the food.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.3] Symbols and claims show gluten-free, vegetarian and vegan status and whether a food is fortified, functional, low-fat or reduced-sugar, which helps people with specific needs choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.4] Labels give cooking and storage instructions, the net quantity, the use-by or best-before date, the origin and any GM ingredients, helping people choose foods safely.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.5] Labels are useful but must be read critically: claims can mislead, small print can hide gluten, and 'may contain' warnings need checking.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s3: 3. Choice of cooking method
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.b] Cooking methods fall into three groups according to what carries the heat to the food. The right choice depends on the food, the eater and the result you want.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.0] Moist methods use water, stock or steam as the medium. Dry methods use hot air or radiant heat (heat given off as rays from a hot element or flame) with little or no added liquid. Fat-based methods use fat or oil as the medium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.1] The food: tender cuts and delicate foods such as fish, eggs and vegetables suit quick methods, because they need little time to become tender. Tough, fibrous cuts need long, moist methods, which soften the fibres.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.2] Nutrition: steaming, stir-frying and grilling use little or no water and short times, so they conserve water-soluble vitamins. Boiling loses them into the cooking water unless that liquid is used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.3] Palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat): dry and fat-based methods brown and crisp the surface, adding colour and flavour, while moist methods give a soft, tender texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.4] Diet: steaming, poaching, boiling and grilling on a rack add little or no fat, which suits a diet that limits fat. Deep-fat frying adds fat and energy because the food absorbs fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.5] Consumer needs: an older person who has difficulty chewing or swallowing needs soft, moist, easily digested food, so a moist method is the better choice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.6] Time and equipment: a pressure cooker shortens cooking time, while stewing and braising are long, slow methods in a covered pan or oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.7] Cost: slow moist cooking makes an inexpensive cut of meat tender and palatable, so you do not have to buy an expensive cut.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.b] In moist methods the food is cooked in water, stock or steam. The temperature of the liquid is what separates them: boiling is hottest, simmering is just below it and poaching is lower again.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.0] Boiling: food is cooked in rapidly bubbling water at boiling point, 100 °C at sea level. It is quick and used for vegetables, pasta, rice, eggs and potatoes, but water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) into the water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.1] Simmering: liquid is kept just below boiling point, with small bubbles breaking the surface. It is gentler than boiling and is used for stocks, soups, stews and stewed fruit.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.2] Stewing: food is cooked slowly and gently in a small amount of liquid in a covered pan or casserole, on the hob or in the oven. It suits tough meat and vegetables, as in Irish stew, a traditional stew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.3] Stewing keeps nutrients because the liquid is served with the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.4] Steaming: food is cooked by the steam from boiling water without touching the water, in a steamer, on a plate over a pan or in a covered bowl in a pan. No fat is added, and nutrients and shape are kept. It suits fish, chicken, vegetables, rice and steamed puddings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.5] Poaching: food is cooked gently in liquid kept slightly below simmering point, about 85 °C, on the hob or in the oven. It suits delicate foods that would break up or toughen in fiercer heat, such as eggs, fish and fruit like pears.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.6] Braising: tough meat or vegetables are browned in fat, then cooked slowly with a little liquid in a covered dish, in the oven or on the hob. The long, slow, moist cooking tenderises tough cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.7] Pressure cooking: food is cooked in a sealed pan in which steam builds up pressure. This raises the temperature above 100 °C and shortens the cooking time. It is used for stocks, stews, tough meat and pulses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.b] Dry methods cook food with hot air or radiant heat rather than liquid. They brown the surface, which adds colour and flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.0] Roasting: meat, poultry, potatoes or vegetables are cooked uncovered in the oven in a roasting tin, usually with a little fat. The surface browns while the centre cooks by conduction and convection.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.1] Baking: food is cooked by the dry heat of an oven, mainly by convection (hot air circulating) and radiation. It is used for cakes, bread, pastry, fish, pasta and potato dishes, as with fish pie and lasagne.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.2] Grilling: food is cooked by radiant heat from an element or flame above or below it. The heat seals the surface, so it is a fast method. It suits tender cuts, steaks, chops, thin fish, sausages, rashers, tomatoes, mushrooms, peppers and kebabs.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.3] Barbecuing: this is grilling outdoors on a grid over burning charcoal or gas, using radiant heat, which gives a smoky flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.4] Grilling lowers the fat content of food, because fat drips away from it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.b] Grilling is fast and the heat is fierce, so food can burn outside while it is raw inside, or dry out. These rules prevent that.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.0] Preheat the grill to the required temperature so that the surface seals on contact.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.1] Oil the grid or the food to prevent sticking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.2] Use thin pieces, so that heat reaches the centre before the surface burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.3] Do not add salt before grilling, because salt draws moisture to the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.4] Turn the food with tongs rather than a fork, so that its juices stay inside instead of running out through holes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.5] Use the correct cooking time, since grilled food dries out and overcooks quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.b] Fat-based methods, usually called frying, cook food in hot fat or oil. They give a crisp, golden surface, but the amount of fat used varies a great deal from one method to another.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.0] Shallow frying: food is cooked in hot fat in a shallow pan, partly covered by the fat and turned once or twice, giving a crisp surface. It is used for eggs, fish, chops and pancakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.1] Deep-fat frying: food is fully immersed in hot oil in a deep pan or fryer, which seals the surface quickly. It is used for chips and fish in batter. The food absorbs some fat, so it is higher in fat and energy than grilled food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.2] Stir-frying: small, thin pieces are tossed and stirred constantly over high heat in a wok or large pan with a little oil. Because the cooking is so quick, it keeps colour, crispness and vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.3] Dry frying: food is cooked in a non-stick pan without added fat, using the fat already in foods such as bacon rashers and sausages.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.4] Pot roasting: meat is cooked in a little fat in a covered pan on the hob, so it cooks in its own juices and steam and stays moist. It is not done in the oven and not uncovered.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.b] A microwave oven is a fast method that suits reheating and cooking small amounts. It works differently from the other methods, so its advantages and limits are worth knowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.0] Microwave ovens cook and reheat quickly with little added fat and water, so vitamins are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.1] They do not brown food unless a grill or combination mode is used, so the surface stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.2] Containers must be microwave-safe, and metal must not be used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.b] Examiners often ask you to give dishes or foods for a method. These are the examples that are accepted.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.0] Baking: apple crumble, sponge, soda bread, fish pie, lasagne, pizza and muffins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.1] Grilling: kebabs, chicken satay, burgers, fish, rashers, sausages, tomatoes, mushrooms and bruschetta.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.2] Steaming: fish, chicken, rice, Christmas pudding (a traditional steamed pudding), fruit and vegetables. Roasting: meat, potatoes and vegetables.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.3] Frying has four types: dry, shallow, deep and stir-fry. The moist methods are boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.4] Classifying methods: moist covers boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking. Dry covers baking, grilling, roasting and barbecuing. Frying covers dry, shallow, deep and stir-frying.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.b] Steaming is a good choice for an older person because it is simple and gives soft food that is easy to eat. It also has weaknesses, so a full evaluation covers ease, nutrition and palatability.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.0] Ease: steaming needs little attention or equipment, a whole meal can cook in a tiered steamer, and it suits small portions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.1] Texture: the moist, soft food is easy to chew and swallow, which suits an older person with difficulty chewing or swallowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.2] Nutrition: steaming adds no fat, so it suits a diet that limits fat. Because the food does not sit in water, water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.3] Palatability: steamed food keeps its colour and moisture, but it can taste bland and does not brown. Overcooking spoils its texture and colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.4] Fish, chicken, rice and vegetables suit steaming; steamed puddings are a traditional example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.5] Herbs, lemon juice and a sauce add flavour without added salt, which suits a diet that limits salt.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s6: 6. Energy value and dietary fibre targets
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.b] Every nutrient releases a set amount of energy per gram, so we can work out how much energy a food supplies from its nutrient content. Carbohydrate is the main energy nutrient in most diets, so you need its figure and how to use it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.0] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram. Sugars and starch have the same energy value per gram, so a gram of sugar gives no more energy than a gram of starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.1] For comparison, protein provides 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, fat 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram and alcohol 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. Fat gives more than twice the energy of carbohydrate for the same weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.2] Fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) is not digested by human enzymes, so it contributes little usable energy. Say 'little', not 'none'.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.3] To calculate the energy from a food, multiply the grams of carbohydrate by 16 to get kilojoules (kJ), or by 3.75 to get kilocalories (kcal).
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.4] Worked example: 30 g of sugar provides 30 × 16 = 480 kJ, or 30 × 3.75 = 112.5 kcal. One kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ), which is why the kcal figure is smaller.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.5] Label point: the nutrition declaration on a food label states carbohydrate and, of which, sugars; fibre is an optional item. The energy figure is calculated by the manufacturer, so it may not match your own calculation exactly.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.6] Settings, comparative study: compare two breakfast cereals, one with added sugar and one without, using the label's carbohydrate, sugar and energy per 100 g. Discuss which supplies more energy and which supplies more fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.7] Settings, activity comparison: work out the energy in a food such as a bar of chocolate and compare it with the time an activity such as walking or running takes to use it up. Use the energy values from the source given with the question.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.b] Carbohydrate, mostly as starch, is the main source of energy in most diets, and it should supply the largest share of the energy in the diet, with fat and protein supplying the rest. The kind of carbohydrate matters, because some carries other nutrients and some does not.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.0] Healthy eating guidelines use the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid (Department of Health and HSE, 2016). Its base shelf is vegetables, salad and fruit, and the second shelf up is wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.1] The top shelf holds foods high in fat, sugar and salt. These are eaten in small amounts and not every day, so they should not be the main source of energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.2] Choose wholemeal and wholegrain starchy foods, because they supply energy together with fibre, vitamins and minerals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.3] Foods with a lot of added sugar supply energy but few other nutrients, so they are described as energy-dense and nutrient-poor ('empty calories').
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.4] Replacing sugary foods and refined starchy foods with wholegrain starchy foods, fruit and vegetables raises the intake of fibre and nutrients without raising the energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.b] Fibre is the part of plant foods that humans cannot digest, and adults are advised to eat a set amount each day. Many Irish adults do not reach it, so you should know the target and the everyday ways to meet it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.0] The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommends a daily intake of dietary fibre for adults of 25 g a day. This is its adequate intake, set in 2010 for normal bowel function.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.1] Irish healthy eating guidance from the Department of Health and the HSE (the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid) also advises eating wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetables every day.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.2] Breakfast: choose a high-fibre cereal (look for at least 3 g of fibre per 100 g on the label), porridge, bran or wholegrain bread or toast. Check the fibre per 100 g when choosing cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.3] Choose wholegrain or wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta and brown flour in baking in place of white versions, because the fibre is in the outer part of the grain that white versions have lost.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.4] Add pulses such as beans, lentils, peas and chickpeas to soups, stews and salads. Half a tin of beans gives about 7.5 g of fibre, and chickpeas, kidney beans and butter beans give about 10 g.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.5] Add two tablespoons of seeds a day, such as sunflower, sesame, pumpkin and linseed, or bran or porridge oats, to cereal, yoghurt, bread and salads.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.6] Eat vegetables at lunch and dinner, about a third of each meal; frozen vegetables are as good as fresh. Leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, and bake or boil potatoes in their jackets, since most of the fibre in a potato is in the skin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.7] Eat 2-3 pieces of fruit a day (a piece gives about 2 g of fibre), and snack on fresh fruit, dried fruit or a handful of nuts instead of biscuits or crisps. Increase fibre gradually and drink plenty of water, because fibre absorbs water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.b] Added sugar supplies energy with few other nutrients, so cutting it down is a main healthy eating target. Strategies fall into two groups: what you buy, and how you prepare and eat food.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.0] Purchasing: read labels and ingredient lists, because added sugars appear under names such as sucrose, glucose syrup, fructose, honey, cane sugar, brown sugar, corn syrup and fruit juice concentrate.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.1] Choose reduced-sugar, lower-sugar or no-added-sugar versions of drinks, jams, cereals and yoghurts, choose fruit canned in juice rather than syrup, and avoid sugar-coated cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.2] Front-of-pack colour (traffic-light) labelling, where used, shows at a glance whether a food is high, medium or low in sugars. It is voluntary, so not every product carries it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.3] Preparing: reduce the sugar in recipes and use spices, vanilla or ripe fruit for flavour. Pureed fruit, banana, dates or apple can sweeten recipes instead of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.4] Do not add sugar to tea, coffee or breakfast cereals. Replace fizzy drinks with water, or with fruit juice diluted with sparkling water, which cuts the sugar per glass.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.5] Eat whole fruit rather than fruit juice or smoothies. Juice contains little fibre, and the sugar in juices and smoothies is released from the fruit's cells so it counts as free sugar, whereas whole fruit keeps its fibre and fills you up.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.6] Snacks and treats: limit sweets, biscuits, cakes, sweet sauces and sugary drinks, and keep fresh fruit, fruit or vegetable sticks, nuts and natural yoghurt as snacks instead.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.b] A diet high in sugar causes harm in several ways, some directly on the teeth and others through the energy and nutrients the diet supplies. Each effect needs its reason to earn marks.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.0] Tooth decay: bacteria in plaque (the sticky film on teeth) ferment sugar to produce acid, which dissolves the enamel of the teeth.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.1] Weight gain and obesity: sugary foods are energy-dense and nutrient-poor, so regular intake adds to excess energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.2] Type 2 diabetes: a diet high in sugar and energy is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes, which is linked to excess body weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.3] Heart disease and high blood pressure: high sugar intake is associated with a higher risk of heart disease and raised blood pressure. High fructose intake has been linked with higher blood pressure, and refined carbohydrate with excess insulin that can raise LDL cholesterol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.4] Nutritional deficiency: foods high in sugar displace fruit, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods from the diet, so vitamins and minerals fall short.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.5] Sugary foods are also low in fibre, so they do little for bowel health.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.b] Artificial sweeteners give a sweet taste without the energy of sugar, or with less of it. They fall into two types, intense and bulk, and each has its uses and limits.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.0] Intense sweeteners, such as aspartame and saccharin, are many times sweeter than sugar, so only tiny amounts are needed and they add almost no energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.1] Bulk sweeteners, such as sorbitol, mannitol and xylitol, have a similar bulk to sugar and provide some energy, though less than sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.2] Uses in food manufacture: they sweeten and improve palatability, and they are used in low-calorie, sugar-free, slimming and reduced-sugar foods, soft drinks and confectionery.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.3] Sorbitol and other bulk sweeteners act as humectants: they retain moisture and stop products drying out.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.4] Bulk sweeteners are used where sugar's bulk is needed, as in confectionery and fudge. Xylitol and sorbitol do not cause tooth decay, so they are used to avoid it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.5] Sweeteners cannot replace all of sugar's functions: they do not provide the bulk, browning, preserving or bulk-texture roles of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.6] Sweeteners are food additives with E numbers. Labels must say 'contains sweetener', and aspartame carries a warning for people with phenylketonuria.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.7] Excess bulk sweeteners can have a laxative effect, so their labels carry a warning.
