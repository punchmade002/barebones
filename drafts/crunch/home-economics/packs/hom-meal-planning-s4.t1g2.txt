TARGET hom-meal-planning-s4: 4. Modified diets
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.b] Coronary heart disease (CHD) is the narrowing of the coronary arteries, the vessels feeding the heart muscle, by fatty deposits. The aim of the CHD diet is to lower LDL cholesterol and blood pressure and to reduce the strain on the heart and arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.0] Replace saturated animal fats with mono- and polyunsaturated oils such as olive, rapeseed and sunflower oil, because saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol, the type that clogs arteries. Use low-cholesterol products where possible.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.1] Choose low-fat dairy products and lean meat, and trim visible fat from meat before cooking. Eat more fish and skinless chicken in place of fatty red meat.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.2] Eat oily fish such as salmon, mackerel and sardines, which supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.3] Increase soluble fibre from oats, beans, lentils, fruit and vegetables, which helps lower LDL cholesterol. Eat more fruit and vegetables in general.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.4] Reduce salt, which helps keep blood pressure down, and use herbs, spices and lemon juice for flavour.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.5] Reduce refined carbohydrate and sugar, such as white bread, sugary foods and sugary drinks, and reduce alcohol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.6] Use low-fat cooking methods: grill, bake, steam or boil food rather than frying it, avoid creamy sauces, and skim fat from stock and gravy.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.7] A sample day's menu: porridge with low-fat milk and fruit; a wholegrain sandwich with chicken and salad; grilled oily fish with vegetables and potatoes; low-fat yoghurt or fruit.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.b] Cholesterol is the substance behind the CHD diet. The body needs some of it, but the type and amount in the blood decide whether it helps or harms the arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.0] Cholesterol is a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell, made mainly by the liver and also obtained from animal foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.1] Cholesterol forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed for some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.2] Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is associated with saturated fat and deposits cholesterol in artery walls, hardening them. This is why LDL is called the harmful cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.3] High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is associated with unsaturated fat and carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.4] Lifestyle changes that reduce CHD risk include maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, avoiding excess alcohol, exercising regularly and reducing stress.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.b] Some foods are designed to do more than feed you. These functional foods can be added to a CHD menu to help protect the heart.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.0] A functional food has health benefits beyond its basic nutritive value, so it does more than supply energy and nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.1] Plant sterol and stanol spreads, yoghurts, cheese spreads and drinks reduce cholesterol absorption and so lower LDL cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.2] Omega-3 enriched eggs and milk supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.3] Prebiotic and probiotic milk and yoghurt improve digestion.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.4] Fortified foods have vitamins and minerals added to them, so they supply nutrients the diet may otherwise lack.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.b] A lacto-vegetarian eats no meat, poultry, fish or eggs, but includes milk and dairy products. Without meat, careful choices are needed to get enough protein, iron, calcium and vitamins.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.0] Protein comes from milk, cheese and yoghurt (high biological value protein), and from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products such as tofu and textured vegetable protein (TVP), and mycoprotein. Combining plant proteins across the day gives a good range of amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.1] Calcium comes from milk, yoghurt and cheese, and from green leafy vegetables, sesame seeds and fortified soya products. Vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus help calcium absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.2] Iron comes from green leafy vegetables, pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit and fortified cereals; vitamin C from fruit and vegetables at the same meal improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.3] Vitamin B12 comes from dairy products, vitamin B group from wholegrains, and vitamin D from fortified spreads and cereals and from sunlight. Omega-3 fats come from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.4] Some cheeses are made with animal rennet, and some mycoprotein products contain egg white, so labels must be checked.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.5] Eggs are excluded, so the diet relies on dairy and pulses for protein and on fortified foods for nutrients that eggs would normally supply.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.6] Other types of vegetarian: lacto-ovo includes eggs and dairy, ovo includes eggs, and pesco includes fish. Some sources also allow eggs in a lacto-vegetarian diet, but the strict definition is dairy yes, eggs no.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.7] People become vegetarian for religious, cultural, moral, health, economic or environmental reasons. A sample day: orange juice, porridge with milk and wholemeal toast; vegetable soup, brown bread, yoghurt and fruit; vegetarian lasagne or a lentil dish with green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.b] A vegan diet excludes all animal-derived foods, including meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and honey. Because dairy and eggs are gone too, a vegan must plan protein, calcium, iron and vitamin B12 from plant and fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.0] Protein comes from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products, vegan mycoprotein, seitan and tempeh; eating pulses with grains or nuts over the day provides all the essential amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.1] Vitamin B12 has no reliable plant source, so it must come from fortified foods such as soya milk, cereals and yeast extract, or from a supplement.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.2] Calcium comes from fortified soya milk and flour, calcium-set tofu, sesame seeds in tahini, nuts, vegan cheese, and broccoli, spinach and other dark green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.3] Iron comes from pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit, dark green vegetables and fortified cereals; vitamin C from peppers, tomatoes, berries and citrus improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.4] Vitamin D comes from fortified foods and sunlight, and omega-3 fats from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.5] Hidden animal ingredients can turn up in stock cubes, some spreads, some bread and sweets (gelatine) and in cheese made with rennet, so choose labelled vegan products and check for gelatine, milk powder, egg, rennet or honey.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.6] A vegan menu example uses vegan mycoprotein mince, lentils or beans, vegetables and fruit, which excludes animal products and still supplies protein, iron, calcium and fortified B12.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.b] Coeliac disease is a lifelong intolerance to gluten, the protein found in wheat, barley and rye. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.0] Gluten damages the villi, the finger-like lining of the small intestine, so food is not properly absorbed and deficiencies of iron, calcium and folate can develop.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.1] Avoid bread, cakes, biscuits, pastries, pizzas and pies made with wheat, ordinary pasta and couscous, seitan, and processed foods such as sausages, some soups, sauces and gravies. Wheat, barley, rye and malt are all sources of gluten.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.2] Build meals around naturally gluten-free staples such as rice, corn (maize), potatoes, buckwheat, soya products and lentils, plus meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fruit and vegetables. Gluten-free versions of bread, pasta and flour are also available.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.3] Check ingredient lists for hidden gluten in stock, sauces, malt products and processed meats, and look for the gluten-free symbol or declaration on products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.4] A gluten-free claim means the food as sold contains no more than 20 mg of gluten per kilogram (Regulation (EU) No 828/2014, FSAI guidance). Wheat and oats used must still appear in the ingredients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.5] Avoid cross-contamination in the kitchen, for example by using a separate toaster and utensils, so crumbs of gluten do not reach safe food. Oats are naturally gluten-free but often contaminated, so use only oats labelled gluten-free.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.6] Gluten-free breads and cereals are often low in fibre, so choose brown rice, beans and other high-fibre gluten-free foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.7] Replace the iron, calcium and folate normally supplied by wheat products with other naturally gluten-free or fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.b] The diabetic diet aims to keep blood glucose stable, maintain a healthy weight and reduce the risk of heart disease. It does not mean no carbohydrate; it means carbohydrate that is low-GI, regular and controlled.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.0] Regular meals and snacks at set times prevent large swings in blood glucose; skipping meals is avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.1] Low-glycaemic-index (GI) carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, oats, pasta and pulses release glucose slowly, so blood glucose rises gradually. High-fibre foods and plenty of vegetables also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.2] Refined sugar, sugar-sweetened soft drinks, sweets and sugar-laden processed foods are avoided or limited, and low-calorie sweeteners can replace sugar.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.3] Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and low-sugar fruits are chosen, as high-sugar fruit should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.4] Saturated fat and salt are reduced, and unsaturated oils are used instead, because diabetes raises the risk of heart disease. Keeping a healthy weight and exercising also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.5] Insulin dose is matched to the carbohydrate eaten in people using insulin, so meal timing and portions must be consistent.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.b] People on modified diets need suitable foods that are easy to find and easy to use. Manufacturers and retailers both respond to this.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.0] Manufacturers produce specialised ranges such as gluten-free, diabetic, vegetarian and coronary heart disease products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.1] Manufacturers give detailed labels and packet recipes, and offer smaller portion sizes for people who need them.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.2] Retailers provide specialist shops or sections, designated shelves and wider ranges of special-diet foods, so customers can find suitable products quickly.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.3] Retailers run in-store cookery demonstrations to show customers how to use special-diet products, and provide information and websites.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.b] Labels let people on special diets check quickly whether a food is suitable. What the label shows, and how far it can be trusted, is what exams focus on.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.0] The name of the food and ingredients must be listed in descending order of weight, so the main ingredients are easy to identify.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.1] Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, allergens must be highlighted in the ingredient list.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.2] Nutrition information shows the energy, fat, saturated fat, sugar, salt and fibre content of the food.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.3] Symbols and claims show gluten-free, vegetarian and vegan status and whether a food is fortified, functional, low-fat or reduced-sugar, which helps people with specific needs choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.4] Labels give cooking and storage instructions, the net quantity, the use-by or best-before date, the origin and any GM ingredients, helping people choose foods safely.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.5] Labels are useful but must be read critically: claims can mislead, small print can hide gluten, and 'may contain' warnings need checking.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s4: 4. Mineral elements
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.b] Minerals are the part of food that is not made by living things at all. The body needs them in small amounts to build structures, run processes and make enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.0] Minerals are inorganic elements (not made by living things) that the body cannot make, so they must be obtained from food. They build body structures, regulate body processes and form parts of enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.1] Major (macro) minerals are needed in larger amounts, above 100 mg a day. They are calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium and sulphur.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.2] Minerals are not destroyed by cooking, because they are elements. They can, however, be lost into cooking water and in the trimming of food. For example, boiling vegetables in a lot of water washes minerals out.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily amount) is the daily intake that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, so some groups need more than others.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.4] Phosphorus works with calcium to harden bones and teeth and forms part of ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and cell membranes. Sources are milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs, wholegrains and nuts, and deficiency is rare.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.b] Calcium is the most important mineral for the skeleton, but it also keeps blood, muscles and nerves working. Because bone is built in childhood and lost in later life, some groups need more of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.0] Milk, cheese and yoghurt are the richest dietary sources of calcium. This is why dairy foods matter for bone health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.1] Canned sardines and canned salmon eaten with their bones, green leafy vegetables such as kale, broccoli and cabbage, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks and fortified breakfast cereals are further sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.2] Calcium works with phosphorus to form the mineral salts that give bones and teeth their hardness. This is why a diet low in calcium leaves bones weak.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.3] Calcium is needed for blood clotting, normal muscle contraction (including the heartbeat) and nerve impulse transmission. It also activates some enzymes and helps regulate blood pressure.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.4] Rickets in children (soft, bent bones and poor teeth) and osteomalacia in adults result from too little calcium, phosphorus or vitamin D.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.5] Osteoporosis, which develops over years, makes bones thin, porous, brittle and prone to fracture, with loss of height and a stooped back. Other effects of low calcium are tooth decay, slow clotting, and muscle cramps (tetany) and poor nerve function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.6] Osteoporosis risk is raised by low calcium and vitamin D intake, lack of weight-bearing exercise, smoking, alcohol, the fall in oestrogen after menopause and family history.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.7] Need is highest when bone is being built (childhood, teenage years, pregnancy, breastfeeding) and when it is lost (women after menopause). Bone density peaks in the late teens and twenties, so teenage intake matters.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.b] Iron is needed to carry oxygen, so a shortage leaves the whole body short of energy. It comes in two forms, and the form decides how easily the body absorbs it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.0] Haem iron comes from animal foods and is absorbed easily. Sources are red meat, liver and offal, kidney, black pudding, poultry and oily fish.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.1] Non-haem iron comes from plant foods and eggs and is absorbed less easily. Sources are eggs, wholegrain and fortified cereals, wholemeal bread, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried apricots and other dried fruit, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.2] Iron is an essential part of haemoglobin, the red protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs around the body to all cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.3] Iron is part of myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle cells, and part of enzymes that release energy from food. It also supports immunity and concentration. This is why a shortage leaves people tired.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.4] Iron deficiency causes anaemia (too few red blood cells or too little haemoglobin). Effects are tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, dizziness, irritability, poor concentration, feeling cold and reduced resistance to infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.5] Causes of iron deficiency are a diet low in iron or poor in vitamin C, blood loss (menstruation, ulcers, operations), high needs in growth spurts, pregnancy and infancy, and poor absorption.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.6] Corrective measures are eating more iron-rich foods together with vitamin C, avoiding tea with meals, and taking an iron supplement on medical advice. This is because vitamin C improves absorption and tea blocks it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.7] Groups at risk are teenage girls, menstruating and pregnant women, infants and toddlers, people with heavy blood loss, and vegetarians and vegans, who eat only non-haem iron. Children are at risk because of fast growth and low-iron foods. For example, vegetarians miss out on the more easily absorbed haem form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.b] Trace minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, yet the body cannot do without them. Each has a job that no other mineral can do.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.0] Trace (micro) minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms a day, and the diet must supply them. They are tiny in amount yet essential. For example, zinc and iodine are needed in milligrams or micrograms.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.1] The trace minerals are iron, zinc, iodine, fluoride, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium and cobalt. Iron is a trace mineral by the amount needed, but it is studied in detail at Ordinary Level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.2] Iron, zinc and iodine each play a specific role in the body that the other minerals cannot replace. Zinc and iodine are set out in the blocks that follow.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.b] Zinc is a trace mineral that works inside enzymes, so it affects growth, healing and defence against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.0] Zinc sources include oysters and other shellfish, red meat, poultry, liver, dairy products, eggs, wholegrain cereals, pulses, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.1] Zinc is a component of many enzymes and supports growth and cell division, which is why a shortage slows growth in children.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.2] Zinc supports wound healing and a healthy immune system. This is because new cells must be made to repair the wound.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.3] Zinc contributes to a normal sense of taste and smell, and to healthy skin and hair and reproductive health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.4] Deficiency causes poor growth, delayed wound healing, dry flaky skin and rashes, loss of taste and appetite, reduced immunity and hair loss.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.b] Iodine is needed to make a hormone that sets the body's pace. Without it the thyroid gland cannot do its job.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.0] Iodine sources include sea fish and shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy products and eggs. Iodised salt also exists as a source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.1] Iodine is part of thyroxine, the hormone from the thyroid gland that controls the rate of metabolism (and growth and brain development).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.2] Deficiency causes goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck as it works harder to make more thyroxine. This is because the gland enlarges as it works harder.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.3] Iodine deficiency causes tiredness and sluggishness (lethargy) and weight gain in adults. It causes delayed mental and physical development in children, and in babies of deficient mothers.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.b] Potassium and sodium work as a pair, one inside cells and one outside, to keep fluid, nerves and muscles balanced.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.0] Potassium sources include bananas, potatoes eaten in their skins, tomatoes, dried fruit, orange juice, pulses, green and leafy vegetables, milk, meat, fish and nuts.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.1] Potassium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) inside cells and, with sodium, helps regulate fluid balance in and around cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.2] Potassium is needed to transmit nerve impulses and for muscle contraction, including the heartbeat. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.3] Potassium works with sodium to regulate blood pressure, and a high potassium intake helps lower it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.4] Deficiency (hypokalaemia) causes muscle weakness, cramps, an irregular heartbeat and tiredness. It is usually caused by severe vomiting, diarrhoea or some medicines rather than by normal diets.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.b] Sodium is essential, and the problem in Ireland is eating too much of it, not too little. Most of it comes from salt, which is sodium chloride.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.0] Sodium is mainly consumed as salt. Table salt, cured and smoked meats and fish such as bacon and ham, cheese, bread, crisps and snack foods, processed and ready meals, sauces, stock cubes and canned foods supply most of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.1] Fresh, unprocessed foods contain only small amounts of naturally occurring sodium. This is why processed foods, such as bacon and ready meals, are the main source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.2] Sodium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) outside cells and, with potassium, regulates fluid balance and blood volume.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.3] Sodium is needed to transmit nerve impulses, for muscle contraction and for enzyme function. It helps the small intestine absorb glucose and some amino acids. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.4] Excess sodium raises blood pressure (hypertension), which increases the risk of stroke, heart disease and kidney problems. Most people in Ireland eat more salt than advised, mostly from processed foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.5] To cut salt, cook from fresh food, do not add salt at the table, read labels (salt is on the nutrition declaration) and choose low-salt products.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.6] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) began a voluntary Salt Reduction Programme in 2003 to cut salt in processed food. Later reformulation work continues under the FSAI-Healthy Ireland Food Reformulation Task Force.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.7] Deficiency is uncommon but causes muscle cramps, headache, nausea and confusion. It can follow heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s9: 9. Alternative (novel) protein foods
[hom-proteins-s9.0.b] Novel (alternative) protein foods replace meat, fish or eggs in the diet. They come from two kinds of source: plants, and micro-organisms (living things too small to see without a microscope, grown in large quantities).
[hom-proteins-s9.0.0] Soya beans are the main plant source. They are made into tofu, tempeh, textured vegetable protein (TVP), miso, soya milk, soya yoghurt and soya cheese, and also soya flour, soya sauce and soya oil.
[hom-proteins-s9.0.1] Other plant products include seitan, which is made from wheat gluten (the protein part of wheat), and pulses such as lentils, chickpeas and beans. Nuts and seeds, quinoa, edamame and hummus also supply plant protein.
[hom-proteins-s9.0.2] The micro-organism sources are fungi, bacteria, yeast and algae.
[hom-proteins-s9.0.3] Mycoprotein is a protein food made from a fungus grown by fermentation (growing it in a controlled tank), so it is a micro-organism product and not a plant. It is sold as Quorn in mince, pieces, burgers and sausages. Quorn is a brand name; the general term is mycoprotein.
[hom-proteins-s9.0.4] Nutritional yeast is a yeast product used as a flavouring in vegan cooking and, when fortified, as a source of B vitamins. It is also used as a source of protein in vegan cooking.
[hom-proteins-s9.1.b] Soya beans are turned into several quite different foods, and the way each is made explains its texture and taste. Some are curdled or ground, others are fermented (changed by moulds, bacteria or yeast).
[hom-proteins-s9.1.0] Tofu: soya milk is curdled and the curds are pressed into blocks. It is bland, so it takes on flavour from marinades or smoking.
[hom-proteins-s9.1.1] Soya milk: soaked soya beans are ground with their soaking water and the mixture is strained, which removes the solid pulp.
[hom-proteins-s9.1.2] Tempeh: cooked soya beans are fermented by a mould, giving a firm, chewy texture. Because the whole beans are used, it is not the same as tofu, which is made from curd.
[hom-proteins-s9.1.3] Miso: soya beans are mixed with rice (or another grain), salt and water and fermented into a paste.
[hom-proteins-s9.1.4] Soya yoghurt: soya milk is fermented in the same way as dairy yoghurt.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.b] TVP is a meat substitute made from soya flour that has had its oil removed. The aim of the process is to turn a protein-rich flour into a fibrous, chewy product, so learn the stages in order.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.0] 1. Soya beans are cleaned and de-hulled (their outer skins are removed), and their oil is extracted.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.1] 2. The de-oiled beans are ground into flour.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.2] 3. Carbohydrate is removed, leaving a protein-rich flour.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.3] 4. Vegetable oil, flavourings, seasonings and nutritional additives such as vitamin B12, iron and methionine (an amino acid) are added, which improves the taste and nutritional value.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.4] 5. The mixture is heated and extruded (forced through a machine under pressure), which forms a textured, fibrous product.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.5] 6. The product is cut, cubed or chopped into pieces such as mince or chunks.
[hom-proteins-s9.2.6] 7. The pieces are dried, packed and labelled.
[hom-proteins-s9.3.b] Mycoprotein is grown rather than farmed or pressed. A fungus is multiplied in tanks and then shaped into a meat-like food in a series of stages.
[hom-proteins-s9.3.0] 1. A fungus is fermented (grown) in carefully controlled conditions so that it grows in large quantities.
[hom-proteins-s9.3.1] 2. The fungal cells are harvested, filtered and drained.
[hom-proteins-s9.3.2] 3. A binder holds the fungal sheets together, and flavour and colour are added. The traditional product uses egg albumen as the binder.
[hom-proteins-s9.3.3] 4. The mass is textured to resemble meat.
[hom-proteins-s9.3.4] 5. It is sliced, cubed or shredded, steamed to set the binder, then packed and labelled.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.b] Soya and mycoprotein products can supply protein in place of meat, but their nutritional value depends on the product.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.0] Soya protein is high biological value (it supplies all the essential amino acids in good proportions). Soya products also supply polyunsaturated fat, fibre and B group vitamins.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.1] Calcium and iron are present in many soya products, especially tofu set with calcium salts.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.2] Mycoprotein is relatively high in protein and fibre and low in saturated fat. Plant alternatives contain no cholesterol.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.3] Advantages of alternatives: they supply high biological value protein, are low in saturated fat and contain fibre. They suit vegetarians and vegans, are relatively inexpensive and versatile, need little preparation and reduce reliance on meat.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.4] Plant foods contain no vitamin B12, so vegans need it from fortified foods or supplements.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.5] Processed alternatives may be high in salt, sugar and additives, so their labels should be checked. Soya is a labelled allergen, and Quorn contains egg in some products.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.6] In the Food Pyramid of the Department of Health healthy eating guidelines, these foods sit on the shelf of meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, which supplies protein and iron.
[hom-proteins-s9.4.7] These foods can replace meat in meals, be used as meat extenders (mixed with meat to make it go further) and be eaten in vegetarian and vegan diets.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.b] Choosing and handling these foods well depends mostly on reading the label and following the pack, because each product keeps and prepares differently.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.0] When selecting, read the label for ingredients, allergens, nutrition information and use-by date. Compare salt, sugar and fat in processed alternatives.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.1] Labels must show ingredients, allergens and a nutrition declaration, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.2] Dried TVP and dried pulses keep in airtight containers in a cool, dry place, which keeps moisture and pests out.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.3] TVP must be rehydrated in hot stock or water before use, and it takes on the flavour of the liquid.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.4] Tofu is sold chilled or in sealed packs. Once opened it should be kept under fresh water, with a lid or cover, in the refrigerator and used within the number of days on the label.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.5] Tofu is drained and pressed before frying, so that it browns well and holds its shape.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.6] Pulses are soaked and boiled before use, or bought tinned and ready to use.
[hom-proteins-s9.5.7] Mycoprotein products are sold chilled or frozen and should be cooked thoroughly as the pack directs. Follow the pack instructions for all of these products.
[hom-proteins-s9.6.b] Most alternatives have little taste or texture of their own, so processing and cooking are what make them appealing and meat-like.
[hom-proteins-s9.6.0] Extrusion gives textured vegetable protein its fibrous, chewy texture, which resembles meat.
[hom-proteins-s9.6.1] Marinating or smoking gives tofu flavour, as it is bland on its own. It can then be marinated and stir-fried or baked.
[hom-proteins-s9.6.2] Fermentation by moulds or bacteria gives tempeh, miso and soya yoghurt their texture and flavour.
[hom-proteins-s9.6.3] Mycoprotein is shaped and then steamed to set its binder, so that the pieces hold together during cooking. It must be cooked thoroughly as the pack directs.
[hom-proteins-s9.6.4] TVP is used in dishes such as bolognese, chilli and shepherd's pie. TVP, tofu and mycoprotein take on the flavour of what they are cooked in, so seasoning and sauces matter.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.b] Protein bars and high-protein products should be judged from the label data rather than from the words high protein on the packet. A high-protein claim does not make a product healthy.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.0] Compare per portion the protein, energy, sugar, saturated fat, salt and fibre, not the protein claim alone.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.1] Bars often contain added sugar, a chocolate coating, saturated fat and salt, so check these as well as the protein.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.2] Protein bars may be made with milk (whey) protein or soya protein, which supply high biological value protein. Bars are portable, which makes them most useful where portability is needed, such as for sport.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.3] A high-protein fruit yoghurt supplies high biological value milk protein, calcium, phosphorus and B group vitamins.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.4] Sweetened fruit preparations raise the free sugar content of a yoghurt. Full-fat versions contain more saturated fat, and low-fat versions may contain more added sugar.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.5] Plain yoghurt with fresh fruit is generally the less processed choice.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.6] Under EU nutrition claims rules (Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006), a food can claim to be a source of protein if at least 12% of its energy comes from protein. It can claim to be high protein if at least 20% of its energy comes from protein.
[hom-proteins-s9.7.7] When reading a data table, pick the product with the most protein and fibre and the least energy, sugar and salt for the person stated, for example a young person who plays sport.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s1: 1. Consumer choices
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.b] Every purchase starts with how much a household can spend. Income sets the limit, and it also decides whether a household buys what it needs or what it merely wants.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.0] Household income sets the limit on spending, so a household on a tight budget compares unit prices (the price per kilo or per item), buys own-brand and bulk items, and delays non-essential purchases.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.1] Disposable income is the money left after tax and essential bills. It decides how much is free for discretionary spending (spending that is a choice, not a necessity) such as eating out or branded clothing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.2] When income rises, households often trade up to branded or higher-quality goods; when it falls, they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.3] Essential needs such as food, heating and shelter are bought first, and luxury wants are the first to be cut when income falls.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.4] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income. A wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.5] Credit and easy-pay arrangements can allow spending above current income, and the debt they create reduces future choice because part of later income is already committed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.b] Beyond money, choices reflect who the buyer is, who influences them and what the product offers. For a large purchase such as a washing machine, buyers weigh several of these together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.0] Personal preference, and values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethical concerns, and cultural or religious practice), all influence what is bought.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.1] Household composition narrows choice: family size, life-cycle stage and special diets, such as a coeliac or diabetic member or an allergy, determine which goods are suitable.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.2] Time pressure pushes busy households towards convenience foods, takeaway meals and online shopping, because these save time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.3] Other people influence choice through word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.4] For a large purchase, buyers weigh the energy rating, reliability of the brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.5] The environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor that some consumers weigh when choosing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.6] Impulse buying is an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout. It results from several of the factors above, such as advertising, values and income, working together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.b] Shoppers often choose by name rather than by comparing every product. Brands and own-brand goods are the two main kinds of product on the shelf, and they differ in price and in how they are promoted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.0] A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product and distinguishes it from competing goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.1] Branded goods are made by a manufacturer and are usually advertised nationally, so they tend to cost more than equivalent goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.2] Own-brand goods are sold under the retailer's own name. They are often cheaper because the retailer avoids the cost of national advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.3] Brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand, which saves the shopper the effort of comparing alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.b] Advertising is communication that informs consumers about goods and services and tries to create a want for them. It happens mostly outside the shop, in the media a consumer meets every day.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.0] Advertising makes people aware of new products and builds brand loyalty, so that consumers ask for the product by name.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.1] It also informs consumers about features, price and offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.2] Media used include television, radio, print, outdoor billboards, websites, social media, and sponsorship of sport and events.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.3] Celebrity endorsement transfers the image or popularity of a well-known person to the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.4] Product differentiation: advertising highlights a feature, such as a special ingredient, that sets one brand apart from similar goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.5] Advertising can make people feel they need products they cannot afford, and it can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.6] Advertising aimed at children and teenagers is especially influential. It can prompt impulse purchases and family purchases, because young consumers have less experience of judging value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.7] The advantage to the consumer is awareness of choice and information. The disadvantages are that the cost of advertising is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, and there is pressure to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.b] Merchandising is the arrangement and presentation of goods in a shop to encourage consumers to buy more, and to buy more often. It is what the retailer does inside the shop, in contrast to advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.0] Store layout: goods are arranged in clearly identifiable departments with space to browse, and essentials such as bread and milk are placed at the back so that shoppers pass other products on the way to them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.1] Route design: aisles are arranged to keep shoppers moving through the store, so that they see more products and spend longer browsing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.2] Product placement: complementary goods are shelved together, such as biscuits beside tea, so that buying one reminds the shopper of the other. Sweets and magazines are placed at the checkout as impulse items.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.3] Shelf position: premium or luxury goods are placed at eye level, where they are seen first, while cheaper products are often placed on lower or higher shelves.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.4] Special offers displayed at the end of aisles draw attention to promotional stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.5] In-store stimuli: background music that slows the pace of shopping, aromas such as freshly baked bread, good lighting and warmth, and colourful displays.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.6] Sampling and demonstrations let shoppers taste or try a product before buying it, which makes a purchase more likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.b] Pricing techniques make a price look lower, or make a purchase look more valuable, than it is. Discount offers and discount stores do lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.0] Charm pricing: a price set just below a round figure, so that the item appears cheaper than it is.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.1] Multi-buy offers, such as three items for a set price, encourage consumers to buy more than they would at a single-item price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.2] Clearance and reduction labels suggest extra value and can prompt impulse buying of goods the shopper did not plan to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.3] Loss leaders: a well-known product is sold at or below cost to draw shoppers into the store, who then buy other items at normal prices.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.4] Coupons and money-off vouchers reward a specific purchase and are often distributed through loyalty schemes or advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.5] Discount offers, and discount stores, lower the cost of goods, but a lower price is not always better value, so the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.b] Loyalty schemes reward repeat custom with points or discounts, and give retailers information about what customers buy. They are one of the shopping systems named in the syllabus.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.0] Customers earn points or tokens for each amount spent. The points are exchanged for money off goods, vouchers or goods after a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.1] Examples in Ireland include the Tesco Clubcard and the Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.2] Schemes encourage saving for special occasions, such as Christmas, by building up points over time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.3] Shoppers may spend more to reach a reward target, and points can take priority over price when they compare shops, so they return to the same shop.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.4] Card use gives retailers data on buying patterns, which helps them target offers and plan stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.5] Disadvantages: the scheme discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned purchases, and ties the shopper to one shop. It also requires the card to be carried.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.b] Easy-pay arrangements spread the cost of a purchase over time, so goods can be bought without paying the full price at once. This is a form of credit, which has both benefits and costs.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.0] Easy-pay schemes: a retailer or utility company lets the consumer pay for goods or a bill in instalments over a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.1] Hire purchase: the consumer uses the goods while paying instalments under an agreement, and ownership passes only when the final instalment is paid.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.2] Hire purchase is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of the credit), and gives the consumer a cooling-off period of ten days.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.3] Advantages of credit: goods can be used immediately, the cost is spread over time, larger items become affordable, and essentials can be bought in an emergency when cash is not available.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.4] Disadvantages: the total cost is higher than the cash price because of interest and charges, easy-pay can encourage buying of non-essential goods and overspending, and the debt reduces future choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.b] Both allow shopping from home, but the consumer cannot inspect the goods before buying them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.0] Television buying: goods are demonstrated on screen and ordered by phone or online, often with a limited-time offer that creates urgency.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.1] Mail order: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or website and delivered by post or courier.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.2] Advantages of mail order: it is convenient for people without time to shop, a wide range is available, there is no travel, and orders can be placed by post or phone.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.3] Disadvantages of mail order: goods cannot be seen, touched or tried on, delivery can take weeks, and postage adds to the cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.4] Distance purchases (those made without meeting the seller) carry a statutory right to cancel within a cooling-off period under the Consumer Rights Act 2022, because the buyer could not see the goods before ordering.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.b] Online shopping offers convenience and easy price comparison, but adds risks around payment, delivery and personal data.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.0] Advantages: access at any hour from home, easy comparison of prices across retailers, a wider range, customer reviews, and delivery to the door.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.1] Disadvantages: goods cannot be seen or handled before purchase, and delivery can be delayed or carry extra charges.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.2] Payment risk: card details can be taken through phishing emails (fake messages that trick people into giving details) or fake websites, so pay only on secure sites that show a padlock symbol and an address beginning https.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.3] Keep records of the order, so that there is proof of what was bought and paid for if something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.4] Overspending risk: stored card details and one-click ordering make impulse purchases quick and easy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.5] Retailers collect data on browsing and buying, which can be used to target advertising at the individual consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.b] Packaging does more than wrap a product. It protects the goods, carries information, and helps to sell the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.0] Packaging protects and preserves the product, so food stays safe and goods are not damaged on the way to the shop or home.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.1] It informs the shopper, because the pack carries the name, instructions and other details.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.2] It attracts attention on the shelf and conveys quality through colour, shape and design.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.3] It gives convenience, such as portion size and easy-open features, and it suggests quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.4] Care labels and safety or hazard symbols on non-food goods, for example on household cleaning agents, explain how to use the product and prevent accidents.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.b] Labels on pre-packed foods are a legal requirement, so shoppers can choose safely and compare products. The detail is kept short here and is consumer-focused.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.0] Mandatory food labels show the name of the food, ingredients listed in descending order of weight (the heaviest first), net quantity, a use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and instructions for use, and the name and address of the food business.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.1] Use-by dates apply to highly perishable foods and the food is unsafe after the date; best-before dates indicate quality, and the food may still be safe after them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.2] The 14 major allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold or capitals, so that people with allergies can spot them quickly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.3] A nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. The per 100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory, while the per-portion figure is optional.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.4] The country of origin must be shown where the law requires it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.5] Claims such as low fat, light or high fibre may be made only where the product meets defined criteria.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.6] Quality marks and symbols help consumers choose. The Bord Bia Quality Mark for Irish food shows that a product meets a defined standard, and the gluten-free symbol and allergen emphasis help people with special diets.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.7] EU food information rules are set out in Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
