TARGET hom-food-preservation-s3: 3. Heat processing
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.b] Food spoils because micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) and enzymes (natural proteins in the food that break it down) keep working. Heat processing stops both, and a clean airtight container then keeps new micro-organisms out.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.0] Heat destroys vegetative bacteria (bacteria in their active, growing form), yeasts and moulds, and denatures (permanently changes the shape of) the enzymes that cause spoilage, so the food stops breaking down.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.1] Sealing the food in a clean airtight container while it is hot, or sealing before heating, prevents re-contamination, because otherwise new micro-organisms would simply re-enter after the heating.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.2] Spores (tough resting forms) of some bacteria, such as Clostridium botulinum, are very heat-resistant and survive boiling at normal pressure, so low-acid foods need higher temperatures held for longer, in a pressure cooker or retort (a large industrial pressure cooker).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.3] Acid foods such as fruit, tomatoes and pickles are easier to preserve by heat because acid hinders micro-organisms and their spores. Low-acid foods such as peas, beans, meat and fish need a higher temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.4] Heat also changes the colour, texture, flavour and nutritive value of food, which is why heat-processed fruit and vegetables differ from fresh produce.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.0.5] Heat-sensitive, water-soluble vitamins (vitamin C and the B group) are reduced by heat processing.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.b] Heat treatments differ in how much they kill. A mild treatment leaves some organisms alive so the food must be chilled, while a severe one kills everything so the sealed food keeps at room temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.0] Pasteurisation is a moderate heat treatment that destroys most food poisoning (pathogenic) bacteria and reduces spoilage organisms, but not all spores, so pasteurised products keep for a limited time and still need refrigeration.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.1] Milk is pasteurised at 72 °C for 15 seconds, then cooled rapidly. Pasteurisation is also used for cream, fruit juice, beer and some canned foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.2] Sterilisation uses a higher temperature for longer, for example milk sterilised in the bottle and canned foods. It destroys all micro-organisms including spores, so the sealed product can be stored at room temperature.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.3] The price of sterilisation is that flavour and colour change and vitamin losses are greater than with pasteurisation.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.4] UHT (ultra-heat treatment) heats milk to about 135 °C for a few seconds, and the milk is then packed aseptically (in a germ-free way) into sterile cartons.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.5] UHT milk keeps for months unopened at room temperature, must be refrigerated after opening, and has a slight cooked flavour.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.1.6] Three processing methods make milk safe: pasteurisation, sterilisation or UHT, and evaporation or dehydration (drying).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.b] Canning seals food in a container and heats it hard enough to sterilise it, so it keeps for a long time without a fridge. Commercial canning in tin cans is different from home bottling in glass jars.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.0] Commercial canning has stages: food is prepared (washed, peeled, trimmed, blanched where needed), packed into cans, covered with brine, syrup or oil, and air is removed (exhausting).
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.1] Then the can is sealed with a double seam, heated in a retort at a high temperature for a set time, and cooled quickly. The heat sterilises the contents and the sealed can prevents re-contamination.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.2] Cans are lined with a lacquer, a protective coating, to prevent the metal reacting with the food.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.3] Canned food is shelf-stable, so it keeps at room temperature for a long time without refrigeration, and it can be eaten straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.4] Foods suited to canning include fruit, tomatoes, vegetables such as peas and beans, fish (tuna, sardines, salmon), meat, soup and baked beans.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.5] Low-acid vegetables such as peas, beans and carrots must be processed in a pressure canner (a sealed pot that heats above 100 °C), because boiling water at normal pressure, which is 100 °C, does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.2.6] A bulging can end or lid indicates gas produced by micro-organisms. Hissing, leaking, rust, deep dents or an off smell are also warning signs, so the contents must be discarded unopened and never tasted.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.b] The very high temperature and long processing time that make canned food safe also change it. Examiners ask what is lost and what is gained.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.0] Vitamin C is heat-sensitive and water-soluble, so it is lost during high-temperature canning, especially into the canning liquid.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.1] Thiamine (vitamin B1) is unstable at high temperature and is reduced by canning.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.2] Canned vegetables are softer, paler and less bright in colour than fresh or frozen produce, and the flavour changes too.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.3] Canned vegetables gain sodium from added salt (brine), so no-added-salt versions are preferred on a low-salt diet.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.4] Fruit canned in heavy syrup gains sugar, and food packed in oil gains fat, which raises the energy content.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.5] Heat destroys micro-organisms and enzymes in the can, which is the purpose of canning. Minerals and fibre are largely kept, and the liquid can be used in cooking to keep the nutrients it holds.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.3.6] One past-paper table showed vitamin C in peas falling from 25 mg per 100 g fresh to 8 mg canned, and sodium rising from 1 mg to 230 mg. The reasons were the very high temperature and long time, and the salt used in canning.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.b] Bottling is the home version of heat preservation. Fruit is heated in glass jars so that the contents are sterilised and sealed.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.0] Bottling packs fruit (and tomatoes) into clean sterilised glass jars with sugar syrup or water, seals them, and heats them in a water bath (or oven) until the contents reach a temperature that destroys organisms and enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.1] The seal closes as the jar cools and a vacuum forms, which holds the lid down and keeps new micro-organisms out.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.2] Jars must be filled hot, leaving headspace, and sealed at once; the contents cool and form a vacuum that holds the lid down.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.3] Jars must be sterilised and undamaged with new or sound lids, and filled leaving headspace (a gap at the top). Hot jars must be lifted carefully.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.4] Label bottled food with the date and store it in a cool, dark, dry place. Discard any jar whose lid is not tight or domed.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.5] Low-acid vegetables and meat should not be bottled in a boiling-water bath at home, because boiling at normal pressure does not destroy Clostridium botulinum spores. They need pressure processing, so home bottling is restricted to fruit and acid foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.4.6] Fruit is routinely bottled in a water bath because acid foods are easier to preserve by heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.b] Jam is fruit boiled with sugar until it sets. It keeps because of heat, a high concentration of sugar and a sealed jar, and it sets because pectin, acid and sugar combine.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.0] Heat: boiling at about 100 °C or higher destroys micro-organisms and inactivates enzymes in the fruit. The fruit is first simmered gently to soften its tissues and release pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.1] Sugar preserves by osmosis: at high concentration it draws water out of microbial cells, so they cannot multiply. Sugar also helps the gel to form and gives sweetness.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.2] Pectin is a natural gelling substance, a carbohydrate found in the cell walls of fruit, mostly in the skin and core. It forms a gel with sugar and acid as the jam cools, so the jam sets. Pectin is not a preservative.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.3] Apples, blackcurrants, redcurrants, gooseberries and lemons or other citrus fruit are high in pectin. Strawberries, raspberries, cherries and pears are low, so add pectin-rich fruit or a commercial pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.4] Acid, from lemon juice or the fruit itself, releases pectin from the cell walls and helps the pectin set. It also improves colour and flavour, and lemon juice is added for low-acid or low-pectin fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.5] Choose just ripe, firm, sound fruit. Under-ripe fruit contains more pectin, whereas over-ripe fruit has less pectin and spoils the set.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.6] Sealing: pour the jam hot into clean sterilised jars and cover at once so micro-organisms cannot re-enter. A vacuum forms as the jam cools, and a waxed disc plus cover is the traditional method.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.5.7] Jam loses some heat-sensitive vitamin C in boiling, its sugar concentration is high, and its flavour and colour change, so it no longer tastes fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.b] A jam method runs in a set order, and each stage protects either the set or the keeping quality. Jam spoils when one of these protections fails.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.0] 1. Wash and sterilise the jars, and prepare the fruit. 2. Simmer the fruit in a heavy pan to soften it and release pectin.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.1] 3. Add the sugar in the correct proportion, with lemon juice if needed, and dissolve it fully before the mixture is boiled rapidly to setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.2] Setting point is confirmed by the wrinkle test: a spoonful of jam dropped onto a cold saucer wrinkles when pushed with a finger. A sugar thermometer reads about 105 °C (220 °F) at setting point.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.3] 4. Skim the scum from the surface with a clean dry spoon. 5. Pot the jam into hot sterilised jars, seal at once, label and store in a cool, dry place.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.4] Jam ferments or grows mould if too little sugar is used, setting point is not reached, jars or lids are not sterilised, the seal fails, or a dirty or wet spoon introduces yeasts and moulds.
[hom-food-preservation-s3.6.5] Storage in a warm or damp place also spoils jam. Mould on the surface or fermentation (bubbles and an alcoholic smell) is the result.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s1: 1. Principles of food preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.b] Food starts to spoil as soon as it is harvested or slaughtered. Preservation means treating food so that spoilage is slowed or stopped and the food can be kept, moved and eaten later, with colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as near as possible to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.0] It reduces waste by keeping surplus produce, such as a glut of home-grown fruit or vegetables, usable for months rather than days, instead of being thrown away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.1] Because less food is thrown away, preservation also reduces the environmental footprint of food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.2] It makes food available out of season and over long distances, so perishable foods (those that spoil quickly) can be transported and stored.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.3] It makes food safer by destroying food poisoning micro-organisms or stopping them from growing, and it lengthens shelf life (the time food stays good to eat).
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.4] It creates foods with distinct textures and flavours, such as jam, chutney, dried fruit, smoked fish and cured meat, which increases variety and choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.b] Spoilage is not caused only by micro-organisms. Several separate processes make food unusable, and each has its own visible effect.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.0] Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions and are naturally present in food. After harvest or slaughter they carry on working, so food ripens, softens, darkens and changes flavour even when no micro-organism is present. Cut apple, banana and potato brown this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.1] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) grow on food, causing decay, rot, souring, off-odours and slime, and in some cases food poisoning. Moulds give fuzzy growth on bread, cheese and jam; yeasts ferment fruit juice and jam, with bubbling and an alcoholic smell; bacteria cause souring, putrefaction and food poisoning.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.2] Putrefaction is the breakdown of proteins by bacteria, producing foul-smelling products.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.3] Oxidation occurs when oxygen reacts with fats, causing rancidity (an unpleasant, stale taste and smell), and with the cut surfaces of fruit and vegetables, causing browning. Vitamin C is also lost by oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.4] Moisture loss through the skins of fruit and vegetables causes shrivelling, wilting, wrinkling and loss of weight. It also dries and hardens bread and cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.5] Incorrect storage temperature allows micro-organisms and enzymes to work quickly, because warmth speeds both of them up.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.6] Poor hygiene and careless handling carry micro-organisms from unwashed hands, equipment, pests or raw food onto prepared food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.7] Chemical contaminants, such as insecticide or pesticide residues, antibiotics, metals and residues from cleaning products, can make food unsafe. Physical damage such as bruising also opens food to spoilage.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.b] Micro-organisms need six conditions to grow and multiply. Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.0] The six conditions are food (nutrients), moisture (available water), warmth (a suitable temperature), time (to multiply), a suitable pH or acidity and, for aerobic ones (those that need air), oxygen. Most bacteria prefer a neutral or slightly acid pH.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.1] Bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply rapidly in the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C, which is why chilled and hot food is kept outside it. Below 5 °C growth is slowed; above 63 °C it is stopped and most are killed by sustained heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.2] Under good conditions one bacterial cell divides about every 20 minutes (binary fission, splitting in two), so numbers rise very fast.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.3] Moulds and yeasts tolerate acid, sugar and low moisture better than most bacteria, so they spoil jam and dried fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.4] Moulds appear as fuzzy colonies or threads; yeasts produce bubbling, gas and a sour or alcoholic smell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.5] Enzymes work best at a moderate temperature and are destroyed (denatured) by heat or strong acid; cold slows them but does not stop them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.6] Micro-organisms cannot multiply in food with little available water, which is why drying and concentrated sugar or salt preserve food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.b] Three groups of micro-organism spoil food, and spores and enzymes also have to be understood. Each acts differently, which is why different methods suit different foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.0] Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause food poisoning and others spoil food by producing acid and gas.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.1] Yeasts are single-celled fungi that ferment sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.2] Moulds are multicellular fungi that grow as visible threads and spread by airborne spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.3] Spores are dormant (resting), resistant forms produced by some bacteria and all moulds; they survive ordinary heat and cold and start growing again when conditions improve.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.4] Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein, and each one acts on a specific substance in food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.b] Osmosis explains why strong sugar or salt solutions preserve food. It is the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane (one that lets water through but not most dissolved substances) from a weaker solution to a stronger one.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.0] The cell membrane of a micro-organism is partially permeable, so water leaves the cell when the surrounding solution is concentrated.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.1] Loss of water makes the cell shrink and stop growing, and at high concentrations it can kill the cell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.2] The same process draws water out of food, which is why salted meat and fish and sugared fruit keep. A strong sugar or salt solution outside a microbial cell draws water out, so the cell shrivels and cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.b] Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of the conditions that micro-organisms and enzymes need, and by preventing re-contamination. There are only a few principles, and each method is an application of one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.0] Destroy micro-organisms and inactivate enzymes with heat, as in canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching. Irradiation also destroys organisms and some enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.1] Slow or stop activity with low temperature: refrigeration slows growth and enzyme action, and freezing makes organisms dormant (inactivated, not killed) and makes water unavailable as ice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.2] Remove available water: dehydration (drying, freeze drying) takes water away, while sugar and salt tie it up and draw it out of organisms by osmosis, as in jam, salted fish and cured meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.3] Alter the pH with acid: vinegar (acetic acid) in pickles and chutney, or acids produced in fermentation, such as lactic acid in yoghurt and sauerkraut and acetic acid in vinegar. This prevents the growth of many spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.4] Use chemical preservatives that inhibit organisms: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives such as sulphur dioxide, sorbates, benzoates and nitrite.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.5] Exclude oxygen and prevent re-entry of organisms with airtight packaging, vacuum sealing, sealed hot jars (a vacuum forms as the contents cool) and moisture-proof wraps for the freezer. Removing oxygen also inactivates enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.6] Prevent re-contamination after treatment, using sterilised jars and lids, clean utensils, good hygiene and sealing straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.7] Most methods combine principles. Jam uses heat, sugar (osmosis), acid and pectin, and a sealed jar; chutney uses heat, vinegar and sugar; smoked fish uses salt, drying and smoke chemicals.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.b] The methods of preservation fall into groups according to the principle they use. Knowing the groups shows the structure of food preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.0] Low temperature: refrigeration and freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.1] Heat: canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.2] Water removal: drying and freeze drying, and sugar or salt that bind water.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.3] Chemicals: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives, with sugar, vinegar, salt, smoking and alcohol being the traditional natural preservatives.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.4] Fermentation and irradiation.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.b] Sugars are the sweet, water-loving carbohydrates, and these two properties explain why they are used so widely in cooking. Starch is the contrast: it is neither sweet nor soluble in cold water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.0] All sugars are sweet, but to different degrees. Fructose is sweeter than sucrose (table sugar), and lactose (milk sugar) is the least sweet of the common sugars.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.1] On a scale where sucrose scores 100, the 2017 marking scheme gives fructose 170 and lactose 15. Learn the order (fructose, then sucrose, then lactose) and use the figures only as that scale.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.2] Sweetness is why sugar is used to sweeten cakes, desserts, drinks and sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.3] Sugars are white crystalline solids that dissolve readily in water, and solubility increases with temperature, so more sugar dissolves in hot water than in cold.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.4] A concentrated sugar solution is a syrup. Syrups are used to sweeten drinks and sauces, to make icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.0.5] Starch is different: it is insoluble in cold water, so it does not dissolve but settles as a cloudy suspension (tiny solid particles spread through the liquid). It is also not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.b] Starch behaves differently from sugar: it is insoluble in cold water and must be heated with a liquid before it thickens anything. It also takes in moisture from the air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.0] Gelatinisation: when starch is heated in a liquid, the granules swell, burst and absorb the liquid, thickening it into a sol (a colloidal solution, where tiny particles stay spread through the liquid).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.1] On cooling, a concentrated starch mixture may set into a gel (a solid-like network that holds the liquid), which is why a cooled custard or sauce can become firm.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.2] Gelatinised starch thickens white sauces, custard, soups, gravies, risotto and fillings for pies and flans.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.3] To prevent lumps, the granules must be separated before they swell. Mix the starch with a little cold liquid first (a cornflour paste), or coat it in fat as in a roux.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.4] A roux is a mixture of fat and flour cooked together before liquid is added. The fat separates the starch granules, and the starch in the flour gelatinises as the liquid is stirred in, giving a base for white sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.5] Starch is hygroscopic: it absorbs moisture from the air, so dry starchy foods such as flour, biscuits and crackers soften or clump if stored in damp air. Store them in airtight containers.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.1.6] Sugar, especially brown sugar, is also hygroscopic, so it can turn hard or lumpy if left open in damp air.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.b] How a carbohydrate food is cooked decides what happens to it. Dry heat browns it, while moist heat softens it and thickens liquids.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.0] Dry heat (baking, roasting, toasting, grilling) on starch makes the granules burst and absorb fat, and dextrinisation occurs: the starch breaks down into brown dextrins. This gives a brown colour and a crisp, flavoursome crust on bread and toast.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.1] Dry heat on sugar causes caramelisation and the Maillard reaction, which brown the surface of the food and give it flavour and a crisp texture.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.2] Moist heat (boiling, steaming, stewing, poaching) on starch causes gelatinisation: the granules swell, burst and thicken the liquid, thickening sauces and puddings. Pasta, rice and potatoes become soft and digestible.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.3] Moist heat softens the cellulose in vegetable cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to digest. The vegetable keeps its fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.2.4] Sugar dissolves in the cooking liquid and forms a syrup, for example when fruit is stewed, so the syrup carries the flavour of the fruit. Sugar also helps the fruit keep its shape.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.b] Enzymes (proteins that speed up chemical reactions) break complex carbohydrates down in stages until they are small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.0] 1. Salivary amylase in the mouth begins breaking starch down into smaller polysaccharides and maltose. This enzyme stops working in the stomach acid, so no carbohydrate digestion takes place in the stomach.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.1] 2. Pancreatic amylase, in pancreatic juice released into the small intestine, completes the breakdown of the remaining starch to maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.2] 3. Maltase, sucrase and lactase, made by the wall of the small intestine and found in the intestinal juice, finish the job. Maltase splits maltose into two glucose units, sucrase splits sucrose into glucose and fructose, and lactase splits lactose into glucose and galactose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.3] Only monosaccharides (single sugar units: glucose, fructose and galactose) are small enough to pass through the wall of the small intestine into the blood.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.3.4] Humans have no enzyme that digests cellulose, so it passes through the intestine as dietary fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.b] Sugar does much more in the kitchen than sweeten. Each property has a use, and exam answers pair the two.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.0] Sweetness: sweetens cakes, desserts and drinks. Solubility: dissolves to make syrups and icing and to bottle fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.1] Preservation: sugar draws water out of micro-organisms, so they cannot grow and spoilage is slowed in jam, jelly, chutney and crystallised fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.2] Fermentation: sugar is the food for yeast, which produces carbon dioxide that makes bread dough rise.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.3] Aeration (trapping air): creamed sugar and fat trap air in cake mixtures such as sponge cakes. Whisking traps air in egg white, and the dissolved sugar keeps the foam stable, which makes meringue.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.4] Maillard reaction: sugar reacts with amino acids (the units that make up protein) when heated, a non-enzymic browning that gives brown colour and flavour to roast meat, toast, roast potatoes and baked goods. Caramelisation is different because it involves sugar alone.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.5] Gel formation: sugar, acid and pectin together set jam into a gel.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.4.6] Caramelisation gives crème caramel and toffee, crystallisation gives confectionery, and inversion is used in jam and boiled sweets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.b] Caramelisation, the Maillard reaction and dextrinisation all brown food, so they are easy to confuse. What differs is what is being heated and with what.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.0] Caramelisation is sugar alone, heated dry until it melts and browns, so it needs no protein, as in caramel sauce.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.1] The Maillard reaction needs sugar and amino acids together, heated dry, so it browns foods that contain both, such as toast and roast meat.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.2] Dextrinisation is starch, not sugar, broken into brown dextrins by dry heat, which is why toast browns even though bread is mostly starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.5.3] Gelatinisation is a different change again: starch heated in a liquid (moist heat) swells and thickens it, while inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by acid or enzyme.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.b] Hydrolysis is the reaction that splits big sugars into small ones, and it is the same reaction that happens in digestion. Inversion is the form of it that cooks use on sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.0] Hydrolysis is the breakdown of a compound by reaction with water: the bond between two units is split and water is added across it. It is the reverse of condensation (joining units with the loss of water).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.1] A disaccharide reacts with water, with enzymes or with heat and acid, and splits into its monosaccharide units. Sucrose gives glucose and fructose, lactose gives glucose and galactose, and maltose gives two glucose units.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.2] Inversion is the hydrolysis of sucrose by heating with an acid (lemon juice, cream of tartar) or by the enzyme invertase. It produces invert sugar, a mixture of glucose and fructose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.3] Invert sugar is sweeter than sucrose (it contains fructose), is more soluble and is less likely to crystallise than sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.6.4] This is why invert sugar is used in jam and boiled sweets, and for smooth icing and fondant. Honey and golden syrup contain invert sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.b] Heating and cooling sugar and starch changes their colour, flavour and texture in three different ways. Each is wanted in some foods and avoided in others.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.0] Caramelisation: sugar heated strongly on its own melts, turns from colourless to golden and then brown, producing caramel with a distinctive, pleasant flavour. Overheating makes it dark and bitter and finally burnt (carbonisation).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.1] Caramel is used in caramel sauce, crème caramel, toffee and brûlée.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.2] Crystallisation: when more sugar is dissolved than the liquid can hold at that temperature (a saturated solution) and the mixture cools, sugar crystals form.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.3] Controlled crystallisation gives the texture of fudge, fondant and sweets, while unwanted large crystals make jam grainy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.4] Crystallisation is prevented by adding acid or glucose syrup (which causes inversion) and by not stirring while the sugar dissolves.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.5] Dextrinisation: dry heat breaks starch down into shorter chains of glucose units called dextrins, which are brown. The 2005 scheme names the brown compound pyrodextrin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.6] Dextrinisation gives toast and the crust of baked bread their brown colour and flavour. Browned flour used for a roux thickens less, because its starch has already been broken into shorter chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.7.7] Dextrinisation is a property of starch, not sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.b] Jam sets because of pectin, a substance in fruit cell walls. Making jam depends on getting the pectin out of the fruit and then giving it the right conditions to set.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.0] Pectin is extracted by simmering the chopped fruit in water until soft. Acid, such as lemon juice, helps release the pectin from the cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.1] Fruit high in pectin includes cooking apples and crab apples, citrus fruit (especially the peel), gooseberries, redcurrants and blackcurrants. Strawberries are low in pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.2] Unripe fruit contains more pectin than ripe fruit, so under-ripe apples are a good source of pectin for jam.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.3] A gel forms only when pectin, sugar and acid are present in the right proportions and the mixture is boiled and then cooled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.4] During cooling, the pectin molecules link into a network that traps the sugar solution (the liquid), so the jam sets.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.5] Too little pectin, as in strawberries, gives a runny jam. Add lemon juice or a pectin-rich fruit to fix this.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.8.6] Pectin test (optional): a little cooled pectin extract added to methylated spirits (alcohol) forms a jelly-like clot if much pectin is present.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.b] This simple test shows that starch does not thicken a cold liquid but does thicken it when heated.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.0] Aim: to show the effect of heat on starch mixed with a liquid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.1] Method: mix a spoonful of cornflour with cold water and observe that it does not dissolve and does not thicken. Then heat the mixture while stirring and observe that it thickens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.2] Variable: temperature is the variable that changes (cold, then heated).
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.3] Control and comparison: the unheated cornflour and cold water mixture is compared with the heated one.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.9.4] Result: cold, the starch settles as a cloudy suspension and the mixture stays thin; heated, the granules swell, burst and absorb the water, and the mixture thickens into a sol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.b] Heating sugar on its own, with no water, shows caramelisation happening step by step.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.0] Aim: to observe how granulated sugar changes when it is heated on its own, with no water added.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.1] Method: place a measured quantity of granulated sugar in a dry, heavy-based pan on a low heat, stirring gently as it melts, and note the colour and smell at regular intervals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.2] Variables: heating time is the independent variable; colour, smell and taste are the dependent variables.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.3] Control: a sample of the same sugar left unheated, for comparison.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.4] Safety: molten sugar is extremely hot and causes severe burns, so use heatproof utensils and keep hands and skin away from the pan.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.10.5] Expected results: the colour darkens gradually from pale to golden, then deep brown, and the caramel smell strengthens.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.b] These two tests show a change in starch caused by dry heat and a change in sucrose caused by acid.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.0] Dextrinisation, aim: to show that dry heat changes starch. Method: toast white bread, or brown flour in a dry pan, or compare a roux cooked for different times.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.1] Dextrinisation, result: the browned starch is darker and thickens less than unbrowned starch, because its chains are shorter. Safety: the pan is hot.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.2] Inversion, aim: to show that acid changes sucrose. Method: boil a sucrose solution with a little lemon juice and cool it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.3] Inversion, result: the invert sugar does not crystallise as readily and is sweeter.
[hom-carbohydrates-s4.11.4] A Benedict's test shows the invert sugar: reducing sugars give an orange-red colour, while sucrose does not.
