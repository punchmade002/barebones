TARGET hom-food-industry-s3: 3. Careers in food and related industries
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.b] Food reaches us through a chain of jobs, from growing and rearing it, through processing and packing, to testing that it is safe. Each job in the chain has a different focus, so learn what each person actually does.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.0] Primary producers supply the raw food, so they come first in the chain: the farmer, the fisherman or fish farmer, and the horticulturist or grower (who produces fruit, vegetables and plants).
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.1] Miller, butcher, baker, confectioner, cheesemaker and brewer or distiller are craft and processing trades, found in small businesses such as retail shops or in food manufacturing.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.2] Production operative: runs and monitors processing and packaging machinery on a food production line, so that products are made and packed to a steady standard.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.3] Food technologist: develops new products and processes, and improves recipes and methods for shelf life (how long food stays safe and fresh) and quality.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.4] Quality control technician: tests raw materials and finished products against specifications (the standards set for the product) and food safety standards.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.5] Food microbiologist: tests food and production surfaces for microbes (micro-organisms) that cause spoilage or food poisoning.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.6] Food engineer and food scientist (for example in process, packaging and research): design, run and maintain processing and packaging plant, and improve products, so that it operates safely and efficiently.
[hom-food-industry-s3.0.7] Agricultural scientist and agricultural adviser: research and advise on crop and livestock production, food quality and farm efficiency.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.b] These are the jobs where food is prepared, served and sold to the public. Some work in the kitchen, some face the customer, and some run the business or buy the supplies.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.0] Chef: plans menus, prepares, cooks and presents food in restaurants, hotels, hospitals and other institutions, so chefs work wherever meals are served in volume.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.1] Caterer: plans and delivers food for events, canteens and institutions such as hospitals and schools, so the food is made for a group rather than one table.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.2] Waiter or waitress, bar staff and receptionist: front-of-house service, meaning the staff who deal directly with customers, serving food and drink and taking orders in hotels, restaurants and bars.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.3] Hotel or restaurant manager: runs the business and controls costs, stock and staffing for a food and accommodation business, so the business stays profitable.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.4] Deli counter assistant, retail and sales assistant: serve customers in shops. The buyer sources ingredients, products and packaging from suppliers at the required quality and price.
[hom-food-industry-s3.1.5] Food stylist, food writer or journalist, and marketing and sales staff: promote food products to retailers, consumers and export markets (sales abroad).
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.b] Some food careers focus on people's health and on keeping the food supply safe and lawful. Two of them, dietitian and nutritionist, are easy to confuse, so note exactly how they differ.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.0] Dietitian: assesses nutritional needs and plans diets for individuals and groups, including people with illness or special needs. It is a protected title, so only people registered with the Dietitians Registration Board at CORU may use it.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.1] The Dietitians Registration Board was set up under the Health and Social Care Professionals Act 2005, and the title dietitian has been protected since 31 October 2016.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.2] Nutritionist: gives advice on healthy eating, often in public health, sport or the food industry, for example advising an athlete on diet. Unlike dietitian, the title is not protected by law in Ireland.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.3] Home Economics teacher: teaches food, nutrition and family and consumer studies in second-level schools, so the career suits someone who wants to pass on these skills. The route is a degree and a teaching qualification, with registration with the Teaching Council.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.4] Environmental Health Officer (EHO): inspects food premises and enforces food hygiene law. EHOs also take samples, investigate complaints and outbreaks, and can serve improvement and closure notices. In Ireland, EHOs working on food are employed by the HSE Environmental Health Service.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.5] Food safety scientist: tests and assesses food safety risks for regulators, producers and retailers. The public analyst does laboratory testing of food.
[hom-food-industry-s3.2.6] Veterinary inspectors in the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine carry out official control of meat and dairy. Sea-fisheries inspectors work for the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority and cover seafood.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.b] Food careers are found in many kinds of workplace, and there is more than one way in. Employers range from factories to state bodies, and routes range from apprenticeships to degrees.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.0] Employers include food manufacturers and processors; retailers and supermarkets; hotels, restaurants and caterers; hospitals and schools; schools and colleges; and people who run their own business.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.1] State bodies employ scientists, advisers, inspectors and marketing staff. They include Bord Bia, Teagasc, Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), the HSE, and the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.2] Entry routes include apprenticeships (for example chef), further education and training such as PLC and other certificate courses, and third-level degrees in food science, dietetics, culinary arts, Home Economics or environmental health.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.3] Other routes are on-the-job training, where a worker learns the skills while employed, and experience gained through a family business, such as a family bakery or farm.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.4] Most food careers need knowledge of hygiene and food safety, teamwork, communication, attention to detail, and an interest in food. Hygiene knowledge matters because food workers handle food that others will eat.
[hom-food-industry-s3.3.5] When studying a local business, list the careers it offers, for example production staff, technician, driver, sales, administrator and chef, and the qualifications each needs.

CANDIDATES hom-food-industry-s1: 1. Structure of the Irish food industry
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.b] The food industry takes raw produce from farms and fisheries, processes it into finished goods, and distributes and sells them through a service sector. It is organised in three levels, each depending on the one before it: a processor needs farmers' produce, and a retailer needs the processor's goods.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.0] Primary sector: resource-based production, meaning farming, horticulture (growing fruit, vegetables and similar crops), fishing and fish farming (mariculture). It produces raw materials such as milk, cattle, sheep, pigs, poultry, eggs, grain, fruit, vegetables, fish and shellfish.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.1] Secondary sector: manufacturing and processing, which converts raw materials into finished food and drink, such as milk into cheese, butter and milk powder, wheat into flour and bread, carcasses into cuts and meat products, or barley into beer and whiskey.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.2] Tertiary sector: services that move, sell, support and regulate food, including transport, wholesale and retail, catering and food outlets, tourism and hospitality, research, education, advice, marketing and regulation.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.3] Agri-food is one of Ireland's largest indigenous industries (industries that grew up in Ireland rather than being brought in from abroad), and most of its raw material is grown or reared on Irish farms.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.4] The industry matters to the economy because it provides employment, much of it in rural areas, and earns income from export sales.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.5] The UK and other EU member states are the main markets for Irish food overall, and much dairy and beef output is exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.b] Besides the three levels, the industry is divided into product sectors. A single sector such as dairy can contain primary, secondary and tertiary activity, so keep the two lists apart.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.0] Dairy and ingredients: milk, cheese, butter, yoghurt, cream and milk powder, plus dairy ingredients sold to other food manufacturers.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.1] Meat and livestock: beef, lamb, pork and poultry, slaughtered and processed in licensed meat plants.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.2] Beverages: beer and stout, whiskey and other spirits, fruit juice and bottled water.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.3] Horticulture: fruit, vegetables and mushrooms, sold fresh or processed.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.4] Seafood: fish and shellfish landed at Irish ports and processed for home and export sale.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.5] Cereals and bakery: flour milling, bread, biscuits and breakfast cereals.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.6] Prepared consumer foods: ready meals, snacks, confectionery and sauces sold in retail packaging.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.b] Ireland produces far more of some foods than its small population can eat, so these are sold abroad. A climate and soil suited to grass means livestock and dairy output is much larger than home demand.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.0] Dairy products, especially cheese, butter, milk powder and infant formula ingredients, are among Ireland's largest food exports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.1] Beef and lamb are exported to EU and non-EU markets.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.2] Pork, poultry and processed meat products are also exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.3] Irish whiskey, beer and stout are exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.4] Seafood, including shellfish and farmed salmon, is exported from Irish ports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.5] Prepared foods and bakery products are sold to retailers and food service companies abroad.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.b] Ireland imports foods it cannot grow, cannot grow all year round, or chooses to buy in for variety and price. Reasons include climate (tropical crops), seasonal gaps, consumer demand for variety and ethnic foods, ingredients for manufacturers and feed for livestock.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.0] Tropical and out-of-season fruit, such as bananas, pineapples, oranges and avocados, which Ireland cannot grow commercially.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.1] Coffee, tea and cocoa, which are grown in warmer countries.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.2] Spices and sugar, which Ireland does not produce in quantity.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.3] Soya and other animal feed ingredients used by Irish livestock producers.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.4] Wine, and some processed foods such as chocolate and confectionery.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.5] Rice, dried pasta, some vegetables and herbs, and sauces are also imported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.6] Ireland also imports some foods it exports, such as cheeses and meat products, for variety and price, so trade runs both ways.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.7] Ireland is a net exporter of food overall, so the value of exports is greater than the value of imports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.b] Several State bodies support, develop and regulate the food industry. Each has its own job, so learn each name together with one function.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.0] Bord Bia (the Irish Food Board) promotes Irish food, drink and horticulture at home and abroad, and develops national and international markets for Irish products.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.1] Bord Bia runs quality assurance schemes for food producers, such as the Sustainable Beef and Lamb Assurance Scheme and the Sustainable Dairy Assurance Scheme, shown by a quality mark on pack.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.2] Bord Bia's Origin Green programme records and verifies the sustainability commitments of food and drink companies: companies set measurable targets and their progress is independently verified.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.3] Bord Bia helps Irish food companies with market research, buyer introductions and trade fairs.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.4] Teagasc, the agriculture and food development authority, provides research, advice and training for farmers and food producers, so they can improve what they grow and make.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.5] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the seafood development agency, develops the seafood sector and promotes seafood at home and abroad.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.6] Enterprise Ireland supports export growth and innovation in Irish companies, including food and drink firms.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.7] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) sets and enforces food safety standards across the sector.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.8] The Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine is the government department responsible for the sector, covering policy and schemes.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.b] A local factory, business or home enterprise can be used to show how a food business fits into the industry structure. Work through the same set of questions for whichever business you choose.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.0] Identify the business's sector, such as dairy, meat, beverages or bakery, and whether its main activity is primary, secondary or tertiary.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.1] Record its raw materials, where they come from, and the finished products it makes.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.2] Note its markets: local shops and customers, other Irish retailers, or export markets.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.3] Assess its contribution to the area through jobs, local suppliers and the income it brings to rural communities, which is why the industry matters locally.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.4] List the careers it offers, such as production operative, technologist, quality staff, chef or sales and marketing roles.
