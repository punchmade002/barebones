TARGET hom-social-change-s1: 1. Changes in settlement patterns from rural to urban
[hom-social-change-s1.0.b] A settlement pattern is the way a population is spread across the land, in scattered rural homes and farms, villages, towns and cities. Over the last century Ireland's pattern shifted towards towns and cities, so a rising share of the population lives in urban areas while the countryside loses people.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.0] Rural means countryside, farms and small villages; urban means towns and cities, where jobs, colleges and services are concentrated.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.1] Urbanisation is the growth in the proportion of a country's population living in towns and cities. It is about a rising share, not just population growth.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.2] Rural depopulation is the fall in the number of people living in the countryside, through migration to towns or abroad. Moving within Ireland is internal migration, while leaving the country is emigration; both can cause rural decline.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.3] Rural depopulation is most marked in remote areas and where farm employment has fallen, because larger and mechanised farms need fewer workers.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.4] Young adults are the group most likely to leave rural areas for education and first jobs, so many villages are left with an older population and fewer children.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.5] Rural areas that lose population also lose local services such as post offices, Garda stations and schools, which makes rural life less attractive.
[hom-social-change-s1.0.6] The movement is not one-way or uniform. Towns, commuter belts (the areas around cities where people live and travel to work) and some rural areas have grown while remote areas lost people, so the overall picture is mixed.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.b] People move to towns and cities because that is where most opportunities are. Examiners want each reason explained, so give the reason and then say why it draws people.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.0] Employment: towns and cities contain most of the jobs in manufacturing, services and office work. Larger, mechanised farms need fewer workers, so farm jobs fell, and the children of small farms could not all inherit land.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.1] Education: third-level colleges and specialist schools and courses are concentrated in urban centres, so many young people move for college and do not return.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.2] Services and infrastructure: hospitals, shops, public transport, entertainment, leisure facilities and fast broadband are more available, and better, in towns and cities.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.3] Housing: towns offer a wider choice of rented and owned accommodation, including apartments for people without land.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.4] Family and social ties: moving closer to friends, relatives, a partner or a partner's workplace draws many people into towns, and towns offer a bigger social life for young adults.
[hom-social-change-s1.1.5] Young adults are the group most likely to leave, so the countryside is left with an older population and fewer children.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.b] Living in a town or city changes how a family spends its time, money and support. There are gains and losses, and a good answer gives both.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.0] Urban families have easier access to childcare, schools, health care, shops and entertainment, which can improve quality of life.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.1] A wider choice of jobs means both parents can more easily find work, and there are more leisure facilities for the family to use.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.2] Long commutes from suburbs to city workplaces reduce the time parents spend at home and add pressure to family routines.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.3] High housing costs and high rents (because heavy demand meets limited housing) can delay marriage and the birth of children until young couples can afford a home, squeeze family budgets and cause overcrowding.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.4] Urban neighbourhoods can face congestion, noise and air pollution, and crime and antisocial behaviour in some areas.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.5] Families in cities are often farther from grandparents and extended family, so there is less daily childcare and practical help.
[hom-social-change-s1.2.6] There is often less community spirit in cities, with neighbours who do not know each other, so families have less informal support.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.b] When people leave the countryside, the families who stay feel it in their relationships and in the services they depend on. The loss feeds on itself, because fewer people mean fewer services, which makes the area less attractive to the next generation.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.0] Older people living alone in rural areas can become isolated and lonely when younger family members move away, because fewer relatives are nearby for daily support.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.1] Children in depopulated areas may attend smaller schools with fewer classmates, and grandparents are less available for childcare.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.2] Teams and clubs struggle to field players, so children have fewer chances to take part in local activities.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.3] Rural decline can cause whole family units to emigrate, weakening the local network of family and neighbourly support.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.4] Withdrawal of services such as shops, post offices, banks, public transport, Garda stations and schools makes daily life harder for families who remain in the countryside. With fewer customers these services close, which makes the area less attractive to the next generation, a cycle of decline.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.5] Family farms may have no heir, because the younger generation has moved away.
[hom-social-change-s1.3.6] There are positives for those who stay: lower housing costs, a strong sense of community, lower crime and pollution, more space and safer play for children, and a slower pace of life.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.b] Rural depopulation is not the whole story, because some rural areas have gained people. Better connections and lower costs have made country living workable for families who would once have had to move to a town.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.0] Better roads and transport let people live in the country and commute to work in towns and cities.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.1] Cheaper housing and living costs make the countryside attractive to families, especially compared with high urban rents.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.2] Lower crime and pollution make rural areas appealing places to raise children.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.3] Improved local services, leisure and community facilities mean country life no longer means going without.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.4] Broadband and working from home allow people to keep a job in a town or city while living in the countryside.
[hom-social-change-s1.4.5] Rural resettlement and local-authority housing moves have brought families into some rural areas.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.b] Governments and state agencies have used job creation and infrastructure to slow rural depopulation. People stay where they can earn a living and where services remain, so most initiatives aim to create local jobs or make country living practical.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.0] Redeployment (decentralisation) of government departments and state agencies to rural and regional towns creates public-sector jobs outside the cities and Dublin.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.1] Rural resettlement schemes help families, including city families, move to and settle in the countryside, which helps keep rural communities viable and schools and services open.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.2] IDA Ireland is the agency responsible for attracting investment from overseas companies. It encourages foreign firms to locate in regional towns as well as the cities, creating jobs.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.3] Enterprise Ireland helps Irish firms to start, grow and export, including firms outside Dublin, so jobs are created locally.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.4] Local Enterprise Offices, one in each local authority area, give advice, mentoring, training and small grants to micro and small businesses. Local people can then create their own jobs, for example in cottage industries, craft and food businesses and tourism.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.5] Údarás na Gaeltachta is the regional authority responsible for the economic, social and cultural development of the Gaeltacht areas, encouraging enterprise and jobs there.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.6] LEADER is the EU-funded rural development programme that supports community-led local projects such as village renewal, tourism and enterprise.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.7] Investment in roads, public transport, electricity and telecommunications, especially broadband (the National Broadband Plan, which aims to bring high-speed broadband to areas the market did not serve), makes rural living and remote or hybrid working practical, and remote-working hubs in towns support this.
[hom-social-change-s1.5.8] Community employment and training programmes, for example in childcare, horticulture and caring, give rural people skills and work experience.
[hom-social-change-s1.6.b] A good way to understand settlement change is to compare family life a hundred years ago with today, and to ask someone who lived through it.
[hom-social-change-s1.6.0] Compare who lived on the land, the size of families, school attendance and leisure then and now.
[hom-social-change-s1.6.1] Interview an older relative or community member about when people left the area, why they left, and what changed.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s5: 5. Social change
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.b] Societies never stand still. Social change is a significant alteration over time in the structures, institutions, relationships, values or behaviour of a society, in other words in the way a society is organised.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.0] In exam terms, social change is change in society caused by law reform, technology or major events. The internet and divorce legislation are good examples.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.1] Change can be gradual, such as the slow shift in attitudes to the role of women or to marriage over several decades, or sudden, such as a new law, a war or a pandemic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.2] Change can be planned, such as a law or a policy, or unplanned. It happens in every society, but the speed differs from one society to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.3] Cultural lag is the gap in time between the appearance of a new material invention and the adjustment of norms, values and institutions to it, because people and organisations take time to catch up. The concept was developed by the sociologist William Ogburn.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.b] Many of the biggest changes come from shifts in how people think and live. These social causes feed one another: more education leads to new attitudes, and new attitudes lead to new family patterns.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.0] Changing attitudes and values: there is more acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce, lone parenthood, same-sex relationships and diversity, less stigma attached to children born outside marriage, and the Church has less influence than before.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.1] Greater equality for women through better education, careers, financial independence and legal equality changes gender roles at home. The marriage bar, the rule forcing women to leave their jobs on marrying, ended in the civil service in 1973.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.2] Urbanisation is the movement of people from rural areas to towns and cities. It weakens close kin ties and extended family contact and brings new lifestyles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.3] Towns have better services and facilities, but also more pressure, traffic and anti-social behaviour. Rural depopulation means rural areas can lose services and older people can become isolated.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.4] Education: more people reach second and third level, which changes attitudes, expectations and the value placed on qualifications. Wider access also links to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.5] Demographic change means changes in the make-up of the population: falling birth rates, smaller families, later marriage and parenthood, longer life expectancy and an ageing population. These alter the structure of households and the demand for services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.6] Migration, the movement of people into and out of Ireland, brings new cultures, languages and religions. Cultural diffusion, the spread of ideas, fashions and practices between societies through travel, migration and the media, has a similar effect.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.7] Globalisation, the growth of economic and cultural links between countries, means events and ideas in one country affect others. Social movements and pressure groups also campaign for change, for example for equality, and gain support for their aims.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.b] Law reform both reflects change in attitudes and drives it, because a new law changes what people may do and what they come to expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.0] Divorce: the referendum was held in November 1995 and passed narrowly. The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 then set up the legal process. Say referendum 1995, Act 1996, not that divorce was available in 1995.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.1] Same-sex marriage was approved by referendum in 2015 and given effect by the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.2] The Civil Partnership Act 2010 provided civil partnership for same-sex couples. This was the earlier arrangement, because since 2015 same-sex couples can marry.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.3] Other legal changes that alter family life include maternity and paternity leave and equality legislation, which support parents at work and promote equal treatment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.b] How a society earns its living shapes family life, because work decides who is at home, how much money the household has and where people live.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.0] Industrialisation moved many workers from farms and rural trades into factories and towns. Service industries have since grown, and work patterns have changed to include flexible hours, job sharing and working from home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.1] Growth of female employment has produced dual-earner households. These have more income but more need for childcare, and can bring role overload and role conflict, which are effects, not causes.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.2] Unemployment, recession, emigration and returning emigrants change family life by splitting relatives apart and bringing them back. High housing costs delay home ownership, marriage and parenthood, while higher standards of living improve comfort.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.3] State welfare: improved social welfare, such as child benefit and pensions, reduces dependence on the family and eases financial strain.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.b] New technology changes daily life faster than norms and institutions can adjust, which is cultural lag in action. Its effects can be positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.0] Modern communications technology, including mobile phones, the internet and social media, changes how families communicate, how children learn and how people work, and it changes how people spend leisure time. Examples are children skilled in IT, video calls and apps.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.1] Positive effects include access to information and contact with distant relatives. Negative effects include addiction, cyber-bullying and less face-to-face time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.2] Labour-saving appliances free time and make shared housework easier, which supports more equal roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.3] Contraception and medical advances affect family size and life expectancy, because families can plan the number of children and people live longer.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.4] Transport shortens distances, so relatives and workers can live further apart and still keep in touch.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.b] Social change affects the family, institutions and everyday life, and its effects can be both positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.0] Changing gender roles: more women take part in paid employment, and household tasks are more often shared between partners, giving more equal roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.1] Smaller families: many couples have fewer children or have them later in life, and fewer marriages, and later ones, are taking place.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.2] Changing family structures: lone-parent, blended and cohabiting families have become more common, so family structures are more diverse, and the extended family has declined.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.3] An ageing population increases demand for health and care services and for support for older people, and the roles of older people change. More families use childcare.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.4] Changes in values, such as greater acceptance of diversity, change the norms people expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.5] Effects can be positive, such as more choice and freedom, or negative, such as stress and strain, role overload, loss of community and social problems. Rapid change can leave some groups behind, for example people whose skills are no longer needed in changing industries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.6] Change affects institutions: the Church loses influence, the State takes on more services, and schools adapt to diversity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.7] Social change can cause tension and conflict between groups that hold different values, for example older and younger generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.b] Not everyone welcomes change, and several forces can slow it down or stop it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.0] Vested interests are groups who benefit from the existing system, so they may oppose changes that would reduce their advantages.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.1] Tradition and religious belief can resist changes in norms and values that conflict with long-held teachings.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.2] Fear of the unknown and uncertainty about the consequences of change make people reluctant to accept it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.3] The cost of change, in money, time and disruption, can slow or prevent it, and generations may disagree about it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.b] Ireland has seen rapid change in recent decades, and each example below links a cause to an effect on family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.0] Divorce: Irish voters approved divorce by referendum in 1995, and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 provided for it. Marriage equality followed, with a referendum in 2015 and the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.1] Women in employment: the marriage bar in the civil service ended in 1973, and more married women have been in paid work since, which changed roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.2] Migration: the Celtic Tiger, a period of rapid economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s, brought increased immigration and with it new languages, cultures and religions.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.3] Emigration in earlier decades and in the 2008 recession years, and return migration, moved relatives apart and then back together, affecting families and communities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.4] Rural depopulation and urban growth shifted people from the countryside to towns, and the influence of the Church has fallen.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.b] Exam questions on social change usually ask for the impact on family life, and marks are given for each cause covered.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.0] Structure a discuss answer by cause (social, economic, technological) and then effect, with one Irish example for each point.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.1] Link each cause to a clear effect, for example urbanisation weakening extended family contact, or dual incomes increasing the need for childcare.

CANDIDATES hom-family-society-s3: 3. Modern family structures
[hom-family-society-s3.0.b] A nuclear family is a parent or parents with their dependent children (children who rely on them for support), living together in a self-contained household. It is the standard picture of family in modern Ireland, and each structure has advantages and challenges rather than being better or worse.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.0] Two parents, married or cohabiting (living together without being married), and their dependent children form one self-contained household. Marking schemes also accept the wording 'parent or parents and children'.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.1] The unit is small, and the number of children is usually small too, so the household is easy to manage and to keep running on a modest budget.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.2] It is mobile, so members can move for work, which often separates the family from relatives. The family is independent of relatives day to day, but may lack nearby relatives' support.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.3] Tasks, child rearing and decision-making are usually shared between both parents, and often both parents work.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.4] Children depend mainly on the parents' income and on State supports such as Child Benefit.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.5] Advantages: privacy, independence and mobility, because the household answers only to itself.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.6] Drawbacks: less help with childcare and with the care of ill members, and more pressure on the two adults, because there are few other people to share the load.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.b] A lone-parent (one-parent) family is one parent living with one or more dependent children. It can arise in several ways, and the parent takes on the work that two adults would normally share.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.0] Causes include separation or divorce, the death of a partner (bereavement), desertion, a parent's choice to raise a child alone, and a parent never having lived with a partner.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.1] The lone parent carries the full economic, emotional and practical responsibility, often on one income, which means financial pressure and a heavy workload with little free time.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.2] Most lone parents are women, but fathers can be lone parents too.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.3] Lone parents who work need reliable and affordable childcare, and may depend on relatives, friends or State supports.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.4] The State supports lone parents through social welfare payments and schemes, such as the One-Parent Family Payment (a payment for lone parents) and the Working Family Payment (a payment that tops up low earnings), along with medical cards and childcare supports.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.5] Voluntary organisations also offer lone parents information and support.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.6] Lone parenthood can be temporary, ending when the children grow up or when a new partner joins the household, which creates a blended family.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.b] An extended family is the nuclear family plus wider relatives (relatives beyond the parents and children), often counted in a multi-generation group, and its members stay closely linked and depend on one another. It was the usual form in rural Ireland in the past.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.0] Members include grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who live in the same household or very close by. Relatives who live far away and are not interdependent do not make an extended family.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.1] A vertical extended family has three or more generations, for example grandparents, parents and grandchildren. A horizontal one has relatives of the same generation, for example brothers with their wives and children.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.2] Members are interdependent, sharing childcare, housework, income and care of older relatives, so costs and tasks are spread widely.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.3] Members may work together as one economic unit, such as a family farm or family business.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.4] There is a strong family tradition, with respect and values passed between generations. Relationships may be more traditional, and historically roles were segregated by gender and age, with men and women carrying out separate tasks.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.5] Households are larger and more crowded, and grandparents often provide much of the childcare.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.6] Advantages: a wide support network and shared costs. Drawbacks: less privacy, and possible conflict between generations and over the authority of older members.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.7] Extended families are the usual form in many cultures, where grandparents often provide much of the childcare.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.b] A blended (also called reconstituted or step) family forms when a parent with children from an earlier relationship has a new partner, so that two family units join. It begins when the new partner and the children live as one family.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.0] Blended families arise through remarriage or cohabitation, and sometimes after the death of a spouse.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.1] Step-parents, step-siblings (no blood link), half-siblings (one shared parent, when the new couple have a child) and extended step-relatives may all be part of the family, creating new relationships. Children may split time between two households.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.2] A joint child of the new partners creates a sibling group that differs in age and background from the earlier children.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.3] The adults must build new relationships and agree rules and discipline, which can be difficult when children have different expectations and routines.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.4] Children may find it hard to accept a step-parent or step-siblings, and rivalry or jealousy can follow. It takes time and patience.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.5] Parents may have financial obligations to children from an earlier relationship as well as to children of the new union, and there are contact arrangements with the non-resident parent.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.6] Advantages: more adults to share income and care, and new relationships and support.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.7] Blended families have become more common in Ireland since divorce became possible and lone-parent families formed new partnerships.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.b] Beyond the four main types, examiners accept other contemporary family structures, and naming them can earn extra credit when a question asks for contemporary family structures.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.0] Same-sex-parent families have two parents of the same sex and their children, who may come by adoption, fostering, previous relationships or assisted reproduction.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.1] Childless (couple) families are a couple with no children.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.2] Cohabiting-couple families are an unmarried couple, with or without children.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.3] Foster families are children placed in a family's care by the State on a temporary basis.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.b] Irish families can be compared with families in other European countries in three ways. Differences come from culture, religion, the economy and how far the State supports families.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.0] Structure: compare the proportions of nuclear, lone-parent, extended and blended households, using the most recent published statistics from Eurostat and the Central Statistics Office.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.1] Size: compare the number of children and members per family, and how family size has changed since the mid-twentieth century. Ireland has historically had a higher birth rate and larger families than most of Europe, a gap that has narrowed.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.2] Roles: compare who works, who cares for children and who cares for older people.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.3] Roles: Nordic countries such as Sweden, Denmark and Norway provide State-funded parental leave and childcare, which supports shared parenting and mothers' employment.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.4] Roles: in many southern European countries, such as Italy and Spain, the family, including grandparents, often provides much of the childcare and care of older people, and adult children may remain at home longer.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.5] Ireland is a mixture: the nuclear family is the most common household structure, relatives, especially grandparents, are still important for childcare, and care follows a family-based approach.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.6] State support: compare how far each country relies on the State and on the extended family to support parents. In Ireland, State support such as childcare and parental leave has grown but still relies on families and on private spending.
