TARGET hom-family-society-s3: 3. Modern family structures
[hom-family-society-s3.0.b] A nuclear family is a parent or parents with their dependent children (children who rely on them for support), living together in a self-contained household. It is the standard picture of family in modern Ireland, and each structure has advantages and challenges rather than being better or worse.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.0] Two parents, married or cohabiting (living together without being married), and their dependent children form one self-contained household. Marking schemes also accept the wording 'parent or parents and children'.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.1] The unit is small, and the number of children is usually small too, so the household is easy to manage and to keep running on a modest budget.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.2] It is mobile, so members can move for work, which often separates the family from relatives. The family is independent of relatives day to day, but may lack nearby relatives' support.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.3] Tasks, child rearing and decision-making are usually shared between both parents, and often both parents work.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.4] Children depend mainly on the parents' income and on State supports such as Child Benefit.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.5] Advantages: privacy, independence and mobility, because the household answers only to itself.
[hom-family-society-s3.0.6] Drawbacks: less help with childcare and with the care of ill members, and more pressure on the two adults, because there are few other people to share the load.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.b] A lone-parent (one-parent) family is one parent living with one or more dependent children. It can arise in several ways, and the parent takes on the work that two adults would normally share.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.0] Causes include separation or divorce, the death of a partner (bereavement), desertion, a parent's choice to raise a child alone, and a parent never having lived with a partner.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.1] The lone parent carries the full economic, emotional and practical responsibility, often on one income, which means financial pressure and a heavy workload with little free time.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.2] Most lone parents are women, but fathers can be lone parents too.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.3] Lone parents who work need reliable and affordable childcare, and may depend on relatives, friends or State supports.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.4] The State supports lone parents through social welfare payments and schemes, such as the One-Parent Family Payment (a payment for lone parents) and the Working Family Payment (a payment that tops up low earnings), along with medical cards and childcare supports.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.5] Voluntary organisations also offer lone parents information and support.
[hom-family-society-s3.1.6] Lone parenthood can be temporary, ending when the children grow up or when a new partner joins the household, which creates a blended family.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.b] An extended family is the nuclear family plus wider relatives (relatives beyond the parents and children), often counted in a multi-generation group, and its members stay closely linked and depend on one another. It was the usual form in rural Ireland in the past.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.0] Members include grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who live in the same household or very close by. Relatives who live far away and are not interdependent do not make an extended family.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.1] A vertical extended family has three or more generations, for example grandparents, parents and grandchildren. A horizontal one has relatives of the same generation, for example brothers with their wives and children.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.2] Members are interdependent, sharing childcare, housework, income and care of older relatives, so costs and tasks are spread widely.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.3] Members may work together as one economic unit, such as a family farm or family business.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.4] There is a strong family tradition, with respect and values passed between generations. Relationships may be more traditional, and historically roles were segregated by gender and age, with men and women carrying out separate tasks.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.5] Households are larger and more crowded, and grandparents often provide much of the childcare.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.6] Advantages: a wide support network and shared costs. Drawbacks: less privacy, and possible conflict between generations and over the authority of older members.
[hom-family-society-s3.2.7] Extended families are the usual form in many cultures, where grandparents often provide much of the childcare.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.b] A blended (also called reconstituted or step) family forms when a parent with children from an earlier relationship has a new partner, so that two family units join. It begins when the new partner and the children live as one family.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.0] Blended families arise through remarriage or cohabitation, and sometimes after the death of a spouse.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.1] Step-parents, step-siblings (no blood link), half-siblings (one shared parent, when the new couple have a child) and extended step-relatives may all be part of the family, creating new relationships. Children may split time between two households.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.2] A joint child of the new partners creates a sibling group that differs in age and background from the earlier children.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.3] The adults must build new relationships and agree rules and discipline, which can be difficult when children have different expectations and routines.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.4] Children may find it hard to accept a step-parent or step-siblings, and rivalry or jealousy can follow. It takes time and patience.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.5] Parents may have financial obligations to children from an earlier relationship as well as to children of the new union, and there are contact arrangements with the non-resident parent.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.6] Advantages: more adults to share income and care, and new relationships and support.
[hom-family-society-s3.3.7] Blended families have become more common in Ireland since divorce became possible and lone-parent families formed new partnerships.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.b] Beyond the four main types, examiners accept other contemporary family structures, and naming them can earn extra credit when a question asks for contemporary family structures.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.0] Same-sex-parent families have two parents of the same sex and their children, who may come by adoption, fostering, previous relationships or assisted reproduction.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.1] Childless (couple) families are a couple with no children.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.2] Cohabiting-couple families are an unmarried couple, with or without children.
[hom-family-society-s3.4.3] Foster families are children placed in a family's care by the State on a temporary basis.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.b] Irish families can be compared with families in other European countries in three ways. Differences come from culture, religion, the economy and how far the State supports families.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.0] Structure: compare the proportions of nuclear, lone-parent, extended and blended households, using the most recent published statistics from Eurostat and the Central Statistics Office.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.1] Size: compare the number of children and members per family, and how family size has changed since the mid-twentieth century. Ireland has historically had a higher birth rate and larger families than most of Europe, a gap that has narrowed.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.2] Roles: compare who works, who cares for children and who cares for older people.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.3] Roles: Nordic countries such as Sweden, Denmark and Norway provide State-funded parental leave and childcare, which supports shared parenting and mothers' employment.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.4] Roles: in many southern European countries, such as Italy and Spain, the family, including grandparents, often provides much of the childcare and care of older people, and adult children may remain at home longer.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.5] Ireland is a mixture: the nuclear family is the most common household structure, relatives, especially grandparents, are still important for childcare, and care follows a family-based approach.
[hom-family-society-s3.5.6] State support: compare how far each country relies on the State and on the extended family to support parents. In Ireland, State support such as childcare and parental leave has grown but still relies on families and on private spending.

CANDIDATES hom-family-society-s1: 1. Defining the family
[hom-family-society-s1.0.b] Everyone knows what a family is, but defining it exactly is harder than it looks. The core idea is kinship, meaning the ties of blood, marriage or adoption that link people.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.0] A family is a group of people related by blood (birth), marriage or adoption, and it is the basic social unit of society. It supports its members and passes values from one generation to the next.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.1] Traditional definition: a group related by blood, marriage or adoption, usually living together and sharing responsibility for its members.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.2] Kinship is the tie that makes a group a family: the links of blood, marriage or adoption, or common ancestry (lineage).
[hom-family-society-s1.0.3] George Peter Murdock (Social Structure, 1949) defined the family as a social group with common residence, economic co-operation and reproduction. This is one classic sociological definition among several, and later sociologists criticise it as too narrow.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.4] Article 41 of the Constitution of Ireland (1937) describes the family as the natural, primary and fundamental unit group of society, and protects it.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.5] Article 41.3 pledges to guard the institution of marriage on which the family is founded, so the protection has traditionally been for the family founded on marriage.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.6] A household is people living at the same address and sharing living arrangements. A family can span more than one household, for example when grandparents living elsewhere, a parent working abroad or separated parents remain part of the family group.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.7] Not every household is a family, because a household may contain unrelated people.
[hom-family-society-s1.0.8] Legal definitions are narrower and focus on marriage, blood and adoption ties, because they decide rights such as inheritance and maintenance. Sociological definitions focus on the relationships and functions of the group, so they can include cohabiting couples, lone parents and same-sex parents.
[hom-family-society-s1.1.b] No single definition fits every society or every household. Each criterion used in a definition leaves some real families out, so a good definition combines several.
[hom-family-society-s1.1.0] A definition based only on marriage excludes cohabiting couples with children and lone-parent families, so sociologists usually include the parent-child relationship.
[hom-family-society-s1.1.1] A definition based only on shared residence excludes family members who live apart, such as a parent working abroad.
[hom-family-society-s1.1.2] A definition based only on blood excludes adoptive and step relationships, which is why adoption is included alongside blood and marriage.
[hom-family-society-s1.1.3] Criteria used in most definitions: blood or adoptive ties, marriage or a committed partnership, shared residence, shared economic life (pooling income and sharing expenses), raising children, and mutual support and obligation.
[hom-family-society-s1.1.4] You should be able to build a working definition by combining criteria, for example blood, marriage or adoption plus mutual support.
[hom-family-society-s1.2.b] Universality means a family of some kind exists in every known human society, although its form (structure) differs from one society to another. It does not mean every society has the same kind of family.
[hom-family-society-s1.2.0] Every known society has a family group that raises children and organises kinship relationships.
[hom-family-society-s1.2.1] What is universal: kinship ties, the rearing of children, the care of dependants, and the passing on of values and culture.
[hom-family-society-s1.2.2] What varies: size, structure (nuclear, extended, polygamous and matrilineal forms are all found in different cultures), who lives together, where couples live after marriage, how descent and inheritance are traced, and the roles of members.
[hom-family-society-s1.2.3] The structure of a family is shaped by economic conditions (such as farming or industry), religion and culture, and law and political conditions, so one family type is not found everywhere and no one form is natural everywhere.
[hom-family-society-s1.2.4] The functions of the family, such as raising children and caring for dependants, appear in all societies even where the structure differs.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.b] Sociologists use a small set of terms to describe how families are organised in different societies. Each one answers a different question: how many partners, whose line, where the couple lives and how people are related.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.0] Monogamy is marriage to one partner at a time; it does not mean one partner for life. Serial monogamy is a succession of single marriages or partnerships, one after another, such as remarriage after divorce.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.1] Polygamy is marriage to more than one spouse at the same time. Polygyny is one husband with several wives, and polyandry is one wife with several husbands.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.2] Patrilineal descent traces family and inheritance through the father's line; matrilineal descent traces them through the mother's line. Bilateral descent traces them through both, which is the pattern in Ireland.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.3] Matrilineal is about descent and inheritance, not about who holds political power or who heads the household.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.4] Patrilocal residence means a couple lives with or near the husband's family; matrilocal residence means they live with or near the wife's family. Neolocal residence means the couple sets up a new home of their own, the common modern pattern. These describe where a couple lives, not who inherits.
[hom-family-society-s1.3.5] Consanguineal relatives are related by blood; affinal relatives are related by marriage (in-laws).
[hom-family-society-s1.3.6] The family of orientation is the family you are born into; the family of procreation is the family you form.
[hom-family-society-s1.4.b] Looking at families in other societies shows which features are found everywhere and which change from place to place. This comparison is the evidence for universality.
[hom-family-society-s1.4.0] The Minangkabau of West Sumatra, Indonesia, are a matrilineal society in which descent is traced and family property is passed through the female line. This shows that a family can be organised around women's lines, though it does not mean women rule.
[hom-family-society-s1.4.1] Kibbutzim in Israel historically raised children in communal children's houses, with parents visiting. This shows that some family functions can be carried out beyond the household, shared with the community while kinship remains. The practice has largely ended.
[hom-family-society-s1.4.2] Other forms include polygamous families in societies where polygyny is accepted, extended households of several generations in many societies, and the nuclear family in industrial societies.
[hom-family-society-s1.4.3] Comparing cases shows which features are universal, such as kinship and child rearing, and which vary, such as residence, descent and household size. The structure varies with the society, but a family group is always present.
[hom-family-society-s1.4.4] You may be given a literature or film extract or a short description of a family in another society. Apply the terms to it by identifying the descent pattern, the residence and the form of marriage, and say what remains universal.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s3: 3. Primary and secondary social groups, kinship, socialisation
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.b] A social group is two or more people who interact regularly and share a sense of belonging, such as a purpose, an identity or a place. Groups differ in how close and how formal their relationships are, and a crowd of strangers is not a group.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.0] A primary group is small, intimate and face-to-face, with long-lasting relationships; examples are the family and close friends.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.1] Primary groups meet emotional needs and give a sense of belonging, so they shape a person's personality, values and sense of identity. They are also where primary socialisation mainly happens.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.2] A secondary group is larger and more formal, with relationships that are impersonal and often short-term or limited to a role; examples are a school class, a workplace, a trade union, a sports club and a political party.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.3] Secondary groups are organised around a specific goal, such as completing a course or producing goods. Members often join by choice and leave when the purpose is finished.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.4] To compare them: primary groups are small, close, lasting and personal, while secondary groups are larger, formal, often temporary and purpose-based. A person belongs to both types at once, and some groups, such as a small close-knit club, sit between the two.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.5] An in-group is a group with which a person identifies and feels loyalty; an out-group is a group that members see as different from or opposed to their own.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.6] A reference group is a group whose norms and values (the standards of behaviour it expects) a person uses to judge their own behaviour, even if they are not a member of it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.0.7] A peer group is made up of people of similar age and status. It is important in adolescence and can act as a primary or a secondary agent of socialisation, depending on how close its members are.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.b] Kinship is the network of family ties between people who are related by blood, marriage or adoption. It is a relationship, not a type of household, so it includes relatives who live apart.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.0] Kinship means family ties: relationships based on blood (common ancestry or lineage), marriage or adoption.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.1] Consanguineal kin are related by blood, for example parents, children, siblings, grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.2] Affinal kin are related through marriage, for example a spouse, a spouse's parents, who are in-laws, and step-relatives. The terms consanguineal and affinal are optional; blood and marriage are enough.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.3] A nuclear family is parents and their dependent children living together as one household.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.4] An extended family includes kin beyond the nuclear family, such as grandparents, aunts, uncles and cousins, who live together or keep in close contact.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.5] The family of orientation is the family a person is born into; the family of procreation is the family a person forms through their own marriage or partnership and children.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.6] Kinship is found in every society, which is part of why the family is called universal.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.1.7] Descent, the line through which kinship is traced, can run through the mother's side, the father's side or both; most modern Western societies trace both sides.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.b] Kin networks matter because relatives are often the first people to turn to when help is needed. They have also changed as society has changed.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.0] Kin networks provide practical and emotional support, including childcare, financial help, care for older members and a sense of identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.1] Kinship obligations include mutual support and, in many societies, inheritance rights between relatives.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.2.2] Kin networks have become looser as families have become smaller and more mobile, but relatives keep in contact through phones and social media.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.b] Socialisation is the lifelong process by which people learn the norms, values, behaviour, roles and skills of their society and so become members of it. Without it a child could not use language or form relationships.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.0] Socialisation is lifelong, so it continues in adolescence and adulthood as well as in childhood.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.1] Primary socialisation takes place in early childhood, mainly within the family, where children learn language, toilet training, manners, self-control, right from wrong and a first sense of identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.2] Secondary socialisation takes place later in wider settings such as school, the peer group, the workplace, religious institutions and the media. There people learn the roles and norms of that setting, such as punctuality, rules, teamwork and respect for authority.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.3] Through socialisation the culture of a society is passed from one generation to the next, which maintains continuity over time. It also produces conformity and social order and lets individuals take part in society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.4] People learn through observation, imitation, rewards and punishments (sanctions), role models and direct instruction. In the family, children learn through observation, imitation and consistent discipline.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.5] Anticipatory socialisation is learning the norms of a role or group that a person hopes to join, such as a student learning workplace norms before starting an apprenticeship.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.3.6] Resocialisation is learning new norms after a major change in life, such as entering the army or prison, where old habits are replaced.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.b] Agents of socialisation are the people and institutions that pass on norms and values. Each one teaches something different.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.0] The main agents are the family, school, peer group, mass media, religion and the workplace.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.1] School socialises formally through the curriculum and informally through the hidden curriculum, the unwritten lessons taught by its rules and routines.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.2] The peer group becomes an important agent in adolescence, when young people test independence and learn the norms of their age group.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.4.3] Mass media, including television, the internet and social media, transmit norms and values and shape consumer habits.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.b] Monogamy is one form of marriage; the other forms are classed as polygamy. It describes how many partners a person has at one time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.0] Monogamy is marriage, or a partnership, with one partner at a time, traditionally one husband and one wife.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.1] Monogamy is the norm in the Western world, and it is the only form of marriage recognised in Ireland. It is not the only form worldwide.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.2] Polygamy is marriage to more than one spouse at the same time; polygyny is one man with several wives, and polyandry is one woman with several husbands. Both are found in some other societies.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.3] Polygamy is illegal in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.4] Bigamy is entering a marriage while still legally married to another person, without the death or divorce of the first spouse. It is an offence in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.5] Serial monogamy is a succession of monogamous marriages, one after another, following divorce or the death of a spouse.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.6] Spouses in a monogamous marriage are expected to remain faithful and loyal to each other, live together, and support one another and their children financially.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s3.5.7] Civil and religious marriages are both legally recognised in Ireland, and both are monogamous. Same-sex marriage is also lawful in Ireland and is monogamous, being a marriage of two people.
