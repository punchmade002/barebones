TARGET hom-energy-nutrition-s2: 2. Energy balance
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.b] Your body takes energy in from food and drink and uses it up all day long. Comparing the two tells you whether your weight will stay steady, rise or fall.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.0] Energy input is the energy in the food and drink consumed. Energy output (also called energy expenditure) is the energy the body uses for BMR (the energy needed just to keep the body alive at rest), physical activity, growth and digestion.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.1] Energy balance is achieved when energy input equals energy output. Nothing is left over to store and nothing extra is needed from the body's stores, so body weight stays steady.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.2] Positive energy balance means input exceeds output. The surplus is converted to fat and stored as adipose (fat) tissue, so weight is gained. Note that 'positive' here does not mean good.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.3] Negative energy balance means output exceeds input. The body makes up the shortfall by using its stored glycogen first and then fat, so weight is lost.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.4] Too little energy for a long time (underweight or prolonged negative balance) leads to weight loss, tiredness and poor resistance to infection.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.0.5] Maintaining energy balance keeps a person at a healthy weight, helps prevent overweight and obesity and the diseases that go with them, and supplies energy for work, study, growth and activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.b] If a person is out of balance, there are only two levers: eat and drink less energy, or use more. Most advice combines both.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.0] To reduce energy intake, cut back on sugary drinks, sweets, fried and fatty foods, and check portion sizes. Grilling or baking instead of frying, and avoiding snacking on high-energy foods, also lower intake.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.1] Eating more fruit, vegetables and fibre helps because fibre fills you up, so you feel full on less energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.2] To increase energy output, add daily walking, cycling, stairs instead of lifts, active leisure or a regular sport or gym, because each extra activity uses energy that would otherwise be stored.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.3] To reduce time spent sedentary (sitting still), limit television, computer and gaming time and break up long periods of sitting.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.4] A gradual change, sustained over time, is more effective than crash dieting, because it is a habit a person can keep up.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.1.5] If weight problems continue, a person can return to balance with these changes plus support from a doctor, a dietitian or a weight management programme.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.b] Case studies give a person's lifestyle and daily menu. The answer should analyse the energy balance first and then recommend changes, so work through it in order.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.0] 1. Identify the person's energy output: occupation, activity level, time spent sitting, age and any growth or pregnancy needs, because these decide how much energy they use.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.1] 2. Estimate the energy input from the menu: note the high-energy items, such as fried foods, cakes, sugary drinks and large portions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.2] 3. State whether the person is in positive or negative energy balance, and what this may do to body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.3] 4. Recommend a change to intake: replace the high-energy items with fruit, vegetables, wholegrain foods or lean protein.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.4] 5. Recommend a change to output: name a specific activity, how often and for how long.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.5] 6. Write a one-day menu that meets the person's energy needs and matches the healthy eating guidelines: breakfast, lunch and dinner, each with two or three items.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.6] Menus may be asked for a person who is obese, a teenager who plays sport, a person with a high energy requirement or a family with young children. A weight-reducing menu uses low-fat milk, grilled or poached foods, wholegrain cereals, vegetables and salad, fruit and water.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.2.7] Diagram: none is needed. A simple balance diagram, with input on one side, output on the other and a tilt for positive or negative balance, is optional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.b] Not all energy comes with goodness attached. Some foods give plenty of energy but almost nothing else the body needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.0] Empty kilocalories (empty calories) are calories from foods that supply energy but little or no nutritional value, such as sweets, sugary soft drinks, crisps, alcohol, cakes and biscuits, and fried snack foods.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.1] They add to energy intake without supplying protein, vitamins, minerals or fibre, so they can cause positive energy balance and weight gain while the diet is still poor in nutrients. Over-indulgence in sugary food can lead to obesity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.3.2] Replacing empty kilocalories with fruit, vegetables, wholegrain foods, lean protein or low-fat dairy gives the same or less energy with more nutrients.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.b] Obesity is a condition of excess body fat that harms health, usually caused by a long-term positive energy balance. Overweight is the milder stage. The causes below are mostly about lifestyle.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.0] Energy imbalance: intake consistently exceeds output, so the surplus is stored as fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.1] Sedentary lifestyle: a job or leisure time spent sitting lowers energy output, so more of the food eaten is left over to be stored.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.2] Lack of exercise: regular physical activity is not part of the daily routine, so output stays low.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.3] Psychological factors: depression, boredom and stress can lead to comfort eating, overeating and snacking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.4] Hormonal imbalance: an underactive thyroid gland slows metabolism and lowers BMR, so the same food intake produces weight gain. This applies to some people only, not as the usual cause.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.5] Unhealthy eating patterns: frequent snacking, skipped meals followed by overeating, and frequent takeaways.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.6] Poor food choices: a high intake of processed foods, sugary drinks and fatty foods. Large portion sizes of energy-dense foods also increase intake without adding to fullness.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.4.7] In children, causes include sweets and fast food, sugary drinks, too much television and computer time, less outdoor play, advertising aimed at children, and parents using food as a reward. Genetic and family factors may also influence weight, but lifestyle causes are stressed.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.b] Carrying excess fat for years strains almost every system in the body. Exams want each risk with a reason how it happens.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.0] Type 2 diabetes: excess fat reduces the body's response to insulin, so blood glucose rises. It can be caused by a sedentary lifestyle and unhealthy diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.1] Coronary heart disease and stroke: obesity raises blood pressure and blood cholesterol levels.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.2] High blood pressure and high cholesterol, both linked to excess weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.3] Gallstones: excess body fat increases the risk of gallstone formation.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.4] Arthritis, joint pain and back pain: extra weight puts stress on the weight-bearing joints and the spine.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.5] Breathing difficulties and sleep apnoea, and some cancers, are also linked to obesity. Varicose veins arise because excess weight increases pressure on the veins in the legs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.6] Depression and poor self-esteem, which can both result from obesity and make it harder to change habits. Difficulties in pregnancy and childbirth, and infertility, are further risks.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.5.7] Reduced life expectancy, as the combined risks shorten life. Corrective measures are to return to energy balance and get support from a doctor, dietitian or weight management programme.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.b] BMI is a simple measure that relates body weight to height and is used to classify body weight in adults. It is a screening measure, not a diagnosis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.0] BMI = weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m²).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.1] Below 18.5: underweight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.2] 18.5 to 24.9: healthy weight range.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.3] 25 to 29.9: overweight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.4] 30 and above: obese.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.5] BMI does not distinguish between muscle and fat, so a muscular athlete may score as overweight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.6] Waist measurement is used alongside BMI, because fat stored around the abdomen carries greater health risk.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.6.7] Children use age and sex charts, not the adult categories above.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.b] Exams give you weight and height, or a table of results, and ask for a category. Follow the steps in order, and keep height in metres.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.0] Step 1: convert height to metres (for example, 175 cm = 1.75 m).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.1] Step 2: square the height (1.75 x 1.75 = 3.06).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.2] Step 3: divide weight by the squared height (sample data: 70 kg / 3.06 = 22.9, in the healthy range). Dividing, not multiplying, is what gives the right answer.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.3] Read a BMI table by finding the row for the height and the column for the weight, and matching the result to the categories above.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s2.7.4] When comparing groups in data, note which group has the higher share in the overweight or obese categories and link this to diet and activity.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s1: 1. Energy requirements and the role of energy
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.b] Every activity the body carries out, from breathing to running, needs energy, and that energy comes from the food and drink we take in. Knowing what energy is for, and how it is measured, is the starting point for working out how much a person needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.0] Energy is what the body needs to carry out every function: breathing, circulating blood, nerve and brain activity, growth and repair, movement and keeping warm.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.1] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal, often called "calories"). 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, and food labels give both units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.2] The body releases energy from carbohydrate, fat and protein, and from alcohol, through chemical reactions, and uses it for every body function.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.3] Energy not needed straight away is stored, because the body cannot waste it: a small store as glycogen (a stored form of carbohydrate) in the liver and muscles, and the large store as fat in adipose tissue (fatty tissue under the skin and around organs).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.0.4] Three main functions of energy are basal metabolism (keeping the body working at rest), growth and physical activity. Marking schemes also credit the working of the internal organs and the production of heat to maintain body temperature.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.b] Even when you are lying still, your body is working hard to keep you alive. The energy for this hidden work is called the basal metabolic rate, and it is the biggest part of what most people use each day.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.0] BMR is the minimum amount of energy the body needs to stay alive when completely at rest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.1] It pays for the functions that continue at rest, such as breathing, the heartbeat and circulation of blood, and the work of the brain, liver, kidneys and other internal organs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.2] It also pays for maintaining body temperature, and for the continuing renewal of cells.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.3] BMR is measured with the person lying at complete rest and awake, in a warm room, about 12 hours after eating (fasting). These conditions mean no movement, cold or digestion adds extra energy use to the result.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.1.4] For most people BMR is the largest single part of the energy used in a day, more than the energy used for activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.b] Two people lying side by side will not burn exactly the same amount of energy. BMR varies with the amount and type of body tissue a person has and with the hormones controlling the speed of their metabolism.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.0] Age: BMR per kilogram is highest in infancy and childhood because of rapid growth, and it falls with age as lean muscle tissue is lost.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.1] Gender: men generally have a higher BMR than women of the same weight, because men have a higher proportion of lean muscle tissue to fat. Do not say it is simply because they are men.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.2] Body size and weight: the larger the body, the more tissue there is to maintain, so the higher the BMR.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.3] Body composition: lean muscle tissue is more active than fat, so it uses more energy at rest. A muscular person has a higher BMR than a person of the same weight with more fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.4] Hormones: the thyroid gland controls the speed of metabolism (the chemical reactions in the body). An overactive thyroid, producing too much thyroid hormone, raises BMR, and an underactive thyroid lowers it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.5] Health: fever, infection and recovery from injury raise BMR, because the body is working harder to fight illness or repair itself.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.6] Pregnancy and lactation (breastfeeding) raise the requirement, and in a cold climate the body works to stay warm, which raises it too; a hot climate has a smaller effect.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.2.7] Activity does not change BMR itself. It is a separate part of the total energy the body uses in a day.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.b] Building a new body takes energy as well as nutrients. This is why people who are growing quickly need a lot of energy for their size.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.0] Energy is needed to build new body tissue, especially muscle, bone, blood and organs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.1] Infants and young children need a high amount of energy for their size because they are growing quickly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.2] Teenagers have adolescent growth spurts (short periods of very rapid growth) which raise the requirement sharply.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.3] Teenage boys generally need more energy than girls at this stage because of their greater body size and muscle.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.3.4] After growth stops, adult energy is needed for the maintenance and repair of tissue, not for new growth.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.b] Physical activity is the most variable part of daily energy use, so it is the factor an individual can change most easily.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.0] The energy used in activity depends on the type, intensity, duration and frequency of the activity.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.1] Occupational activity: a manual worker, such as a builder or farm worker, or an athlete uses far more energy than someone in a desk job such as an office worker.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.2] Leisure activity: walking, cycling, gardening and playing sport add to daily energy use.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.4.3] Sedentary lifestyle (little activity and much sitting) means low energy output, so less food energy is needed to meet the body's needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.b] A person's daily energy requirement is more than their BMR. It adds the energy for activity and for dealing with food, and then rises or falls with circumstances such as climate, pregnancy and health.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.0] Total daily energy use is made up of BMR, plus the energy for physical activity, plus the small amount used to digest, absorb and process food (the thermic effect of food). BMR on its own is not the total requirement.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.1] Occupation: manual and athletic work increases the requirement, while sedentary work lowers it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.2] Climate: in cold conditions the body burns more energy to keep its temperature stable, partly through the muscular work of shivering, so energy needs are higher than in a warm climate.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.3] Pregnancy: extra energy is needed for the growth of the baby and the placenta and for the mother's added tissue, especially in the later months.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.4] Lactation: extra energy is needed to make breast milk.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.5] Health: fever, illness, burns and recovery from injury or surgery raise energy needs. Prolonged bed rest lowers the activity part of the requirement.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.6] The factors examiners list are age, gender, body size or weight, occupation, level of physical activity, climate, pregnancy, lactation, health or illness, growth spurts, sedentary lifestyle and BMR.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s1.5.7] When a person is described, such as a teenager who plays sport, a builder, an office worker, a pregnant woman or an older retired person, name the factor, say whether it raises or lowers the requirement, and give the reason.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s3: 3. Energy value of the nutrients
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.b] Only three nutrients supply energy in food: protein, carbohydrate and fat (lipid). Each releases a fixed amount of energy, measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal), for every gram eaten, and these values are the basis of every energy calculation.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.0] Protein supplies 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, carbohydrate supplies 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram and fat (lipid) supplies 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram. A simple table of these three values is the best way to learn them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.1] Fat is the most concentrated energy source, giving more than twice the energy per gram of protein or carbohydrate. This is why a high-fat diet is energy-dense and contributes strongly to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.2] Vitamins, minerals and water supply no energy, so they are not energy nutrients, even though the body cannot work without them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.3] Alcohol is not a nutrient, but it does supply energy, at 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. It supplies no protein, vitamins or minerals, so it is a source of empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrition).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.4] Fibre is a carbohydrate that the body cannot digest, so it supplies very little usable energy. Do not count it with the starches and sugars, and do not give it 16 kJ per gram.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.5] 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, so dividing kJ by 4.2 gives kcal. Check: 37 kJ is about 9 kcal, 17 kJ is about 4 kcal, 16 kJ is about 3.75 kcal and 29 kJ is about 7 kcal.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.b] A food's energy value is worked out from how many grams of each energy nutrient it contains. Food composition tables and labels give these amounts per 100 g, so the method is multiply, add, then state the unit.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.0] 1. Find the grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat in the amount eaten, from the food label or a food composition table (which gives values per 100 g).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.1] 2. Multiply the grams of each nutrient by its energy value per gram: protein by 17, carbohydrate by 16 and fat by 37.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.2] 3. Add the three results to give the total energy value of the food.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.3] 4. Give the unit, kJ, and convert to kcal if asked by dividing by 4.2.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.4] Scaling to a portion: if the table gives values per 100 g and the portion is 150 g, multiply each per-100 g value by 150 divided by 100 (or 1.5).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.5] Without scaling, the answer is for 100 g, not for the portion eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.b] Seeing the method on sample figures shows where the marks are: each multiplication, the total and the unit. These are invented sample foods, not facts about any named product.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.0] Worked example (sample data): 100 g of a food with 12 g protein, 60 g carbohydrate and 8 g fat gives 12 x 17 = 204 kJ, 60 x 16 = 960 kJ and 8 x 37 = 296 kJ.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.1] These add to a total of 1460 kJ. In kcal this is about 348 (1460 divided by 4.2).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.2] Second worked example (sample data): 100 g of a food with 20 g protein, 50 g carbohydrate and 10 g fat gives 20 x 17 = 340 kJ, 50 x 16 = 800 kJ and 10 x 37 = 370 kJ.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.3] These add to a total of 1510 kJ, which is about 360 kcal.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.4] Percentage contribution: divide the energy from one nutrient by the total energy, then multiply by 100. In the 1460 kJ sample, fat gives 296 out of 1460, which is about 20 per cent of the energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.b] Section B data questions give a table of composition per 100 g for several foods, such as protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre, calcium and iron. The sum is only half the answer, because you must also say what the figures mean for a diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.0] To rank foods by energy value, calculate the kJ for each food using the method above, then put them in order and name the highest and the lowest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.1] A high-fibre food helps bowel health, because fibre adds bulk and keeps waste moving through the gut.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.2] A high-fat food is a disadvantage on a low-fat diet, because fat has the most energy per gram and contributes to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.3] A high-carbohydrate food is a good source of energy for the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.4] The 2007H bread comparison is an example of this type of question: calculate, rank, then comment on how suitable each bread is for a given diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.b] Carbohydrate supplies the largest share of energy in the diet. It is the body's main and most readily available energy source.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.0] Sugars and starches are digested to glucose, which cells use for energy. Any glucose not needed straight away is stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.1] Starchy foods such as bread, potatoes, rice and pasta supply carbohydrate together with fibre, vitamins and minerals, so they give energy with useful nutrition. Cereals and fruit are also sources.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.2] Sugary foods and drinks supply carbohydrate with little nutrition, so they are a source of empty kilocalories.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.3] Healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods, preferably wholegrain, with less from added sugars.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.b] Fat packs far more energy into each gram than the other nutrients, so it contributes strongly to the energy in the diet even when it is a smaller part of the weight of the food. It is the second largest source of energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.0] Fats and oils are lipids, mostly triglycerides, made of glycerol and fatty acids.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.1] Fat supplies the most energy per gram of the three energy nutrients, more than twice that of carbohydrate or protein.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.2] Surplus energy from fat is stored as adipose tissue, an energy reserve, which is why high-fat diets contribute strongly to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.3] Saturated fats come mainly from animal foods and some plant oils, such as palm oil. Unsaturated fats come from vegetable oils, nuts, seeds and oily fish.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.4] Sources of fat include butter, oils, fatty meat, nuts, cheese and oily fish.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.b] Protein can supply energy, but this is not its main job. It makes the smallest contribution to the energy in the diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.0] Protein is made of amino acids. The body uses it mainly for growth and repair of tissue, but it can also supply energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.1] Protein is not stored in the body as protein, so surplus protein is broken down and used for energy or converted to fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.2] When carbohydrate and fat are short, protein is used for energy at the expense of its building role.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.3] High biological value (HBV) protein, from meat, fish, eggs, milk and cheese, contains all essential amino acids. Beans are also a protein source.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.4] Protein is not a major energy source in a healthy diet: its value per gram is close to that of carbohydrate, but it is eaten in smaller amounts.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.b] How much each nutrient contributes to a diet depends on how much of it is eaten and on how much energy each gram of the food carries.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.0] Energy density is the energy per gram of a food. Foods rich in fat and sugar are the most energy-dense.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.1] Wholegrain starchy foods, fruit, vegetables and lean protein give nutrients with less energy per gram, so they are a better choice than empty kilocalories.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.2] Healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods, preferably wholegrain, with less from fat, especially saturated fat, and less from added sugars.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.3] The typical Irish diet gets more of its energy from saturated fat and from added sugar in drinks and processed foods than the guidelines recommend.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.4] Calculating the contribution of each nutrient: multiply the grams eaten in a day by the energy value per gram, then divide each result by the total and multiply by 100 to give its percentage share.
