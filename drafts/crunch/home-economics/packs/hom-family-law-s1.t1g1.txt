TARGET hom-family-law-s1: 1. The Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976
[hom-family-law-s1.0.b] When a relationship breaks down, one person can be left without money to live on. The Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976 gives a financially dependent spouse and dependent children a legal right to maintenance, which is regular financial support paid by one person for the benefit of a dependant (someone who relies on another for money).
[hom-family-law-s1.0.0] A dependent spouse or civil partner is one who does not have the financial resources to support themselves, so the test for the right is dependency.
[hom-family-law-s1.0.1] A dependent spouse can claim maintenance from the other spouse if the relationship breaks down and no other means of support is available. Spouses owe each other support according to their means.
[hom-family-law-s1.0.2] Maintenance can be sought for the dependent spouse and for the dependent children, which is why the Act protects the whole family, not just one person.
[hom-family-law-s1.0.3] Maintenance is regular support, not a one-off payment or a gift.
[hom-family-law-s1.0.4] Maintenance covers the day-to-day costs of the dependant, such as food, clothing, housing and other necessities.
[hom-family-law-s1.0.5] The amount depends on the means and needs of the people involved: the court considers what the paying person can afford and what the dependant needs.
[hom-family-law-s1.1.b] The Act sets exact limits on which children count as dependent, and these limits are the detail examiners reward. It also says who can be asked to pay and in what circumstances.
[hom-family-law-s1.1.0] A dependent child is under 18, or aged 18 to under 23 and in full-time education, or a child of any age with a disability who cannot support themselves.
[hom-family-law-s1.1.1] The payer for a spouse's maintenance is the other spouse, and for a child's maintenance it is either parent.
[hom-family-law-s1.1.2] Maintenance can be sought whether the couple are living together or apart, so the payer can be ordered to pay even where the parents continue to share the same house after separating.
[hom-family-law-s1.1.3] For a child, the marital status of the parents does not matter, so the parents of a child born outside marriage owe the child support too.
[hom-family-law-s1.2.b] Getting maintenance follows three steps, moving from a private agreement to the courts only if the parties cannot agree.
[hom-family-law-s1.2.0] 1. The parties try to reach a maintenance agreement, a written arrangement setting out the amount and how often payments are made. It is a private arrangement between them.
[hom-family-law-s1.2.1] 2. If no agreement can be reached, the dependant applies to court. The application goes to the District Court or the Circuit Court.
[hom-family-law-s1.2.2] 3. The court hears the application and, if it is granted, makes a maintenance order setting out how much the supporting spouse or parent must pay and how often.
[hom-family-law-s1.2.3] A maintenance order is made by the court and can be enforced by it, whereas an informal agreement is not enforced in the same way, so an order gives the dependant stronger protection.
[hom-family-law-s1.2.4] The court can vary (change) an order if circumstances change, for example if the payer's income falls or the child's needs grow.
[hom-family-law-s1.3.b] A court order is only useful if the money actually arrives. When the payer stops paying, the court has a specific tool to collect it.
[hom-family-law-s1.3.0] If the supporting spouse or parent fails to pay, the court can make an Attachment of Earnings Order.
[hom-family-law-s1.3.1] An Attachment of Earnings Order deducts an amount from the wages of the person who owes maintenance and pays it directly to the dependant.
[hom-family-law-s1.3.2] It matters because the dependant does not have to chase the payer, and the money arrives before the payer can spend it.
[hom-family-law-s1.3.3] An Attachment of Earnings Order is not a fine or penalty. It is a way of collecting what is already owed.
[hom-family-law-s1.3.4] Maintenance owed under a court order is enforceable, so the dependant can return to court if payments stop.
[hom-family-law-s1.4.b] Family breakdown can involve separation or divorce, but the right to maintenance does not depend on which stage a couple has reached. The Act also has a limited job, which is money.
[hom-family-law-s1.4.0] Separation: spouses live apart, either by private agreement or by court order; separation does not end the marriage.
[hom-family-law-s1.4.1] Divorce: a legal process that ends a marriage, governed by the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 as amended by the Family Law Act 2019.
[hom-family-law-s1.4.2] Maintenance can be claimed during a separation or after one, and can exist alongside a divorce, so the dependant's support is protected whatever the legal status of the marriage.
[hom-family-law-s1.4.3] Protection under this Act is available whether the dependant is a spouse, a civil partner or a child, which is why it is described as protection for the whole family.
[hom-family-law-s1.4.4] The Act deals with money (income). It does not deal with the home, violence or children at risk, because each of those has its own Act.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s2: 2. Cultural variations and the marriage relationship
[hom-marriage-s2.0.b] The arrangements around marriage are not the same everywhere. They differ in who may marry, who chooses the partner and how many partners a person may have.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.0] The minimum age for marriage varies between countries and cultures; in Ireland both partners must be at least 18.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.1] In an arranged marriage, parents or families choose the spouse on the basis of background and suitability, usually with the couple's consent. This contrasts with a marriage freely chosen by the couple themselves.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.2] Arranged marriage is traditional in some communities, for example Sikh, Japanese and Travelling communities, but not for every member of those communities.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.3] Some cultures restrict the choice of spouse by religion, social class or kinship (family relationship), meaning a person may not marry someone from a particular group.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.4] Monogamy, meaning one husband and one wife at a time, is the norm in Western countries, including Ireland, so one marriage at a time is also what the law there recognises.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.5] Polygamy, meaning more than one spouse at a time, is the general term and is not permitted in Ireland. Marrying again without the death or divorce of the first spouse is bigamy, which is a criminal offence.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.6] Polygamy has two forms: polygyny, where one man has more than one wife, and polyandry, where one woman has more than one husband.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.7] Same-sex marriage is legal in some countries, including Ireland since the Marriage Act 2015, and not recognised in others.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.b] The same legal marriage can be celebrated in different ways, depending on the couple's beliefs. The choice of ceremony is one more way marriage varies between people and cultures.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.0] Marriage may be a civil ceremony before a registrar or a religious ceremony; both are legally valid when the marriage is registered.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.1] Some couples choose a secular or humanist ceremony, which has no religious content.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.2] Interfaith marriages join partners of different religions, so the couple must agree how the ceremony and their family's religious life will be arranged, for example which traditions the children will follow.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.b] Weddings are full of customs that carry meaning for the people who follow them. These customs differ by culture and religion, in dress, symbols, venue and the form of the ceremony.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.0] Dress and symbols such as a veil and a wedding ring, and throwing rice or confetti, are wedding customs that have been popular for centuries in many cultures.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.1] Jewish weddings are held under a canopy, which symbolises the couple's future home.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.2] At the end of a Jewish wedding, a glass is broken under foot to recall the destruction of the ancient Temple in Jerusalem.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.3] Quaker couples marry at a public gathering with no priest or official present, because Quakers believe God alone joins a couple as husband and wife.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.4] The ceremony can take place in a church, mosque, synagogue, registry office or other approved location, depending on the couple's religion and the law of the country.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.b] Marriage is more than a private promise. It is a public commitment that carries legal obligations, and couples keep it going through how they live day to day.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.0] Spouses make a public commitment to each other, usually through vows or declarations before witnesses during the ceremony, sealed by registration of the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.1] Because marriage carries legal obligations, spouses owe each other the rights and responsibilities below, which the law can enforce.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.2] Commitment is maintained, so that the marriage lasts, by working on the relationship, communicating positively and remaining faithful and loyal.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.3] Commitment is also shown through sharing responsibility for the home, money and children, for example through shared finances and a shared home.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.b] A right is something each spouse is entitled to receive from the other because they are married. These rights are the other side of the responsibilities each partner takes on.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.0] Each other's company: spouses are entitled to live together as a couple.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.1] Sexual relations: each spouse is entitled to sexual relations with the other, and consummation is the basis of the marriage. An unconsummated marriage can be annulled (declared never to have been valid) in some circumstances.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.2] Faithfulness: each spouse is entitled to the other's loyalty and faithfulness within a monogamous marriage, because the marriage is a promise to one partner only.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.3] Inheritance: under the Succession Act 1965, a spouse who dies without a will (intestate) leaves the whole estate to the surviving spouse if there are no children.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.4] If there are children and no will, the surviving spouse takes two-thirds of the estate and the children share one-third.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.5] Legal right share: a surviving spouse can claim a share of the estate even where a will leaves them less, so a will cannot cut them out. The share is one half if there are no children and one third if there are children.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.b] Marriage also places duties on each partner, towards each other and towards their children. Some of these are backed by law and some depend on commitment.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.0] Financial support: each spouse must support the other (a dependent spouse) and any dependent children. The Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976 allows a spouse to seek maintenance through the court.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.1] Joint guardianship: married parents are both guardians of their children and share responsibility for their care and upbringing, under the Guardianship of Infants Act 1964 as amended by the Children and Family Relationships Act 2015.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.2] Children's needs: parents must provide for their children's physical, emotional, social, moral, educational and spiritual needs.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.3] Commitment: spouses must work on the relationship, show loyalty and keep their commitment to the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.4] Positive communication: spouses must talk openly about decisions, finances and family matters to keep the marriage functioning.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.5] Caring for a sick spouse is also a responsibility, since marriage means looking after your partner when they are ill.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s4: 4. When a marriage breaks down
[hom-marriage-s4.0.b] Counselling is the first option for a couple in difficulty. It helps partners talk through their problems while there is still a chance to put things right, so that separation or divorce does not become necessary.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.0] Counselling aims to help a couple resolve their problems before they become grounds for separation or divorce, which is why it is the first step rather than the last.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.1] The service is confidential, so partners can speak openly. They may attend together, separately or, where relevant, with their children.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.2] Several visits may be needed before the couple reach a resolution, because problems built up over time are rarely settled in one session.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.3] The counsellor facilitates the discussion and may offer advice, but does not decide for the couple. The couple work out their own way forward.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.4] Counsellors may refer a couple to a specialist counsellor for a particular problem, such as addiction or difficulties in sexual relations.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.5] Marriage counselling is provided by Accord and by other independent counselling services in Ireland.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.b] Mediation is an alternative to court for a couple who have already decided to separate or divorce. Instead of a judge deciding the outcome, the couple work out the terms themselves with help.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.0] The Family Mediation Service of the Legal Aid Board offers a confidential service to couples who have decided to separate or divorce. It was established in 1986, is State-funded and is free, whereas private mediators charge.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.1] An impartial, trained mediator meets the couple together and helps them negotiate their own agreement. The mediator does not take sides or decide for them.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.2] The issues negotiated include parenting arrangements, custody and access to children, finances and maintenance, and the family home and other property.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.3] Mediation keeps children out of conflict, encourages communication between the partners, and helps them accept the separation.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.4] Mediation is less costly, less adversarial and quicker than court proceedings, which pit the spouses against each other.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.5] The outcome is a written agreement that each partner can bring to their solicitor to be made legally binding.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.b] Legal separation is a legally binding agreement under which spouses live apart but remain married. It lets a couple settle practical arrangements without going to court.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.0] The spouses agree to live apart and set out the terms in a deed of separation, a legally binding written agreement.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.1] Typical terms are living apart, care and access arrangements for the children, maintenance, and what happens to the family home.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.2] A solicitor draws up the deed, and each spouse should have their own solicitor. A mediator may help them reach the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.3] Legal separation can be done without going to court if both spouses agree to the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.4] The spouses are still married, so neither can remarry. Only a divorce or a nullity decree allows that.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.5] Where the spouses cannot agree, a court can grant a judicial separation. This is a court order, so it is different from a deed of separation, which the couple agree themselves.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.b] Nullity and annulment both declare that a marriage was never valid, but only a legal nullity decree has effect in the eyes of the State. Do not confuse either with divorce, which ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.0] Legal nullity is a declaration by a court (the Circuit Court or High Court) that the marriage was never valid in law. The parties are then free to marry other people.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.1] Couples married in a civil or State-recognised religious ceremony need a legal nullity decree before either spouse can remarry.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.2] A marriage may be void, for example where one party is already married or in a civil partnership, the parties are within the prohibited degrees of relationship, or the formalities were not followed.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.3] Nullity may also be granted for a lack of capacity or free consent, or where a marriage is voidable, for example because one spouse cannot sustain a normal marital relationship, including inability to consummate.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.4] Church annulment is granted by a Church marriage tribunal, which studies the marriage and declares it invalid in the eyes of the Church.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.5] A church annulment has no legal effect in the State, so the couple remain legally married. A civil nullity decree or a divorce is still needed before either can remarry civilly.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.6] Nullity says the marriage never validly existed. Divorce ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.b] Divorce is a court decree that legally ends a valid marriage. It was introduced in Ireland by the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996, after the 1995 referendum.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.0] The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 was amended by the Family Law Act 2019, which shortened the living-apart period from four of the previous five years to two of the previous three, and came into force on 1 December 2019.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.1] First condition: the spouses must have lived apart for at least two of the previous three years. The couple can live apart under the same roof, and they do not have to be legally separated first.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.2] Second condition: there must be no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, meaning no realistic chance of the spouses getting back together.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.3] Third condition: proper provision has been or will be made for each spouse and for any dependent children, so that nobody is left without support.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.4] The couple must also have a link to Ireland: either living here permanently, or one of them having lived here for at least one year before applying.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.5] When granting a divorce, the court can make orders on custody and access, maintenance, the family home and other property, and succession rights, which extinguish a spouse's right to inherit from the other.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.6] A decree of divorce is final, and each spouse is then free to remarry.
