TARGET hom-food-industry-s2: 2. Small businesses and home enterprises
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.b] Not every food business is a large factory. Small food businesses produce in small quantities, often family-run and rural, for local or niche (specialised) markets, and a home enterprise is the smallest kind.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.0] A small business produces in small quantities and sells locally or to a niche market, which is why it is often family-run and based in a rural area.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.1] A home enterprise (also called a cottage industry) is a small food business started and run from a domestic kitchen or a small unit near the home, for example making jams, chutneys, baking, chocolate or honey.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.2] An artisan producer makes food in limited quantities by traditional, non-industrial skills and hand methods, such as hand-baked bread, farmhouse cheese or chutneys. Cashel Blue (Co. Tipperary) and Gubbeen (Co. Cork) are Irish farmhouse cheeses.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.3] Speciality foods are produced in small quantities for a particular market by small businesses or home enterprises, often with local ingredients, little or no additives and a traditional or natural image. Examples include cheese, chutney, jam, honey, bread, home baking, smoked fish, chocolate, yoghurt and ice cream.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.4] Speciality foods also include foods made for special diets, such as gluten-free (for coeliacs), vegan or diabetic foods, and organic foods.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.5] Artisan describes how a food is made (by hand, using traditional skills), while speciality describes the product and its market. The two overlap, and neither is a legally protected term.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.6] Small producers often sell through farmers' markets, food fairs, local shops, restaurants and online, and direct to customers.
[hom-food-industry-s2.0.7] Many small producers start from a home kitchen and move to a dedicated unit as demand and production grow.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.b] The role of small producers is what they contribute to the food industry and to the country. Each contribution below is a separate point you can develop.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.0] They create employment, often in rural areas where other jobs are limited, which helps keep rural communities alive.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.1] They build a skilled workforce, because staff in small firms often handle every stage of production and so learn every stage.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.2] They diversify the industry by making products that large manufacturers do not produce, and they bring out new exclusive ranges as markets change.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.3] They identify and enter old and new niche markets, meeting consumer demand for local, natural, traditional or special-diet foods.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.4] They use locally produced ingredients, which supports local farmers and suppliers and reduces food miles (the distance food travels from producer to consumer).
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.5] They promote quality ingredients and innovation, and work to very high standards of hygiene.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.6] They strengthen Ireland's reputation for high-quality food and drink, and promote the region they are based in.
[hom-food-industry-s2.1.7] They support rural communities and farm diversification (farmers earning extra income by making food products rather than selling raw produce only).
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.b] Why consumers buy speciality foods is a different question from what small producers contribute to the industry. Features that attract consumers, such as variety, few additives and hand-made quality, are things mass-produced food does not give them.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.0] Consumers want greater product variety and flavour than mass-produced food offers, with recipes that large manufacturers do not make.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.1] There is little or no use of additives, so the foods are seen as natural, less processed or healthier. This is a perception, not a guarantee.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.2] Foods are hand-made with close attention to each batch, using traditional skills, which gives them a quality, premium image and a natural, traditional look.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.3] Many consumers are interested in provenance (where food comes from) and traceability, such as knowing the named farm or maker and the Irish origin of the ingredients.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.4] Consumers want to support local producers and jobs, and sustainability through fewer food miles and organic or sustainable methods, including support for local goods in a recession.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.5] There are more routes to market: farmers' markets, food festivals and food tourism, specialist shops, restaurants, supermarkets that stock local lines, online selling, media coverage and food awards.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.6] There is growing demand for special-diet foods (coeliac, vegan, diabetic), which small producers can supply in small runs.
[hom-food-industry-s2.2.7] Changing food culture, through travel and multicultural eating, has increased interest in different and unusual foods.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.b] Small producers rarely start big. They grow step by step, helped by consumer demand and by public bodies that give training and advice.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.0] Rising consumer demand for local, speciality and traditional foods has encouraged new small producers to start.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.1] Farmers' markets and food festivals give small producers a direct route to customers without a large distributor, so they can test products cheaply.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.2] Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs), a network of offices run through the local authorities (31 teams), give start-up and development supports to small businesses: training, business courses, mentoring, advice and networking.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.3] Food supports include the Food Starter Programme for people at the idea stage, and the Food Academy, which gives training and mentoring to new food companies and is run with Bord Bia.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.4] Bord Bia and Enterprise Ireland give food companies, including smaller firms, market development and export advice.
[hom-food-industry-s2.3.5] The typical growth path is to start at a farmers' market or from the kitchen, move to local shops, then to supermarkets or export, and move from the home kitchen to a registered unit as demand grows.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.b] Making food at home or on a small scale does not lessen the need for safety. A small producer must follow the same food law as a large company, though the paperwork can be scaled to the business.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.0] A new food business, including one run from home, must register with the competent authority (the official body in charge) before it starts trading.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.1] For most businesses, such as bakers, caterers and jam and chutney makers, the competent authority is the HSE Environmental Health Service, which inspects the premises for food safety and hygiene.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.2] The Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine registers premises handling foods of animal origin such as meat, dairy and eggs. Seafood premises are registered with the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.3] EU food hygiene law, Regulation (EC) No 852/2004, is given effect in Ireland by the European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006. It requires every food business to operate a food safety management system based on HACCP principles.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.4] HACCP identifies the points in production where food safety hazards could occur and sets controls for each one. For a small producer the system can be simple and proportionate to the size and nature of the business, so the standards are the same but the paperwork is scaled.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.5] Prepackaged food must carry the information required by Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.6] The required label details are the name of the food, ingredients with allergens emphasised, the business's name and address, date marking and storage instructions. Small firms follow the same labelling rules.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.7] Traceability means producers must know their suppliers and customers, one step back and one step forward (Regulation (EC) No 178/2002), so that a problem food can be traced and recalled.
[hom-food-industry-s2.4.8] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) provides guidance, including a guide to food law for artisan and small food producers, and food safety training to small food businesses.

CANDIDATES hom-food-industry-s1: 1. Structure of the Irish food industry
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.b] The food industry takes raw produce from farms and fisheries, processes it into finished goods, and distributes and sells them through a service sector. It is organised in three levels, each depending on the one before it: a processor needs farmers' produce, and a retailer needs the processor's goods.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.0] Primary sector: resource-based production, meaning farming, horticulture (growing fruit, vegetables and similar crops), fishing and fish farming (mariculture). It produces raw materials such as milk, cattle, sheep, pigs, poultry, eggs, grain, fruit, vegetables, fish and shellfish.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.1] Secondary sector: manufacturing and processing, which converts raw materials into finished food and drink, such as milk into cheese, butter and milk powder, wheat into flour and bread, carcasses into cuts and meat products, or barley into beer and whiskey.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.2] Tertiary sector: services that move, sell, support and regulate food, including transport, wholesale and retail, catering and food outlets, tourism and hospitality, research, education, advice, marketing and regulation.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.3] Agri-food is one of Ireland's largest indigenous industries (industries that grew up in Ireland rather than being brought in from abroad), and most of its raw material is grown or reared on Irish farms.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.4] The industry matters to the economy because it provides employment, much of it in rural areas, and earns income from export sales.
[hom-food-industry-s1.0.5] The UK and other EU member states are the main markets for Irish food overall, and much dairy and beef output is exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.b] Besides the three levels, the industry is divided into product sectors. A single sector such as dairy can contain primary, secondary and tertiary activity, so keep the two lists apart.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.0] Dairy and ingredients: milk, cheese, butter, yoghurt, cream and milk powder, plus dairy ingredients sold to other food manufacturers.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.1] Meat and livestock: beef, lamb, pork and poultry, slaughtered and processed in licensed meat plants.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.2] Beverages: beer and stout, whiskey and other spirits, fruit juice and bottled water.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.3] Horticulture: fruit, vegetables and mushrooms, sold fresh or processed.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.4] Seafood: fish and shellfish landed at Irish ports and processed for home and export sale.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.5] Cereals and bakery: flour milling, bread, biscuits and breakfast cereals.
[hom-food-industry-s1.1.6] Prepared consumer foods: ready meals, snacks, confectionery and sauces sold in retail packaging.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.b] Ireland produces far more of some foods than its small population can eat, so these are sold abroad. A climate and soil suited to grass means livestock and dairy output is much larger than home demand.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.0] Dairy products, especially cheese, butter, milk powder and infant formula ingredients, are among Ireland's largest food exports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.1] Beef and lamb are exported to EU and non-EU markets.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.2] Pork, poultry and processed meat products are also exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.3] Irish whiskey, beer and stout are exported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.4] Seafood, including shellfish and farmed salmon, is exported from Irish ports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.2.5] Prepared foods and bakery products are sold to retailers and food service companies abroad.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.b] Ireland imports foods it cannot grow, cannot grow all year round, or chooses to buy in for variety and price. Reasons include climate (tropical crops), seasonal gaps, consumer demand for variety and ethnic foods, ingredients for manufacturers and feed for livestock.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.0] Tropical and out-of-season fruit, such as bananas, pineapples, oranges and avocados, which Ireland cannot grow commercially.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.1] Coffee, tea and cocoa, which are grown in warmer countries.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.2] Spices and sugar, which Ireland does not produce in quantity.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.3] Soya and other animal feed ingredients used by Irish livestock producers.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.4] Wine, and some processed foods such as chocolate and confectionery.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.5] Rice, dried pasta, some vegetables and herbs, and sauces are also imported.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.6] Ireland also imports some foods it exports, such as cheeses and meat products, for variety and price, so trade runs both ways.
[hom-food-industry-s1.3.7] Ireland is a net exporter of food overall, so the value of exports is greater than the value of imports.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.b] Several State bodies support, develop and regulate the food industry. Each has its own job, so learn each name together with one function.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.0] Bord Bia (the Irish Food Board) promotes Irish food, drink and horticulture at home and abroad, and develops national and international markets for Irish products.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.1] Bord Bia runs quality assurance schemes for food producers, such as the Sustainable Beef and Lamb Assurance Scheme and the Sustainable Dairy Assurance Scheme, shown by a quality mark on pack.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.2] Bord Bia's Origin Green programme records and verifies the sustainability commitments of food and drink companies: companies set measurable targets and their progress is independently verified.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.3] Bord Bia helps Irish food companies with market research, buyer introductions and trade fairs.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.4] Teagasc, the agriculture and food development authority, provides research, advice and training for farmers and food producers, so they can improve what they grow and make.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.5] Bord Iascaigh Mhara (BIM), the seafood development agency, develops the seafood sector and promotes seafood at home and abroad.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.6] Enterprise Ireland supports export growth and innovation in Irish companies, including food and drink firms.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.7] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) sets and enforces food safety standards across the sector.
[hom-food-industry-s1.4.8] The Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine is the government department responsible for the sector, covering policy and schemes.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.b] A local factory, business or home enterprise can be used to show how a food business fits into the industry structure. Work through the same set of questions for whichever business you choose.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.0] Identify the business's sector, such as dairy, meat, beverages or bakery, and whether its main activity is primary, secondary or tertiary.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.1] Record its raw materials, where they come from, and the finished products it makes.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.2] Note its markets: local shops and customers, other Irish retailers, or export markets.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.3] Assess its contribution to the area through jobs, local suppliers and the income it brings to rural communities, which is why the industry matters locally.
[hom-food-industry-s1.5.4] List the careers it offers, such as production operative, technologist, quality staff, chef or sales and marketing roles.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s9: 9. Food legislation
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.b] Food law protects consumers by making sure food is safe and honestly described. It comes from two sources, the EU and Ireland, and the two work together.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.0] An EU regulation applies directly in every member state, so it is not an Irish law. Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 and Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 are EU regulations, not Irish Acts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.1] Ireland also makes national regulations (called statutory instruments) to give an EU regulation effect, which means they set out how it is enforced and which bodies enforce it.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.2] The Oireachtas (the Irish parliament) passes Acts, such as the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998.
[hom-food-safety-s9.0.3] The purpose of all of this law is consumer protection: food that is safe to eat and described honestly.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.b] Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 on the hygiene of foodstuffs is an EU regulation, which applies directly in every member state. It lays down hygiene rules for food businesses at all stages after primary production (the growing or rearing of the raw food). Ireland gives effect to it through national regulations.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.0] The European Communities (Hygiene of Foodstuffs) Regulations 2006, amended in 2009 and 2010, give effect to Regulation (EC) No 852/2004 in Ireland.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.1] The Regulations set hygiene standards for food premises, equipment, food handling, water supply, waste and the personal hygiene of food handlers, so that every step where food could be contaminated is covered.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.2] They require food businesses to put in place and operate a food safety system based on HACCP (a system that finds the points where food could become unsafe and controls them).
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.3] They require staff to be trained and supervised in food hygiene and to follow hygiene procedures, so that the people handling food know how to prevent contamination.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.4] They require food premises to be registered with the relevant competent authority (the official body in charge of checking them).
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.5] They permit authorised officers, for example environmental health officers, to inspect premises, and to close them or have food destroyed where public health is immediately threatened.
[hom-food-safety-s9.1.6] How this protects the consumer: food is prepared in clean premises by trained staff under a system that prevents contamination, and unfit food can be taken off sale.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.b] Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 on the provision of food information to consumers is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014. It applies from 13 December 2014, and the nutrition declaration from 13 December 2016. It lists the information that must appear on pre-packed food, so that shoppers can choose safely.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.0] A label must give the name of the food and a list of ingredients in descending order of weight at the time of manufacture, so the main ingredient comes first.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.1] Fourteen named allergens must be emphasised wherever they appear in the list of ingredients, so that people with an allergy can spot them at once.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.2] A label must give the net quantity of the food, and any special storage conditions or instructions for use where needed.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.3] A label must give the name and address of the food business operator, so the food can be traced to a named business.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.4] Country of origin or place of provenance must be given where leaving it out would mislead the consumer, and for certain meats and fresh produce.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.5] Drinks over 1.2% alcohol must show their alcoholic strength.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.6] A nutrition declaration gives the energy value, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt of the food.
[hom-food-safety-s9.2.7] Labelling must not mislead the consumer about the nature, identity, properties, origin or method of production of the food. Nutrition and health claims are regulated separately.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.b] Two parts of the label matter most for safety: the allergens and the date mark. Both help the consumer avoid harm, but they work in different ways.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.0] The 14 allergens that must be emphasised are cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphur dioxide and sulphites (above a set level), lupin and molluscs.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.1] A use-by date must appear on highly perishable food, because such food can become unsafe to eat. After this date the food must not be eaten.
[hom-food-safety-s9.3.2] A best-before date (the date of minimum durability) shows the date until which the food keeps its quality, so food past this date is a matter of quality, not safety.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.b] Alongside the rules on hygiene and labelling, there is a general framework that sets the principles for all food law. In Ireland this is the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998. Across the EU it is Regulation (EC) No 178/2002, known as general food law.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998 provides the Irish legal framework for food safety. It set up the FSAI and gave it the functions of coordinating food safety law and its enforcement.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.1] The 1998 Act also names the official agencies that carry out controls under service contracts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.2] Regulation (EC) No 178/2002 sets the general principles and requirements of food law across the EU.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.3] Under general food law, food that is unsafe must not be placed on the market, and responsibility for safety rests with the food business.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.4] Food businesses must be able to trace food one step back to their supplier and one step forward to their customers, so that unsafe food can be found and withdrawn quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.5] General food law set up the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the Rapid Alert System for Food and Feed.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.6] Risk analysis under general food law has three parts: risk assessment (scientific, carried out by EFSA), risk management (decisions by the authorities) and risk communication. The precautionary principle may apply where the science is uncertain.
[hom-food-safety-s9.4.7] The result is a common high level of protection of health and consumer interests from farm to fork, with fast withdrawal of unsafe food.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.b] Rules only protect consumers if someone checks that businesses follow them. Regulation (EU) 2017/625 sets out the rules for official controls, which are checks by public authorities that food businesses comply with food law at every stage of the food chain.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.0] Regulation (EU) 2017/625 is given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Official Controls in Relation to Food Legislation) Regulations 2020.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.1] Official controls include inspection, sampling and analysis, and audit (a formal check of a business's records and systems).
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.2] Official controls are regular and risk-based, so that higher-risk businesses are checked more often. They are carried out without prior warning in most cases.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.3] Inspectors can take samples of food for testing as part of an official control.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.4] In Ireland the FSAI is the competent authority with overall responsibility. The official agencies (HSE, DAFM, SFPA and local authorities) carry out controls on its behalf under service contracts.
[hom-food-safety-s9.5.5] How this protects the consumer: independent checks on all food businesses, with enforcement action such as improvement notices, closure and withdrawal where the law is broken.
