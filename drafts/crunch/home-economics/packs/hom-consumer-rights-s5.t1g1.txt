TARGET hom-consumer-rights-s5: 5. Voluntary and statutory consumer protection bodies
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.0.b] When something goes wrong as a consumer, there are two kinds of organisation that can help. Consumer protection bodies are either established by law with legal powers, or operate voluntarily through advice, research and self-regulation.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.0.0] Statutory bodies are established by an Act of the Oireachtas (the Irish parliament) and are funded by the State. They have legal powers to investigate, enforce and, in some cases, prosecute.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.0.1] Voluntary bodies are independent or industry-based. They advise, research, lobby and handle complaints, but they have no legal powers, so they cannot compel a trader to act.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.0.2] Self-regulation means an industry sets its own standards. For example, the advertising industry sets its own code, and a voluntary body handles public complaints made under that code.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.0.3] A consumer with a complaint about a trader should usually approach the trader first, because most problems are settled at that stage. If that fails, use the statutory or voluntary body that fits the problem.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.b] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) is the state body that enforces consumer law. It was established by statute in 2014 and took over the work of the National Consumer Agency and the Competition Authority.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.0] It enforces the Consumer Protection Act 2007 and other consumer law, so it acts on behalf of all consumers through the law.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.1] It investigates unfair and illegal trading practices, including false or misleading advertising and pricing, such as a price that is not what the trader claims.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.2] It can prosecute offences under consumer law, which is a legal power that voluntary bodies do not have.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.3] It informs consumers of their rights through its website and helpline, and runs consumer information campaigns, including on personal finance.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.4] It enforces product-safety rules for non-food products, which protects consumers from unsafe goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.5] It promotes competition and advises government on consumer issues.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.1.6] It does not resolve individual disputes between a consumer and a trader; it deals with breaches of the law and with information for consumers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.2.b] Citizens Information provides free, confidential information and advice on rights and entitlements, including consumer rights. The Citizens Information Board is the statutory body behind it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.2.0] The Citizens Information Board is a statutory body that funds and supports (oversees) Citizens Information Centres and services.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.2.1] Citizens Information Centres are local, independent services that give free, confidential information on consumer issues, so a consumer can get advice face to face.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.2.2] Services cover consumer issues, social welfare entitlements, housing, health and other rights.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.2.3] The Citizens Information website and phone service give access to information on rights from anywhere in Ireland, so a consumer does not have to visit a centre.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.3.b] An ombudsman is an independent official who investigates complaints that have not been resolved directly with the body concerned. Ireland has two that matter to consumers, one for public bodies and one for financial providers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.3.0] The Office of the Ombudsman investigates complaints about the administrative actions of public bodies, such as government departments, the HSE and local authorities.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.3.1] The Financial Services and Pensions Ombudsman (FSPO) investigates complaints about financial service providers, such as banks, insurers, credit unions and pension providers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.3.2] The FSPO replaced the Financial Services Ombudsman and the Pensions Ombudsman in 2018, so one office now covers both areas.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.3.3] Both are a last resort: the consumer must first use the provider's or body's own complaints procedure, and only then go to the ombudsman.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.3.4] Investigations are free of charge to the complainant.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.4.b] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) protects consumers from food-related risks. It is the body to turn to for unsafe or mislabelled food.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.4.0] It was established under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998, which is why it counts as a statutory body.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.4.1] It sets and monitors food safety standards and gives consumers advice on food.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.4.2] It works with official control agencies, such as the HSE and local authorities, that inspect food businesses on its behalf.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.5.b] Marking schemes accept a few more statutory bodies, each protecting consumers in a particular area. Know the full name and one role for each.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.5.0] The Central Bank of Ireland regulates financial service providers and protects consumers of financial services.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.5.1] The National Standards Authority of Ireland (NSAI) sets and certifies standards, so consumers can trust that products meet an agreed standard.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.5.2] European Consumer Centre Ireland advises consumers on cross-border purchases within the EU, such as faulty goods bought online from a trader in another EU country. It is run by the CCPC as part of an EU network.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.5.3] The small claims procedure is a court route for settling a dispute, not a consumer protection body, so it is not the answer to a question that asks for a body.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.6.b] The Consumers' Association of Ireland (CAI) is an independent, non-profit organisation that represents consumers. It was founded in 1966.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.6.0] It researches and tests goods and services and publishes its findings in Consumer Choice, so consumers can compare products before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.6.1] It provides objective information and advice on consumer products and services, including a personal advisory service.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.6.2] It lobbies government on consumer issues, meaning it presses for changes that favour consumers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.6.3] It represents consumers on a number of bodies, so consumers have a voice where decisions are made.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.7.b] The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) is a self-regulatory body for advertising, set up by the advertising industry itself. Older material calls it the Advertising Standards Authority for Ireland (ASAI).
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.7.0] It promotes high standards in advertising through a code, requiring that advertisements are legal, decent, honest and truthful.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.7.1] It investigates complaints from the public about advertisements, such as false claims or offensive content.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.7.2] Where a complaint is upheld, the advertiser is asked to amend or withdraw the advertisement. The ASA has no legal powers, so it relies on the industry complying with the code.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.8.b] Some voluntary help comes from industry groups and local services rather than from a single national organisation.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.8.0] Trade associations look after the interests of their members and may have codes of practice and complaints procedures, so a consumer can complain to the association about a member.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.8.1] Citizens Information Centres, as local services, are accepted as voluntary in some schemes because they are staffed partly by volunteers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.b] Exam questions often describe a problem and ask which body helps. Work out whether the problem is about the law, a code, money, public services, food or a purchase from abroad.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.0] A misleading price or advertising claim goes to the CCPC (which enforces the law) or the ASA (which enforces the advertising code).
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.1] A complaint about a bank or insurer goes to the provider first, then to the FSPO.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.2] A complaint about a public service goes to the Office of the Ombudsman.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.3] To find consumer information, use Citizens Information, the CCPC or the CAI.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.4] Faulty goods from an EU trader bought online go to European Consumer Centre Ireland.
[hom-consumer-rights-s5.9.5] Unsafe or mislabelled food goes to the FSAI.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s4: 4. Consumer protection legislation
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.b] The Consumer Rights Act 2022 gives consumers statutory rights (rights set by law) and remedies when they buy goods, services or digital content from traders. It applies to contracts made on or after 29 November 2022 and replaced the earlier law on sales and services for consumer contracts.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.0] A trader is a person who supplies goods, services or digital content in the course of business, so the Act applies to shops, online sellers and service providers alike.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.1] A consumer is an individual acting for purposes that are not part of their business, so someone buying a washing machine for their home is a consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.2] Statutory rights apply automatically to every contract between a trader and a consumer, whether or not the trader mentions them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.3] A trader cannot remove statutory rights through terms and conditions, small print or notices; a 'no refunds' sign does not override the Act.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.0.4] Digital content, such as downloaded software, music or films, is covered by the same standards of quality, fitness and description as goods, because it is bought in the same way and can be just as faulty.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.b] Goods must meet four standards at the time they are supplied, and the consumer is entitled to all of them whatever the trader says.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.0] Satisfactory quality: goods must be of a standard a reasonable person would consider satisfactory, taking account of price and description. This includes being durable, safe, free from minor defects, and having an acceptable appearance and finish.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.1] Fit for purpose: goods must be fit for the purposes for which goods of that type are normally used, and for any particular purpose the buyer made known to the trader, for example asking for a coat that is waterproof.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.2] As described: goods must match any description given by the trader, the label or the advertisement, so a 'leather' bag that is plastic does not meet this right.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.3] Correspond to sample or model: goods must match a sample or model shown to the consumer before purchase.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.4] A fault that is present when the goods are supplied is the trader's responsibility, even if it appears only later.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.1.5] The retailer is responsible for faulty goods and must deal with the complaint, even if the fault was made by the manufacturer. The consumer approaches the trader, not the manufacturer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.b] A service is measured against what a competent provider would deliver and against what the trader said or agreed, so the consumer is protected even though there is no physical product to inspect.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.0] Reasonable care and skill: a service must be carried out with the care and skill a competent provider would show, in other words to a high standard by a suitably qualified person.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.1] Materials: any materials used in a service must be of satisfactory quality and fit for their purpose, so a repair using poor parts is a breach even if the work itself was careful.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.2] Time: where no time was agreed, the service must be carried out within a reasonable time, so a trader cannot leave a job half-finished for months.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.3] Price: where no price was agreed in advance, the consumer pays only a reasonable price for the service, which protects them from an inflated bill afterwards.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.2.4] Matching what was said or agreed: the service must match what the trader told the consumer or agreed with them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.b] When goods or services do not meet their statutory standard, the remedies are applied in a set order: the consumer starts with the gentlest fix and moves to the next only if that fails.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.0] First remedy for goods: repair or replacement at the trader's cost, within a reasonable time and without significant inconvenience to the consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.1] Second remedy for goods: a price reduction or a refund, where repair or replacement is not possible, is not carried out within a reasonable time, or causes significant inconvenience.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.2] Short-term right to reject: the time limit for rejecting faulty goods outright is 30 days, so they can be rejected within 30 days of supply (delivery) and the consumer receives a full refund, without having to accept a repair first.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.3] Services that are not performed to the required standard must be repeated by the trader, or the consumer can ask for a price reduction.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.3.4] A manufacturer's guarantee is extra to statutory rights and cannot reduce them. A statutory right is set by law and applies automatically, whereas a guarantee is a voluntary promise from the manufacturer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.b] The Act protects buyers from faulty goods and services, not from every disappointment, so exam scenarios often turn on spotting when no remedy is owed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.0] There is no statutory right to a refund merely because the buyer changed their mind or dislikes the colour. Any refund, exchange or credit note in that case is the shop's own policy, a favour rather than a legal duty.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.1] Goods bought in a sale are covered by the same statutory rights as goods bought at full price, so a sale does not weaken the consumer's position.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.2] Exception: a fault that the trader pointed out before the sale is not covered by the statutory rights for that fault.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.3] A consumer who was not told of a fault before purchase keeps the full statutory remedies for it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.4] Misuse or ordinary wear and tear is not a fault for which the trader is liable, because the goods were not faulty when supplied.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.4.5] Proof of purchase, such as a receipt, bank statement or order confirmation, is needed to show the goods came from this trader.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.b] The Consumer Protection Act 2007 is about how traders behave in advertising, pricing and selling, rather than what can be claimed for a faulty purchase. It prohibits unfair commercial practices and protects consumers from false and misleading claims, and it replaced the Consumer Information Act 1978.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.0] Misleading actions: giving false or deceptive information about a product, its price, its origin or the trader's identity, which could lead a consumer to buy something they would not otherwise choose.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.1] Misleading omissions: leaving out or hiding material information that a consumer needs in order to make a decision.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.2] Aggressive practices: harassment, coercion, undue influence or pressure that limits the consumer's freedom to choose.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.3] Prohibited practices: a list of practices banned in all cases, such as falsely claiming that a product has an approval it does not have, or using a quality mark without permission.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.4] The Act is enforced by the Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC), which investigates and can prosecute offences. It does not settle an individual consumer's dispute with a trader.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.5.5] The consumer benefits from protection against false advertising, honest price information, and a state body that takes action.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.b] Price claims must be accurate, including claims about reductions and comparisons, because a price is often what persuades a consumer to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.0] False or misleading information about the price of goods or services is prohibited.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.1] A claimed previous price or a claimed reduction must be accurate; a false or misleading reduction, such as a 'was' price the item never sold at, is an unfair practice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s4.6.2] A recommended retail price must not be used misleadingly to suggest a saving the consumer would not in fact make.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s3: 3. Consumer responsibility: information and rights
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.b] A purchase involves several parties, and each has a different relationship to the consumer. Knowing who is who tells you where to go when something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.0] The consumer is an individual who buys goods or services for personal use, not for business, so a shopper buying a kettle for the kitchen is a consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.1] The retailer or trader sells goods or services in the course of business and is the consumer's first point of contact for a complaint, because the consumer's contract is with the seller.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.2] Under consumer law the retailer is responsible for faults in goods, even when the manufacturer made the fault, and must deal with the complaint.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.3] The manufacturer makes the product and is the other party to any manufacturer's guarantee (a written promise to repair or replace a faulty product).
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.4] A guarantee is a contract between the manufacturer and the consumer. It is usually offered in addition to the consumer's statutory rights (rights set by law), not instead of them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.0.5] The supplier or distributor moves goods from the manufacturer to the retailer, so the consumer rarely deals with this party directly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.b] Informed decisions rest on reliable information. It comes from the product itself, from the seller and from independent sources, and each should be checked against the others.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.0] Labels, instructions and care labels give the contents or ingredients, how to use and care for the product, and warnings about hazards, so they should be read before buying and using.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.1] Unit pricing shows the price per kilogram or per litre, which allows comparison between different pack sizes.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.2] Advertising and sales material is useful but should be checked against the product label and independent information, because the seller wants to make the sale.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.3] Independent consumer publications and testing reports compare products on quality and value for money. The Consumers' Association of Ireland publishes comparative findings in Consumer Choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.4] Citizens Information gives free, confidential information on consumer rights, so a consumer can ask about a problem without cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.5] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission publishes guidance on consumer rights and on unfair trading.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.6] Terms and conditions, the returns policy and guarantee wording should be read before purchase, as they set out what the seller offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.7] Marks and symbols are information too: quality marks such as the Bord Bia Quality Mark, the CE mark (the manufacturer declares the product meets EU safety requirements), the energy rating label on appliances, and safety and hazard symbols.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.1.8] Reviews, word of mouth and online comparison sites are useful but may be biased, so check more than one source.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.b] A consumer's part in a transaction is to be informed, careful and honest. These are the consumer's duties, which are different from the rights the consumer is entitled to.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.0] Know your rights and the laws that protect consumers: the basic rights and remedies under consumer law apply whether or not the seller mentions them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.1] Be informed about goods and services before buying, and compare value, not only price, by weighing price against quality, durability and the cost of running or maintaining the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.2] Read and understand labels and terms before buying, including ingredients, dates, guarantee conditions and cancellation terms.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.3] Follow the manufacturer's instructions and care labels; a fault caused by misuse is unlikely to be the trader's responsibility.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.2.4] Keep receipts and proof of purchase, since they show when, where and what was bought, and are needed for a claim.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.b] The consumer's duties continue after the sale. A responsible consumer also acts quickly when something goes wrong and plans spending so that problems are less likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.0] Report faults promptly, stop using a faulty or unsafe product, and complain where appropriate, as delay can make a problem harder to prove.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.1] Give accurate, truthful information when complaining, including a clear description of the problem and the redress (the remedy) wanted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.2] Plan purchases and set a budget: check whether a product is needed before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.3] Resist pressure selling and impulse buying, and do not be rushed by sales talk, because a quick decision leaves no time to compare or check.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.4] Check the total cost, including interest and charges, before agreeing to credit or easy-pay arrangements, because the instalments can add up to far more than the cash price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.3.5] Use resources responsibly and consider the environment: reduce waste, and reuse and recycle packaging.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.b] Traditional buying principles still shape how a consumer should approach a purchase, even though the law now gives buyers more protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.0] Caveat emptor is a Latin principle meaning 'let the buyer beware': it places the duty of checking goods on the buyer, so the buyer had to inspect goods before buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.1] Modern consumer law limits caveat emptor by giving statutory rights that apply automatically, but inspecting goods and asking questions before purchase remains the best protection.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.2] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income; a wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.4.3] Vague claims such as 'the best' or 'new' that lack supporting evidence should be questioned before they influence a purchase, because they sound impressive but promise nothing that can be checked.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.b] Consumer rights are what a buyer is entitled to expect. Papers list them in slightly different ways, so learn the rights below as the common set.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.0] Right to safety: goods and services must not endanger health or life.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.1] Right to honest and truthful information about goods and services, so that buyers can decide on accurate facts.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.2] Right to choice: a range of goods and services is available to choose from.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.3] Right to value for money: goods and services should be of a quality that matches the price paid, and goods should be of satisfactory quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.4] Right to redress: a consumer whose goods or service are faulty can seek repair, replacement or a refund.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.5] Right to services completed to a high standard, by a qualified person, using good materials.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.6] There is no right to a refund simply for a change of mind in a shop purchase; a retailer may offer an exchange or a credit note as goodwill.
[hom-consumer-rights-s3.5.7] Rights and responsibilities are linked: each right is only as useful as the consumer's knowledge and action, so a right to information works only if labels are read.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s8: 8. National agencies in food safety
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.b] Consumers cannot see how hygienically their food was produced, so the state sets up bodies to check it on their behalf. In Ireland one central body coordinates the system and other bodies do the inspecting.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.0] National agencies protect consumers by setting and enforcing food safety standards, inspecting food businesses, testing food and acting when food is unsafe.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.1] Food businesses must be monitored independently, because a business cannot be relied on to judge its own hygiene and customers have no way of checking it themselves.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.2] An outbreak or contamination can cross counties and countries, so a national system is needed to trace it and respond quickly.
[hom-food-safety-s8.0.3] The Irish system has one central body, the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI), which coordinates, and a group of official agencies that carry out the inspecting.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.b] The FSAI is the state body at the centre of food safety in Ireland. It sets the direction and coordinates the work, while others carry out most of the inspections.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.0] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) is the state body responsible for food safety in Ireland, established under the Food Safety Authority of Ireland Act 1998.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.1] The FSAI operates under the Department of Health, the government department responsible for health policy. The FSAI advises the Minister on food safety, but the Department itself does not inspect food premises.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.2] It co-ordinates the enforcement of food safety legislation in Ireland, working through the official agencies, and it audits their work rather than inspecting most premises itself.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.3] It protects consumers by ensuring that food is safe to eat and that food information is accurate.
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.4] It works with food businesses to make sure food is produced safely, for example through guidance and training on hygiene and HACCP systems (the system of identifying and controlling hazards in food production).
[hom-food-safety-s8.1.5] It takes action, through the agencies, when a food premises is in breach of food regulations, because it is the agencies that can inspect and enforce on the ground.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.b] Beyond coordinating enforcement, the FSAI informs the public, handles emergencies and processes applications for new food products. It has one clear limit: it is about safety, not promotion.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.0] It gives consumers and food businesses authoritative advice and publishes food safety information, so that both groups can rely on one trusted source.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.1] It carries out scientific work, which gives its advice and decisions an evidence base.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.2] It processes applications for novel foods and for new methods of food processing, so that new products are assessed before they reach consumers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.3] It also handles food alerts, product withdrawals and recalls, works with the agencies on traceability and leads on food incidents.
[hom-food-safety-s8.2.4] It does not promote Irish food at home or abroad; that is the role of Bord Bia (the Irish Food Board), which is why an exam statement that the FSAI promotes Irish food is false.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.b] The FSAI does not carry out most food controls itself. Official agencies do this work under service contracts with the FSAI, which means the FSAI can coordinate one national system without employing every inspector.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.0] The official agencies are the Health Service Executive (HSE), the Department of Agriculture, Food and the Marine, the Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority and the local authorities.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.1] Official agencies carry out inspections, sampling and analysis of food in the places they cover, and they enforce the rules on the FSAI's behalf.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.2] The Sea-Fisheries Protection Authority carries out the official food safety controls on fish and shellfish, from production and landing through processing to distribution, but not retail.
[hom-food-safety-s8.3.3] Local authorities carry out food controls under contract to the FSAI in their own areas, mainly veterinary inspections of food premises that handle meat and other animal products.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.b] The HSE is the national health service, and its Environmental Health Service does most of the day-to-day inspection of food premises. Its staff are environmental health officers, who work for the HSE and not for the FSAI.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.0] Environmental health officers (EHOs), working in the HSE's Environmental Health Service, carry out the inspections of food premises, including restaurants, cafés, shops, hospital kitchens, manufacturers and wholesalers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.1] The service registers new food premises, so that every business handling food is known to the authorities before it starts trading.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.2] It inspects food premises and farms.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.3] It handles hygiene complaints and issues closure orders and enforcement notices where food premises are unsafe, which stops a risky business from continuing to sell food.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.4] It ensures food safety at public gatherings and events.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.5] It monitors compliance with food, labelling, additives and contaminants legislation, and takes samples of food for testing.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.6] It responds to rapid alerts and recalls, and it investigates food poisoning outbreaks.
[hom-food-safety-s8.4.7] It educates and advises food businesses on compliance with the law.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.b] Inspectors can see a kitchen, but only a laboratory can tell what is in a food. The Public Analyst Laboratories provide that testing, and they analyse rather than inspect.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.0] The Public Analyst Laboratories are run by the HSE.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.1] They analyse food and water, including samples taken by environmental health officers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.2] They test food for compliance with food law and labelling law, for example its composition, additives, contaminants and microbiological quality (the bacteria in it).
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.3] They test samples submitted by members of the public, for a fee.
[hom-food-safety-s8.5.4] Their results help ensure that food sold to consumers is safe and wholesome, and they give the evidence needed for legal action.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.b] This Department looks after food at the farm end of the food chain, where animals and animal products come from. Its controls follow food from the farm to the shop.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.0] It monitors food safety in meat and meat products, milk and milk products, and eggs and egg products.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.1] It monitors the use of pesticides in food production, so that residues (traces left on or in food) do not reach consumers.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.2] It enforces EU controls on the import of animal-based foods from countries outside the EU.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.3] It is responsible for the registration and traceability of cattle, pigs and sheep, so that an animal can be traced if a problem is found.
[hom-food-safety-s8.6.4] It ensures correct hygiene procedures are followed from the dairy farm, to the dairy, through to the retailer and the consumer.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.b] Because the inspecting is shared between several bodies, they need common ways of tracking and removing unsafe food. The FSAI coordinates all of these.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.0] The FSAI and the official agencies work together on inspections, sampling, alerts, product withdrawals and recalls.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.1] Traceability allows an unsafe product to be traced back to its supplier (one step back) and forward to the businesses that received it (one step forward).
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.2] A product withdrawal removes a product from the supply chain, whereas a product recall also collects it from consumers by asking them to return it.
[hom-food-safety-s8.7.3] Rapid alerts pass warnings about unsafe food quickly to the agencies that must act on them, so that action can be taken before more people are harmed.
