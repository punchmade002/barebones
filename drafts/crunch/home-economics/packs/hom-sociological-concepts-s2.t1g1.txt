TARGET hom-sociological-concepts-s2: 2. Role, status, socio-economic groupings, social mobility
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.b] Everyone occupies positions in society, and each position carries a certain standing in other people's eyes. Sociologists call such a position a status, and one person holds several at the same time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.0] A status is a social position, such as mother, teacher, employee or citizen. One person holds several at once, for example daughter, student, goalkeeper and citizen, so a status describes one part of who a person is, not the whole person.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.1] An ascribed status is given at birth or without choice, for example sex, age, ethnic origin or the family into which a person is born.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.2] An achieved status is gained through effort, choice or ability, for example a qualification, a job or a marriage.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.3] Being a parent or a spouse is usually treated as achieved because it follows from choices a person makes. Social class at birth is ascribed, but class in adulthood is mostly achieved through occupation.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.4] A master status is a status that dominates how others see a person and outweighs the person's other statuses, for example being a serving prisoner.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.5] Status can be based on occupation, education, wealth, family background or achievement. In a society that rewards achievement a person can raise their status, which links directly to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.6] Societies that allocate status mainly by birth are described as closed; societies that allocate it mainly by achievement are described as open.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.b] A status tells you where a person stands; a role tells you how they are expected to behave there. Roles are learned through socialisation (the process of learning the ways of a society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.0] A role is the set of behaviours and expectations attached to a status: the rights, duties and behaviour considered appropriate for a person according to their position in society. Examples include teacher, student, adult, child and parent.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.1] Status and role go together. A teacher (the status) is expected to prepare lessons, mark work and treat pupils fairly (the role). Likewise, the role of a student includes attending class, completing homework and respecting the teacher.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.2] A person plays many roles at once, such as mother, employee, daughter and club member.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.3] Roles change over the life course: a person moves from the role of child to adolescent to adult, and each stage brings different expectations. They also change over time, for example gender roles within the family.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.4] Role conflict occurs when the demands of two roles are incompatible, so meeting one means failing the other.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.5] For example, a parent who is also a manager is asked to work late when a child is ill, or a person is expected to be both a loyal friend and an honest employee.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.6] Role overload occurs when a person has too many roles and cannot meet all the demands placed on them, which reduces leisure time and increases stress. For example, a working parent also cares for an elderly relative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.7] Role conflict and role overload are most common where people hold several demanding roles at once.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.8] A role model is a person whose behaviour others copy, such as a parent, teacher or sports star.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.b] People in a society are not all in the same economic and social position. Socio-economic groups sort people into categories according to that position, and they show that society is arranged in layers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.0] A socio-economic group (also called a social class) is a category of people with a similar economic and social position, defined mainly by occupation and linked to income, education, wealth, housing and lifestyle.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.1] Social stratification is the division of a society into layers, a hierarchy of unequal ranks. A socio-economic scheme is one way of describing those layers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.2] In Ireland the Central Statistics Office (CSO) groups the population into social classes for the census, using the occupation of each person. The groups run from professional workers, managerial and technical, non-manual, skilled manual and semi-skilled to unskilled, with a further group for all others gainfully occupied and unknown.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.3] Occupation, not education, decides which class a person is placed in, so a qualification affects the class only through the job it leads to. Level of education is one of the strongest factors separating socio-economic groups, because it governs access to skilled and professional work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.4] Examples of occupations, one per group: doctor or solicitor (professional); manager or technician (managerial and technical); factory worker (semi-skilled); labourer (unskilled). Electrician, plumber, carpenter or motor mechanic are skilled manual occupations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.5] Factors that determine socio-economic group are occupation, income, education level, wealth, housing and family background.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.6] Socio-economic group affects life chances, meaning a person's likelihood of access to opportunities such as educational attainment, health, housing and income. It also shapes housing choices and household expenditure.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.7] Higher socio-economic groups are more likely to complete second level and go on to third level, while lower groups face higher early school leaving and lower expectations. This affects equality of opportunity in education.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.b] People do not always stay in the socio-economic group they start in. Social mobility is the movement of an individual or a group between socio-economic groups, enabled by education, income and occupation. It does not mean geographical mobility, which is moving house or country.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.0] Upward mobility is movement to a higher socio-economic group, for example a labourer's child who becomes a teacher, or a skilled worker who becomes a manager after completing a degree.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.1] Downward mobility is movement to a lower socio-economic group, for example after the loss of a skilled job with no replacement at the same level.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.2] There are two time frames for measuring social mobility. Intergenerational mobility compares a person's position with the position of their parents.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.3] Intragenerational mobility, sometimes called career mobility, compares a person's position at different points in their own working life, for example from apprentice to manager.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.4] Structural mobility is movement caused by changes in the occupational structure, such as the growth of professional and service jobs and fewer farm jobs, so that many people move even when their relative positions do not change.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.5] Exchange mobility is movement in both directions that balances out, so that the overall pattern of relative positions stays the same.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.6] Factors that help people move up include education and qualifications, skills and training, promotion in a job, a good income, free second-level education, access to third level (grants, free fees schemes), equality legislation, ambition and family support. Education is the main route to upward mobility in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.7] Barriers to mobility include low family income, poor access to education and third-level education, early school leaving, discrimination, unemployment and living in an area with few employment opportunities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.8] Social mobility is more likely in an open society, where status is achieved, than in a closed society, where status is ascribed.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s1: 1. Society, culture, norms and mores, values
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.b] Sociology is the study of how people live together in groups and societies. Its starting point is the society itself, because human life is organised, not random.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.0] A society is a group of people who live in a defined territory and share a way of life, a set of institutions (the established organisations and arrangements of social life) and a sense of belonging.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.1] Social life in a society is organised: people depend on one another and follow common rules, which is why daily life is predictable rather than chaotic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.2] The term applies at different scales, such as Irish society, a small rural community or Western society, so always be clear which group you mean.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.b] Culture is what makes the way of life of one society different from another. It is much wider than the arts, because it covers almost everything a group of people share and hand on.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.0] Culture is the whole way of life of a society, passed from one generation to the next. It includes language, customs, beliefs, values, norms, mores, music, art, dress, food, religion, technology and objects. Irish culture is a good example.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.1] Culture is learned, not inherited biologically, so a child raised in any society picks up that society's way of life through socialisation (the process of learning how to live in a society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.2] Material culture is the physical objects a society makes and uses, such as clothing, tools, buildings and housing. Non-material culture is the intangible side: language, beliefs, values, norms, customs and rituals.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.3] Cultures differ between societies, and a culture changes over time. Differences show up in language, dress, food, religious practice, wedding customs and attitudes to age and gender roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.4] Members of a society share common symbols and meanings, though not every member values every symbol.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.5] Safe Irish examples of culture include Irish traditional music, the GAA, the Irish language as a cultural symbol, St Patrick's Day, Christmas customs and Irish wedding customs. Symbols like these carry shared meanings for those who value them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.6] A subculture is a smaller group within a society with some distinct values, norms or styles of its own, such as a youth music scene, a sporting club or a religious minority. It still shares the main culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.7] Ethnocentrism is judging another culture by the standards of your own culture. Cultural relativism is understanding a culture on its own terms, by its own standards, rather than by those of another culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.b] Every society has rules about how to behave, most of them unwritten. These rules come in different strengths, from everyday habits to rules with serious moral weight.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.0] A norm is an accepted, expected way of behaving in a given situation. It is a rule, mostly unwritten. The standard example is forming a queue; others are greeting people, keeping quiet in a library, turning up on time and dressing suitably for an interview or a funeral.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.1] Norms vary between cultures, between social settings and over time. For example, what is acceptable at a match is not acceptable in church, and what was normal fifty years ago may not be now. Norms about punctuality, personal space and greeting also differ from one culture to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.2] Mores (pronounced MOR-ays) are norms with strong moral weight, whose breach is seen as seriously wrong, such as the ban on theft, violence or disrespect to the dead. Breaking a more brings serious disapproval or punishment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.3] Customs, also called folkways, are everyday conventions and traditional ways of doing things, such as queueing, shaking hands, a birthday custom or a wedding custom. Breaking them is rude, not wrong, and brings only mild disapproval. This helps separate a norm from a more.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.4] Laws are norms that are written down and enforced by the State through officially appointed bodies such as the courts. Some mores become laws, such as the bans on theft and assault, but many norms are not laws, such as queueing.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.b] A norm only works if there is some pressure to follow it. Societies apply that pressure through sanctions, and the result is either conformity or deviance.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.0] A sanction is how a society enforces norms: a reward for conforming to a norm or a penalty for breaking one.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.1] Positive sanctions reward conformity, for example praise, a prize, an award or promotion.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.2] Negative sanctions punish breaking norms, for example a frown, ridicule, ostracism (being left out) or, at the formal end, fines and imprisonment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.3] Informal sanctions come from family, friends and neighbours. Formal sanctions come from official bodies such as the courts, schools and employers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.4] Conformity means following norms. Deviance is behaviour that breaks a norm.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.5] What counts as deviance depends on the society and the time, because norms vary between cultures and change over time, so the same behaviour may be deviant in one place and normal in another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.b] Values sit behind norms. A norm is the specific rule; a value is the broad belief that the rule expresses.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.0] Values are the beliefs and attitudes a society holds about what is right and wrong, good and bad, important and worth striving for.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.1] Values are general, abstract and long-lasting; norms are the specific rules that carry values into everyday behaviour. A value of respect for life lies behind the norm against violence, and a value of honesty lies behind the norm of not cheating in exams.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.2] Examples of values given in Irish and Western societies include family, education, honesty, fairness and equality, respect for others, religious belief, freedom, community and care for the vulnerable. These are examples of values, not a claim about every person.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.3] Core values are held strongly and change slowly; peripheral values are held less firmly and change more readily.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.4] Values are learned from the family first, then from school, peers, religion and the media. They differ between families, groups and generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.5] Values change over time, for example attitudes to marriage, gender equality and diversity. The changes feed through into new norms and laws.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.6] Values can conflict, such as personal freedom against duty to family, or career against family time. Individuals must balance them, and societies resolve such conflicts through norms and laws.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.b] The terms in this subsection are not separate lists. They fit together as one system, with culture as the umbrella.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.0] Culture is the umbrella. Values, norms, mores, customs, language, beliefs and symbols are its parts.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.1] Values are the broad beliefs. Norms and mores are the rules that put them into practice.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.2] Sanctions enforce norms and mores, and laws are the formal, written version of them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.3] A chain to remember: a value of honesty leads to a norm of not cheating, and cheating brings a sanction such as a teacher's disapproval or a penalty.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.b] Exams often ask you to explain four or five terms together, such as culture, norms, values and role. Each term is marked separately, so a clear layout earns the marks.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.0] Give each term its own line or short paragraph so that each one can be credited separately.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.1] Open with a one-sentence definition that uses the correct sociological meaning. Use the wording the schemes credit: way of life of a society for culture, accepted way of behaving for norms, and beliefs about what is right and wrong for values.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.2] Add the point that separates the term from the one most often confused with it, such as folkways from mores or norms from values.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.3] Give a specific example from Irish or everyday life for each term, such as queueing for a norm or Irish culture for culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.4] Where a term links to another in the set, say how: for example, values underpin norms, and norms are enforced by sanctions. A three-mark term answer is one definition point, one example or extension point and, where terms are related, a link.
