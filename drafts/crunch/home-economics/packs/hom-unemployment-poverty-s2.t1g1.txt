TARGET hom-unemployment-poverty-s2: 2. Causes of unemployment
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.b] These causes come from when and where work is available rather than from the workers themselves. Some jobs exist only at certain times of year, and some regions simply have far fewer jobs than others.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.0] Seasonal unemployment means jobs that depend on the time of year, for example in construction, farming, fishing, tourism and hotels, which slow down in winter because of weather and fewer visitors. It is predictable and temporary.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.1] Seasonal workers in these industries are often laid off each winter and re-employed in spring or summer, so they are out of work only in the off-season.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.2] Geographical unemployment means jobs are concentrated in certain regions, usually the larger cities and their commuter areas, so people living far from employment centres are more likely to be out of work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.3] Moving to where the jobs are costs money and means leaving family, which is why many people stay out of work in areas with few vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.4] Unemployment black spots are areas that lost their main employer, such as a factory closure, and keep high unemployment for many years.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.5] When a local firm closes, job losses spread to suppliers, shops and services that depended on its workers' spending, which adds to the unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.6] Outward migration (rural depopulation) is when young and skilled people leave rural areas to find work. Shops, schools, post offices and services then close, so fewer local jobs remain.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.0.7] Rural depopulation is both a cause and a result of unemployment: people leave because there is no work, and their leaving removes more jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.b] Changes in production methods and in the structure of industry reduce the number of jobs available, and workers whose skills are out of date are the first to lose out.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.0] Technical (technological) unemployment happens when machines, automation, computers and robots replace workers, for example automated production lines and robots in manufacturing, self-service checkouts, and self-service and online banking that reduces the number of bank tellers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.1] Difficulty adapting means workers whose skills no longer match industry needs struggle to find new work, particularly older workers and those with few qualifications, because retraining is hard.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.2] Ireland's economy has shifted away from primary industries such as agriculture and fishing, and secondary industries such as manufacturing, which employ fewer people than in the past, towards services, information technology and pharma/science, which need new skills.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.3] Globalisation (the global economy) means manufacturers may relocate production to countries with lower labour costs, removing jobs from Ireland.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.4] A world recession also cuts demand for Irish exports and so jobs, because Ireland is an open economy that depends on world trade and foreign investment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.1.5] Rising educational requirements mean many jobs now require qualifications, so people with few or none are excluded from applying.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.2.b] Firms hire workers to meet demand for what they sell. Unemployment rises when demand falls, because firms cut output and staff.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.2.0] A recession reduces consumer spending, so firms reduce production and make workers redundant. National and international recession was the main cause of the rise in unemployment after 2008, and economic growth reverses it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.2.1] Cheaper imported goods reduce demand for Irish-made products, and the firms producing them cut jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.2.2] Online shopping from abroad and cheaper foreign holidays move spending away from Irish shops and tourism businesses, so those businesses lose sales and jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.2.3] Unemployment reduces household income, which reduces spending further and causes more job losses in local shops and services. This is a downward spiral.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.2.4] High wage and other costs can reduce the competitiveness of Irish firms against rivals abroad, leading to lost sales and job losses. High wages are one factor, not the only cause.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.3.b] Residual unemployment is the hard core of people who stay out of work even when the economy is growing and vacancies exist.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.3.0] Causes include poor skills or education, a long time out of work, ill health or disability, age, discrimination, and living in an area with few jobs or far from jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.3.1] Residual unemployment persists during economic growth, so growth alone does not bring unemployment to zero.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.3.2] Long-term unemployed people make up much of the residual group, because time out of work makes employers less willing to hire them.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.b] Some groups face barriers in addition to the general level of demand for labour. Each group has its own typical reason for higher unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.0] Travellers and ethnic minorities can face discrimination in recruitment and may have lower qualifications.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.1] Asylum seekers may face restrictions on the right to work while their application is decided. Refugees and migrants may face language barriers and non-recognition of foreign qualifications.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.2] People with disabilities or long-term ill health may be excluded because employers doubt their ability to do the work, and access and transport barriers can also keep them out.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.3] Homeless people lack an address, clothing or hygiene facilities, which makes applying for and keeping a job very difficult.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.4] People with criminal records face employers' mistrust.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.5] Homeless people, people with criminal records and early school leavers face employers who are unaware of their abilities, and often lack skills or qualifications; early school leavers may lack qualifications and literacy.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.6] Long-term unemployed people may have out-of-date skills after time out of work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s2.4.7] Residents of unemployment black spots face few local vacancies and may need to travel or move to find work.

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s1: 1. Defining unemployment
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.b] Being out of work is not the same as being unemployed. Unemployment is being without paid work while being willing and able to take up work, and statisticians apply a strict three-part test.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.0] Three conditions must all be met: the person has no paid employment, is available to start work, and is actively seeking work. Missing any one of them means the person is not counted as unemployed.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.1] Paid work is the test, so unpaid work such as volunteering or caring at home does not make a person employed.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.2] Available for work: the person could start work within a short period, normally within two weeks, so someone who could not take a job for months is not counted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.3] Actively seeking work: the person has taken specific steps to find work, such as applying for posts, answering advertisements, contacting employers or registering with Intreo, the Department of Social Protection's public employment service. Registering is one such step, not proof on its own.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.4] Students, people in full-time home duties, retired people and those unable to work through illness or disability are not counted as unemployed, because they are not available for work or are not seeking it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.0.5] A person in part-time work who wants more hours is counted as employed in the standard measure (such a person is underemployed).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.b] To judge how serious unemployment is, a count of unemployed people has to be set against the number of people who could be working. That is what the unemployment rate does.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.0] The labour force is the number of people in employment plus the number of unemployed people.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.1] The unemployment rate is the number of unemployed people divided by the labour force, multiplied by 100.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.2] A rate is more useful than a raw total when comparing periods or areas, because the size of the labour force changes over time.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.3] The Central Statistics Office (CSO) measures unemployment in Ireland using the definition set by the International Labour Organization (ILO). Its Labour Force Survey is a household survey that gives the standardised unemployment rate, the official measure, published monthly and quoted seasonally adjusted.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.1.4] The Live Register is the Department of Social Protection's count of people claiming jobseeker payments (signing on), mainly Jobseeker's Benefit and Jobseeker's Allowance. It includes some part-time, casual and seasonal workers, so it is not the official unemployment figure.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.b] Unemployment is also described by how long it lasts, because a short gap between jobs is a very different problem from years without work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.0] Short-term unemployment is a brief spell out of work, usually between jobs or after leaving education.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.1] Frictional unemployment is short-term unemployment caused by the normal time workers take to move from one job to another, for example while applications are made and interviews arranged.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.2] Long-term unemployment is being out of work for one year or more.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.3] Long-term unemployment is harder to escape because skills, habits and confidence erode with time, which makes re-entry to work harder.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.2.4] Young people have tended to have higher rates of unemployment than older workers, especially in downturns.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.b] The extent of unemployment means how many people are unemployed, as a rate and as a number, and how that changes over time. It follows the economic cycle: it rises in recession and falls in growth.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.0] The Labour Force Survey, a household survey by the Central Statistics Office, produces the standardised unemployment rate. The Live Register, kept by the Department of Social Protection, counts people claiming jobseeker payments, including some part-time workers, so it is not the standard measure.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.1] Unemployment was low in the boom years of the 2000s, then rose sharply after the economic downturn that began in 2008, with construction and related professions worst affected.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.2] Retail and service industries also shed jobs during the downturn as consumer spending fell.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.3] Unemployment peaked at roughly 15% in the early 2010s and then fell steadily as the economy recovered and jobs returned in construction, retail, services and multinational firms.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.4] Emigration rose during the downturn. It held the measured rate down, because people who left are no longer in the labour force, so the rate alone understates the damage.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.5] Unemployment is not spread evenly. It is higher among young people and among people with few or no qualifications, and rates differ between regions.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.6] Unemployment black spots, areas that lost their main employer, tend to keep higher rates for longer than areas with plenty of work. Long-term unemployment forms a hard core that remains when the overall rate falls.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s1.3.7] The COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 caused a sudden temporary rise in joblessness, and the State paid a temporary pandemic unemployment payment.

CANDIDATES hom-work-leisure-s2: 2. Changes in patterns of work
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.b] New technology changes how work is organised, how many workers are needed and which skills are in demand. Some effects are gains for workers and some are losses, and exam answers need both.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.0] Automation (machines and robots taking over repetitive production and processing tasks) means fewer workers are needed for the same output, because the machines do the routine work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.1] Increased output with fewer workers reduces working hours in some industries, which gives people more leisure time, but it also removes jobs at the lower-skilled end.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.2] Computers and electronic communication change office work. They create new skilled jobs to design, install, maintain and operate the technology, so jobs shift towards programming, supervising and maintaining.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.3] Electronic communication allows home working (teleworking), which suits some people. The downside is that it makes it harder to switch off from work, and it reduces face-to-face social contact at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.4] Technology also makes work safer and cleaner in some settings, because dangerous and dirty tasks are carried out by machines. This means fewer injuries and healthier workplaces.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.5] Workers whose skills become out of date, or who lose unskilled and manual jobs, need retraining. Companies can also relocate production to low-cost countries, which removes jobs at home.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.6] Technology has also changed office and service work: banking, shopping and booking online mean fewer counter and clerical jobs and more technical ones.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.b] Employment has moved from extracting and manufacturing towards services. To describe the shift you first need to know the three kinds of industry.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.0] Primary industry extracts resources from the land and sea: agriculture, fishing, forestry and mining or quarrying (such as peat).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.1] Secondary industry manufactures and processes primary raw materials into finished goods, for example food processing, clothing, engineering and construction.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.2] Tertiary (service) industry provides services such as tourism, leisure, healthcare, transport, media, retail, education, banking and finance, hospitality and IT. Note that tertiary means services, not third-level education.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.3] Automation and mechanisation reduced the number of manufacturing and farming jobs because machines do more of the work. Service industries expanded and absorbed many workers as incomes rose and demand for services grew.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.4] Companies relocated production to lower-cost countries, causing job losses in manufacturing in Ireland. Growth of tourism, finance and technology firms added service jobs.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.5] The effects on people were that manual and unskilled workers were displaced and rural areas lost jobs, while urban areas gained service jobs. Service work brought more part-time and shift work and a new demand for qualified workers.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.6] Setting: compare Ireland's current occupational structure with that of fifty years ago, noting the shift from farming, factory and other manual work to service and professional work. Describe the pattern without figures.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.b] As jobs have shifted towards services and technology, employers increasingly expect higher qualifications. Education has become the way into work and the way to move up within it.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.0] Service and technology jobs need literacy, numeracy, computing and communication skills, which are developed through education.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.1] Employers use qualifications to select between applicants, so a larger share of applicants now hold them. The Leaving Certificate is the minimum for many jobs, and many need a further education or higher qualification.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.2] Qualifications widen employment options and allow career advancement, because they show an employer what a worker can do.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.3] Early school leavers and unskilled workers find it difficult to acquire work, as many jobs require a minimum level of education. They are most at risk of unemployment and low pay.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.4] Continuing education, such as evening courses, distance learning and part-time courses, allows adults to gain qualifications, keep pace with technology, improve promotion prospects or change career.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.5] Upskilling programmes (courses that build on skills a worker already has) also let adults keep their skills up to date.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.6] Reskilling (training in new skills) allows workers whose jobs have disappeared to move into new occupations.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.b] Many more women, including married women and mothers, are now in paid work than in the middle of the twentieth century. The causes are social and economic, and each one needs a short reason.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.0] Women are better educated than in the past, and qualifications open access to professional and skilled work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.1] Equality legislation removed barriers: the Civil Service (Employment of Married Women) Act 1973 removed the marriage bar (the rule forcing married women to leave civil service jobs).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.2] The Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 first legislated for equal pay for equal work. Both Acts are now covered by the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.3] Income from paid work gives women financial independence, self-esteem, status and a stronger personal identity, as well as social contact outside the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.4] The growth of service industries and part-time work created many jobs that suited women's skills and flexible hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.5] Rising living, housing and child care costs, and the need for a second income, encourage women into paid employment. More marital breakdown and lone parenthood also make earning necessary.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.6] Flexible hours, job-sharing, term-time work, maternity and parental leave and career breaks make it possible to combine paid work with home and family responsibilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.7] More child care facilities, labour-saving appliances and smaller families free up women's time, and changing attitudes make work more accepted. The result is financial independence for women and more dual-earner families (both parents earning).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.b] Pay, safety and time off have all improved, which made paid work more attractive. These improvements also enabled women to take part in the workforce more fully.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.0] Better pay, including the introduction of the minimum wage, plus pension entitlement and paid holiday arrangements, make paid work more attractive.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.1] Statutory protections for workers (those set down in law) cover health and safety, pay and working time. Employment is protected by legislation, as are equal pay and equality law.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.2] Safer and cleaner workplaces, made possible by technology, reduce risk of injury and illness. Working hours are also shorter than in the past.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.3] Statutory leave includes maternity leave, parental leave and force majeure leave (urgent family reasons). Parental leave and career breaks let workers take time away for family reasons and return to their job.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.4] Facilities for working parents, such as crèches and breastfeeding breaks, make it easier to stay in work after having a child.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.5] Improved conditions have enabled many women to return to the workforce after raising children, because work no longer has to be given up for family life.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.b] Working time is now arranged in many ways other than the traditional full-time, fixed-hours week. These arrangements let workers fit paid work around family life.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.0] Flexitime: employees choose their start and finish times within agreed core hours, when everyone must be at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.1] Job-sharing: two people share one full-time post, dividing the hours, pay and duties between them.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.2] Part-time and term-time working: reduced hours, or work arranged around the school year, which suits parents of school-age children.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.3] Compressed or reduced working weeks, such as working longer days across fewer days, and teleworking: working from home or another remote location using electronic communication.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.4] Career breaks: an agreed period of unpaid leave, after which the worker returns to their job.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.5] Parental leave: under the Parental Leave Act 1998 (as amended), parents are entitled to unpaid leave to look after a young child, currently 26 weeks per child for a child under 12 (longer if the child has a disability or long-term illness).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.6] It is taken in one block or in agreed blocks, and it is not paid by the employer by right.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.7] Related leaves exist under other laws: maternity leave (Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004), paternity leave (Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016), parent's leave (Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019), carer's leave (Carer's Leave Act 2001) and force majeure leave.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.b] The law now gives employees a right to ask for flexible hours or to work remotely. The employer does not have to agree, but must handle the request fairly.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.0] The Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 provides these rights, and the flexible and remote working provisions commenced on 6 March 2024.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.1] Parents and carers of children up to 12 (or up to 16 if the child is disabled or long-term ill), and carers, can request flexible working. Any employee can request remote working.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.2] The employer must consider the request and reply in writing within set time limits, giving reasons for any refusal, so workers get a clear answer.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.3] The Act also provides paid domestic-violence leave (5 days, from November 2023) and unpaid medical care leave (from 3 July 2023).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.b] Taken together, the changes in technology, industry, education, women's work and working hours affect individuals and families in both helpful and difficult ways.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.0] Workers displaced from manufacturing may need retraining before they can find work in services, so the availability of work changes for individuals.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.1] Flexible working helps parents plan childcare and family life more smoothly. It means more time with children, less stress and an easier balance of work and family.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.2] Blurred boundaries between work and home can increase stress and make it harder to switch off.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.3] Flexible working can also mean lower income, slower promotion, a shortfall in pension and resentment from colleagues.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.4] Families in which both parents work gain income but may face role overload and role conflict, because family tasks still have to be done.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.5] Employers gain access to a wider, more skilled labour pool and can keep staff by offering flexible arrangements.
