TARGET hom-dairy-eggs-s1: 1. Milk: nutritional significance and contribution to the diet
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.b] Milk is a complete food for young mammals, meaning it supports a growing baby animal, and a useful source of most nutrients for people. It is widely available, so it is a staple of the Irish diet.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.0] Water makes up about 87% of milk, so a glass of milk contributes to hydration as well as supplying nutrients.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.1] Protein makes up about 3.5% of whole milk. It is mainly casein, with whey proteins making up the rest, and it is high biological value (HBV) protein, which means it contains all the essential amino acids (the ones the body cannot make).
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.2] Protein from milk is used for growth and repair, and for making hormones and enzymes.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.3] Fat in whole milk is about 3.5 to 4%, mostly saturated (fat that is solid at room temperature and raises cholesterol). It is held in small globules, so it is easily digested, and cholesterol is present.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.4] The fat carries the fat-soluble vitamins A and D, so these are highest in whole milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.5] Lactose is the milk sugar, a disaccharide (a double sugar) at about 4 to 5%. It is the only significant carbohydrate in milk, it supplies energy, and it aids the absorption of calcium. Milk contains almost no starch.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.6] Minerals: calcium and phosphorus are present in useful amounts for bones and teeth, with traces of magnesium, potassium and sodium. Milk is low in iron.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.7] Vitamins: A and D, and the B group vitamins, including riboflavin (B2) and B12. Skimmed milk keeps the B vitamins but loses A and D with the fat.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.0.8] Milk supplies energy from lactose and fat, so the more fat a milk contains, the more energy it gives.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.b] Milk is a good source of protein and calcium, but it is not a complete diet for an adult. Knowing what it lacks explains why it must be balanced with other foods.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.0] Milk contains no vitamin C, so it cannot be the only source of this vitamin.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.1] Milk contains no fibre, so it does not help bowel movement the way cereals, fruit and vegetables do.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.2] Milk contains very little starch, so its carbohydrate is almost all lactose.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.1.3] Milk is low in iron, so it should not be relied on to prevent iron deficiency.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.b] Nutritive value means the nutrients a food contains and what each one does in the body. A good answer names the nutrient, its function and the milk fact.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.0] Protein (HBV) is for growth and repair, and fat and lactose give energy.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.1] Calcium and phosphorus build and maintain bones and teeth, and vitamins A, D and the B group support the body's functioning, while water gives hydration.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.2.2] Milk lacks vitamin C, fibre and iron, so a full answer also states what it does not provide.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.b] Dietetic value means how milk suits different people and different diets. Each point is strongest when it is given with its reason.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.0] Protein supports growth and repair in children, adolescents and pregnant women, who need extra for their body's needs.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.1] Calcium and phosphorus support bone and tooth development and maintenance, and are needed by older people and nursing mothers.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.2] Lactose and fat give energy for an active lifestyle.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.3] Milk is easily digested, so it suits invalid and elderly diets, and convalescents.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.4] Low-fat milks suit low-cholesterol and weight-watching diets, because less fat means less saturated fat and energy.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.5] Milk is versatile, used in drinks, cereals, sauces, soups, puddings and baking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.6] Milk is inexpensive, widely available and quick to use with no preparation, and nothing is wasted.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.3.7] Milk can be fortified (have nutrients added) to add nutrients that are otherwise low.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.0] Milk is not suitable as the only food, because it has no vitamin C or fibre and little iron.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.1] Whole milk is high in saturated fat, so it is not ideal for people cutting cholesterol or calories.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.2] Lactose intolerance means a person lacks the enzyme lactase and cannot digest lactose, the sugar in milk. Lactose-free milk exists for them.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.4.3] Milk allergy is a different condition, a reaction to the milk protein.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.5.2] Lower-fat choices are advised, and cheese in small amounts.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.0] Milk can be used on cereal or porridge, in smoothies and milky drinks, in soups, sauces and batters, and in puddings and baking.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.6.1] Yoghurt can be eaten as a snack, on cereal, in smoothies, or used as a dip or dressing.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.7.6] Milk can be used in young children's diets in cereals, sauces, soups and puddings to add protein and calcium, and it can be fortified.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.0] Whole milk is full fat, about 3.5 to 4% fat, with the most energy and vitamin A of the common types, and is the most popular type.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.2] Semi-skimmed milk has slightly more protein, carbohydrate and calcium per 100 ml, so it is the milk type higher in calcium, protein and carbohydrate than whole milk.
[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.3] Skimmed milk has 0.1 to 0.4% fat. Most of the fat is removed along with vitamins A and D, so it suits low-fat and low-cholesterol diets, while the protein, lactose and calcium remain.

[hom-dairy-eggs-s1.8.5] Semi-skimmed and skimmed milk are not fortified unless the label says so.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s4: 4. Sources
[hom-proteins-s4.0.b] Protein comes from both animal and plant foods, so everyone can meet their needs whatever they eat. Exam answers earn marks for naming a variety of foods from each group.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.0] Animal protein sources are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Cheese and yoghurt belong in the list because they are made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.1] Plant protein sources are soya beans and soya products, pulses (lentils, peas and beans), cereals (wheat, rice, maize and oats), nuts and seeds.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.2] Cereals such as wheat, rice and maize supply protein as well as carbohydrate, so everyday foods like wheat, bread and pasta are plant protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.3] Potatoes and vegetables contain only small amounts of protein, so they are not counted as main protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.b] Albumin is the protein most people meet first, in the white of an egg. It is a good example of a protein that is tied to one particular food.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.0] Albumin is the main protein of egg white (ovalbumin), and it is also found in lean meat.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.1] Lactalbumin, a related albumin, is found in the whey of milk, the watery part left when the casein is removed.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.2] Albumin is a soluble globular protein. It dissolves in water and coagulates (sets) when heated, which is why raw egg white turns solid and white on cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.b] Casein is the main protein of milk, so it follows milk into every food made from milk. Cheese-making depends on changing it.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.0] Casein (the older term is caseinogen) is the main protein of milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.1] Casein is present in cheese and yoghurt, the products made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.2] Casein is coagulated by the enzyme rennin, which is the first step in making cheese because it forms the curd. Acid also coagulates it, which is why milk curdles when it sours.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.b] Gelatine is the protein behind jelly, but it is not found in raw meat. It is made when another protein, collagen, is heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.0] Gelatine is not present in raw meat; it forms when collagen from animal bones, skin and connective tissue is heated in moisture.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.1] Gelatine sets to a gel when it cools, so it is used in jelly, aspic, gelatine-set mousses and desserts, marshmallows and some sweets.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.2] Gelatine comes from animal sources, so foods set with it are not vegetarian.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.b] Gluten is the protein that gives wheat bread its structure. It does not exist in dry flour; it is formed when the flour is wet.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.0] Gluten is the protein of wheat and wheat flour.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.1] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin, both already present in the flour, are mixed with water. Flour itself contains gliadin and glutenin, not formed gluten.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.2] Gluten is elastic and stretchy, and kneading develops it. It gives bread dough its elastic, stretchy texture and traps the carbon dioxide made by yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.3] Foods that depend on wheat gluten include bread, pasta, cakes, pastry and other flour products.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.b] Each of these proteins is tied to a food or food group. Pairing every named protein with one food is the quickest way to answer a naming question.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.0] Albumin: found in egg white and meat, and it sets when heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.1] Casein: found in milk, cheese and yoghurt, and it is coagulated by rennin.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.2] Gelatine: found in set desserts such as jelly, and it is made from collagen.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.3] Gluten: found in wheat flour and bread, and it traps gas in dough.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.b] Meat and fish are made of muscle and connective tissue, and each contains its own proteins. Higher Level questions ask you to name them.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they contract to move the muscle. They are found in the flesh of meat and fish.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.1] Collagen is the main protein of connective tissue and is found in meat, fish and bones.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.2] Collagen in meat and fish is the source of gelatine when it is heated in moisture; this is why cheap tough cuts of meat tenderise on slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.3] Elastin in meat and globulin in egg are also acceptable answers when asked for proteins in meat or egg.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s4: 4. Mineral elements
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.b] Minerals are the part of food that is not made by living things at all. The body needs them in small amounts to build structures, run processes and make enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.0] Minerals are inorganic elements (not made by living things) that the body cannot make, so they must be obtained from food. They build body structures, regulate body processes and form parts of enzymes and hormones.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.1] Major (macro) minerals are needed in larger amounts, above 100 mg a day. They are calcium, phosphorus, potassium, sodium, chloride, magnesium and sulphur.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.2] Minerals are not destroyed by cooking, because they are elements. They can, however, be lost into cooking water and in the trimming of food. For example, boiling vegetables in a lot of water washes minerals out.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily amount) is the daily intake that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, so some groups need more than others.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.0.4] Phosphorus works with calcium to harden bones and teeth and forms part of ATP (the cell's energy carrier) and cell membranes. Sources are milk, cheese, meat, fish, eggs, wholegrains and nuts, and deficiency is rare.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.b] Calcium is the most important mineral for the skeleton, but it also keeps blood, muscles and nerves working. Because bone is built in childhood and lost in later life, some groups need more of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.0] Milk, cheese and yoghurt are the richest dietary sources of calcium. This is why dairy foods matter for bone health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.1] Canned sardines and canned salmon eaten with their bones, green leafy vegetables such as kale, broccoli and cabbage, calcium-set tofu, fortified plant milks and fortified breakfast cereals are further sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.2] Calcium works with phosphorus to form the mineral salts that give bones and teeth their hardness. This is why a diet low in calcium leaves bones weak.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.3] Calcium is needed for blood clotting, normal muscle contraction (including the heartbeat) and nerve impulse transmission. It also activates some enzymes and helps regulate blood pressure.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.4] Rickets in children (soft, bent bones and poor teeth) and osteomalacia in adults result from too little calcium, phosphorus or vitamin D.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.5] Osteoporosis, which develops over years, makes bones thin, porous, brittle and prone to fracture, with loss of height and a stooped back. Other effects of low calcium are tooth decay, slow clotting, and muscle cramps (tetany) and poor nerve function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.6] Osteoporosis risk is raised by low calcium and vitamin D intake, lack of weight-bearing exercise, smoking, alcohol, the fall in oestrogen after menopause and family history.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.1.7] Need is highest when bone is being built (childhood, teenage years, pregnancy, breastfeeding) and when it is lost (women after menopause). Bone density peaks in the late teens and twenties, so teenage intake matters.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.b] Iron is needed to carry oxygen, so a shortage leaves the whole body short of energy. It comes in two forms, and the form decides how easily the body absorbs it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.0] Haem iron comes from animal foods and is absorbed easily. Sources are red meat, liver and offal, kidney, black pudding, poultry and oily fish.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.1] Non-haem iron comes from plant foods and eggs and is absorbed less easily. Sources are eggs, wholegrain and fortified cereals, wholemeal bread, pulses, dark green leafy vegetables, dried apricots and other dried fruit, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.2] Iron is an essential part of haemoglobin, the red protein in red blood cells that carries oxygen from the lungs around the body to all cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.3] Iron is part of myoglobin, which stores oxygen in muscle cells, and part of enzymes that release energy from food. It also supports immunity and concentration. This is why a shortage leaves people tired.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.4] Iron deficiency causes anaemia (too few red blood cells or too little haemoglobin). Effects are tiredness, pale skin, breathlessness, dizziness, irritability, poor concentration, feeling cold and reduced resistance to infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.5] Causes of iron deficiency are a diet low in iron or poor in vitamin C, blood loss (menstruation, ulcers, operations), high needs in growth spurts, pregnancy and infancy, and poor absorption.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.6] Corrective measures are eating more iron-rich foods together with vitamin C, avoiding tea with meals, and taking an iron supplement on medical advice. This is because vitamin C improves absorption and tea blocks it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.2.7] Groups at risk are teenage girls, menstruating and pregnant women, infants and toddlers, people with heavy blood loss, and vegetarians and vegans, who eat only non-haem iron. Children are at risk because of fast growth and low-iron foods. For example, vegetarians miss out on the more easily absorbed haem form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.b] Trace minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, yet the body cannot do without them. Each has a job that no other mineral can do.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.0] Trace (micro) minerals are needed in only tiny amounts, measured in milligrams or micrograms a day, and the diet must supply them. They are tiny in amount yet essential. For example, zinc and iodine are needed in milligrams or micrograms.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.1] The trace minerals are iron, zinc, iodine, fluoride, copper, selenium, manganese, chromium and cobalt. Iron is a trace mineral by the amount needed, but it is studied in detail at Ordinary Level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.3.2] Iron, zinc and iodine each play a specific role in the body that the other minerals cannot replace. Zinc and iodine are set out in the blocks that follow.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.b] Zinc is a trace mineral that works inside enzymes, so it affects growth, healing and defence against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.0] Zinc sources include oysters and other shellfish, red meat, poultry, liver, dairy products, eggs, wholegrain cereals, pulses, nuts and seeds.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.1] Zinc is a component of many enzymes and supports growth and cell division, which is why a shortage slows growth in children.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.2] Zinc supports wound healing and a healthy immune system. This is because new cells must be made to repair the wound.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.3] Zinc contributes to a normal sense of taste and smell, and to healthy skin and hair and reproductive health.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.4.4] Deficiency causes poor growth, delayed wound healing, dry flaky skin and rashes, loss of taste and appetite, reduced immunity and hair loss.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.b] Iodine is needed to make a hormone that sets the body's pace. Without it the thyroid gland cannot do its job.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.0] Iodine sources include sea fish and shellfish, seaweed, milk and dairy products and eggs. Iodised salt also exists as a source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.1] Iodine is part of thyroxine, the hormone from the thyroid gland that controls the rate of metabolism (and growth and brain development).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.2] Deficiency causes goitre, a swelling of the thyroid gland in the neck as it works harder to make more thyroxine. This is because the gland enlarges as it works harder.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.5.3] Iodine deficiency causes tiredness and sluggishness (lethargy) and weight gain in adults. It causes delayed mental and physical development in children, and in babies of deficient mothers.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.b] Potassium and sodium work as a pair, one inside cells and one outside, to keep fluid, nerves and muscles balanced.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.0] Potassium sources include bananas, potatoes eaten in their skins, tomatoes, dried fruit, orange juice, pulses, green and leafy vegetables, milk, meat, fish and nuts.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.1] Potassium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) inside cells and, with sodium, helps regulate fluid balance in and around cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.2] Potassium is needed to transmit nerve impulses and for muscle contraction, including the heartbeat. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.3] Potassium works with sodium to regulate blood pressure, and a high potassium intake helps lower it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.6.4] Deficiency (hypokalaemia) causes muscle weakness, cramps, an irregular heartbeat and tiredness. It is usually caused by severe vomiting, diarrhoea or some medicines rather than by normal diets.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.b] Sodium is essential, and the problem in Ireland is eating too much of it, not too little. Most of it comes from salt, which is sodium chloride.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.0] Sodium is mainly consumed as salt. Table salt, cured and smoked meats and fish such as bacon and ham, cheese, bread, crisps and snack foods, processed and ready meals, sauces, stock cubes and canned foods supply most of it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.1] Fresh, unprocessed foods contain only small amounts of naturally occurring sodium. This is why processed foods, such as bacon and ready meals, are the main source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.2] Sodium is the main positive ion (electrolyte) outside cells and, with potassium, regulates fluid balance and blood volume.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.3] Sodium is needed to transmit nerve impulses, for muscle contraction and for enzyme function. It helps the small intestine absorb glucose and some amino acids. This is why a shortage causes cramps.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.4] Excess sodium raises blood pressure (hypertension), which increases the risk of stroke, heart disease and kidney problems. Most people in Ireland eat more salt than advised, mostly from processed foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.5] To cut salt, cook from fresh food, do not add salt at the table, read labels (salt is on the nutrition declaration) and choose low-salt products.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.6] The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) began a voluntary Salt Reduction Programme in 2003 to cut salt in processed food. Later reformulation work continues under the FSAI-Healthy Ireland Food Reformulation Task Force.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s4.7.7] Deficiency is uncommon but causes muscle cramps, headache, nausea and confusion. It can follow heavy sweating, vomiting or diarrhoea.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s4: 4. Dietary guidelines
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.b] Dietary guidelines are official advice, based on scientific evidence, on the types and amounts of food that keep a population healthy. They turn nutrition science into practical advice for the general healthy population, and are revised as evidence changes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.0] Evidence comes from nutrition research, national food consumption surveys (which record what people actually eat) and health data on diet-related diseases.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.1] Expert committees review this evidence and turn it into practical advice on foods rather than nutrients, setting the types of food to eat and the amounts to limit. The guidelines are reviewed as new evidence appears.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.2] The Department of Health publishes the healthy eating guidelines for Ireland and the Food Pyramid, which is one of the tools used to explain them (the current pyramid is called 'Healthy Food for Life', 2016).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.3] The HSE supports the guidelines, and the Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) and safefood are other Irish bodies in this field.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.4] The guidelines are written for the general healthy population, so they are not a substitute for advice for individuals with special medical needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.5] The aims are to make sure people get enough nutrients, to prevent deficiency, and to reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure, stroke, some cancers and osteoporosis (weak, brittle bones).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.0.6] One consistent message also helps consumers, schools and food planners, because everyone is working from the same advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.b] The Irish guidelines can be given as about eight principles. Together they say which foods to eat most, which to eat in moderation and which to keep small.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.0] Eat plenty of vegetables, salad and fruit: about five to seven portions a day, spread across meals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.1] Base meals on starchy foods, choosing wholegrain bread, cereals, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.2] Include protein from lean meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans, lentils and nuts, with fish eaten regularly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.3] Choose milk, yoghurt and cheese in lower-fat forms, as they supply calcium and HBV protein (high biological value protein, which contains all the essential amino acids).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.4] Use fats and oils sparingly, and prefer unsaturated oils such as rapeseed and olive oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.5] Limit foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt, which are not needed for health and should be occasional.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.6] Drink water as the main drink, and keep alcohol within recommended limits.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.1.7] Eat regular meals and prepare food with methods that add little fat, such as steaming, grilling and baking.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.b] Each guideline is linked to a health benefit or a health risk. Examiners want the guideline and its reason together.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.0] Fruit and vegetables supply fibre, vitamins, minerals and antioxidants (substances that protect cells from damage), which protect against chronic disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.1] Wholegrain starchy foods give more fibre and nutrients than refined white flour products, and promote bowel health because fibre helps prevent constipation and bowel disorders.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.2] Saturated fat raises blood cholesterol, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.3] Excess salt is linked to high blood pressure, which increases the risk of stroke and heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.2.4] Sugar adds energy without nutrients, contributes to obesity, and causes tooth decay.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.b] An RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance) is a planning value: the amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of nearly all healthy people in a group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.0] An RDA is set for each nutrient and differs by age, sex and life stage, such as growth, pregnancy, lactation (breastfeeding) or adolescence.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.1] RDAs are used to plan menus and to check that a diet supplies enough of each nutrient over the day, by comparing the day's nutrient total with the RDA. They also guide food-labelling and supplement standards and institutions such as schools and hospitals.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.2] An RDA is a guide for a group; it is not a precise target for an individual.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.3] Intake far above the RDA does not give extra benefit and can be harmful for some nutrients, so supplements should be used with care.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.3.4] Nutrients that are often low in Irish diets, such as iron and calcium, are the ones to check against the RDA when planning a menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.b] A food composition table is a reference book of food values. It lets you turn a menu into numbers, so you can check it against the RDAs and the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.0] A food composition table gives the nutrient and energy content of a food, usually per 100 g of edible food. Irish tables are produced from national food analysis.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.1] Tables state whether values are for raw or cooked food, so the matching version must be used because cooking changes the nutrient content.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.2] Method: for each food, work out the grams eaten, divide by 100, then multiply by the value per 100 g.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.3] Example (sample data): 150 g of a food with 3 g fibre per 100 g supplies 150 / 100 x 3 = 4.5 g fibre.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.4.4] Add the results for all foods in the menu to give the day's total for each nutrient and for energy, then compare the total with the RDA. Where a total is low or high, adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.b] A computer nutrition program does the same job as a food composition table, but automatically. It is quick to use, though it is only as good as the information put in.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.0] Nutrition programs contain a built-in food composition database; the user enters each food and the quantity eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.1] The program totals the energy and nutrients for the day and compares them with the guidelines or RDAs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.2] Check that the portion size entered matches the food as eaten, and that the correct cooking method is selected, because the results depend on correct portions and the right cooking state.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.3] A program shows which nutrients are low or high, which helps the planner adjust the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.5.4] Advantages are that it is fast and accurate and holds many foods and recipes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.b] The Food Pyramid turns the healthy eating guidelines into the types and relative amounts of food needed each day. Foods with similar nutrients are grouped on shelves, with the base shelf eaten most and the top shelf least.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.0] The current Irish pyramid has six shelves. At the base are vegetables, salad and fruit, with wholemeal cereals and breads, potatoes, pasta and rice above them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.1] Next come milk, yogurt and cheese, then meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts (including lean choices), then a small shelf of fats, spreads and oils, to be used sparingly.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.2] The top shelf holds foods and drinks high in fat, sugar and salt. These are not needed for health, have no recommended servings, and should be occasional. Water is the main drink.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.3] Servings: five to seven servings of vegetables, salad and fruit a day, three servings of milk, yogurt and cheese (five for ages 9 to 18), and three to five servings of wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.4] The number and size of servings from the other shelves are adjusted for age, sex, health, activity level and life stage, for example children, teenagers, pregnant and breastfeeding women and older adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.5] To plan meals, take most foods from the large lower shelves, some from the middle and very little from the top, and vary the foods within each shelf. Include vegetables or fruit at every meal, and choose wholemeal and lower-fat versions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.6] Spread the choices across breakfast, lunch, dinner and snacks so the day covers every shelf and matches the person's portions. This supports energy balance and prevents both deficiency and excess.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.7] The pyramid guides what to eat and roughly how much, not an exact diet, and it does not replace advice for medical conditions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.6.8] Diagram: draw the Food Pyramid, label each shelf with the main foods on it, and mark the base shelf and the top shelf.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.b] A serving (portion) is a standard measure of food used to plan and count intake against the pyramid. It makes diets easy to compare, because everyone counts in the same units.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.0] A portion is a standard amount of food used for planning, counting and comparing diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.1] One piece of fruit, such as an apple, or one serving of vegetables counts as one portion of fruit or vegetables.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.2] One glass of milk counts as one portion of milk, yoghurt or cheese in the dairy group.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.3] Portion size should be adjusted to the person's age and activity: teenagers and active adults need larger portions from the starchy foods shelf than older, less active adults.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.7.4] Serving sizes and numbers rise for growing teenagers and active people and fall for older, less active adults. Pregnant and breastfeeding women need extra servings of some groups.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.b] Food supplements are concentrates of important nutrients. They can fill a gap in a diet, but they cannot do everything that food does.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.0] Food supplements are concentrates of nutrients such as vitamins, minerals, omega-3 fatty acids (polyunsaturated fatty acids) and amino acids, sold in tablet, capsule or powder form.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.1] They may be used to fill gaps where diet is restricted, where allergies or intolerances exclude foods, or where a person has special needs.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.2] Folic acid prevents neural tube defects (defects of the baby's brain and spine) in a developing baby when taken before conception and in early pregnancy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.3] Iron supplements can treat anaemia caused by low iron intake, under medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.4] Older people may absorb nutrients less efficiently, so they may need supplements, particularly vitamin D.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.5] Their popularity is growing for reasons such as busy lifestyles, poor diets, media and advertising, a wish to prevent illness, special needs (vegans, restricted diets, illness), sports performance and convenience.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.6] Supplements cannot replace a varied diet, because food supplies fibre and other nutrients that supplements do not contain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.8.7] High doses of some vitamins and minerals can be harmful, especially fat-soluble vitamins, so supplements should not be taken above the recommended levels. They can also cost a lot, so take medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.b] Healthy eating is only part of staying healthy. The lifestyle guidelines cover activity, mental wellbeing and habits that harm the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.0] Regular physical activity: build activity into daily life, such as brisk walking, cycling, sport or active housework, and include muscle-strengthening and weight-bearing exercise (which loads the bones, such as walking or running).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.1] Physical activity improves cardiovascular fitness, maintains muscle and bone, supports weight control and mental wellbeing, and may raise protective HDL cholesterol (the protective form of cholesterol).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.2] Mental health: get adequate sleep and rest, keep a realistic workload, and use stress management, relaxation and social contact.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.3] Avoid tobacco and second-hand smoke, which damage the lungs and blood vessels and greatly raise the risk of cancer and coronary heart disease.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.4] Limit alcohol: it supplies empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrients), harms the liver and judgement, and must never be used to manage stress.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.5] Drink plenty of water and keep a healthy body weight.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.6] Regular health checks and good personal hygiene complete the guidelines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s4.9.7] Together these habits reduce the risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension (high blood pressure) and heart disease.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s1: 1. Fat-soluble vitamins
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.b] Vitamins are organic compounds that the body needs in very small amounts. They are grouped by what they dissolve in, and that one difference decides how they are absorbed, stored and lost.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.0] Vitamins are needed for normal growth, health and chemical reactions in the body. The body cannot make enough of most of them, so they must come from food.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.1] There are two classes: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) and water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C). For example, vitamin A is fat-soluble and vitamin C is water-soluble.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.2] RDA (recommended dietary allowance) is the daily amount that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, because needs change at different life stages.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.b] Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat, so they are absorbed together with dietary fat. This also explains why they are stored in the body and why too much can be harmful.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.0] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed in the small intestine along with dietary fats and are carried in the lymphatic system (the body's drainage network) before entering the blood.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.1] Because they need fat to be absorbed, a diet very low in fat, or a condition that stops fat being absorbed, can reduce vitamin intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.2] The liver and fatty (adipose) tissue store fat-soluble vitamins, so the body keeps a reserve between meals and deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.3] Because they are stored, excess intake from supplements or large amounts of liver can build up to toxic levels (toxicity). Water-soluble vitamins are mostly excreted in urine instead.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.4] Excess vitamin A (retinol) is harmful in pregnancy because it can harm the developing baby, so high-dose vitamin A supplements and large amounts of liver are avoided during pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.b] Vitamin A comes in two forms: one ready-made in animal foods and one made from a plant pigment. Its main jobs are vision, healthy surface tissues and protection of cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.0] Retinol is the active, ready-made (preformed) form of vitamin A, found only in animal foods: liver, fish liver oils, oily fish, dairy products (butter, full-fat milk and cheese), eggs and fortified margarine (margarine with vitamin A added).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.1] Beta-carotene is a plant pigment and a provitamin (a substance the body converts into a vitamin). The body converts it into retinol in the intestine, so beta-carotene is not itself vitamin A.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.2] Good beta-carotene sources include carrots, sweet potatoes, apricots, mango, red and orange peppers, tomatoes and dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach and kale, where the green hides the orange pigment.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.3] Retinol combines with the protein opsin to form rhodopsin (visual purple), the light-sensitive pigment in the rod cells of the retina. This allows vision in dim light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.4] Vitamin A keeps the cells lining the eyes, mouth, respiratory tract, urinary tract and skin healthy and moist, which protects them against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.5] Vitamin A supports growth, cell repair and a healthy immune system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.6] Beta-carotene acts as an antioxidant, neutralising free radicals (unstable molecules that damage cells).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.b] Without enough vitamin A, the eye pigment cannot be rebuilt and the body's moist surface linings break down. The eyes show the first signs.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.0] Night blindness is the earliest sign: poor vision in dim light because rhodopsin is not regenerated.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.1] Xerophthalmia: the eyes become dry and damaged, and in severe cases the cornea (the clear front of the eye) is destroyed and blindness follows.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.2] Dry, rough skin and dry, brittle hair, because the skin and other surface tissues are no longer kept healthy and moist.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.3] Poor growth, because vitamin A supports growth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.4] Lowered resistance to infection, because the mucous membranes (moist linings) dry out, crack and lose their protective role.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.5] In severe cases deficiency causes blindness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.b] Vitamin D is unusual because the body can make it in the skin when sunlight falls on it, which is why it is called the sunshine vitamin. It is the vitamin that lets the body use calcium to build bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.0] Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) forms in the skin when ultraviolet B from sunlight acts on a cholesterol derivative in the skin. It is also found in animal foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.1] Ergocalciferol (vitamin D2) is the plant and fungal form, made when mushrooms and yeast are exposed to ultraviolet light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.2] Dietary sources include oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, sardines and herring, egg yolk, liver, butter, fortified margarine and spreads, fortified milk and some fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.3] Vitamin D increases the absorption of calcium and phosphorus from the small intestine, which is why it is the vitamin needed to absorb calcium.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.4] Vitamin D raises blood calcium levels, working with parathormone (the parathyroid hormone) when blood calcium falls, so blood calcium stays at the right level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.5] Vitamin D supports the mineralisation of bones (the deposit of calcium and phosphorus that hardens them) and the formation of teeth, so it builds and maintains strong bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.6] In Ireland, weak winter sunlight means that dietary sources of vitamin D matter most in the winter months. People who cover up, stay indoors or have dark skin also make less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.b] Low vitamin D means too little calcium reaches the bones, so they stay soft. The condition has a different name in children and in adults.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.0] Rickets in children: bones soften and bend, causing bow legs, delayed growth and poor tooth formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.1] Osteomalacia in adults: bones become soft, weak and painful, and fractures can occur, because they are not properly mineralised.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.2] Low vitamin D lowers calcium absorption, which is a contributing factor in osteoporosis, where bone density falls, and in dental decay.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.b] Vitamin E is the fat-soluble vitamin whose main job is as an antioxidant: it stops oxidation damaging fatty parts of the body's cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.0] Tocopherols are the active forms of vitamin E. Alpha-tocopherol is the form the human body uses most, and the main form in the body.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.1] Rich sources are vegetable oils such as sunflower, wheatgerm and olive oil, nuts and seeds such as almonds and sunflower seeds, wholegrain cereals, avocado, green leafy vegetables and egg yolk.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.2] Vitamin E is an antioxidant that protects the membranes of cells and polyunsaturated fatty acids from oxidation (damage by free radicals).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.3] Vitamin E also helps protect the vitamin A and vitamin C in the body, and supports healthy skin and immune function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.4] Deficiency is rare in healthy people. In people who cannot absorb fat, and in premature babies, it can cause nerve and muscle damage and the early breakdown of red blood cells (a form of anaemia).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.b] Vitamin K is the clotting vitamin: the K comes from the German word for coagulation. It has two forms, one from plants and one made by bacteria in the gut.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.0] Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) comes from plants: green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, cabbage, kale and sprouts, and vegetable oils and liver.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.1] Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) is made by bacteria in the large intestine and is also found in dairy products, eggs and meat. The gut bacteria are therefore a second source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.2] The liver needs vitamin K to make the clotting factors, including prothrombin, that allow blood to clot. This is its primary function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.3] Vitamin K activates osteocalcin, a protein that binds calcium into bone, so it supports bone formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.4] Deficiency impairs blood clotting, causing bleeding and easy bruising. Long courses of antibiotics or poor fat absorption can also reduce vitamin K.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.5] The HSE offers every newborn baby a vitamin K injection within hours of birth, because their gut bacteria are not yet established and they have low stores, so they risk bleeding. It is the parents' choice.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.b] All four fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water, but they differ in what destroys them: heat, oxygen, light or alkalis. Knowing this explains how storage and cooking affect each one.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.0] Vitamin A is insoluble in water but soluble in fats and organic solvents.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.1] Vitamin A is heat-stable in normal cooking but is destroyed by prolonged high temperatures.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.2] Vitamin A is destroyed by oxygen (it is oxidised), so foods kept exposed to air lose it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.3] Vitamin D is insoluble in water and is relatively heat-stable in normal cooking and storage, so cooking oily fish loses little.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.4] Vitamin E is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking, but it is destroyed by oxidation, including by rancid fats, by light and air, and by frying at high temperature for long periods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.5] Vitamin K is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.6] Vitamin K is destroyed by light (ultraviolet) and by alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.b] To judge how good a food is as a source of vitamins, use food composition tables and food labels, and think about whether the food is fresh, processed or fortified.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.0] Food composition tables give the vitamin content of foods, usually per 100 g, so fresh and processed foods can be compared on the same basis (like for like).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.1] The mandatory nutrition declaration on a food label lists energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. Vitamins appear only when added or when a claim is made, so not listing vitamins does not mean the food has none.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.2] Where vitamins are listed, the label states the amount per 100 g (or per serving) and as a percentage of the reference intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.3] In Ireland, the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014 give effect to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) advises on food labelling and nutrition in Ireland.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.4] Fortified foods have vitamins added that the natural food does not contain, or that were lost in processing. Examples are margarine with added vitamins A and D and fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.5] Fresh produce loses vitamin C in storage, while frozen vegetables, blanched before freezing, usually retain more vitamin C than long-stored fresh vegetables.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.6] Canning and prolonged cooking destroy heat-sensitive vitamins, so a processed food may contain less of a vitamin than its fresh form.
