TARGET hom-lipids-s6: 6. Fats and oils as a food commodity
[hom-lipids-s6.0.b] Fats and oils are the most concentrated fat foods in the diet. Knowing what they supply, and what they leave out, explains how they should be used and chosen.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.0] Fats and oils are almost pure fat, supplying 82–99.9% fat. Butter and margarine are about four-fifths fat or more and oils are almost pure fat, so the balance is mostly water in butter, margarine and spreads, and water varies inversely with fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.1] They supply energy at 37 kJ or 9 kcal per gram, which is more than twice the energy in a gram of protein or carbohydrate, so a small amount of fat adds a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.2] They supply fat-soluble vitamins (those that dissolve in fat, not water) and fatty acids. Butter has vitamins A and D naturally, vegetable oils supply vitamin E, and essential fatty acids (ones the body cannot make) come from plant oils.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.3] Margarine and spreads are usually fortified with vitamins A and D, which are not naturally present in plant oils, so they match the vitamins that butter provides.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.4] They supply little or no protein, carbohydrate, fibre or minerals. Butter and margarine contain traces of minerals such as calcium and sodium (from added salt).
[hom-lipids-s6.0.5] Spreads contain water as well as fat, so a lower-fat spread contains more water than a butter or margarine of the same type.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.6] The type of fat depends on its source. Animal fats (butter, lard, dripping) are saturated and raise LDL (the 'bad' cholesterol), olive oil is monounsaturated, and plant and nut oils are polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.7] Spreads and oils are eaten daily but in small amounts, yet much of the fat in the diet is hidden in processed, convenience and takeaway foods, meat products and dairy, so total fat intake is greater than the visible fats suggest.
[hom-lipids-s6.0.8] In the Food Pyramid, fats, spreads and oils are used sparingly, choosing mono- or polyunsaturated types.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.b] The three main commodities start from different raw materials. Butter comes from milk, oils from plants, and margarine from plant oils that are then hardened.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.0] Butter is made by churning cream. The fat droplets clump together, the buttermilk is drained off, and the butter is worked and salted or left unsalted.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.1] In the EU, butter is at least 80% and under 90% milk fat, with at most 16% water. It is an animal, saturated fat and a water-in-oil emulsion (tiny water droplets spread through the fat).
[hom-lipids-s6.1.2] Oils are pressed or extracted from seeds, nuts and fruits such as olives, rapeseed and sunflower. They are then refined (cleaned, bleached and deodorised) to remove impurities, giving a bland oil with a higher smoke point (the temperature at which it starts to give off smoke).
[hom-lipids-s6.1.3] Extra virgin olive oil is made from olives by mechanical pressing without chemical refining, so it keeps its flavour. It is used for dressing and gentle cooking.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.4] Margarine and spreads are made from vegetable oils, and sometimes fish oils. Spreads are lower in fat than margarine and butter and contain more water.
[hom-lipids-s6.1.5] The types on sale are butter, margarine, low-fat spreads, functional dairy spreads (cholesterol-lowering), blended spreads (butter with vegetable oil), oils (olive, rapeseed, sunflower, vegetable), lard, suet, dripping and cooking fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.b] The right fat depends on what it is for, what the label says and your health needs. Reading the label first makes each choice easier.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.0] Read the label for the type of fat, ingredients, energy per 100 g, total fat, saturates, salt, additives and allergens (milk, soya).
[hom-lipids-s6.2.1] The ingredients list shows the largest ingredient first, so a spread listing water or vegetable oil first contains mainly those.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.2] Choose by health: more mono- and polyunsaturated fat and less saturated fat. Olive or rapeseed oil are healthier choices, and low-fat or functional spreads suit people watching weight or cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.3] Choose spreads with few hydrogenated fats in the ingredients list when reducing trans fat (a fat formed during hydrogenation that is harmful to the heart) in the diet, and avoid partially hydrogenated fats.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.4] Choose by use. Oils have higher smoke points than butter, so oils suit frying and butter suits spreading and baking.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.5] Hard margarine or block fat suits pastry and creaming because it is plastic (firm yet workable) at room temperature. Low-fat spreads are unsuitable for cooking and baking because of their high water content.
[hom-lipids-s6.2.6] Other factors are cost, taste, dietary needs (dairy-free, vegan, allergy), brand, packaging, shelf life, organic, and whether it is Irish (the Bord Bia quality mark).
[hom-lipids-s6.3.b] Fats spoil in two ways, which is why storage advice differs. Air, light and heat make fats go rancid, while fat also soaks up smells from other foods.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.0] Rancidity (the off smell and taste of spoiled fat) is the main storage problem. Oxidative rancidity is caused by air, light and heat, and hydrolytic rancidity by water breaking down the fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.1] Store fats and oils in dark or opaque, airtight containers in a cool place, away from light and heat, to limit oxidative rancidity. Buy sensible quantities and use them before the date.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.2] Store butter and spreads in the refrigerator, with a cover or in the original wrapper or tub, because fats absorb strong odours such as onion and fish. Freeze butter for longer storage.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.3] Store nuts and seeds in airtight containers in a cool place, because their oils go rancid quickly.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.4] Oily fish and fatty meat are stored in the fridge and used promptly, or frozen.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.5] Keep used frying oil covered and strain it, so food particles do not speed up its breakdown. Do not top up old oil, and discard it when it is dark, smokes at a low temperature or foams.
[hom-lipids-s6.3.6] Foods that contain fat, such as pastries, cakes, biscuits, crisps, nuts and whole-grain flours, are kept in airtight tins or packs.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.b] How fat is handled in the kitchen changes both the energy in a dish and how it turns out. Some methods add almost no fat, while others depend on it for texture.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.0] Methods that add little or no fat are grilling, steaming, poaching, microwaving and baking on a rack. Trimming visible fat from meat before cooking reduces the fat in the finished dish, as does removing skin, using a non-stick pan and cutting the fat in recipes.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.1] Frying adds fat and therefore energy, because the food absorbs fat. Shallow frying uses a little fat and transfers heat evenly to food, giving it a golden colour.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.2] Deep-fat frying in a deep pan at the correct, controlled temperature limits the amount of oil that food absorbs. Do not overheat the oil, and drain the food on absorbent paper.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.3] Creaming butter or margarine with sugar traps air in the mixture, giving cakes a light texture.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.4] Rubbing fat into flour makes shortcrust pastry. The fat coats the flour proteins and limits gluten, which shortens the pastry. Fat is layered in flaky and puff pastry.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.5] Fat is also used for frying and roasting (heat transfer and browning), greasing tins, emulsions such as mayonnaise, hollandaise and dressings, roux and sauces, and for flavour, richness and moistness.
[hom-lipids-s6.4.6] Fat from roasted meat collects in the tin and can be strained and used as dripping for frying.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.b] Heat changes fat, and not always for the better. Knowing what happens when fat is overheated, and how manufacturers process fats, explains the safety rules and the labels.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.0] Fat melts when heated, but overheating breaks it down (its smoke point is reached and acrolein, an irritant, forms). Repeated heating lowers the smoke point and produces off flavours.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.1] Fat that reaches flash point (the temperature at which it ignites) can catch fire, so never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended.
[hom-lipids-s6.5.2] Processing includes refining, hydrogenation (turning oil into fat), blending, emulsifying, and adding vitamins and colour. Hydrogenation can form trans fatty acids, and the EU limit is 2 g per 100 g of fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.b] Margarine is made by turning liquid vegetable oil into a spreadable solid fat and mixing it with a water phase. The stages must be written in order.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.0] 1. Extract the oil from seeds such as sunflower, rapeseed, soya or palm, then refine it to remove impurities.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.1] 2. Hydrogenate it. Hydrogen gas is passed through the heated oil in the presence of a nickel catalyst (a substance that speeds up the reaction), which adds hydrogen at the double bonds and makes the liquid oil a semi-solid fat. The catalyst is then removed.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.2] 3. Blend the hydrogenated fat with other oils to give the texture (plasticity) and spreadability needed.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.3] 4. Prepare the water phase from water or skimmed milk, salt, colour (carotene), flavour and vitamins A and D. Lecithin is added as an emulsifier, which keeps the fat and water from separating.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.4] 5. Emulsify by mixing the fat and water phases, with churning, to form a stable water-in-oil emulsion.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.5] 6. Chill rapidly in a votator, a scraped-surface heat exchanger, while working the mixture so that a plastic, spreadable fat forms.
[hom-lipids-s6.6.6] 7. Weigh the margarine and pack it in foil-wrapped blocks or plastic tubs. An airtight, light-proof pack protects the fat from oxidation and absorbed smells.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.b] The label tells shoppers what is in the pack and how to store it, and the law sets out what it must show.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.0] The label gives the name and type (margarine or spread), and lists the ingredients in descending order by weight with allergens such as milk and soya highlighted.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.1] It also gives a nutrition declaration per 100 g (energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein, salt), the net quantity, a date mark (best before) and storage instructions, the manufacturer's name and address, and the country of origin and batch (lot) code.
[hom-lipids-s6.7.2] In Ireland, food labelling follows Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 as given effect by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, with the FSAI giving guidance on food safety and labelling.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.b] Low-fat spreads and functional spreads are different products for different needs. One lowers the fat in the diet, and the other lowers blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.0] Low-fat spreads have about half the fat of butter and more water, so they are lower in energy and suit a calorie-controlled diet.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.1] Low-fat spreads are made from vegetable oils, water and milk proteins, and are low in saturated fat and high in unsaturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.2] Low-fat spreads are suitable for spreading on bread but unsuitable for frying or baking because of their high water content.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.3] Functional dairy spreads contain added plant stanol or sterol esters, which block the absorption of cholesterol in the small intestine so that more is excreted. Examples are Benecol (plant stanol ester) and Flora pro.activ (plant sterol).
[hom-lipids-s6.8.4] Functional dairy spreads are made from vegetable oil, buttermilk, plant stanol or sterol ester, water and salt. They contain no hydrogenated fat and virtually no trans fatty acids.
[hom-lipids-s6.8.5] The cholesterol-lowering effect depends on eating the spread regularly as part of a healthy diet, and it lowers blood (LDL) cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.b] Data questions give the nutrition figures for several spreads and ask you to judge them. The method is to compare the figures, match them to dietary advice and give a verdict.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.0] Compare the energy value per 100 g or per serving of each spread.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.1] Compare total fat, saturated fat, monounsaturated fat and polyunsaturated fat, and the salt content (and omega-3 where it is given) for each spread.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.2] Lower energy and lower total fat suit weight control, and lower saturated fat reduces the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s6.9.3] Higher monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fat, and a lower saturated fat content, suit a diet aimed at protecting the heart. Lower salt suits blood pressure.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.4] Check the water content and whether the spread is suitable for frying or baking.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.5] Compare butter, a spread and a light spread, and state the fat content differences. A lower fat content follows from a higher water content.
[hom-lipids-s6.9.6] Reach a verdict for each product, such as suiting weight reduction or CHD risk reduction, and give a reason for each judgement that refers to the figures.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s3: 3. Sources
[hom-lipids-s3.0.b] Lipids (fats and oils) reach our diet from three kinds of source: animal, vegetable and marine. Animal sources come from the meat, milk and eggs of land animals, and some are fat in its almost pure form.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.0] Meat fat is a major source of lipid in meat products. It includes the visible fat on bacon, the fat trimmed from beef and the fat in sausages. Fatty cuts of lamb and pork, burgers and pâtés also contain it.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.1] Marbling, the fine streaks of fat running inside the muscle of meat, is hidden fat, so a cut can look lean and still contain fat that cannot be trimmed off.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.2] Butter, cream, whole milk, cheese and ice cream supply fat. Milk fat also carries the fat-soluble vitamins A and D, meaning vitamins that dissolve in fat rather than water.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.3] Egg yolk contains fat and lecithin (a fat-like substance that helps fat and water mix). The egg white contains no fat.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.4] Lard is rendered pig fat, which means fat melted down and strained, and is used in pastry and frying.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.5] Suet is the hard fat from around the kidneys and loins of cattle and sheep, used in suet pastry and puddings.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.6] Dripping is the fat that collects from roasted meat, used for frying and roasting.
[hom-lipids-s3.0.7] Visible fat can be seen and cut off or avoided, such as the fat on meat, butter, cooking oil and spreads. Hidden fat is built into foods such as cakes, pastries, biscuits, chocolate, crisps, fried foods, sausages, cheese, ready meals and takeaways.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.b] Plants store fat in their fruits, seeds and nuts, and people press this fat out as oil or process it into spreads. Vegetable (plant) sources are the group most often named in exam answers alongside animal and marine.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.0] Vegetable oils include olive, sunflower, corn, soya, rapeseed (canola), sesame and groundnut (peanut) oils, plus coconut oil and palm oil.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.1] Olives and avocados are fruits that supply lipid, which is unusual because most fruits contain almost none.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.2] Nuts such as almonds, hazelnuts, walnuts, peanuts and Brazil nuts are a concentrated source of lipid. So are seeds such as sunflower, sesame and flax (linseed).
[hom-lipids-s3.1.3] Soya beans are a legume (a bean or pea crop) rich in both fat and protein.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.4] Cereals and cereal products supply small amounts of lipid, mainly in wheat germ and oats. Wheat germ is the embryo of the wheat grain, the part that would grow into a new plant, so it holds the grain's fat.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.5] Chocolate contains cocoa butter, which is the plant fat in cocoa.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.6] Margarine and vegetable spreads are made from processed plant oils, so they count as vegetable sources. Shortening is another processed plant fat.
[hom-lipids-s3.1.7] Vegetable oils are also hidden in biscuits, pastries, crisps, fried and takeaway foods and ready meals, which is why these foods are high in fat even though no oil is visible.
[hom-lipids-s3.2.b] Fish and other sea foods give a third group of lipid sources, quite different from the fat of land animals. How much fat a fish has, and where it keeps it, decides whether it counts as a rich source.
[hom-lipids-s3.2.0] Oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout store their fat in the flesh. They are a marine source of omega-3 fatty acids, a type of polyunsaturated fat, so the fat itself is the valuable part.
[hom-lipids-s3.2.1] Fish-liver oils, such as cod-liver oil, provide polyunsaturated fat along with the fat-soluble vitamins A and D. Fish-oil supplements are another source of these.
[hom-lipids-s3.2.2] White fish such as cod, haddock and whiting store their fat mainly in the liver, so the flesh is low in fat.
[hom-lipids-s3.2.3] Shellfish, such as prawns and mussels, contain only small amounts of lipid.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.b] Fats are made of fatty acids, which are saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated. Each food has a pattern in which one type is largest, and that pattern decides how healthy the fat is.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.0] Mostly saturated fats are butter, cream, cheese, lard, suet, dripping, fat on meat and hard cooking fats. Animal fats such as these are generally saturated and associated with raised LDL (the blood cholesterol that harms arteries).
[hom-lipids-s3.3.1] Coconut oil and palm oil are high in saturated fat despite coming from plants, so a food being a plant product does not make its fat unsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.2] Olive oil, avocado and rapeseed (canola) oil are mostly monounsaturated, as are many nuts such as almonds, hazelnuts and peanuts.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.3] Standard sunflower oil, corn oil and soya oil are mostly polyunsaturated, as are walnuts, seeds and oily fish. Plant and nut oils are associated with HDL, the blood cholesterol that protects the heart.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.4] Sesame oil is a mixture of mono- and polyunsaturated fat, so it does not fit a single class.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.5] Most foods contain all three fatty acid types, and a food is named for the type with the largest share. Butter, for example, contains a substantial share of monounsaturated fat as well as saturated fat, and olive oil contains some saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.6] The healthy swap is saturated fat for unsaturated fat, for example butter for olive oil.
[hom-lipids-s3.3.7] Small amounts of naturally occurring trans fat appear in dairy and in beef and lamb fat. Industrial trans fat is limited in the EU to 2 g per 100 g of fat (Regulation (EU) 2019/649, applying from 1 April 2021).
[hom-lipids-s3.4.b] A poster turns the three groups of sources into a picture you can recall quickly in an exam. It works as revision rather than being examined itself.
[hom-lipids-s3.4.0] Group the sources into three columns headed animal, vegetable (plant) and marine.
[hom-lipids-s3.4.1] Give one example food for each column and the type of fatty acid that predominates in it.
[hom-lipids-s3.4.2] Add the main health message for each food, such as the role of omega-3 in oily fish or the saturated fat in butter.
[hom-lipids-s3.4.3] Show the source of each food with a small picture or label so the groups are clear at a glance.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s4: 4. Properties
[hom-lipids-s4.0.b] As a fat is heated it passes through three different stages, each at a higher temperature than the last: it melts, then it smokes, then it can burst into flame. Knowing these temperatures explains how fats are chosen and used in cooking.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.0] Melting point is the temperature at which a solid fat turns to liquid. Fats are mixtures of triglycerides, so they melt over a range rather than at one sharp temperature, and solid fats melt at about 30–40 °C. Oils are already liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.1] Fats with a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids have lower melting points, because their kinked chains do not pack tightly. Fats with more saturated fatty acids, whose chains are straight and pack closely, have higher melting points.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.2] Smoke point is the temperature at which an overheated lipid begins to decompose and gives off a continuous blue haze. Smoke points are about 200 °C for solid fats and about 250 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.3] On decomposition the fat splits into glycerol and fatty acids, and the glycerol breaks down further to release acrolein, which has an acrid, irritating smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.4] Free fatty acids, impurities and food particles left in the oil lower its smoke point, and repeated use builds these up, so used or dirty fat smokes at a lower temperature. This is why frying fat is strained and replaced.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.5] A fat with a high smoke point suits frying, while butter, with a lower smoke point, burns.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.6] Flash point is the temperature at which the vapour from an overheated lipid ignites spontaneously and bursts into flame. Flash points are about 310 °C for solid fats and about 325 °C for oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.0.7] A fat that reaches its flash point is a serious fire hazard. Never leave a deep-fat fryer unattended, never heat fat until it smokes, and smother a fat fire with a fire blanket or lid, never water.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.b] This practical compares how common fats and oils behave on heating, and links the results to their fatty acids and to impurities. Care is needed because the fats become very hot.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.0] Aim: to compare the melting, smoke and flash points of common fats and oils, for example butter, lard, olive oil and sunflower oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.1] 1. Use a small amount of each fat or oil, so any smoke or fire is limited.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.2] 2. Heat each gently, preferably on a hot plate rather than a flame, which is safer for a class demonstration, and read the temperature with a thermometer.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.3] 3. Record the order in which the fats melt, and which fats smoke first. Do not heat beyond the smoke point.
[hom-lipids-s4.1.4] Conclusion: link the order of melting points to how saturated the fatty acids are, and the point at which smoke appears to the fat's impurities.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.b] Oil and water do not mix normally, but they can be forced to, and in some foods they stay mixed. This is the basis of mayonnaise, butter, milk and many sauces.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.0] An emulsion is a mixture of two liquids that do not normally mix (immiscible), such as oil and water, with one dispersed as tiny droplets in the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.1] Vigorous shaking, whisking or blending forces the liquids together by breaking one liquid into droplets that spread through the other.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.2] A temporary emulsion, such as French dressing (oil and vinegar), separates again on standing because nothing keeps the droplets apart.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.3] A permanent (stable) emulsion, such as mayonnaise, hollandaise sauce, ice cream, cream, milk or butter, stays mixed because an emulsifying agent coats the droplets.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.4] An emulsifier has a hydrophilic (water-attracting) end and a hydrophobic (water-repelling) end, which sit at the boundary between the oil and the water.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.5] In an oil-in-water emulsion, oil droplets are dispersed in water, as in mayonnaise, milk and cream.
[hom-lipids-s4.2.6] In a water-in-oil emulsion, water droplets are dispersed in fat, as in butter, margarine and some spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.b] Making mayonnaise and a French dressing side by side shows the difference between a temporary and a permanent emulsion.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.0] Aim: to show emulsion formation and the effect of an emulsifier, using mayonnaise (egg yolk, oil, and vinegar or lemon juice) and a French dressing (oil and vinegar).
[hom-lipids-s4.3.1] 1. Make the mayonnaise by adding the oil to the egg yolk slowly while whisking, so the droplets are small and evenly spread.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.2] 2. Shake the oil and vinegar together for the French dressing, and leave both to stand.
[hom-lipids-s4.3.3] Result: the French dressing separates, but the mayonnaise stays stable because the egg yolk is the emulsifier. Comparing with and without an emulsifier shows this.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.b] Two kinds of additive help keep an emulsion together, and they do different jobs. An emulsifier makes the emulsion, while a stabiliser helps it stay that way.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.0] An emulsifying agent is a molecule with a hydrophilic (water-loving) end and a hydrophobic (water-hating, fat-loving) end. The hydrophilic end attaches to the water and the hydrophobic end to the oil, so the droplets are coated and cannot join together.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.1] Emulsifying agents include lecithin (found in egg yolk and soya; the food additive E322), mustard, and mono- and diglycerides such as glycerol monostearate (GMS; mono- and diglycerides of fatty acids are E471).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.2] The function of an emulsifier is to reduce the tension between oil and water so they mix, and to make and keep the emulsion stable.
[hom-lipids-s4.4.3] Stabilisers do not form the emulsion. They thicken the water phase and stop droplets joining together (coalescing).
[hom-lipids-s4.4.4] Examples of stabilisers are starch, pectin, gelatine and gums such as xanthan gum and guar gum. They are used in dressings, sauces, ice cream and low-fat spreads.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.b] Some fats can be spread or shaped without being runny or rock hard. This property, called plasticity, is why butter and margarine behave so differently from oil.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.0] Plasticity is the ability of a fat to be shaped, spread or worked (creamed, rubbed in) at a given temperature without being fully liquid or fully hard.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.1] It is due to a mixture of solid and liquid fat within the fat: the solid fat forms a network that holds the liquid fat. The proportion of saturated fatty acids only decides how much of the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.2] Butter, lard, margarine and shortening are plastic over a range of temperatures. Oils are not plastic, a very unsaturated fat is too liquid to hold its shape, and a very saturated fat is hard and brittle when cold.
[hom-lipids-s4.5.3] Plastic fats are valued in pastry and cake making because they can be creamed, which traps air, or rubbed into flour and layered. Plasticity also gives butter and margarine their spreadability.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.b] Liquid oils can be turned into solid fats by changing their fatty acids. This process, hydrogenation, is how margarine is made from vegetable oils.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.0] Hydrogenation is the addition of hydrogen to the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, using a nickel catalyst under heat and pressure.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.1] It converts unsaturated fatty acids to saturated ones, so a liquid oil becomes a semi-solid or solid fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.2] It is used to make margarine and spreads, hard margarines, shortenings and baking fats from liquid vegetable oils. The fats are solid, plastic and more stable against oxidative rancidity.
[hom-lipids-s4.6.3] Partial hydrogenation forms trans fatty acids, so the process is now controlled. Full hydrogenation gives a hard saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.b] Rancidity is the spoilage of fats and oils, which gives them an unpleasant taste and smell. It happens in two different ways, with different causes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.0] Oxidative rancidity: oxygen from the air reacts at the double bonds of unsaturated fatty acids, which also breaks the chain. This produces aldehydes and ketones, which cause the rancid smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.1] Light, heat and traces of metal ions speed up oxidative rancidity. It affects polyunsaturated oils most because they have the most double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.2] Hydrolytic rancidity: water, together with the enzyme lipase from the food itself or from micro-organisms such as moulds and bacteria, breaks fats down into fatty acids and glycerol. The free fatty acids, for example butyric acid in butter, give the smell.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.3] Hydrolytic rancidity can occur in a freezer, because lipase is not destroyed by freezing. Blanching vegetables before freezing destroys these enzymes.
[hom-lipids-s4.7.4] Fats are protected from rancidity when stored in airtight, opaque containers in a cool, dark place, kept dry, bought in small amounts and used before the date. Refrigerating or freezing, using less-unsaturated fats, adding antioxidants and not reusing frying oil repeatedly also help.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.b] Antioxidants are the main chemical defence against oxidative rancidity. They are added to fats and oils so that they keep their flavour for longer.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.0] An antioxidant is a substance that slows oxidation. It delays oxidative rancidity by reacting with oxygen or free radicals before they attack the double bonds.
[hom-lipids-s4.8.1] Natural antioxidants include vitamin E (tocopherols, E306), present in some vegetable oils, and vitamin C (ascorbic acid, E300).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.2] Synthetic antioxidants added to fats include butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA, E320) and butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT, E321).
[hom-lipids-s4.8.3] Antioxidants are added to oils, margarines, spreads, snack foods and fried foods to keep their flavour and extend their shelf life. They appear in the ingredients list by name or E number under the additives rules (Regulation (EC) No 1333/2008).

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s1: 1. Fatty acids
[hom-lipids-s1.0.b] Fatty acids are the building blocks of fats and oils (lipids). They are sorted into three classes by the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain, and that count decides how the fat behaves.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.0] A fatty acid is a long chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. It has a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end and a methyl group (–CH₃) at the other.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.1] Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds between carbon atoms, so every carbon carries its full quota of hydrogen atoms. Saturated means saturated with hydrogen.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.2] Monounsaturated fatty acids have one double bond (C=C) in the carbon chain, so two neighbouring carbons each hold one hydrogen fewer.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.3] Polyunsaturated fatty acids have two or more double bonds in the carbon chain.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.4] Saturated fats are generally solid at room temperature, while fats rich in monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids are generally liquid oils. Coconut oil and palm oil are plant oils that are rich in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.5] Examples: stearic acid (or butyric acid) is saturated, oleic acid is monounsaturated, and linoleic acid (or linolenic acid) is polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.b] The shape of the carbon chain explains why some fats are solid and others are liquid. Higher Level questions ask you to describe and draw each type.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.0] Saturated fatty acids contain only single C–C bonds and are fully saturated with hydrogen. The chain is straight and flexible, so the chains pack tightly together and the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.1] Each cis C=C double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid puts a kink (bend) in the chain, so the molecules pack less tightly and the fat stays liquid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.2] A monounsaturated fatty acid has one double bond and so one kink. A polyunsaturated fatty acid has two or more double bonds, so it has one kink for each cis double bond and is liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.3] Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, with the first double bond between the third and fourth carbon atoms counted from the methyl (omega) end. Omega-6 fatty acids have their first double bond at the sixth carbon from the methyl end.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.4] Diagram: draw a zig-zag or a simple row of carbons for a saturated, a monounsaturated and a polyunsaturated fatty acid. Label the –COOH (carboxyl) end, the –CH₃ (methyl) end and mark each C=C double bond.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.b] A double bond can hold its two hydrogen atoms in two different arrangements. The arrangement changes the shape of the chain and how the fat acts in the body.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.0] Cis and trans describe the hydrogen atoms at one double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid, not how many double bonds there are.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.1] In a cis fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on the same side of the carbon chain (both above or both below), so the chain bends.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.2] In a trans fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight, like a saturated fatty acid.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.3] Most naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids are cis, for example those in plant oils such as cooking oils and in oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.4] Trans fatty acids form when oils are partially hydrogenated in food manufacture, so they are found in some processed foods such as biscuits, pastries and fried takeaway foods. Small natural amounts occur in the fat of ruminant animals, in dairy fat and in beef and lamb fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.b] Most fatty acids can be made by the body, but a few cannot. These essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated and must come from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.0] Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that the body cannot manufacture (or cannot make in enough quantity), so they must be obtained from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.1] Linoleic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) and linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid) are the two essential fatty acids most often named.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.2] Arachidonic acid is also counted as essential by examiners. Strictly the body forms it only from linoleic acid, when enough linoleic acid is eaten.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.3] Essential fatty acids are needed for growth, for the structure of cell membranes, for the nervous system and brain, and for the immune response.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.4] The body uses essential fatty acids to make prostaglandins, hormone-like compounds that regulate blood clotting, blood pressure and inflammation.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.5] Good sources include vegetable oils such as sunflower, corn, soya and rapeseed oil, nuts and seeds, and oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.b] Omega-3 fatty acids are a family of essential polyunsaturated fats with several health benefits. They come from a limited range of foods, so it helps to know the sources by name.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.0] Omega-3 fatty acids help reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, circulatory disease and the formation of blood clots, and they can help raise HDL cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.1] Omega-3 fatty acids are linked with healthy brain activity and reduced inflammation, and they may reduce the risk of certain cancers.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.2] Oily fish are the main animal source: salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.3] Plant sources include walnuts, flaxseed (linseed), soya beans and rapeseed (canola) oil.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.4] Some eggs and milk are enriched with omega-3 by changing the diet of the hens or cows, and fish-oil supplements are also accepted as a source.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.b] The type of fatty acid in the diet affects the amount of cholesterol carried in the blood. This is why the balance of saturated, unsaturated and trans fats matters for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.0] Cholesterol travels in the blood bound to proteins, forming lipoproteins (carriers of cholesterol). LDL and HDL are these carriers, not types of cholesterol itself.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.1] LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol through the blood to the tissues and can deposit it on artery walls, where it narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis) and raises the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.2] HDL (high-density lipoprotein) removes cholesterol from the circulation, carrying it away from the tissues to the liver for removal.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.3] Saturated fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.4] Unsaturated cis fatty acids, both monounsaturated and polyunsaturated, reduce LDL cholesterol and help to raise HDL cholesterol, so they are generally good for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.5] Trans fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of CHD. They are thought to do more harm than saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.6] Monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats can lower total cholesterol when they replace saturated fats in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.7] Essential fatty acids must be eaten because the body cannot make them, and omega-3 fatty acids add the heart and brain benefits.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s5: 5. Functions and energy value
[hom-lipids-s5.0.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do far more in the body than provide energy. Each function below follows from where fat sits in the body or what it does as it passes through the digestive system.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.0] Lipids are a concentrated source of energy, supplying the body with more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein, so a small amount of fat gives a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.1] Excess energy from any food is stored as adipose tissue (the body's stored fat), which is a reserve of energy for when food intake is low.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.2] Adipose tissue lies under the skin and insulates the body against heat loss, which helps to keep body temperature constant.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.3] Adipose tissue around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against knocks and pressure, and holds them in place.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.4] Lipids slow the emptying of the stomach, which helps to delay hunger, so meals containing fat are more satisfying.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.b] Beyond energy and body protection, lipids carry nutrients, build cell structures and affect how food tastes.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.0] Lipids carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K and help the body absorb them, because these vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water. Dairy fat, oily fish and egg yolk supply them.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.1] Lipids are a source of essential fatty acids, the fatty acids the body cannot make for itself and must get from food.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.2] Phospholipids (fat-like lipids containing phosphorus) and cholesterol form part of the structure of every cell membrane, the thin layer around each cell.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.3] Fat also adds flavour and a smooth texture to food, although this is a cooking benefit and not a biological function.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.4] Digestion link: the enzyme lipase digests triglycerides (the main fats in food) to fatty acids and glycerol, and any excess is stored as adipose tissue.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.b] Cholesterol is a lipid that is often blamed for heart disease, yet the body needs it. It is different from the saturated fat in food, and students often confuse the two.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.0] Cholesterol is a fat-like substance called a sterol, found only in animal foods such as egg yolk, offal (liver and kidney), meat, butter, cream, cheese, prawns and other shellfish. Plant foods contain none.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.1] The liver makes cholesterol itself, in fact most of what the body has, so the amount eaten has less effect on blood cholesterol than the saturated fat in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.2] Saturated fat in the diet raises blood LDL cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol does. A plant oil such as coconut oil is cholesterol-free but can still be high in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.3] The body uses cholesterol to make cell membranes, bile salts that emulsify fat (break it into tiny droplets) for digestion, and steroid hormones such as oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone and cortisol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.4] Vitamin D is made in the skin from a derivative of cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.5] Too much cholesterol in the blood, especially LDL, builds up on artery walls and narrows them, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke. It is also a main constituent of most gallstones.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.6] To lower blood cholesterol, eat less saturated and trans fat and more unsaturated fat, oily fish, oats, pulses, fruit and vegetables (their fibre helps). Keep to a healthy weight, be active and do not smoke.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.7] Functional spreads containing plant stanols or sterols also help to lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.b] Foods high in saturated fat are energy-dense and raise blood LDL cholesterol. A diet high in them over years raises the risk of several diseases, and each has its own mechanism.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.0] Obesity: saturated-fat foods are energy-dense, and surplus energy from them is stored as adipose tissue, which can lead to obesity.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.1] Coronary heart disease and high blood pressure: raised LDL cholesterol narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis), increasing the risk of coronary heart disease, high blood pressure (hypertension) and stroke.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.2] Type 2 diabetes: excess body fat reduces the effectiveness of insulin (the hormone that controls blood sugar), which is called insulin resistance, so the risk of Type 2 diabetes increases.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.3] Gallstones: cholesterol is a main component of gallstones, which can form in the gall bladder.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.4] Varicose veins and infertility are also linked to a high-fat diet; the first four effects carry the most weight.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.5] Lifestyle changes recommended for someone at risk of CHD: a low-fat, low-cholesterol, low-salt diet; losing weight; not smoking; limiting alcohol; exercise; and managing stress.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.6] Irish healthy eating guidelines from the Department of Health advise replacing saturated fats with unsaturated (mono- and polyunsaturated) fats and using fats and oils in small amounts, which is the fats, spreads and oils shelf of the Food Pyramid.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.7] Other advice: choose lean meat and low-fat dairy, and grill, steam or bake instead of frying. Saturated fat should supply less than 10% of daily energy (HSE and Department of Health guidelines).
[hom-lipids-s5.4.b] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal). Fat has the highest energy value per gram of the three nutrients, which is why fatty foods are so energy-dense.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.0] Fat provides 37 kJ or 9 kcal of energy per gram, which is more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein. The true/false statement that 1 gram of fat provides 20 kilocalories is false.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.1] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ or 3.75 kcal per gram; protein provides 17 kJ or 4 kcal per gram; alcohol provides 29 kJ or 7 kcal per gram.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.2] Energy from fat is calculated by multiplying the grams of fat by 37 kJ, or by 9 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.3] Worked example (sample data): 20 g of fat gives 20 × 37 = 740 kJ, or 20 × 9 = 180 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.4] A large part of the energy in the average Irish diet comes from fat, much of it hidden in processed and convenience foods, meat products, dairy, cakes, pastries, biscuits, chocolate, fried foods and takeaways, and much of that is saturated.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.5] Guidance is to reduce total fat, especially saturated and trans fat, replacing them with unsaturated fat and complex carbohydrate. Over-consumption of fat leads to obesity because fat is energy-dense and stores readily.

CANDIDATES hom-food-additives-s2: 2. Packaging and labelling
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.b] Food packaging is made from six main materials, and each one suits certain foods because of its properties. Knowing a material's strengths tells you what it is used for.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.0] Metals (aluminium and tin) are strong, airtight and can be heat-processed, so they are used for canned fish and fruit, takeaway containers and aerosol whipped cream. Tins are lacquered inside so the metal does not react with the food.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.1] Glass (jars and bottles) is hygienic, transparent, impervious, resealable and reusable, so it is used for beverages, mayonnaise, sauces, preserves and cooking oil. Its drawbacks are that it is heavy and breakable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.2] Plastics (polythene, PET and similar polymers) are lightweight, flexible, moisture-proof, transparent and can be heat-sealed. They are used for polythene bags, PET soft drink bottles, yoghurt pots, dairy spreads and dried pasta, but are mostly non-biodegradable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.3] Polystyrene is lightweight, insulating, shock-absorbing, easily moulded and stackable, which suits egg trays and takeaway trays and containers. It is not readily biodegradable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.4] Paper and cardboard are light, inexpensive, easy to print on and renewable. They are used for cereal boxes, egg cartons and paper bags for bread, cakes, flour and sugar, but they are not moisture-proof unless coated.
[hom-food-additives-s2.0.5] Waxed cartons use a wax coating to make the board waterproof, so they are cheap, lightweight, printable and heat-sealable. They are recyclable or biodegradable according to the scheme.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.b] A pack has to do a job for the food inside it, so examiners expect you to list the qualities a good pack needs. Which qualities matter most depends on the food.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.0] Packaging must be strong and durable enough to survive handling and transport, so the food arrives undamaged.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.1] It must be airtight or moisture- and vapour-proof, depending on the food, so the food does not dry out, go soft or spoil.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.2] It must be hygienic and safe, and must not react with or taint the food, so it should be odourless.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.3] It should be easy to open and, where needed, easy to reseal, so the consumer can use it a little at a time.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.4] It should be lightweight, stackable and easy to store, which makes it cheaper to transport and easier to shelve.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.5] It should be heat resistant where the food is heated in the pack, and printable so that labels can be applied.
[hom-food-additives-s2.1.6] It should be attractive to the consumer and economical to produce.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.b] Packaging does more than wrap food. It has four jobs that examiners ask for, and a fifth, convenience, is also credited.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.0] Protection: packaging guards food against physical damage and against contamination by micro-organisms and dirt, so the food stays safe and intact.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.1] Containment: it holds the product in the portion size intended for the consumer.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.2] Information: it carries the product name, ingredients, nutrition and storage details, as well as weight, allergens, cooking instructions and branding.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.3] Tamper evidence: a sealed pack shows whether the contents have been interfered with, so the consumer can tell if it has been opened.
[hom-food-additives-s2.2.4] Convenience: packs that are easy to open, reseal, store and carry make the food easier to use.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.b] Packaging that protects food can also create waste. How much harm it does depends on whether the material is renewable, biodegradable (breaks down naturally) and recyclable (reprocessed into new products).
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.0] Metal and plastic packaging is not biodegradable and is made from non-renewable resources, but both can be recycled.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.1] Glass can be reused and recycled indefinitely without losing quality.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.2] Paper and cardboard are made from a renewable resource, are biodegradable and are widely recyclable.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.3] Polystyrene is bulky and not readily biodegradable, so its environmental impact depends on whether it is collected for recycling.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.4] The impact is reduced by using less packaging, reusing containers and recycling materials.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.5] In Ireland, the Re-turn deposit return scheme supports recycling of plastic drinks bottles and aluminium and steel cans by refunding a deposit: 15c for containers under 500 ml and 25c for 500 ml and over.
[hom-food-additives-s2.3.6] The scheme launched nationwide on 1 February 2026 for PET plastic bottles and aluminium or steel cans from 150 ml to 3 litres, under the Separate Collection (Deposit Return Scheme) Regulations 2021 (S.I. 599 of 2021).
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.b] Two kinds of packaging answer particular problems: one makes food last longer, the other makes the pack itself break down after use.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.0] Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP): the air is removed and replaced with a controlled mixture of carbon dioxide, nitrogen and oxygen, and the pack is heat sealed.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.1] The gas mixture slows the growth of bacteria, yeasts and moulds, so the shelf life of the food is extended. It also helps keep the food's colour.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.2] MAP is used for fresh meat, fish, fruit and vegetables, baked goods and cheese.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.3] Biodegradable packaging breaks down into the raw materials of nature, with micro-organisms doing much of the work.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.4] Examples of biodegradable packaging: paper bags for bread, cakes, sugar and flour, and cardboard for cereals, takeaway foods and eggs.
[hom-food-additives-s2.4.5] Diagram: a labelled sealed pack showing the gas mixture inside the tray and the heat-sealed film lid.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.b] Irish food labels follow Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, and the FSAI coordinates enforcement.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.0] The European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014 (S.I. No. 556 of 2014, in force 13 December 2014) give effect in Ireland to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, and official agencies working under FSAI service contracts carry out enforcement.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.1] The name of the food must be stated, along with a list of ingredients in descending order by weight, so the main ingredient comes first.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.2] Allergens such as milk, egg, nuts and gluten cereals must be emphasised within the ingredients list, so people with allergies can spot them. The Regulation lists 14 allergens, including cereals containing gluten, milk, eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, fish, crustaceans, molluscs, soya, celery, mustard, sesame, lupin, sulphur dioxide and sulphites.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.3] The net quantity must be stated, and the name and address of the food business operator must appear on the label.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.4] A use-by date is given for highly perishable food and is a food safety date, so do not eat after it. A best-before date (the date of minimum durability) is a quality date, after which the food may still be safe.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.5] Storage conditions and cooking or usage instructions must be given, for example keep refrigerated or stir before use.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.6] The country of origin or place of provenance is given where the law requires it.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.7] Additives are identified by their class name followed by the specific name or E number, for example preservative: E202.
[hom-food-additives-s2.5.8] Labels also carry a lot or batch code, which lets a product be traced and recalled if there is a problem.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.b] The nutrition declaration is a standard table that lets shoppers compare foods on the same terms. Claims on the front of a pack are separate, and need careful reading.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.0] The mandatory nutrition declaration gives seven items: energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.1] Values are given per 100 g or 100 ml, which allows fair comparison between products, and often per portion as well.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.2] Claims such as low fat, high in fibre or contains live cultures must be accurate and not misleading, and nutrition and health claims must meet the EU rules, set by Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006.
[hom-food-additives-s2.6.3] Low fat does not necessarily mean low sugar, salt or energy, so the full declaration must be read.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.b] A label is the consumer's main source of information at the point of purchase. It helps shoppers choose, stay safe and avoid waste.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.0] Labels allow consumers to compare products on energy, fat, sugar and salt, rather than being guided by brand, pictures or health claims.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.1] Consumers following a low-salt, low-saturated-fat or high-fibre diet can select products by their declared values.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.2] Ingredients and allergen emphasis protect people with coeliac disease, milk, egg, nut or other allergies.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.3] Use-by and best-before dates, storage and reheating instructions protect safety and reduce food waste.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.4] The origin on the label lets consumers choose to buy Irish.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.5] The manufacturer's details and the lot code support traceability and complaints.
[hom-food-additives-s2.7.6] Quality assurance (such as the Bord Bia mark), organic, fair-trade and gluten-free marks guide consumers with particular ethical or dietary choices.
