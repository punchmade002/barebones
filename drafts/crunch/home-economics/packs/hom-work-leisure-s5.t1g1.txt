TARGET hom-work-leisure-s5: 5. Reconciling employment with family responsibilities
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.b] Reconciling paid work with family life depends on how a household divides its roles and tasks between its members. Over time, the roles people expect of men and women have changed, and that change shapes how dual-earner families run.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.0] A dual-earner family (also called a dual-career family) is one in which both partners are in paid employment, so the household has two incomes and two sets of work demands.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.1] A role is the set of behaviours and responsibilities expected of a person in a position such as parent, employee, partner or homemaker (the person who runs the home). Sex roles, also called gender roles, are the tasks and behaviour expected of men and women.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.2] Traditional sex roles gave the man the main earning role, as breadwinner, and the woman the main responsibility for childcare and home care, as homemaker.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.3] Sex roles have loosened as more women take up paid work and more men take an active part in childcare and housework, so tasks are increasingly shared rather than fixed by sex.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.4] Fathers can now take paternity leave and parent's leave, and some families have a stay-at-home father, which shows that caring is no longer seen as only a woman's job.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.5] The change is real but not complete: attitudes have changed, yet women on average still do more of the household and caring work.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.0.6] Children learn sex roles from the way tasks are shared at home, as well as from school, peers and the media, so what they see their parents do shapes what they expect for themselves.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.b] When both partners work, someone still has to do the childcare and the housework. Role overload is what happens when one person is asked to do too many of these jobs at once.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.0] Role overload occurs when one person tries to perform several roles at the same time, for example employee, parent, homemaker and carer, and cannot meet the demands of all of them. It is about too many roles to cope with at once.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.1] Effects on the individual: less leisure and rest time, tiredness, stress and poorer health, with a risk of burnout (complete exhaustion from long-term stress) if the load continues.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.2] Effects on work: difficulty finding time for training, extra hours or promotion, and less energy for them, because the person has little left after the demands of home. There may also be pressure to take work home.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.3] Effects on the family: tension between partners and other family members, less time with children, and neglect of family and household tasks that no one has time to do.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.4] Vulnerable members, such as young children, an elderly relative or a family member who is ill, may have no one available to care for them.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.5] Women often carry a larger share of the strain because they usually remain the main carers at home, so they end up doing a full job and then the home tasks as well.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.1.6] Overload can be reduced by sharing tasks, using labour-saving appliances, buying in outside help such as a cleaner or childcare, keeping routines simple, planning meals, and using leave and flexible work arrangements.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.b] Role conflict is a different problem from overload. Here a person may be able to manage each role, but two roles pull in opposite directions at the same moment and a choice has to be made.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.0] Role conflict arises when the expectations of one role clash with the expectations of another, so that both cannot be met and one has to give way. It is two roles pulling in different directions.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.1] Example: a child becomes sick and needs a parent at home while a work deadline or meeting requires the same parent to be at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.2] Priority choice: a parent must decide which role to put first at a given moment, and that decision often causes guilt about the role that lost out.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.3] Conflict between partners: one partner may not support the other's employment, or the couple may compete over career priorities, so each feels the other is getting in the way.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.4] Conflict with the employer: an employer may expect availability, such as late meetings or travel, that clashes with school hours and childcare arrangements.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.5] Effects include stress, guilt, and tension and arguments between partners. Employment may suffer through absence or missed promotion.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.2.6] Outcome for the family: children may feel lonely or neglected, and the family may need outside help to resolve the conflict.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.b] Every household has two sets of jobs: caring for the children and caring for the home. How a dual-earner couple divides them decides whether the family copes or falls into overload and conflict.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.0] Parental responsibilities include feeding, dressing, supervising, school runs and collections, helping with homework, health care, emotional support, discipline and play, and transporting and supporting children.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.1] Home-care responsibilities include cooking, cleaning, laundry, shopping, budgeting, bills, repairs, the garden and maintaining the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.2] Fair sharing: in the modern pattern, partners agree which tasks each one owns, so that tasks are not done only when one partner asks for help. Fathers are more involved in childcare.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.3] Labour-saving appliances, such as washing machines, dishwashers, microwave ovens and slow cookers, reduce the time needed for home-care tasks, which frees time for the family.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.4] Outside help, such as paid childcare, a childminder, cleaning services, or grandparents and other relatives, takes over some parental and home-care duties.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.5] Older children can take on age-appropriate tasks, such as tidying their rooms or setting the table, which builds independence.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.6] Benefits of sharing: less overload, stronger parent-child bonds including father-child bonds, and fairer family relationships. When both parents take an active part in care, children see shared parenting as normal, which models gender equality and shows that men and women have equal roles.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.3.7] Drawbacks if tasks are not shared or are poorly managed: resentment and arguments, one partner carrying a double shift (a full day of paid work followed by the home tasks), and children given too much responsibility.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.b] Having two earners changes family life in both good and bad ways. The balance depends on how tasks are shared, how childcare is arranged and how flexible the employers are.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.0] Positive: a second income raises household living standards and reduces financial worry, and it gives security if one job is lost.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.1] Positive: both partners gain independence and self-esteem, and parents gain wider social contact through work.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.2] Positive: children may become more independent when they take on responsibilities, and they see their parents working, which gives them a role model for work and equal roles.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.3] Positive: when parents share tasks, children learn that care and housework are shared responsibilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.4] Negative: less time for shared family activities, conversation and leisure, and tiredness and stress, which can weaken family relationships. The couple may also have less time for each other.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.5] Negative: children may feel lonely, neglected or left out, especially at times such as after school or when they are ill.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.6] Negative: extra responsibility falls on older children, who may become carers for younger siblings. Less time for home tasks can also mean meals eaten out or convenience foods.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.4.7] Negative: childcare costs can absorb a large part of the second income, and the family may face role overload and role conflict.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.b] Employers and employees use working arrangements that give more control over when and where work is done, and families add supports at home. Together they make it easier to hold a job and care for a family.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.0] Flexitime: the employee chooses start and finish times within agreed limits, so that the working day can fit around school hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.1] Job-sharing: two employees share one full-time post, dividing the hours and responsibilities between them.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.2] Parental leave: unpaid leave taken to care for a child, which may be taken in blocks or spread over an agreed period. Under the Parental Leave Act 1998 (as amended) it is 26 weeks unpaid leave per child under 12.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.3] Other leave arrangements are provided under the Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004, the Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016, the Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019 and the Carer's Leave Act 2001, and force majeure leave covers family emergencies.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.4] Career break: a temporary period of unpaid leave that allows the employee to return to the same job later.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.5] Teleworking: working from home reduces commuting time and lets a parent be available around the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.6] Part-time and term-time work: reduced hours allow the employee to be at home during school hours or school holidays. Compressed working week: longer working days over fewer days free up time for family commitments.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.5.7] At home and at work: help from childcare, after-school care and relatives, sharing housework, labour-saving appliances, planning such as meal planning, shopping lists and online shopping, and family meetings to share the load. Employers can help with flexible hours, crèche facilities and understanding about family emergencies.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.b] A class survey shows how real households divide tasks and whether traditional sex roles still hold. This investigation uses a questionnaire about twenty home tasks.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.0] Aim: to find out how male and female family members share twenty named home tasks in a sample of households.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.1] Method step 1: list twenty home tasks, such as cooking, ironing, grocery shopping, car maintenance, paying bills, childminding, gardening, cleaning the bathroom and DIY.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.2] Method step 2: design a questionnaire that asks who usually does each task: mainly male, mainly female, shared or someone else.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.3] Method step 3: survey a sample of students, or their parents, and ask respondents to answer about their own household.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.4] Method step 4: keep responses anonymous, so that no family or individual can be identified and people can answer honestly.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.5] Analysis: count the answers in each category for each task and express each category as a percentage of the total answers, so you can find which tasks are mostly male, mostly female or shared.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.6] Conclusion: the results show whether the sample's home tasks are still divided on traditional sex-role lines or are shared. Compare the results with traditional roles and link them to dual-earner households.
[hom-work-leisure-s5.6.7] Limits: a small sample, bias in who answers and how honest the answers are can all weaken the conclusions.

CANDIDATES hom-work-leisure-s2: 2. Changes in patterns of work
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.b] New technology changes how work is organised, how many workers are needed and which skills are in demand. Some effects are gains for workers and some are losses, and exam answers need both.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.0] Automation (machines and robots taking over repetitive production and processing tasks) means fewer workers are needed for the same output, because the machines do the routine work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.1] Increased output with fewer workers reduces working hours in some industries, which gives people more leisure time, but it also removes jobs at the lower-skilled end.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.2] Computers and electronic communication change office work. They create new skilled jobs to design, install, maintain and operate the technology, so jobs shift towards programming, supervising and maintaining.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.3] Electronic communication allows home working (teleworking), which suits some people. The downside is that it makes it harder to switch off from work, and it reduces face-to-face social contact at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.4] Technology also makes work safer and cleaner in some settings, because dangerous and dirty tasks are carried out by machines. This means fewer injuries and healthier workplaces.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.5] Workers whose skills become out of date, or who lose unskilled and manual jobs, need retraining. Companies can also relocate production to low-cost countries, which removes jobs at home.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.0.6] Technology has also changed office and service work: banking, shopping and booking online mean fewer counter and clerical jobs and more technical ones.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.b] Employment has moved from extracting and manufacturing towards services. To describe the shift you first need to know the three kinds of industry.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.0] Primary industry extracts resources from the land and sea: agriculture, fishing, forestry and mining or quarrying (such as peat).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.1] Secondary industry manufactures and processes primary raw materials into finished goods, for example food processing, clothing, engineering and construction.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.2] Tertiary (service) industry provides services such as tourism, leisure, healthcare, transport, media, retail, education, banking and finance, hospitality and IT. Note that tertiary means services, not third-level education.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.3] Automation and mechanisation reduced the number of manufacturing and farming jobs because machines do more of the work. Service industries expanded and absorbed many workers as incomes rose and demand for services grew.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.4] Companies relocated production to lower-cost countries, causing job losses in manufacturing in Ireland. Growth of tourism, finance and technology firms added service jobs.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.5] The effects on people were that manual and unskilled workers were displaced and rural areas lost jobs, while urban areas gained service jobs. Service work brought more part-time and shift work and a new demand for qualified workers.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.1.6] Setting: compare Ireland's current occupational structure with that of fifty years ago, noting the shift from farming, factory and other manual work to service and professional work. Describe the pattern without figures.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.b] As jobs have shifted towards services and technology, employers increasingly expect higher qualifications. Education has become the way into work and the way to move up within it.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.0] Service and technology jobs need literacy, numeracy, computing and communication skills, which are developed through education.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.1] Employers use qualifications to select between applicants, so a larger share of applicants now hold them. The Leaving Certificate is the minimum for many jobs, and many need a further education or higher qualification.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.2] Qualifications widen employment options and allow career advancement, because they show an employer what a worker can do.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.3] Early school leavers and unskilled workers find it difficult to acquire work, as many jobs require a minimum level of education. They are most at risk of unemployment and low pay.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.4] Continuing education, such as evening courses, distance learning and part-time courses, allows adults to gain qualifications, keep pace with technology, improve promotion prospects or change career.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.5] Upskilling programmes (courses that build on skills a worker already has) also let adults keep their skills up to date.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.2.6] Reskilling (training in new skills) allows workers whose jobs have disappeared to move into new occupations.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.b] Many more women, including married women and mothers, are now in paid work than in the middle of the twentieth century. The causes are social and economic, and each one needs a short reason.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.0] Women are better educated than in the past, and qualifications open access to professional and skilled work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.1] Equality legislation removed barriers: the Civil Service (Employment of Married Women) Act 1973 removed the marriage bar (the rule forcing married women to leave civil service jobs).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.2] The Anti-Discrimination (Pay) Act 1974 first legislated for equal pay for equal work. Both Acts are now covered by the Employment Equality Acts 1998 to 2015.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.3] Income from paid work gives women financial independence, self-esteem, status and a stronger personal identity, as well as social contact outside the home.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.4] The growth of service industries and part-time work created many jobs that suited women's skills and flexible hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.5] Rising living, housing and child care costs, and the need for a second income, encourage women into paid employment. More marital breakdown and lone parenthood also make earning necessary.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.6] Flexible hours, job-sharing, term-time work, maternity and parental leave and career breaks make it possible to combine paid work with home and family responsibilities.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.3.7] More child care facilities, labour-saving appliances and smaller families free up women's time, and changing attitudes make work more accepted. The result is financial independence for women and more dual-earner families (both parents earning).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.b] Pay, safety and time off have all improved, which made paid work more attractive. These improvements also enabled women to take part in the workforce more fully.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.0] Better pay, including the introduction of the minimum wage, plus pension entitlement and paid holiday arrangements, make paid work more attractive.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.1] Statutory protections for workers (those set down in law) cover health and safety, pay and working time. Employment is protected by legislation, as are equal pay and equality law.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.2] Safer and cleaner workplaces, made possible by technology, reduce risk of injury and illness. Working hours are also shorter than in the past.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.3] Statutory leave includes maternity leave, parental leave and force majeure leave (urgent family reasons). Parental leave and career breaks let workers take time away for family reasons and return to their job.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.4] Facilities for working parents, such as crèches and breastfeeding breaks, make it easier to stay in work after having a child.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.4.5] Improved conditions have enabled many women to return to the workforce after raising children, because work no longer has to be given up for family life.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.b] Working time is now arranged in many ways other than the traditional full-time, fixed-hours week. These arrangements let workers fit paid work around family life.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.0] Flexitime: employees choose their start and finish times within agreed core hours, when everyone must be at work.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.1] Job-sharing: two people share one full-time post, dividing the hours, pay and duties between them.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.2] Part-time and term-time working: reduced hours, or work arranged around the school year, which suits parents of school-age children.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.3] Compressed or reduced working weeks, such as working longer days across fewer days, and teleworking: working from home or another remote location using electronic communication.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.4] Career breaks: an agreed period of unpaid leave, after which the worker returns to their job.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.5] Parental leave: under the Parental Leave Act 1998 (as amended), parents are entitled to unpaid leave to look after a young child, currently 26 weeks per child for a child under 12 (longer if the child has a disability or long-term illness).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.6] It is taken in one block or in agreed blocks, and it is not paid by the employer by right.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.5.7] Related leaves exist under other laws: maternity leave (Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004), paternity leave (Paternity Leave and Benefit Act 2016), parent's leave (Parent's Leave and Benefit Act 2019), carer's leave (Carer's Leave Act 2001) and force majeure leave.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.b] The law now gives employees a right to ask for flexible hours or to work remotely. The employer does not have to agree, but must handle the request fairly.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.0] The Work Life Balance and Miscellaneous Provisions Act 2023 provides these rights, and the flexible and remote working provisions commenced on 6 March 2024.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.1] Parents and carers of children up to 12 (or up to 16 if the child is disabled or long-term ill), and carers, can request flexible working. Any employee can request remote working.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.2] The employer must consider the request and reply in writing within set time limits, giving reasons for any refusal, so workers get a clear answer.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.6.3] The Act also provides paid domestic-violence leave (5 days, from November 2023) and unpaid medical care leave (from 3 July 2023).
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.b] Taken together, the changes in technology, industry, education, women's work and working hours affect individuals and families in both helpful and difficult ways.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.0] Workers displaced from manufacturing may need retraining before they can find work in services, so the availability of work changes for individuals.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.1] Flexible working helps parents plan childcare and family life more smoothly. It means more time with children, less stress and an easier balance of work and family.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.2] Blurred boundaries between work and home can increase stress and make it harder to switch off.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.3] Flexible working can also mean lower income, slower promotion, a shortfall in pension and resentment from colleagues.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.4] Families in which both parents work gain income but may face role overload and role conflict, because family tasks still have to be done.
[hom-work-leisure-s2.7.5] Employers gain access to a wider, more skilled labour pool and can keep staff by offering flexible arrangements.

CANDIDATES hom-family-caring-s1: 1. Roles and responsibilities in the family
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.b] A family is not the same from one decade to the next. It passes through stages, and each stage changes who does what and how heavy the workload is. A role is the pattern of behaviour expected of a person in a position such as parent or child, and a responsibility is a duty that goes with that role.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.0] 1. Couple (establishment) stage: partners set up a shared home and divide household tasks, often according to paid work, time and skills.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.1] 2. Childbearing (expanding) stage: parents take on the provider, nurturing, educating and socialising roles for the first time, and the workload is at its peak.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.2] 3. Child-rearing stage (children of school age and adolescents): parents keep these roles while children take on more household tasks and independent responsibilities, and parents move from direct control towards guidance.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.3] 4. Launching stage: grown children leave home, so parents' roles change from daily care to advice and support at a distance, and the workload eases.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.4] 5. Later life stage (a retired couple, or one partner alone): older members may move from giving care to needing it, and adult children often take on practical, financial and emotional support for them.
[hom-family-caring-s1.0.5] Roles are not fixed. They are shaped by the stage the family is in, by each member's age, health, paid work and skills, and by social attitudes. Textbooks label the stages slightly differently, so describe each one as well as naming it.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.b] Parents carry a bundle of different roles at once. These roles can be shared between two parents or carried by one, and they matter because a parent who fills them well, with firm and fair explained rules, gives children the best start.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.0] Provider: supplying food, clothing, shelter and money for the family's needs.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.1] Nurturer: giving love, affection and emotional security, so that children feel safe and valued.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.2] Educator: teaching skills, language, values and good habits through daily routines and example.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.3] Socialiser: introducing children to the norms, beliefs and behaviour expected in family and community, so they learn how to get on with others.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.4] Protector: ensuring safety, supervising children and making decisions in their best interests.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.5] Manager: planning the household budget, meals, schedules and maintenance of the home.
[hom-family-caring-s1.1.6] Disciplinarian: setting boundaries and consequences that are explained and applied consistently, because children cope better with rules they understand.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.b] Adolescents are not just receivers of care. As they grow, they take on real roles and responsibilities that prepare them for adult life, and these are best treated as growing responsibility rather than just chores.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.0] Contributing to the household: helping with cooking, cleaning, shopping and minding younger siblings, so the workload is shared and parents have more time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.1] Managing their own schoolwork, timekeeping and personal belongings, which builds the self-discipline they will need as adults.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.2] Developing independence: making decisions about friends, leisure and part-time work within agreed limits, so freedom grows alongside trust.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.3] Respecting family rules and the privacy of others in shared space, so that everyone in the home can live together comfortably.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.4] Sharing in family decisions, such as holidays or how to spend leisure time, which gives them a voice in family life.
[hom-family-caring-s1.2.5] Some adolescents earn money through part-time work, or give informal care to a sibling or grandparent.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.b] Who does what in a family has long depended on whether a person is male or female. These expectations have changed a great deal, but they have not disappeared.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.0] Traditional segregated roles (separate, with each partner's tasks kept apart) assigned men as breadwinners and women as homemakers and primary carers of children. The father earned and the mother ran the home, in contrast to joint roles, where partners share tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.1] Where women work outside the home, they often still carry the larger share of domestic work and childcare, a pattern called the double shift, or second shift (a paid job followed by unpaid work at home).
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.2] Men's roles have expanded to include direct childcare, cooking and shared decision-making, and some fathers take the primary caring role.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.3] Gender stereotypes can limit children's roles and experience, for example expecting girls to help in the kitchen and boys to do outdoor jobs.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.4] Sharing household tasks equally reduces overload and resentment, and gives children a model of shared responsibility.
[hom-family-caring-s1.3.5] Adult daughters often take on more of the care of ageing parents than sons do.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.b] Family roles have shifted a lot in recent times because society, money and technology have all changed. Each factor below matters because it changes who earns, who cares and who does the housework.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.0] Women's participation in paid employment has increased, so many households depend on two earners and domestic roles have had to be shared. Greater gender equality and changed attitudes support this.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.1] Lone-parent families have increased, so one adult often carries the provider, carer and household roles alone.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.2] Smaller families, by choice or because of later marriage and childbearing, mean each child receives more parental time and money.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.3] Greater acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce and blended families has created new parenting roles, such as step-parent.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.4] Weaker extended-family and community support means parents have fewer relatives and neighbours to share childcare and other tasks.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.5] Longer life expectancy means some families care for parents or grandparents while still raising children, a pattern called the sandwich generation (adults caught between caring for children and ageing parents at once).
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.6] Rising housing, childcare and education costs make two incomes necessary for many households, shifting the provider role to both partners. Unemployment can also reverse traditional provider and carer roles.
[hom-family-caring-s1.4.7] Longer working hours and commuting reduce the time available for family meals, shared activities and housework. Labour-saving appliances cut housework time so tasks can be divided differently, while mobile phones and the internet allow remote working and contact but also blur work and home time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.b] Role conflict arises when the demands of two or more roles held by one person cannot be met at the same time. It is common in modern families, where one adult may be a worker, parent, partner and carer all at once.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.0] Work-family conflict: a parent is needed at work during the hours a child is ill or has a school event.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.1] Role overload: one person carries too many responsibilities, such as employee, parent, carer and homemaker, for the time available.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.2] Conflict between partners over who does which tasks, or whose career takes priority.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.3] Intergenerational conflict: an adult may have to care for an older parent while still raising children at home.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.4] Consequences include fatigue, stress, less quality time with the family, reduced quality of parenting, tension between partners and guilt.
[hom-family-caring-s1.5.5] Reducing conflict: explicit agreement on the division of tasks, flexible working arrangements, shared decision-making and help from extended family or services, such as childcare.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.b] One way to see how roles have changed is a short case study or interview with an older person, asking about family life fifty to one hundred years ago. A planned set of questions gives you something to compare with family life today.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.0] 1. Decide who to interview, such as a grandparent or an older neighbour, and agree a time, so they are ready to talk.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.1] 2. Ask who earned the money in the family and who cared for the children, to show the provider and carer roles at that time.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.2] 3. Ask who did the housework and what tasks children were expected to do, to show how household work was divided.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.3] 4. Ask about family size, since the number of children affects how much care and work each person carried.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.4] 5. Record the answers accurately in your own notes, and write up only what the person told you, with no invented findings.
[hom-family-caring-s1.6.5] 6. Compare the answers with family life today, looking for the kinds of change expected: more women in paid work, smaller families, shared tasks, labour-saving appliances and more mobile adolescents.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s2: 2. Role, status, socio-economic groupings, social mobility
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.b] Everyone occupies positions in society, and each position carries a certain standing in other people's eyes. Sociologists call such a position a status, and one person holds several at the same time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.0] A status is a social position, such as mother, teacher, employee or citizen. One person holds several at once, for example daughter, student, goalkeeper and citizen, so a status describes one part of who a person is, not the whole person.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.1] An ascribed status is given at birth or without choice, for example sex, age, ethnic origin or the family into which a person is born.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.2] An achieved status is gained through effort, choice or ability, for example a qualification, a job or a marriage.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.3] Being a parent or a spouse is usually treated as achieved because it follows from choices a person makes. Social class at birth is ascribed, but class in adulthood is mostly achieved through occupation.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.4] A master status is a status that dominates how others see a person and outweighs the person's other statuses, for example being a serving prisoner.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.5] Status can be based on occupation, education, wealth, family background or achievement. In a society that rewards achievement a person can raise their status, which links directly to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.0.6] Societies that allocate status mainly by birth are described as closed; societies that allocate it mainly by achievement are described as open.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.b] A status tells you where a person stands; a role tells you how they are expected to behave there. Roles are learned through socialisation (the process of learning the ways of a society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.0] A role is the set of behaviours and expectations attached to a status: the rights, duties and behaviour considered appropriate for a person according to their position in society. Examples include teacher, student, adult, child and parent.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.1] Status and role go together. A teacher (the status) is expected to prepare lessons, mark work and treat pupils fairly (the role). Likewise, the role of a student includes attending class, completing homework and respecting the teacher.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.2] A person plays many roles at once, such as mother, employee, daughter and club member.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.3] Roles change over the life course: a person moves from the role of child to adolescent to adult, and each stage brings different expectations. They also change over time, for example gender roles within the family.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.4] Role conflict occurs when the demands of two roles are incompatible, so meeting one means failing the other.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.5] For example, a parent who is also a manager is asked to work late when a child is ill, or a person is expected to be both a loyal friend and an honest employee.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.6] Role overload occurs when a person has too many roles and cannot meet all the demands placed on them, which reduces leisure time and increases stress. For example, a working parent also cares for an elderly relative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.7] Role conflict and role overload are most common where people hold several demanding roles at once.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.1.8] A role model is a person whose behaviour others copy, such as a parent, teacher or sports star.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.b] People in a society are not all in the same economic and social position. Socio-economic groups sort people into categories according to that position, and they show that society is arranged in layers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.0] A socio-economic group (also called a social class) is a category of people with a similar economic and social position, defined mainly by occupation and linked to income, education, wealth, housing and lifestyle.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.1] Social stratification is the division of a society into layers, a hierarchy of unequal ranks. A socio-economic scheme is one way of describing those layers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.2] In Ireland the Central Statistics Office (CSO) groups the population into social classes for the census, using the occupation of each person. The groups run from professional workers, managerial and technical, non-manual, skilled manual and semi-skilled to unskilled, with a further group for all others gainfully occupied and unknown.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.3] Occupation, not education, decides which class a person is placed in, so a qualification affects the class only through the job it leads to. Level of education is one of the strongest factors separating socio-economic groups, because it governs access to skilled and professional work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.4] Examples of occupations, one per group: doctor or solicitor (professional); manager or technician (managerial and technical); factory worker (semi-skilled); labourer (unskilled). Electrician, plumber, carpenter or motor mechanic are skilled manual occupations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.5] Factors that determine socio-economic group are occupation, income, education level, wealth, housing and family background.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.6] Socio-economic group affects life chances, meaning a person's likelihood of access to opportunities such as educational attainment, health, housing and income. It also shapes housing choices and household expenditure.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.2.7] Higher socio-economic groups are more likely to complete second level and go on to third level, while lower groups face higher early school leaving and lower expectations. This affects equality of opportunity in education.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.b] People do not always stay in the socio-economic group they start in. Social mobility is the movement of an individual or a group between socio-economic groups, enabled by education, income and occupation. It does not mean geographical mobility, which is moving house or country.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.0] Upward mobility is movement to a higher socio-economic group, for example a labourer's child who becomes a teacher, or a skilled worker who becomes a manager after completing a degree.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.1] Downward mobility is movement to a lower socio-economic group, for example after the loss of a skilled job with no replacement at the same level.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.2] There are two time frames for measuring social mobility. Intergenerational mobility compares a person's position with the position of their parents.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.3] Intragenerational mobility, sometimes called career mobility, compares a person's position at different points in their own working life, for example from apprentice to manager.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.4] Structural mobility is movement caused by changes in the occupational structure, such as the growth of professional and service jobs and fewer farm jobs, so that many people move even when their relative positions do not change.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.5] Exchange mobility is movement in both directions that balances out, so that the overall pattern of relative positions stays the same.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.6] Factors that help people move up include education and qualifications, skills and training, promotion in a job, a good income, free second-level education, access to third level (grants, free fees schemes), equality legislation, ambition and family support. Education is the main route to upward mobility in Ireland.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.7] Barriers to mobility include low family income, poor access to education and third-level education, early school leaving, discrimination, unemployment and living in an area with few employment opportunities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s2.3.8] Social mobility is more likely in an open society, where status is achieved, than in a closed society, where status is ascribed.
