TARGET hom-meal-planning-s1: 1. Food choices
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.b] Nobody picks food in a vacuum. A food choice results from several pressures acting together, and each one can be named and explained on its own, with an example.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.0] Culture: the shared customs and traditions of a group decide which foods are normal, acceptable or celebratory, and certain foods are linked with a particular country, tradition or festival.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.1] Travel, television and immigration spread foods from one culture to another, so people often eat dishes that did not belong to their own country's tradition.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.2] Religion: some faiths restrict foods, so a person's beliefs can rule certain foods out. Jews and Muslims do not eat pork, and some Hindus follow a vegetarian diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.3] Eating patterns: the number and timing of meals, snacking, eating out and whether the family eats together all shape what is bought and eaten.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.4] Irregular family routines change what is bought and cooked, because people who eat at different times tend to rely on quick foods rather than a shared cooked meal.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.5] Sensory aspects: taste, smell, appearance, texture and temperature decide whether a food is accepted, and unfamiliar flavours are often refused at first.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.0.6] Likes and dislikes, the way food is presented and early exposure to fatty, salty and sugary food shape preferences, which is why children who grow up on these foods often keep choosing them.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.b] The remaining factors are about knowledge, the body, the shops and the money in a person's pocket. Name each factor, then explain it with an example in a sentence.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.0] Nutritional awareness: knowledge of healthy eating guidelines and of food labels steers people towards lower-fat, lower-sugar and higher-fibre products, while low awareness leads to poorer choices.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.1] Health status: allergies and intolerances, coeliac disease, diabetes, coronary heart disease, high blood pressure and weight management limit or direct what a person can safely eat.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.2] Because of these conditions people buy foods such as lactose-free, gluten-free, low-cholesterol and allergy-safe products.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.3] Availability: seasonal produce, the range stocked by local shops, the distance to a supermarket, access to a car and the foods sold in a school canteen or vending machine determine what is on offer.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.4] Finance: income sets the budget. People with more money buy expensive foods more often, eat out and meet foreign foods on holidays.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.5] People on low incomes tend to buy cheaper and more processed foods, own brands, special offers and inexpensive protein such as pulses, eggs and tinned fish.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.1.6] Marketing and advertising: packaging, brand image, special offers, shelf placement, endorsements by celebrities or influencers and social media encourage purchases, and advertising to children and teenagers is especially influential.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.b] Two further influences are examined even though they are not separate syllabus factors. They fit under eating patterns, availability, nutritional awareness and culture.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.0] Convenience and lifestyle: lack of time or cooking skill increases the use of ready meals and takeaways, so busy households choose food that is quick to prepare.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.1] Eating food away from home covers restaurants, cafés, hotels, takeaways, delicatessens and pub food. More people do it because they have more money and choice, less time and less cooking skill.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.2] Health and wellbeing: people choose foods for health reasons, such as less fat, salt and sugar and more fibre, which is an example of nutritional awareness at work.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.2.3] Responsible living: people also choose foods for ethical and environmental reasons, such as organic, Fairtrade, local and seasonal foods, less food waste, animal welfare and sustainability. These choices reflect personal values and culture.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.b] Irish eating habits have changed a great deal over the last century. These changes describe the whole population over time, so they are trends such as more convenience food and less home cooking.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.0] Potatoes and bread have been partly replaced by pasta, rice and a wider range of breads, because a wider food range has become available.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.1] Convenience, ready-meal and processed foods have become more common, bringing more salt, sugar, saturated fat and additives and less fibre into the diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.2] Eating out, takeaways and fast food have increased, as has eating on the go. This includes eating food away from home in restaurants, cafés, hotels, takeaways, delicatessens and pubs.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.3] Meals have become more irregular: family members often eat at different times, so the main meal, now in the evening, is less often eaten together.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.4] Foreign holidays, television, the internet, travel, immigration and food bloggers have introduced new cuisines and ingredients to Irish diets, such as ethnic cuisines.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.5] Microwaving, stir-frying and barbecuing are now common cooking methods in Irish homes, and people also have less time for cooking at home.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.6] Greater health and nutritional awareness has encouraged more fruit, vegetables, wholegrain, organic, functional and fortified foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.3.7] Intermittent fasting and plant-based eating are recent trends, reflecting people's interest in health.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.b] Very young children do not choose for themselves. Parents decide, and they weigh up several things.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.0] Parents make most food choices for pre-school children, weighing nutrition and the food pyramid, cost, allergies and health, and the degree of processing.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.1] They also consider a portion size suited to a small child, ease of handling, packaging and labelling, and family habits.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.2] Young children often refuse new foods at first and may need to be offered a food many times before accepting it. This reluctance is called food neophobia.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.4.3] Involving children in preparation and presenting food attractively encourages them to try new foods, because the food becomes familiar and appealing.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.b] Teenagers start to make their own food decisions, and the influences on them are different from those on young children.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.0] Teenagers' choices are influenced by peer groups, advertising and social media, independence and a wish to eat out.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.1] They have money of their own from pocket money or part-time work, which gives them the buying power to choose for themselves.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.2] Body image, convenience and busy lives also play a part in what teenagers choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.3] Many teenagers prefer fatty, salty and sugary foods, which they have often been exposed to since early childhood.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.4] A teenager may choose a vegetarian diet for cultural, religious, moral or ethical reasons such as animal welfare, or for health, environmental, economic or family reasons, or because of peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.5.5] Culture and religion affect young people through foods linked with cultures, religious restrictions, travel, television, immigration and multicultural influences.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.b] Students in school or living away from home share many teenage influences, but time, money and facilities limit what they can choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.0] Students in post-primary school choose according to personal preference, cost, nutritional awareness, advertising and peers.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.1] Health status and allergies, and activity level, also affect their choices.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.2] The range sold in the canteen or vending machines, and whether there are facilities to reheat food, decide what is practical.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.6.3] Students living away or on a budget face the same factors, with limited time, money and cooking skill, so they tend to choose quick, cheap foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.b] Higher Level exams give a table or chart and ask you to comment on it. A clear routine earns the marks.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.0] Describe the overall trend first, then support it with specific figures read from the data.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.1] Compare two groups, such as parents and children or boys and girls, by stating which group is higher and by how much.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.2] Suggest reasons for differences by drawing on the factors that shape food choices, because the data only shows what people choose, not why.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.3] Relate the results to healthy eating guidelines, saying whether consumption meets them or falls short, for example whether five or more portions of fruit and vegetables a day (Department of Health Food Pyramid) are being reached.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.7.4] Comment on what the results mean for planning meals or for health and health education.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.8.b] These are practical contexts that help you practise applying the factors.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.8.0] Comparing meal patterns between countries, such as when the main meal is eaten and how many meals are normal.
[hom-meal-planning-s1.8.1] A theme day or week on the foods of a culture or period, such as a traditional Irish breakfast.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s5: 5. The Irish diet
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.b] Before the mid-twentieth century most Irish households ate a simple diet based on home-grown and home-made foods. Knowing what people ate then, and why, is the starting point for judging how the diet has changed.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.0] Potatoes were the staple food (the main food of the diet), eaten in large quantities with milk, butter and buttermilk. This made the diet high in starch.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.1] Oatmeal (porridge) and home-baked wholemeal bread, such as soda bread and brown bread, supplied carbohydrate and fibre, so fibre intake was naturally high.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.2] Vegetables were seasonal and home-grown, such as cabbage, turnips and carrots, while fresh fruit was limited. Eggs and fish were eaten where available, especially in coastal areas.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.3] Meat, especially bacon and salted meat, was eaten occasionally rather than daily, so the diet was relatively low in fat and sugar.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.4] The main meal, called dinner, was eaten at midday, and the family ate together at set times. Food was cooked from raw on an open fire or range.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.5] Processed and convenience foods were largely absent, so meals were prepared from basic ingredients.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.6] The diet was limited in variety, and poorer households could go short of food, so it was not simply 'healthy'.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.0.7] A typical day's menu then: porridge or bread and tea for breakfast; potatoes, cabbage and bacon or fish for dinner at midday; bread, butter and tea in the evening.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.b] Over the last hundred years the Irish diet has moved from basic, home-prepared food towards processed, imported and ready-made food. Each change affects nutrition, and each has reasons behind it.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.0] Refined white flour and white bread replaced much of the wholemeal bread and oatmeal, which lowered the fibre content of the diet because refining removes the fibre-rich outer layers of the grain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.1] Rice and pasta partly replaced potatoes, and processed breakfast cereals replaced porridge for many families. The variety of foods and cooking methods increased.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.2] Sugar, sweets, biscuits, cakes and soft drinks increased in the diet, adding empty kilocalories (energy with few nutrients). This made the diet more energy-dense and nutrient-poor.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.3] Fat intake rose, from butter, cream, fatty meat, processed meat, cakes, pastries and fried foods.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.4] Processed, frozen, canned and ready-made convenience foods became common, with microwave cooking, freezers and short preparation times, so fewer meals are prepared from raw ingredients.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.5] Imported fruit and vegetables became available all year round, while home-grown seasonal produce declined. Supermarkets replaced local shops.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.6] Eating out and takeaway meals became more frequent. Holidays abroad, immigration and television cooking introduced new dishes and ethnic foods.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.7] Food labelling, health information and health campaigns gave consumers more knowledge of nutritional value, so low-fat, reduced-sugar, wholegrain, organic, vegetarian and special-diet products became widely available.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.1.8] Reasons for the changes include rising incomes, urbanisation, women working outside the home, time pressures, food industry growth and advertising, refrigeration, supermarkets and education.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.b] Today's eating patterns are shaped by busy lifestyles, convenience and growing health awareness. These trends are examined separately from the changes in the foods themselves.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.0] Meals are irregular, and many people eat snacks and graze instead of eating planned meals. Meals, especially breakfast, are often skipped.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.1] The main meal is often eaten in the evening rather than at midday, and family members may eat at different times, in front of the television or on the go.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.2] Convenience and ready-made foods are chosen for speed, and microwave and barbecued foods form part of many diets.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.3] Eating out and takeaways are a regular part of many households' diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.4] Consumers show more health awareness, reading labels and choosing low-fat, low-sugar, organic, functional and plant-based foods, with allergen and special-diet products widely available.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.5] Television, advertising, social media and food marketing influence food choices, particularly for children and teenagers. Busy work and study timetables, cost, food trends and celebrity chefs also play a part.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.6] Concern for sustainability and locally produced food is growing.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.2.7] Consequences include more obesity, type 2 diabetes and heart disease, linked to poor diet and inactivity, but also improved choice and food safety.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.b] The Department of Health healthy eating guidelines and Food Pyramid are the benchmark for judging the Irish diet. Compared with them, the typical Irish diet is lower in fibre, fruit and vegetables, higher in saturated fat, and can be too high in salt and sugar.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.0] Fibre: intake is below the guidelines because fewer wholegrain foods, fruit and vegetables are eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.1] Fruit and vegetables: intakes fall short of the five to seven portions a day recommended.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.2] Saturated fat: intake is above the level recommended, from butter, fatty meat and processed foods.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.3] Salt and sugar: intakes can be too high, especially from processed foods, sweets and drinks.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.4] Fish: intake is lower than recommended, particularly oily fish. Many people also eat from the top shelf of the Food Pyramid (foods high in fat, sugar and salt) too often.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.5] Iron and calcium: intakes are relatively low for some groups, especially teenage girls and women of child-bearing age.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.6] Sugary drinks add empty kilocalories, and water is advised as the main drink.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.3.7] Strengths of the Irish diet: widespread protein intake, the availability of lower-fat products, and a good milk and potato tradition.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.b] Low fibre is one of the four malnutrition concerns the syllabus names. Here malnutrition means an unbalanced diet, not only too little food. Write each concern as cause, effect and correction.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.0] Cause: refining flour removes the bran and germ (the fibre-rich outer layers of the grain), so white bread and rice are low in fibre. Processed and convenience foods and few fruit and vegetables lower fibre, and wholegrain cereals are not eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.1] Effects: constipation, diverticular disease and other bowel disorders, and a higher risk of bowel cancer, because too little fibre slows the movement of waste through the bowel.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.2] Fibre fills you up, so less fullness is linked to overeating and obesity. Low fibre is also linked to higher blood cholesterol and a raised risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.3] Corrective measures: choose wholemeal or wholegrain bread and pasta, oats or porridge, brown rice and high-fibre breakfast cereals. Eat fruit and vegetables with their skin where possible, add pulses such as beans and lentils, and use nuts and seeds.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.4] Build up fibre gradually and drink plenty of fluid (water), so that the bowel is not upset.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.4.5] The Irish Cancer Society gives information on diet, fibre and cancer risk.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.b] Too much saturated fat, the solid fat found mainly in animal foods and processed foods, is the second concern. It is linked to heart disease, so examiners expect effects as well as causes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.0] Sources: butter, cream, full-fat dairy, fatty and processed meats, pastries, cakes, biscuits, chocolate, fried and takeaway foods, and coconut and palm oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.1] Causes: fast food and convenience foods, frying, snacking, large portions of fatty meat, and advertising.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.2] Effects: raised blood cholesterol (LDL), which increases the risk of coronary heart disease and stroke. Excess energy from fat can lead to obesity and type 2 diabetes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.3] Corrective measures: use lower-fat milk, dairy and spreads, and choose lean meat. Grill, bake or steam instead of frying, and use unsaturated oils such as rapeseed or olive oil.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.4] Replace some meat meals with oily fish, which supplies unsaturated fats. Limit the top shelf foods of the Food Pyramid and read labels.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.5.5] The Irish Heart Foundation gives information on saturated fat, salt and heart health.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.b] Iron is the third concern, and intakes are relatively low for some groups. Iron makes haemoglobin, so too little iron affects the whole body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.0] Iron is needed to make haemoglobin, the red pigment in red blood cells that carries oxygen around the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.1] Sources: lean red meat, liver, eggs, wholegrain and fortified cereals, pulses, dark green vegetables and dried fruit.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.2] Effects: iron-deficiency anaemia, with tiredness, pale skin, weakness and poor concentration. Headaches, breathlessness and poor resistance to infection can also occur.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.3] Causes: low intake of red meat and iron-rich foods, the blood loss of menstruation, and rapid growth in adolescence, which increase the need for iron. Pregnancy, dieting and vegetarian diets without careful planning can also leave intake too low.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.4] Tea and coffee with meals, and phytates (compounds in some plant foods), reduce iron absorption.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.5] Corrective measures: include lean red meat, liver, eggs, wholegrain or fortified cereals, pulses and dark green vegetables. Eat foods rich in vitamin C, such as citrus fruit or fruit juice, at the same meal, because vitamin C improves iron absorption.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.6.6] Avoid tea with meals, and take supplements only on medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.b] Calcium is the fourth concern. It does several jobs in the body, so low intake has effects on bones, teeth, muscles and blood.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.0] Calcium is needed for the formation of bones and teeth, blood clotting, muscle contraction and normal nerve function.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.1] Sources: milk, yoghurt, cheese, fortified plant milks, dark green vegetables, nuts and seeds such as sesame seeds, and fish eaten with its bones, such as sardines.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.2] Effects: poor bone growth in children, weak bones and osteoporosis (brittle bones) in later life, dental decay, muscle cramps, and slow or impaired blood clotting.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.3] Causes: low intake of milk, yoghurt and cheese (through dieting, dislike, cost, or replacement by soft drinks), and low vitamin D, which is needed to absorb calcium. Poor sunlight and little oily fish lower vitamin D.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.4] Corrective measures: have three servings a day of milk, yoghurt and cheese (lower fat) or fortified plant alternatives. Eat fish with edible bones, dark green vegetables, nuts and seeds, and add fortified foods.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.7.5] Get vitamin D from oily fish, eggs and safe sunlight, and do weight-bearing exercise. Take supplements only on medical advice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.8.b] A common activity is to compare a typical day's menu from the start of the twentieth century with one from today. The same five steps work for any menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.8.0] Step 1: list every food and drink eaten in the day, with portion sizes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.8.1] Step 2: identify the sources of fibre, saturated fat, salt, sugar, iron and calcium in the menu.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.8.2] Step 3: compare each nutrient with the healthy eating guidelines, saying whether it is too high, too low or about right.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.8.3] Step 4: recommend specific changes, such as wholegrain instead of white bread, a serving of fish, or a lower-fat dairy choice.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.8.4] Step 5: rewrite one meal or the whole day to show the changes.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.9.b] Section B often has a data question on Irish food consumption or the Food Pyramid. A fixed routine lets you pick up the marks for reading, describing, explaining and recommending.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.9.0] Read the title, the units, the years covered and the groups surveyed before drawing conclusions.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.9.1] Identify the trend by comparing the first and last data points, noting a rise, a fall or a small change, and any change of direction in between. Compare groups too, such as men and women or age groups.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.9.2] Quote the figures from the data exactly, and state which food or nutrient they describe.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.9.3] Compare the data with the healthy eating guidelines and link the difference to a health risk such as heart disease, obesity or anaemia.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s5.9.4] Suggest a reason for the trend, such as convenience foods, eating out, advertising, income or changes in family routines, and finish with a recommendation.

CANDIDATES hom-consumer-rights-s1: 1. Consumer choices
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.b] Every purchase starts with how much a household can spend. Income sets the limit, and it also decides whether a household buys what it needs or what it merely wants.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.0] Household income sets the limit on spending, so a household on a tight budget compares unit prices (the price per kilo or per item), buys own-brand and bulk items, and delays non-essential purchases.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.1] Disposable income is the money left after tax and essential bills. It decides how much is free for discretionary spending (spending that is a choice, not a necessity) such as eating out or branded clothing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.2] When income rises, households often trade up to branded or higher-quality goods; when it falls, they trade down to cheaper alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.3] Essential needs such as food, heating and shelter are bought first, and luxury wants are the first to be cut when income falls.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.4] A needs-based decision is guided by necessity and income. A wants-based decision is more open to advertising and impulse buying.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.0.5] Credit and easy-pay arrangements can allow spending above current income, and the debt they create reduces future choice because part of later income is already committed.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.b] Beyond money, choices reflect who the buyer is, who influences them and what the product offers. For a large purchase such as a washing machine, buyers weigh several of these together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.0] Personal preference, and values and lifestyle (health, convenience, ethical concerns, and cultural or religious practice), all influence what is bought.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.1] Household composition narrows choice: family size, life-cycle stage and special diets, such as a coeliac or diabetic member or an allergy, determine which goods are suitable.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.2] Time pressure pushes busy households towards convenience foods, takeaway meals and online shopping, because these save time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.3] Other people influence choice through word of mouth, sales staff, peers and current trends.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.4] For a large purchase, buyers weigh the energy rating, reliability of the brand, ease of use and design, size or capacity for the family, purchase and running cost, quality marks, after-sales service and guarantee, and whether to pay cash or use credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.5] The environmental impact of a product or its packaging is a modern factor that some consumers weigh when choosing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.1.6] Impulse buying is an unplanned purchase triggered by a display, an offer or an item at the checkout. It results from several of the factors above, such as advertising, values and income, working together.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.b] Shoppers often choose by name rather than by comparing every product. Brands and own-brand goods are the two main kinds of product on the shelf, and they differ in price and in how they are promoted.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.0] A brand is a name, logo or design that identifies a manufacturer's product and distinguishes it from competing goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.1] Branded goods are made by a manufacturer and are usually advertised nationally, so they tend to cost more than equivalent goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.2] Own-brand goods are sold under the retailer's own name. They are often cheaper because the retailer avoids the cost of national advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.2.3] Brand loyalty is habitual repeat buying of one brand, which saves the shopper the effort of comparing alternatives.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.b] Advertising is communication that informs consumers about goods and services and tries to create a want for them. It happens mostly outside the shop, in the media a consumer meets every day.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.0] Advertising makes people aware of new products and builds brand loyalty, so that consumers ask for the product by name.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.1] It also informs consumers about features, price and offers.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.2] Media used include television, radio, print, outdoor billboards, websites, social media, and sponsorship of sport and events.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.3] Celebrity endorsement transfers the image or popularity of a well-known person to the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.4] Product differentiation: advertising highlights a feature, such as a special ingredient, that sets one brand apart from similar goods.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.5] Advertising can make people feel they need products they cannot afford, and it can encourage impulse buying and the use of credit.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.6] Advertising aimed at children and teenagers is especially influential. It can prompt impulse purchases and family purchases, because young consumers have less experience of judging value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.3.7] The advantage to the consumer is awareness of choice and information. The disadvantages are that the cost of advertising is added to the price, claims can exaggerate, and there is pressure to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.b] Merchandising is the arrangement and presentation of goods in a shop to encourage consumers to buy more, and to buy more often. It is what the retailer does inside the shop, in contrast to advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.0] Store layout: goods are arranged in clearly identifiable departments with space to browse, and essentials such as bread and milk are placed at the back so that shoppers pass other products on the way to them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.1] Route design: aisles are arranged to keep shoppers moving through the store, so that they see more products and spend longer browsing.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.2] Product placement: complementary goods are shelved together, such as biscuits beside tea, so that buying one reminds the shopper of the other. Sweets and magazines are placed at the checkout as impulse items.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.3] Shelf position: premium or luxury goods are placed at eye level, where they are seen first, while cheaper products are often placed on lower or higher shelves.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.4] Special offers displayed at the end of aisles draw attention to promotional stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.5] In-store stimuli: background music that slows the pace of shopping, aromas such as freshly baked bread, good lighting and warmth, and colourful displays.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.4.6] Sampling and demonstrations let shoppers taste or try a product before buying it, which makes a purchase more likely.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.b] Pricing techniques make a price look lower, or make a purchase look more valuable, than it is. Discount offers and discount stores do lower the cost, but the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.0] Charm pricing: a price set just below a round figure, so that the item appears cheaper than it is.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.1] Multi-buy offers, such as three items for a set price, encourage consumers to buy more than they would at a single-item price.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.2] Clearance and reduction labels suggest extra value and can prompt impulse buying of goods the shopper did not plan to buy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.3] Loss leaders: a well-known product is sold at or below cost to draw shoppers into the store, who then buy other items at normal prices.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.4] Coupons and money-off vouchers reward a specific purchase and are often distributed through loyalty schemes or advertising.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.5.5] Discount offers, and discount stores, lower the cost of goods, but a lower price is not always better value, so the consumer should still compare total value.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.b] Loyalty schemes reward repeat custom with points or discounts, and give retailers information about what customers buy. They are one of the shopping systems named in the syllabus.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.0] Customers earn points or tokens for each amount spent. The points are exchanged for money off goods, vouchers or goods after a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.1] Examples in Ireland include the Tesco Clubcard and the Dunnes Stores ValueClub card.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.2] Schemes encourage saving for special occasions, such as Christmas, by building up points over time.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.3] Shoppers may spend more to reach a reward target, and points can take priority over price when they compare shops, so they return to the same shop.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.4] Card use gives retailers data on buying patterns, which helps them target offers and plan stock.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.6.5] Disadvantages: the scheme discourages shopping around, can lead to unplanned purchases, and ties the shopper to one shop. It also requires the card to be carried.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.b] Easy-pay arrangements spread the cost of a purchase over time, so goods can be bought without paying the full price at once. This is a form of credit, which has both benefits and costs.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.0] Easy-pay schemes: a retailer or utility company lets the consumer pay for goods or a bill in instalments over a set period.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.1] Hire purchase: the consumer uses the goods while paying instalments under an agreement, and ownership passes only when the final instalment is paid.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.2] Hire purchase is governed by the Consumer Credit Act 1995, which requires a written agreement showing the cash price, the cost of credit and the APR (annual percentage rate, the yearly cost of the credit), and gives the consumer a cooling-off period of ten days.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.3] Advantages of credit: goods can be used immediately, the cost is spread over time, larger items become affordable, and essentials can be bought in an emergency when cash is not available.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.7.4] Disadvantages: the total cost is higher than the cash price because of interest and charges, easy-pay can encourage buying of non-essential goods and overspending, and the debt reduces future choice.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.b] Both allow shopping from home, but the consumer cannot inspect the goods before buying them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.0] Television buying: goods are demonstrated on screen and ordered by phone or online, often with a limited-time offer that creates urgency.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.1] Mail order: goods are chosen from a catalogue, leaflet or website and delivered by post or courier.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.2] Advantages of mail order: it is convenient for people without time to shop, a wide range is available, there is no travel, and orders can be placed by post or phone.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.3] Disadvantages of mail order: goods cannot be seen, touched or tried on, delivery can take weeks, and postage adds to the cost.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.8.4] Distance purchases (those made without meeting the seller) carry a statutory right to cancel within a cooling-off period under the Consumer Rights Act 2022, because the buyer could not see the goods before ordering.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.b] Online shopping offers convenience and easy price comparison, but adds risks around payment, delivery and personal data.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.0] Advantages: access at any hour from home, easy comparison of prices across retailers, a wider range, customer reviews, and delivery to the door.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.1] Disadvantages: goods cannot be seen or handled before purchase, and delivery can be delayed or carry extra charges.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.2] Payment risk: card details can be taken through phishing emails (fake messages that trick people into giving details) or fake websites, so pay only on secure sites that show a padlock symbol and an address beginning https.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.3] Keep records of the order, so that there is proof of what was bought and paid for if something goes wrong.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.4] Overspending risk: stored card details and one-click ordering make impulse purchases quick and easy.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.9.5] Retailers collect data on browsing and buying, which can be used to target advertising at the individual consumer.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.b] Packaging does more than wrap a product. It protects the goods, carries information, and helps to sell the product.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.0] Packaging protects and preserves the product, so food stays safe and goods are not damaged on the way to the shop or home.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.1] It informs the shopper, because the pack carries the name, instructions and other details.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.2] It attracts attention on the shelf and conveys quality through colour, shape and design.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.3] It gives convenience, such as portion size and easy-open features, and it suggests quality.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.10.4] Care labels and safety or hazard symbols on non-food goods, for example on household cleaning agents, explain how to use the product and prevent accidents.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.b] Labels on pre-packed foods are a legal requirement, so shoppers can choose safely and compare products. The detail is kept short here and is consumer-focused.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.0] Mandatory food labels show the name of the food, ingredients listed in descending order of weight (the heaviest first), net quantity, a use-by or best-before date, storage conditions and instructions for use, and the name and address of the food business.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.1] Use-by dates apply to highly perishable foods and the food is unsafe after the date; best-before dates indicate quality, and the food may still be safe after them.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.2] The 14 major allergens must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold or capitals, so that people with allergies can spot them quickly.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.3] A nutrition declaration gives energy and the amounts of fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt. The per 100 g (or 100 ml) figure is mandatory, while the per-portion figure is optional.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.4] The country of origin must be shown where the law requires it.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.5] Claims such as low fat, light or high fibre may be made only where the product meets defined criteria.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.6] Quality marks and symbols help consumers choose. The Bord Bia Quality Mark for Irish food shows that a product meets a defined standard, and the gluten-free symbol and allergen emphasis help people with special diets.
[hom-consumer-rights-s1.11.7] EU food information rules are set out in Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014.
