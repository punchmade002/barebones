TARGET hom-food-preservation-s6: 6. Fermentation
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.b] Fermentation is the controlled breakdown of carbohydrates, mainly sugars, by micro-organisms (tiny living things such as yeasts and bacteria). It produces new substances called by-products, and these give fermented foods their taste, texture and keeping quality.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.0] The micro-organisms used are yeasts, bacteria and moulds, each carrying out a different type of fermentation. Yeast is a fungus, not a bacterium.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.1] Fermentation is usually carried out without oxygen and releases a small amount of energy, which escapes as heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.2] Alcoholic fermentation: the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae converts glucose into ethanol (the alcohol in drinks) and carbon dioxide in the absence of oxygen, so this type needs no air.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.3] Chemical equation for alcoholic fermentation: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, with energy released. In words: glucose gives ethanol and carbon dioxide.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.4] Lactic acid fermentation: lactic acid bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Streptococcus convert lactose (the sugar in milk) or other sugars, such as those in cabbage, into lactic acid without oxygen.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.0.5] Acetic acid fermentation: acetic acid bacteria such as Acetobacter oxidise ethanol into acetic acid, the acid in vinegar. Unlike the other two types, this stage needs a supply of oxygen.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.1.b] Micro-organisms ferment only when their needs are met. Providing or withholding these conditions is how food producers decide whether fermentation happens.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.1.0] A fermentable sugar (the substrate, the material the organism feeds on) is needed. The sugar is the starting point: yeast does not make alcohol from starch directly, so bread dough gets its sugar from flour and barley is malted first.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.1.1] Moisture and warmth are needed, because the organisms grow in liquid or moist food and work fastest at their own optimum (best) temperature. Too much heat kills yeast.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.1.2] Time is needed, and a starter culture (a known culture of the right micro-organism added to the food) is often used.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.1.3] Alcoholic and lactic fermentations need the absence of oxygen, while the acetic acid stage needs oxygen.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.b] Fermentation is useful because of what it leaves behind. Each by-product has a job in the finished food, so always link the by-product to what it does.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.0] Carbon dioxide is a gas that makes bread dough rise and gives sparkling quality to drinks such as beer and sparkling wine when it is retained in a sealed container.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.1] Ethanol is the alcohol in beer, wine and spirits, and at high concentration it inhibits spoilage micro-organisms. It evaporates during baking, so little remains in bread.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.2] Lactic acid gives a sour taste to yoghurt, sauerkraut, cheese and cultured sour cream, and lowers the pH of the food (makes it more acidic), which helps preserve it.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.3] Acetic acid gives vinegar its sharp taste and also lowers the pH, inhibiting spoilage micro-organisms.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.4] Energy is released as heat during fermentation.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.2.5] Flavour compounds formed during fermentation give cheese, yoghurt, bread and fermented drinks their distinctive flavours.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.3.b] Yeast, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, is the micro-organism behind bread, beer and wine. In each, it ferments sugar, but the by-product that matters most is different.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.3.0] Bread: yeast ferments the sugars present in flour and dough, and the carbon dioxide is trapped in the gluten network (the stretchy protein structure of dough), raising the dough.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.3.1] Bread: baking kills the yeast, and the ethanol produced evaporates during baking, so little alcohol remains in the loaf.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.3.2] Beer: yeast ferments sugars (maltose) released from malted barley, and hops are added for flavour. The carbon dioxide produced is kept in the sealed container to carbonate the beer.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.3.3] Wine: yeast ferments the sugars in grape juice, and fermentation stops when the sugar is used up or the alcohol level becomes toxic to the yeast.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.3.4] Yeast needs a supply of sugar, nutrients and a suitable temperature to grow and ferment.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.b] Milk is fermented by bacteria and, in some cheeses, by moulds. Here only the fermentation step matters: lactic acid made from lactose changes the milk.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.0] Yoghurt: the starter cultures Lactobacillus bulgaricus (now named Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus, though exams use the older name) and Streptococcus thermophilus convert lactose into lactic acid.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.1] Yoghurt: the lactic acid lowers the pH of the milk, so the milk protein casein coagulates (sets solid) and gives the thick, sour product.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.2] Soya yoghurt is fermented soya milk.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.3] Cheese: starter bacteria produce lactic acid, and rennet, an enzyme, coagulates the milk into curds (solid) and whey (liquid).
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.4] Cheese ripening: enzymes and micro-organisms such as bacteria and moulds break down proteins and fats, developing the flavour and texture of the cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.4.5] Mould-ripened cheese: Penicillium camemberti forms the white rind of Camembert-type cheese, and Penicillium roqueforti forms the blue veins of blue cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.5.b] Fermentation also produces vinegar, pickled-style vegetables and a meat substitute. Each uses a different organism, so match the organism to the food.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.5.0] Vinegar is made in two stages: yeast ferments fruit or grain sugars into ethanol, then acetic acid bacteria with air oxidise the ethanol to acetic acid.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.5.1] The acetic acid stage needs oxygen, so the liquid is left exposed to air.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.5.2] Sauerkraut (and kimchi): shredded cabbage is salted, so the salt draws out juice and favours the lactic acid bacteria already on the cabbage. They ferment its sugars while many spoilage organisms are inhibited.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.5.3] Mycoprotein (Quorn): the fungus Fusarium venenatum is grown by fermentation on a sugar-based medium in large vats (fermenters), then harvested and processed into a meat substitute.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.6.b] Several vegetarian protein foods are made by fermenting soya. Examiners have asked for these by name as fermented products.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.6.0] Tempeh is cooked soya beans that have been fermented.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.6.1] Miso is a paste of soya beans with rice, salt and water, which is fermented.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.6.2] Soya yoghurt is soya milk fermented in the same way as dairy yoghurt.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.7.b] Fermentation changes food so that spoilage micro-organisms cannot grow in it. The fermented food stays edible longer than the raw ingredient did.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.7.0] Fermentation preserves food by producing acids, ethanol and carbon dioxide that lower the pH or inhibit spoilage micro-organisms, together with a controlled population of harmless organisms.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.7.1] Examples are milk turned into yoghurt and cheese, cabbage into sauerkraut, grape juice into wine and wine into vinegar.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.7.2] Salt used in sauerkraut and other brined foods draws water out of micro-organisms by osmosis.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.7.3] Fermented foods are not sterile; the controlled micro-organisms outcompete the spoilage organisms that would otherwise grow, and some fermented foods need chilling.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.7.4] The same organisms can spoil food: a yeast fermenting jam or fruit juice, or a mould spoiling cheese. In food production fermentation is controlled and wanted.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.8.b] Fermentation has to be steered so that it makes the intended food rather than a spoiled one. Producers control what the organism feeds on, what organism is present and the conditions it grows in.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.8.0] Substrate: the food must contain fermentable sugar; barley is malted first to release maltose, which yeast can ferment.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.8.1] Starter culture: a pure culture of known strains is added in a known amount to ensure the intended product forms.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.8.2] Temperature: each micro-organism has an optimum temperature, and temperatures that are too high kill yeast.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.8.3] Hygiene: equipment is cleaned and sterilised so that unwanted micro-organisms do not compete with the starter culture, and unwanted organisms are kept out.
[hom-food-preservation-s6.8.4] Timing: fermentation is stopped when the required acidity, alcohol level or flavour is reached, for example by chilling, pasteurising or removing the yeast.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s1: 1. Moulds and yeasts
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.b] Moulds are one of the micro-organisms that spoil food. Knowing what kind of living thing a mould is explains how it feeds and why it grows where it does.
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.0] Moulds are multicellular, filamentous fungi: their bodies are made of many cells joined in thread-like filaments.
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.1] Moulds are not plants because they have no chlorophyll (the green pigment plants use to make their own food), and they are not bacteria.
[hom-food-safety-s1.0.2] Moulds are saprophytes, which means they feed on dead organic matter such as bread, cheese, jam, fruit and decaying plant material. They digest it outside their bodies and then absorb the digested products.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.b] A mould's visible body is built from thread-like filaments. Each part has a name and a job, and the parts are the labels exams ask for on a diagram.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.0] Hyphae (singular hypha): thread-like filaments that grow over and into the food and absorb its nutrients, which is how the mould feeds.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.1] Mycelium: the interwoven mass of hyphae that forms the visible growth, such as the fluffy or powdery layer on bread.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.2] Sporangiophore: an upright hypha that rises from the mycelium and carries a sporangium at its tip, lifting the spores up so they can escape into the air.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.3] Sporangium: a round, spore-filled case at the tip of a sporangiophore; it bursts open when ripe and releases spores into the air.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.4] Conidia: in Penicillium, and also in Aspergillus, spores are borne in chains at the tips of branched conidiophores (spore-bearing hyphae) instead of inside a round sporangium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.1.5] Diagram: a mould growing on a food surface, showing the mycelium spreading across the food, a sporangiophore rising from it and a sporangium at its tip; label hypha, mycelium, sporangiophore and sporangium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.b] Mould spores are almost everywhere, which is why food left out so often goes mouldy. Knowing where they come from shows how they reach food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.0] Moulds are saprophytes: they feed on dead organic matter, so they grow on decaying food and plant material.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.1] Spores are carried in the air, on dust, on food packaging and on unclean utensils, and they settle on food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.2] Soil and damp indoor surfaces act as reservoirs (stores) from which spores spread to food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.3] Already-mouldy food is also a source: a mouldy item releases spores that spread to food stored beside it.
[hom-food-safety-s1.2.4] Rhizopus is the common bread mould; it grows on bread, fruit and other moist starchy or sugary foods.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.b] Mould spores only grow into a mould when six conditions are met. Each one gives a way to stop growth, and each has a reason you can give.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.0] Food: moulds feed on the organic matter in food, such as bread, cheese, jam and fruit.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.1] Warmth: most moulds are mesophiles (they prefer moderate temperatures); Penicillium grows best at 20 to 25 °C, and cold slows growth.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.2] Moisture: moulds need moisture to grow, so frozen and very dry foods are less at risk.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.3] Oxygen: moulds are aerobic (they need oxygen), so they grow on the surface of food, such as jam, or through open-textured foods such as bread.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.4] pH: moulds prefer slightly acidic conditions, around pH 4 to 6; strong acids inhibit their growth.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.5] Time: moulds need about 24 to 48 hours to establish visible growth, because the spores must germinate and the mycelium must spread.
[hom-food-safety-s1.3.6] Freezing at −18 °C inactivates mould growth, but it does not kill the mould, so growth can start again after thawing.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.b] One type of mould is often asked about in detail. Penicillium is a good choice because its features are easy to remember.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.0] Penicillium is a saprophytic mould with a green-blue, powdery appearance, which comes from its chains of conidia.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.1] It grows on bread, jam, cheese and fruit, and favours temperatures of 20 to 25 °C.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.2] Penicillium reproduces asexually only, by conidia, so it never forms a zygospore.
[hom-food-safety-s1.4.3] Heat above 75 °C destroys Penicillium, whereas freezing only inactivates its growth.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.b] Other moulds can be given as your named example. Their differences are in colour, spore structure and whether they reproduce sexually.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.0] Rhizopus (the black bread mould) is saprophytic, with fluffy white mycelium and black pin-head sporangia that hold its spores.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.1] Rhizopus reproduces both sexually and asexually, so it can form a zygospore as well as spores.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.2] Mucor is saprophytic, with white hyphae and grey sporangia, and reproduces both sexually and asexually.
[hom-food-safety-s1.5.3] Aspergillus is saprophytic and black or green; it favours 20 to 25 °C and reproduces asexually only, by conidia.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.b] Moulds reproduce by releasing spores, tiny cells that spread through the air. Some moulds can also reproduce sexually, which gives a tough resting stage.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.0] Asexual reproduction, first stage: once the mycelium is established, a hypha (the sporangiophore) grows upwards and its head forms a sporangium, or in some moulds chains of conidia.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.1] When the sporangium ripens it bursts, releasing spores into the air, so they travel to new food.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.2] A spore that lands on suitable food germinates (starts to grow) and develops a new mycelium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.3] Sexual reproduction (Rhizopus and Mucor): two compatible hyphae grow beside each other and fuse.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.4] The dividing wall between the fused hyphae breaks down and a thick-walled zygospore forms.
[hom-food-safety-s1.6.5] The zygospore protects the spores until conditions are suitable; then it germinates, hyphae grow out and spores are released.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.b] Yeasts are single-celled fungi. Each cell is a single oval or round unit, larger and more regular in shape than a bacterium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.0] Yeast is a eukaryote, meaning its cell has a true nucleus; it is a fungus, not a bacterium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.1] Cell wall: a rigid outer layer that gives the cell its shape, so it holds together, and protects it.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.2] Cell membrane: a thin layer inside the cell wall that controls which substances pass in and out, so the cell takes in food and keeps unwanted substances out.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.3] Nucleus: a membrane-bound structure containing the genetic material, so the cell is a eukaryote, for example unlike a bacterium.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.4] Vacuole: a fluid-filled storage space within the cytoplasm.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.5] Granular cytoplasm: the jelly-like contents of the cell, containing food reserves stored as granules.
[hom-food-safety-s1.7.6] Diagram: a yeast cell labelled cell wall, cell membrane, nucleus, vacuole and granular cytoplasm.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.b] Yeasts live wherever sugar and moisture are found. They reproduce in a different way from moulds, by budding rather than by spores.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.0] Yeasts occur naturally on the skins of fruit, especially grapes, and in soil, in the air, on plant surfaces and on sugary foods.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.1] Yeasts need sugar as food, moisture, warmth and time to grow.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.2] Yeast is facultative, meaning it can do either: it respires with oxygen, or ferments sugar when oxygen is absent.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.3] In fermentation, yeast converts sugar into carbon dioxide and alcohol.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.4] Reproduction is asexual, by budding: a small bud grows from the parent cell, enlarges and breaks away as a new cell.
[hom-food-safety-s1.8.5] Yeast spoils sugary foods such as juices, jam and syrups by fermenting them. Baker's and brewer's yeast is Saccharomyces cerevisiae.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.b] Bacteria are prokaryotes: single cells with no true nucleus, so their genetic material lies free in the cytoplasm. This is the main contrast with fungi, whose cells have a nucleus.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.0] Cell wall: a rigid layer, made of peptidoglycan, that gives the bacterial cell its shape and protects it.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.1] Cell membrane: controls the movement of substances in and out of the cell.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.2] Cytoplasm: the jelly-like interior where the cell's chemical reactions take place.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.3] Ribosomes: small structures in the cytoplasm where proteins are made, so the cell can build and repair itself.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.4] Flagella: whip-like tails that rotate to move the bacterium; not all bacteria have them.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.5] Nucleoid: the region containing the single circular loop of DNA, lying free in the cytoplasm; plasmids are small extra loops of DNA.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.6] Bacteria reproduce asexually by binary fission, where one cell divides in two; under ideal conditions some divide about every 20 minutes.
[hom-food-safety-s1.9.7] Diagram: a bacterial cell labelled cell wall, cell membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes, flagella and nucleoid.

CANDIDATES hom-food-safety-s4: 4. Uses of micro-organisms in food production
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.b] Not all micro-organisms spoil food. Some are added on purpose, because their feeding changes the food in useful ways, and this process is called fermentation.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.0] Fermentation is the breakdown of organic substances, such as sugars, by bacteria or yeast, usually in the absence or shortage of oxygen. It is used to make many foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.1] The by-products of fermentation include carbon dioxide, energy, acids (such as lactic acid and acetic acid) and alcohol, which is why fermented foods taste and behave differently from the raw ingredients.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.2] In lactic acid fermentation, bacteria turn sugars such as lactose (the sugar in milk) into lactic acid. This is the basis of yoghurt and cheese.
[hom-food-safety-s4.0.3] In alcoholic fermentation, yeast turns sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide. This is the basis of bread, beer and wine.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.b] Milk contains the sugar lactose, which lactic acid bacteria feed on. Their acid changes the milk's flavour and texture, which is how milk becomes cheese or yoghurt.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.0] Starter cultures are lactic acid bacteria added to milk to begin fermentation. They ferment the lactose in milk into lactic acid, giving cheese and yoghurt their sour flavour and texture.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.1] In cheese-making, the lactic acid works together with rennet to curdle the milk and set the curd, so acid on its own is not enough. Ripening by further bacteria or moulds then develops the flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.2] Yoghurt is made by fermenting milk, typically with Lactobacillus bulgaricus and Streptococcus thermophilus. The lactose becomes lactic acid, so the milk thickens and tastes sour. Some yoghurts add other strains.
[hom-food-safety-s4.1.3] Probiotic (functional) yoghurts and drinks contain live bacteria that may benefit the intestinal flora (the helpful bacteria living in the gut) when eaten, so they are examples of functional foods.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.b] Moulds are fungi that grow as fine threads. In a few foods a particular mould is added deliberately, and each gives the food its own flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.0] Blue-veined cheese: the mould Penicillium roqueforti grows through the cheese, producing blue-green veins and a sharp flavour. Cashel Blue, made in Co. Tipperary, is an Irish example; Roquefort and Stilton are others.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.1] Surface-ripened soft cheese: Penicillium camemberti grows on the outside and gives the white rind of Camembert and Brie.
[hom-food-safety-s4.2.2] Soy sauce is produced by the mould Aspergillus oryzae, which breaks down the protein and starch in soya beans by fermentation, giving the sauce its savoury flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.b] Yeast is a single-celled fungus, not a bacterium. It ferments sugar into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and different foods make use of different parts of that result.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.0] In bread-making (including sourdough), yeast ferments the sugars in the dough and produces carbon dioxide, which makes the dough rise. This is why yeast is called a biological raising agent.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.1] The alcohol produced in the dough evaporates during baking, so bread is not alcoholic.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.2] In brewing, yeast ferments the sugars in malted barley (barley that has been germinated) to produce alcohol and carbon dioxide, giving beer.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.3] In wine-making, yeast ferments the sugars in grape juice into alcohol. Spirits are distilled from fermented liquids.
[hom-food-safety-s4.3.4] Vinegar is made by acetic acid bacteria, such as Acetobacter, which oxidise (add oxygen to) alcohol to form acetic acid.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.b] Micro-organisms are also used in meat products and as foods in their own right. Some provide protein or vitamins rather than flavour.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.0] Salami, pepperoni and chorizo are made by adding bacterial starter cultures to cured meat. The bacteria ferment its sugars to lactic acid, which flavours the product and helps to preserve it.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.1] Quorn is a meat substitute made from mycoprotein, which comes from the fungus Fusarium venenatum grown by fermentation in large fermenters. It is high in protein and fibre and low in saturated fat.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.2] Mycoprotein is a novel protein food, meaning a new kind of food produced by fermentation rather than from animals. It is from a fungus, not from yeast, bacteria or plants.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.3] Mushrooms and truffles are large fungi that are eaten directly as food.
[hom-food-safety-s4.4.4] Yeast extracts and yeast-based food supplements are used as sources of B vitamins, and bacteria are also used in food supplements.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.b] Using micro-organisms on purpose gives foods qualities that the raw ingredients do not have.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.0] Fermentation adds flavour and changes texture, for example the sourness of yoghurt, the veins of blue cheese and the rise of bread dough.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.1] Fermented foods can have a longer keeping time, because acids such as lactic acid and acetic acid are produced, for example in salami and vinegar.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.2] Micro-organisms improve nutritional value, since yeast extracts supply B vitamins and mycoprotein supplies protein.
[hom-food-safety-s4.5.3] They add variety to the diet, from cheeses, yoghurts and breads to meat substitutes such as Quorn.

CANDIDATES hom-food-preservation-s1: 1. Principles of food preservation
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.b] Food starts to spoil as soon as it is harvested or slaughtered. Preservation means treating food so that spoilage is slowed or stopped and the food can be kept, moved and eaten later, with colour, flavour, texture and nutritive value as near as possible to fresh.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.0] It reduces waste by keeping surplus produce, such as a glut of home-grown fruit or vegetables, usable for months rather than days, instead of being thrown away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.1] Because less food is thrown away, preservation also reduces the environmental footprint of food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.2] It makes food available out of season and over long distances, so perishable foods (those that spoil quickly) can be transported and stored.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.3] It makes food safer by destroying food poisoning micro-organisms or stopping them from growing, and it lengthens shelf life (the time food stays good to eat).
[hom-food-preservation-s1.0.4] It creates foods with distinct textures and flavours, such as jam, chutney, dried fruit, smoked fish and cured meat, which increases variety and choice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.b] Spoilage is not caused only by micro-organisms. Several separate processes make food unusable, and each has its own visible effect.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.0] Enzymes are proteins that speed up chemical reactions and are naturally present in food. After harvest or slaughter they carry on working, so food ripens, softens, darkens and changes flavour even when no micro-organism is present. Cut apple, banana and potato brown this way.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.1] Micro-organisms (bacteria, yeasts and moulds) grow on food, causing decay, rot, souring, off-odours and slime, and in some cases food poisoning. Moulds give fuzzy growth on bread, cheese and jam; yeasts ferment fruit juice and jam, with bubbling and an alcoholic smell; bacteria cause souring, putrefaction and food poisoning.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.2] Putrefaction is the breakdown of proteins by bacteria, producing foul-smelling products.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.3] Oxidation occurs when oxygen reacts with fats, causing rancidity (an unpleasant, stale taste and smell), and with the cut surfaces of fruit and vegetables, causing browning. Vitamin C is also lost by oxidation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.4] Moisture loss through the skins of fruit and vegetables causes shrivelling, wilting, wrinkling and loss of weight. It also dries and hardens bread and cheese.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.5] Incorrect storage temperature allows micro-organisms and enzymes to work quickly, because warmth speeds both of them up.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.6] Poor hygiene and careless handling carry micro-organisms from unwashed hands, equipment, pests or raw food onto prepared food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.1.7] Chemical contaminants, such as insecticide or pesticide residues, antibiotics, metals and residues from cleaning products, can make food unsafe. Physical damage such as bruising also opens food to spoilage.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.b] Micro-organisms need six conditions to grow and multiply. Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.0] The six conditions are food (nutrients), moisture (available water), warmth (a suitable temperature), time (to multiply), a suitable pH or acidity and, for aerobic ones (those that need air), oxygen. Most bacteria prefer a neutral or slightly acid pH.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.1] Bacteria that cause food poisoning multiply rapidly in the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C, which is why chilled and hot food is kept outside it. Below 5 °C growth is slowed; above 63 °C it is stopped and most are killed by sustained heat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.2] Under good conditions one bacterial cell divides about every 20 minutes (binary fission, splitting in two), so numbers rise very fast.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.3] Moulds and yeasts tolerate acid, sugar and low moisture better than most bacteria, so they spoil jam and dried fruit.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.4] Moulds appear as fuzzy colonies or threads; yeasts produce bubbling, gas and a sour or alcoholic smell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.5] Enzymes work best at a moderate temperature and are destroyed (denatured) by heat or strong acid; cold slows them but does not stop them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.2.6] Micro-organisms cannot multiply in food with little available water, which is why drying and concentrated sugar or salt preserve food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.b] Three groups of micro-organism spoil food, and spores and enzymes also have to be understood. Each acts differently, which is why different methods suit different foods.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.0] Bacteria are single-celled organisms; some cause food poisoning and others spoil food by producing acid and gas.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.1] Yeasts are single-celled fungi that ferment sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.2] Moulds are multicellular fungi that grow as visible threads and spread by airborne spores.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.3] Spores are dormant (resting), resistant forms produced by some bacteria and all moulds; they survive ordinary heat and cold and start growing again when conditions improve.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.3.4] Enzymes are biological catalysts made of protein, and each one acts on a specific substance in food.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.b] Osmosis explains why strong sugar or salt solutions preserve food. It is the movement of water through a partially permeable membrane (one that lets water through but not most dissolved substances) from a weaker solution to a stronger one.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.0] The cell membrane of a micro-organism is partially permeable, so water leaves the cell when the surrounding solution is concentrated.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.1] Loss of water makes the cell shrink and stop growing, and at high concentrations it can kill the cell.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.4.2] The same process draws water out of food, which is why salted meat and fish and sugared fruit keep. A strong sugar or salt solution outside a microbial cell draws water out, so the cell shrivels and cannot multiply.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.b] Every preservation method works by removing or controlling one or more of the conditions that micro-organisms and enzymes need, and by preventing re-contamination. There are only a few principles, and each method is an application of one or more of them.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.0] Destroy micro-organisms and inactivate enzymes with heat, as in canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching. Irradiation also destroys organisms and some enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.1] Slow or stop activity with low temperature: refrigeration slows growth and enzyme action, and freezing makes organisms dormant (inactivated, not killed) and makes water unavailable as ice.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.2] Remove available water: dehydration (drying, freeze drying) takes water away, while sugar and salt tie it up and draw it out of organisms by osmosis, as in jam, salted fish and cured meat.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.3] Alter the pH with acid: vinegar (acetic acid) in pickles and chutney, or acids produced in fermentation, such as lactic acid in yoghurt and sauerkraut and acetic acid in vinegar. This prevents the growth of many spoilage and food poisoning bacteria.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.4] Use chemical preservatives that inhibit organisms: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives such as sulphur dioxide, sorbates, benzoates and nitrite.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.5] Exclude oxygen and prevent re-entry of organisms with airtight packaging, vacuum sealing, sealed hot jars (a vacuum forms as the contents cool) and moisture-proof wraps for the freezer. Removing oxygen also inactivates enzymes.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.6] Prevent re-contamination after treatment, using sterilised jars and lids, clean utensils, good hygiene and sealing straight away.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.5.7] Most methods combine principles. Jam uses heat, sugar (osmosis), acid and pectin, and a sealed jar; chutney uses heat, vinegar and sugar; smoked fish uses salt, drying and smoke chemicals.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.b] The methods of preservation fall into groups according to the principle they use. Knowing the groups shows the structure of food preservation.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.0] Low temperature: refrigeration and freezing.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.1] Heat: canning, bottling, jam and chutney making, pasteurisation, sterilisation and blanching.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.2] Water removal: drying and freeze drying, and sugar or salt that bind water.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.3] Chemicals: sugar, salt, vinegar, smoke, alcohol and permitted additives, with sugar, vinegar, salt, smoking and alcohol being the traditional natural preservatives.
[hom-food-preservation-s1.6.4] Fermentation and irradiation.
