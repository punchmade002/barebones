TARGET hom-digestion-metabolism-s2: 2. Digestion and absorption of carbohydrates
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.b] Food carbohydrates are too big to pass into the blood, so digestion chops them into small sugar units. This is done by hydrolysis, the splitting of the glycosidic bonds (the links) that join sugar units together.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.0] Digestion turns large food molecules into small soluble ones by hydrolysis, using enzymes. Enzymes are biological catalysts, each specific to one substrate, and they work at about 37 °C, body temperature.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.1] Starch is a polysaccharide made of many glucose units joined by glycosidic bonds, so it is a very large molecule that must be broken down before it can be absorbed.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.2] Maltose is two glucose units joined together; sucrose is glucose joined to fructose; lactose is glucose joined to galactose. These three are disaccharides (double sugars).
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.3] Hydrolysis adds water across a glycosidic bond, splitting a disaccharide into two monosaccharides or a polysaccharide into smaller units. The end products are the monosaccharides glucose, fructose and galactose, the only forms small enough to be absorbed.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.4] Each carbohydrate-digesting enzyme acts on one substrate, and its name ends in -ase and names that substrate: amylase on starch, maltase on maltose, sucrase on sucrose and lactase on lactose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.0.5] Diagram: glucose unit linked to a second glucose unit by a glycosidic bond, with the water added during hydrolysis labelled, giving two monosaccharides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.b] Carbohydrate digestion starts in the mouth, where chewing and saliva begin to break down starch.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.0] Chewing breaks food into smaller pieces (mechanical digestion), which gives the enzymes more surface to work on.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.1] Organ and secretion: the salivary glands secrete saliva, which contains salivary amylase.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.2] Substrate and product: salivary amylase hydrolyses starch into dextrins (shorter chains of glucose units) and maltose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.3] Digestion in the mouth is brief, because food is chewed and swallowed quickly, so much of the starch reaches the small intestine undigested.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.1.4] Diagram: the alimentary canal, with the mouth, salivary glands and oesophagus labelled.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.b] Starch digestion pauses in the stomach, because the conditions there do not suit the enzyme that arrives from the mouth.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.0] The acid of the stomach, hydrochloric acid, denatures salivary amylase (changes its shape so it no longer works) and stops its action on starch.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.1] The stomach secretes no enzyme that digests carbohydrate.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.2.2] Starch, dextrins and maltose leave the stomach as part of the chyme (the semi-liquid mixture of food and gastric juice) and pass into the duodenum, the first part of the small intestine.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.b] Most starch is digested in the small intestine. Pancreatic juice finishes the starch, then enzymes from the intestine wall split the disaccharides into single sugars.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.0] Organ and secretion: the pancreas, a gland, secretes pancreatic juice, which contains pancreatic amylase. It enters the duodenum by the pancreatic duct.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.1] Pancreatic amylase hydrolyses the remaining starch and dextrins into maltose. It is a different enzyme from salivary amylase but acts on the same substrate.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.2] Pancreatic juice is alkaline, which neutralises the acidic chyme so the intestinal enzymes can work.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.3] The wall of the small intestine secretes intestinal juice from glands in its wall. It contains maltase, sucrase and lactase, which finish the job, and each acts only on its own substrate.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.4] Maltase hydrolyses maltose into two glucose molecules.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.5] Sucrase hydrolyses sucrose into glucose and fructose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.6] Lactase hydrolyses lactose into glucose and galactose, so the products of the disaccharidases are not all glucose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.3.7] Non-starch polysaccharides (dietary fibre) are not hydrolysed by human enzymes, so they pass into the large intestine. Fibre is the carbohydrate that is not digested.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.b] Putting the stages in order shows how starch travels from the mouth to the blood and on to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.0] Mouth: salivary amylase begins starch digestion.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.1] Stomach: acid stops amylase action, and food is mixed to form chyme.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.2] Duodenum: pancreatic amylase and intestinal juice complete digestion to monosaccharides.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.3] Villi of the small intestine: monosaccharides are absorbed into the capillaries inside each villus.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.4] Hepatic portal vein: carries all the monosaccharides, glucose, fructose and galactose, from the small intestine to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.5] Liver: converts fructose and galactose to glucose, and glucose then enters the general circulation.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.4.6] Diagram: the digestive system, labelled mouth, salivary glands, stomach, pancreas, small intestine, liver and hepatic portal vein, with where each enzyme acts.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.b] Only monosaccharides are small enough to cross the wall of the small intestine. The wall is built to absorb them quickly and has two different ways of moving them across.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.0] The small intestine is very long and its lining is folded into villi, with microvilli on their cells, so the surface area for absorption is large.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.1] Each villus has a thin wall, so sugars cross quickly, and a capillary network that carries them away. It also has a central lacteal, which is used for fat.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.2] Glucose and galactose are absorbed by active transport, which uses energy and carrier proteins and is coupled with sodium ions.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.3] Fructose is absorbed by facilitated diffusion, which moves it down its concentration gradient through carrier proteins without using energy.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.4] Monosaccharides leave the villi in the blood of the capillaries, which join to form the hepatic portal vein. The vein carries all of them to the liver.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.5.5] Diagram: cross-section of a villus showing the capillary network, the lacteal and the direction of glucose and fructose movement.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.b] Once glucose is in the blood it has several possible fates: it is used for energy, stored, or converted to fat. Two pancreatic hormones keep the blood glucose level steady.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.0] Glucose in the blood is carried to the cells, where cells oxidise it in respiration, releasing heat and energy, some of which is stored in ATP. It is the main fuel for the brain and red blood cells.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.1] Carbohydrate spares protein: because glucose supplies the energy, protein can be used for growth and repair instead.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.2] Surplus glucose is stored as glycogen, a branched polysaccharide of glucose, in the liver and muscles. Glycogen is the animal store, whereas starch is the plant store.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.3] Insulin, released from the beta cells of the pancreas, promotes the uptake of glucose into cells and its storage as glycogen, which lowers blood glucose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.4] Glucagon, released from the alpha cells of the pancreas, causes the liver to break down glycogen and release glucose, which raises blood glucose.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.5] When glycogen stores are full, the liver converts surplus glucose into fatty acids and triglycerides, which are stored as fat in adipose tissue.
[hom-digestion-metabolism-s2.6.6] Insulin and glucagon are hormones released into the blood, not digestive secretions, so they are a different job of the pancreas from making pancreatic juice.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s2: 2. Composition, structure and classification
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.b] Every carbohydrate molecule is built from just three elements (a compound is a substance made of two or more elements, and a formula shows the atoms in one molecule). This small set of elements is what separates carbohydrates from proteins.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.0] Carbohydrates contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. They contain no nitrogen, which is how they differ from proteins, because proteins do contain nitrogen.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.1] In sugars the hydrogen and oxygen atoms are in a 2:1 ratio, the same as in water, which is why carbohydrates are described as hydrated carbon (carbon combined with water's elements).
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.0.2] The 2:1 ratio does not mean a carbohydrate contains water; it only means the hydrogen and oxygen atoms are present in the same proportion as in water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.b] Carbohydrates are grouped by the number of sugar units (saccharide units) in each molecule. One unit makes a monosaccharide, two make a disaccharide and many make a polysaccharide.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.0] Monosaccharides (simple sugars) have one sugar unit: glucose, fructose and galactose, each with the formula C₆H₁₂O₆.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.1] Disaccharides (double sugars) have two sugar units: sucrose, lactose and maltose, each with the formula C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.2] Polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates) have many sugar units joined in long chains: starch, glycogen, cellulose and pectin, with the general formula (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ. Pectin is a polysaccharide found in fruit.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.3] Starch, glycogen and cellulose are all made of glucose units, so cellulose is a polysaccharide and therefore a carbohydrate even though people cannot digest it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.4] The three monosaccharides share one formula and the three disaccharides share one formula, so they differ in how their atoms are arranged, not in how many atoms they have.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.5] Sugars (monosaccharides and disaccharides) are sweet and soluble, while polysaccharides are not sweet.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.6] Dietary language uses a simpler three-way grouping: sugar, starch and fibre (cellulose). Chemical language uses monosaccharide, disaccharide and polysaccharide, so learn both.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.1.7] Revision table: monosaccharide, one unit, C₆H₁₂O₆, glucose, fructose, galactose. Disaccharide, two units, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, sucrose, lactose, maltose. Polysaccharide, many units, (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ, starch, glycogen, cellulose, pectin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.b] A monosaccharide is the building block of all the larger carbohydrates. Knowing its formula and ring shape explains how sugar units later link together.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.0] A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit that cannot be split by hydrolysis (breaking a bond by adding water) into a simpler sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.1] Glucose has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆, which means six carbon atoms, twelve hydrogen atoms and six oxygen atoms per molecule.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.2] In solution, glucose forms a six-membered ring: five carbon atoms and one oxygen atom make up the ring, and the sixth carbon is a CH₂OH group above the ring.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.3] Hydroxyl (–OH) groups are attached to the other carbon atoms of the ring; these groups take part in linking sugar units together.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.4] Fructose and galactose have the same formula as glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, but their atoms are arranged differently (they are isomers). Fructose can form a five-membered ring, as in sucrose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.5] Diagram: glucose molecule drawn as a ring, with the ring oxygen, the carbon atoms numbered 1 to 6, the CH₂OH group and the hydroxyl groups labelled.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.6] In the usual ring diagram (alpha-glucose) the –OH on carbon 1 points down, on carbon 2 down, on carbon 3 up and on carbon 4 down, with CH₂OH up on carbon 5.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.2.7] A simpler ring of C, C, C, C and O with H and OH groups and a CH₂OH group is also accepted.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.b] Sugar units are joined by a reaction that gives off water. Repeating it once makes a disaccharide, and repeating it many times makes a polysaccharide, a polymer (a long molecule built from many repeating units).
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.0] Two monosaccharides join by a condensation reaction, in which one molecule of water is eliminated as the bond forms: C₆H₁₂O₆ + C₆H₁₂O₆ → C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ + H₂O.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.1] The bond between two sugar units is called a glycosidic link, the –O– bridge formed where the water is lost.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.2] Glucose + glucose gives maltose; glucose + fructose gives sucrose; glucose + galactose gives lactose. Fructose is not in lactose or maltose.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.3] Polysaccharides form in the same way: many monosaccharide units (glucose) join in a long chain, with a molecule of water lost at each link. Chains may be straight or branched.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.4] Starch is a mixture of amylose, which has straight chains of glucose, and amylopectin, which has branched chains.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.5] Glycogen is a highly branched polymer of glucose, the form in which animals store carbohydrate, in the liver and muscles.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.6] Cellulose has straight, unbranched chains of glucose units held side by side by hydrogen bonds, forming strong fibres in plant cell walls.
[hom-carbohydrates-s2.3.7] Diagram: two glucose units joined by a glycosidic link, labelled with the water molecule released, and the resulting maltose molecule. For a polysaccharide, draw a longer chain of blocks, one straight and one branched.
