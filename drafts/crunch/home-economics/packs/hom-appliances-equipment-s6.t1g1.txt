TARGET hom-appliances-equipment-s6: 6. A microwave oven
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.0.b] A microwave oven cooks, reheats and defrosts food using microwaves, a form of electromagnetic radiation (energy that travels as waves) made electrically inside the oven. It heats the food, not the air around it, which is why it is quick and uses less energy for small quantities.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.0.0] A conventional (solo) microwave is the basic type, used for cooking, reheating and defrosting food.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.0.1] A microwave with a grill adds a grill element, so food can be browned and toasted, which a solo microwave cannot do.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.0.2] A combination oven joins the microwave with a fan oven and grill, so it can brown, crisp and bake as well as use microwaves.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.0.3] Flatbed models have no turntable.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.b] Each part passes the energy on to the next: mains electricity is stepped up, turned into microwaves, guided into the oven and spread around the food. Learn the parts in that order.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.0] Transformer: increases the domestic mains voltage of 230 V to the much higher voltage the magnetron needs. It changes the voltage, not the frequency.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.1] Magnetron: converts high-voltage electrical energy into microwave (electromagnetic) energy, at a frequency of about 2.45 GHz.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.2] Waveguide: a metal channel that carries the microwaves from the magnetron into the metal cooking cavity.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.3] Mode stirrer (stirrer fan): a rotating metal blade or fan that scatters the microwaves around the cavity, so they are not concentrated in one spot.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.4] Turntable: a rotating glass plate that moves the food through areas of high and low microwave energy, so that it is heated more evenly.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.5] Metal cavity: a metal-lined (enamelled steel) box that reflects the microwaves, so they stay in the oven and bounce around the food.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.6] Door and safety: a glass door with a perforated metal screen, whose holes are much smaller than the wavelength, so microwaves cannot escape but you can see in. A safety seal surrounds the door, and a door interlock (a switch) stops microwave production as soon as the door is opened.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.7] Controls and timer: you choose the power level and cooking time, and the timer stops the magnetron when the time has elapsed. A child lock, an interior light and a ventilation fan that cools the magnetron are also fitted.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.1.8] Diagram: a labelled cross-section of a microwave oven showing the transformer, magnetron, waveguide, mode stirrer, turntable, cavity and door interlock.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.b] The oven turns electricity into microwaves, delivers them to the food, and the food itself turns them into heat. The working principle follows the parts in order.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.0] 1. The transformer raises the mains voltage, and the magnetron converts the electrical energy into microwaves when the oven is switched on.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.1] 2. The waveguide directs the microwaves into the cavity, where metal walls reflect them and the mode stirrer or turntable spreads them through the food so it is hit evenly.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.2] 3. Glass, ceramic, paper and most plastics let microwaves pass through, food absorbs them (mainly the water, and also fats and sugars), and metal reflects them.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.3] 4. The penetration depth of microwaves in food is only a few centimetres, so they heat its outer layers.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.4] 5. Water molecules are polar (they have a positive and a negative end), so the rapidly alternating field makes them rotate and vibrate; the resulting friction produces heat.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.5] 6. Heat then moves towards the centre of the food by conduction, which is why thick food may not heat through evenly and why standing time is needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.6] 7. The oven switches off when the timer finishes or the door opens. Food does not become radioactive.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.2.7] Food does not brown because its surface does not get hot enough: the evaporating water keeps the surface near 100 °C, and browning needs a higher temperature. A grill, a combination mode or a browning dish is needed to brown.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.b] Each guideline exists because of how microwaves behave, so give a reason with every one you write.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.0] Containers: use only containers labelled microwave-safe, such as glass, ceramic, paper or suitable plastic, because some plastics are not microwave-safe.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.1] Never use metal containers, foil or dishes with metallic trim, because metal reflects microwaves and can cause arcing (sparks) and damage the oven.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.2] Pierce the skins of potatoes, sausages, egg yolks, tomatoes and other foods with skins, so that steam can escape and the skin does not burst. Do not cook whole eggs in their shells, because steam pressure makes them burst.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.3] Cut food into even pieces and arrange it in a circle with the thicker parts towards the outside, since the outer parts heat first. Do not pile food up.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.4] Cover food (a lid or microwave-safe film with a vent) to retain moisture and prevent splashing, leaving a gap for steam to escape.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.5] Stir, turn or rearrange food during cooking so that heat spreads evenly through it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.6] Timing: follow the times in the instructions and start with short times, adding more if needed. Allow standing time after cooking, so that heat continues to conduct to the centre of the food.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.7] Use the defrost setting or a lower power level for thawing frozen food, turn and break up the food, and cook it straight after defrosting.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.3.8] Check that reheated food, especially poultry, minced meat and other high-risk food, is piping hot throughout, to destroy bacteria.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.4.b] A microwave is simple and safe when used properly, and it lasts longer when it is kept clean and undamaged.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.4.0] Safety: never run the oven empty, and do not use it if the door, seal or hinges are damaged. Use oven gloves, open covers away from the face to avoid steam burns, stir liquids before use because they can overheat in the container, and keep children from using it unsupervised.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.4.1] Care: unplug before cleaning, wipe the cavity, door seal and turntable with a damp cloth and mild detergent after spills, and do not use abrasives. Steam-cleaning with a bowl of water and lemon juice loosens food, and the vents must be kept clear.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.4.2] When choosing a microwave, consider initial and running cost, wattage and capacity, type (solo, grill or combination), pre-set programmes and features, size and space, ease of cleaning, safety features, and the guarantee and after-sales service.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.b] Modern microwaves add controls that take the guesswork out of power and timing, and extra heating methods that fix the oven's main weakness, no browning.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.0] Variable power settings allow delicate foods to be cooked at reduced power, and allow thawing at defrost power. Inverter technology gives a steady low power.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.1] Auto weight defrost: the user enters the weight of the food and the oven sets the power and time.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.2] Pre-set or sensor programmes for foods such as vegetables, popcorn or reheat remove the need to choose time and power manually. Sensor models detect steam from the food and adjust the cooking time automatically.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.3] A jet power, quick-start or quick-reheat button heats food rapidly at full power.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.4] A temperature probe inserted into the food switches the oven off when the set internal temperature is reached.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.5] A combination oven adds a grill or convection oven, so food can brown and crisp. A crisping dish and, in some models, a steam function are also available.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.6] A child lock stops the oven being started by children, and a keep-warm setting holds cooked food at a low temperature. An interior light and low standby use are also features.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.7] A digital control panel, display, clock, timer, memory settings and recipe memory, plus smart or app control, make the oven easier to operate. Self-cleaning or easy-clean walls make maintenance easier.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.5.8] Anti-bacterial linings reduce the build-up of bacteria inside the cavity.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.b] A microwave suits modern life because it heats only the food, quickly. It also has limits, and a good evaluation gives both sides.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.0] Microwaves heat the food itself rather than the oven cavity, so no preheating is normally needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.1] Cooking time is short, so less heat escapes into the room than from a conventional oven, and less energy is used for small and medium quantities.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.2] A microwave is efficient for small and medium portions, and for reheating and defrosting, which saves time.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.3] A conventional oven may be more economical when several large dishes are cooked together.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.4] Matching the power and time to the quantity of food, and using residual standing heat, reduces energy waste.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.5] Switch the oven fully off rather than leaving its display on standby, and choose a correctly sized, energy-rated model.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.6] Short cooking with little or no added water helps food keep colour, flavour and some nutrients, and no added fat is needed for many foods.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.7] It suits busy lifestyles (reheating, ready meals, defrosting) and is simple and safe to use for children, older people and people living alone. The container can often serve as the serving dish, reducing washing-up.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.6.8] Limits: it does not brown or crisp (unless combined with a grill), it can heat unevenly and overcook edges, and it is not good for large joints or for baking in the same way.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.b] Microwaves are electromagnetic waves, and the microwave oven relies on electromagnetic induction in the transformer and on the electric field of the waves.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.0] Microwaves have a frequency of about 2.45 GHz, which gives a wavelength of about 12 cm. They are non-ionising radiation, so they do not make food radioactive. The wavelength is the basis of the cavity size and the spacing of the waves in food.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.1] The transformer works by electromagnetic induction: an alternating current in the primary coil induces an alternating voltage in the secondary coil, and the voltage ratio equals the ratio of the turns in the two coils.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.2] It is a step-up transformer, because the secondary coil has more turns. It produces several kilovolts for the magnetron from the 230 V supply, and the high voltage is rectified before it is fed to the magnetron.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.3] The magnetron is a vacuum tube. Electrons moving in a magnetic field set up oscillations in resonant cavities, and the oscillations are output as microwaves.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.4] A polar molecule has a positive end and a negative end. Water, fat and sugar molecules are polar, so the alternating electric field of the microwaves, which reverses billions of times per second, makes them rotate back and forth; friction between them converts this motion to heat.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.5] The metal screen in the door has holes of about 1 mm, much smaller than the wavelength, so the waves are reflected and do not leave the oven while you can still see in.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.6] The door interlock is a switch wired in series with the magnetron, so that microwave production stops when the door is opened.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s6.7.7] Diagram: a sketch of water molecules aligning with the alternating electric field, with arrows showing the direction of the field at two instants.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s2: 2. Heat transfer and the principles of cooking
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.b] Cooking is more than making food hot. Heat changes the food in several useful ways, and each change is a reason examiners expect you to give.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.0] Cooking makes food safe by destroying harmful micro-organisms, so food must be heated right through to the centre, because bacteria deep inside a thick piece can survive if the heat never reaches them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.1] Heat denatures protein (changes its structure), so that digestive enzymes can act on it more easily.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.2] Cooking gelatinises starch (the starch grains swell and burst in water and heat), which makes rice, potatoes and pasta easier to digest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.3] Cooking softens the cellulose in plant cell walls, so vegetables become tender and easier to chew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.4] Heat develops flavour and changes colour and texture, which makes food more appetising and gives variety in the diet.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.0.5] Cooking destroys enzymes and most spoilage micro-organisms, which extends the time food stays fit to eat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.b] Whatever the method, cooking is heat moving from a source into the food. The cook controls how much heat reaches the food, for how long, and whether moisture or fat is present.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.0] Too low a temperature or too short a time leaves food undercooked and unsafe, because the centre never gets hot enough; too high a temperature or too long a time dries, toughens and burns the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.1] Thickness governs cooking time: thin or small pieces cook quickly and evenly, while a large joint needs lower heat for longer so that the centre cooks before the outside burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.2] Moist methods prevent food drying out; dry methods need added fat, basting (spooning hot fat or juices over the food) or a covered dish to keep food juicy.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.3] Food type governs the method: cuts rich in connective tissue need long, slow cooking, while delicate foods need short cooking at gentle heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.1.4] Cooked food must be served promptly or cooled quickly, because the danger zone of 5 to 63 °C is where bacteria multiply fastest.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.b] Conduction is heat transfer through a solid from one molecule to the next, without the material itself moving. It is the way heat passes from a hot pan into the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.0] As molecules at the hot end are heated they vibrate faster and pass energy to their neighbours, until the whole piece is hot.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.1] Metals such as aluminium, copper and steel conduct heat quickly, which is why saucepans have metal bases; glass, ceramic, wood, plastic and air conduct heat slowly (they are insulators), which is why handles and oven gloves are made from them.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.2] Pan-based methods begin with conduction: the cooker heats the pan base, which heats the liquid or fat in the pan, and that then heats the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.3] Boiling, poaching, stewing, shallow frying and stir-frying all depend on conduction through the pan base.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.4] Searing a steak in a hot pan: heat conducts from the pan surface into the outer layers of the meat and then gradually towards the centre.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.5] Baking trays and cake tins conduct heat into the base of a cake, so the base sets before the top.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.6] A large solid piece of food takes longer to cook through, because the heat has to travel inwards from the surface molecule by molecule.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.2.7] Diagram: arrows from a gas flame through a pan base into the food, labelled as conduction, with the direction of heat flow marked.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.b] Convection is heat transfer through a liquid or gas by currents. The heated fluid expands and becomes less dense so it rises, and cooler, denser fluid sinks to take its place and is heated in turn.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.0] Natural convection occurs in a pot of boiling water, in a stew and in the hot air of a conventional oven, because the heated fluid rises on its own and the cooler fluid sinks.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.1] Forced convection: the fan in a fan oven circulates the hot air, so food cooks faster and more evenly than in a conventional oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.2] Deep-fat frying: convection currents in the hot oil carry heat to every surface of the immersed food at once.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.3] Steaming: hot steam rises from boiling water and carries heat to the food, and it also condenses on cooler food, releasing its latent heat (the heat stored in steam) to cook it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.4] Examples: boiling, stewing, steaming, deep-fat frying, and baking or roasting in an oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.3.5] Diagram: a pot of water with arrows showing hot water rising from the base, cooling at the surface and sinking at the sides.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.b] Radiation is heat transfer by infrared rays that travel in straight lines from a hot source and heat the first solid surface they strike. No solid, liquid or gas is needed to carry it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.0] The surface struck by radiant heat absorbs it, so browning and crisping happen on that surface while the inside of the food cooks by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.1] Grilling uses an element or flame above or below the food to heat the surface, sealing and browning it; thin pieces cook quickly because the heat is intense.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.2] Barbecuing and toasting use radiant heat from glowing coals, gas flames or electric elements.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.3] Dark, dull (matt) surfaces absorb radiant heat more readily than shiny surfaces, which reflect it; this is why foil reflects heat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.4] Radiant heat weakens with distance from the source, so the height of the grill rack is adjusted to control how much heat the food receives.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.4.5] Diagram: straight arrows from a grill element to the top surface of a piece of food, labelled as radiation.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.b] In practice most cooking uses more than one type of heat transfer at once. Microwave cooking is a special case, because the heat is made inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.0] Roasting uses radiation from the oven walls and element, convection by the hot air, and conduction inside the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.1] Boiling uses conduction through the pan base, then convection in the water, which is at 100 °C when boiling.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.2] In microwave cooking, a magnetron produces microwaves (electromagnetic waves) that make the water molecules in the food vibrate, and this makes heat inside the outer layer of the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.5.3] Once the heat is made in the outer layer, it spreads inwards by conduction.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.b] Each type of food reacts to heat in its own way, so the principles above are applied differently to each one. The right choice gives food that is safe, tender and keeps its colour and nutrients.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.0] Meat: tough cuts, rich in collagen, need long, slow, moist cooking so that collagen converts to gelatine; tender cuts can be grilled or fried quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.1] Fish: its flesh has little connective tissue and sets quickly, so it needs short cooking at gentle heat; poaching, steaming, grilling, baking and frying all suit it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.2] Eggs: egg proteins set (coagulate) at moderate temperatures, so eggs and custards need gentle heat; high heat makes scrambled egg rubbery and custard curdle.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.3] Vegetables and fruit: short cooking in a little boiling water, or steaming, keeps colour, texture and water-soluble vitamins, which would otherwise dissolve into the cooking water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.4] Cereals, rice and pasta: starch needs water and heat to gelatinise, so they are cooked in plenty of boiling water or steam.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.5] Milk and cheese: milk scorches on direct heat and cheese turns stringy at high temperatures, so sauces containing them are cooked gently.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.6] Fats and oils: fat overheats, smokes and breaks down if heated too far, so frying oil must be watched, an oil with a high smoke point chosen for deep-fat frying, and it should not be reused too often.
[hom-cooking-methods-s2.6.7] Pastry and cakes: the fat is kept cold when rubbed in, the dry heat of the oven sets the flaky structure, and the oven should not be opened early when baking a cake.

[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.b] Before buying, compare the cost, size and capacity of each model against what the household needs. A good choice is value for money, which means quality against price, not simply the lowest price.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.0] Initial cost: compare the purchase price, including delivery and installation, across several shops and brands, so you know the true cost of getting the appliance working in your home.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.1] Running cost: this is the energy and water the appliance uses over its life. Compare it with the purchase price, since a cheap appliance can cost more to run, and the energy label shows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.2] Size and space: the appliance must fit the available space and allow room for doors to open, air to circulate (ventilation) and access to plumbing and power points.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.3] Capacity: match it to household size, such as litres of fresh and frozen food for a fridge or kilograms of laundry for a washing machine, and litres for a kettle.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.0.4] Payment method: compare cash, debit or credit card and credit agreements, and the total amount payable under any credit, because credit adds to the final cost.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.b] Cost and size are not the whole story. A buyer also checks how long the appliance will last, who will fix it, and how easy and safe it is to use every day.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.0] Brand reputation and reliability: established brands with a record of durable products, good reviews and available spare parts are less likely to need early repair.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.1] Guarantee and after-sales service: check the length of the guarantee, what it covers, and how quickly repairs, servicing, spare parts, delivery and installation are available.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.2] Ease of operation and cleaning: clear controls, removable parts and filters, smooth surfaces, dishwasher-safe parts and cord storage reduce the time and effort of use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.3] Safety features: automatic cut-outs, interlocks, child lock, cool-touch surfaces and recognised safety marks reduce the risk of injury or fire.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.1.4] Family needs and modern features: choose features the household will actually use, such as programmes, timers and smart controls, rather than paying for extras that stay unused.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.b] The same criteria apply to every appliance, but how much each one matters depends on the size and price of what you are buying. Apply them to one large and one small example.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.0] Large appliances (cooker, fridge, washing machine, dishwasher, tumble dryer) are expensive and long-lived and usually delivered and installed, so cost, energy efficiency, size and servicing weigh most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.1] Small appliances (kettle, toaster, iron, food processor, mixer) are cheaper and portable, so storage, cleaning and cord length weigh more.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.2.2] Examples to use for the individual studies: a washing machine, fridge-freezer or dishwasher as the large appliance, and a kettle, toaster, food processor or iron as the small one.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.b] Design is how an appliance looks and works, and construction is what it is made of and how well it is built. Together they decide how safe, durable and easy to clean it is.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.0] Design affects safety, durability, ease of use and cleaning, and how well the appliance fits the style and colour of the kitchen.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.1] Controls should be clearly labelled, easy to reach and simple to read; large, simple controls suit older users and children.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.2] Weight, handles and grip should suit the person using the appliance, so that lifting and pouring are safe, and non-slip feet give stability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.3] Robust construction and good-quality materials, such as stainless steel for surfaces and blades, increase durability. Other materials used include heat-resistant or cool-touch plastic, glass and enamelled steel.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.4] Smooth, sealed surfaces and removable parts are easier to clean and give fewer places for food and bacteria to collect. Sealed joints and a strong flex and plug add durability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.5] Design for safety and efficiency: insulation, interlocks, automatic cut-off, energy-saving settings and quiet running make the appliance safer and cheaper to use.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.3.6] Examples of safe design: an interlock stops a machine working while its door is open, so fingers cannot reach moving parts.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.b] The energy label lets buyers compare the energy efficiency of appliances at a glance, on a common scale. An efficient appliance costs less to run and does less harm to the environment.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.0] The EU energy label rates an appliance from A to G, where A is the most efficient and G the least efficient. This scale applies to fridges, freezers, washing machines, washer-dryers and dishwashers from 1 March 2021, and to tumble dryers from 1 July 2025.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.1] The label is displayed on refrigerating appliances (fridges, freezers and fridge-freezers), washing machines, washer-dryers, dishwashers and tumble dryers. Two named appliances are enough if asked to name two.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.2] A-rated appliances use the least electricity, so they cost least to run and cause the least harm to the environment; G-rated appliances use the most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.3] The label gives annual energy consumption in kilowatt-hours (kWh), which indicates likely running cost and environmental impact. Washing machine and dishwasher labels give energy per 100 cycles.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.4] It also gives, depending on the appliance, the noise level in decibels, capacity and water use. A refrigeration label shows the fresh and frozen capacity in litres.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.5] The current label carries a QR code that links to the product's entry in the EU product database (EPREL) for more detail.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.6] Energy in kWh equals power in kW multiplied by hours used. Higher Level answers can use this to compare the running costs of two appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.4.7] Efficiency in use is also the consumer's job: use full loads, eco programmes and lower temperatures, switch off standby, and choose the correct size.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.b] A star rating on a fridge-freezer or freezer tells you how cold the frozen-food compartment is and how long frozen food keeps in it.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.0] The star rating applies to the frozen-food compartment of fridges and fridge-freezers and to freezers, not to the fresh-food section.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.1] One star: the compartment holds frozen food at −6°C for up to one week.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.2] Two stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −12°C for up to one month.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.3] Three stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C for up to three months.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.4] Four stars: the compartment holds frozen food at −18°C to −25°C for up to one year, and fresh food can be frozen in it. Only four stars allows this.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.5.5] The star rating measures storage performance, not energy efficiency, so the energy label is used to compare running costs.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.b] Marks on an appliance or its packaging tell the buyer that a standard has been met. Each mark means something different, so know what each one does and does not show.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.0] The CE mark is the manufacturer's declaration that the product meets the health, safety and environmental requirements of the European Union. It is not independent testing.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.1] The double insulation symbol, a square inside a square, shows that the appliance has two layers of insulation (a Class II appliance), so it needs no earth wire and its plug has no earth connection. It is common on plastic-bodied small appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.2] The Irish Standard mark of the National Standards Authority of Ireland (NSAI) shows that the product has been certified to a standard.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.3] An ISO 9000 quality mark shows that the maker's quality-management system has been assessed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.4] Marks should appear on the appliance or its packaging, and a mark that cannot be found on the product does not show certification.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.5] Safety marks show that a product has been tested to a standard, but they do not guarantee its performance or reliability.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.6.6] A manufacturer's guarantee or a retailer's own-brand label is not an independent mark.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.b] Before buying, a consumer gathers information from several sources. Some are independent, some come from sellers and may be biased, and some are personal experience.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.0] Independent sources: consumer magazines and websites that test and compare products publish independent results on appliance models.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.1] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC), the body for consumer protection and information, publishes guidance for consumers on their rights when buying appliances.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.2] Citizens Information offers free, independent information on consumer rights and on buying goods and services. The European Consumer Centre Ireland helps with cross-border purchases, and libraries also hold information.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.3] Manufacturers' leaflets and brochures give specifications, guidelines for use and guarantee terms, but they are promotional documents.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.4] Product labels and packaging show the energy rating, safety marks, capacity and guarantee details, so you can compare models for example in the shop.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.5] Sales staff can explain features, but their advice may be influenced by the stock and margins of the shop. Advertisements highlight strengths, so check their claims against independent sources.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.6] Word of mouth, online user reviews and in-shop demonstrations reflect other people's experience; read them critically because they can be unreliable.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.7.7] Benefits of getting information before buying: compare price, installation and running cost; find the most energy-efficient model; compare features, design, size and ease of operation; reduce the influence of advertising; avoid a costly mistake.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.b] An appliance affects the environment at three points: when it is chosen, while it is used and when it is thrown out. A responsible consumer thinks about all three.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.0] Choosing: pick the best energy class you can afford and the correct size, and prefer repairable, durable appliances with eco and low-water programmes and little packaging.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.1] Using: run full loads, use eco and short cycles and lower temperatures, switch off standby, defrost freezers, and do not leave fridge doors open, because each wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.2] Maintaining: descale and maintain appliances so that they keep working efficiently.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.3] Disposing: repair, sell or donate an appliance while it still works, so it is not wasted.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.4] Maintaining also helps because limescale and ice build-up make an appliance work harder and use more energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.5] Under the European Union (Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment) Regulations 2014, a retailer must take back an old appliance of similar type free of charge, one for one, when a new one is bought, so old appliances do not end up in landfill.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.6] Collection is on delivery if notice was given, and larger shops must accept very small items without a purchase.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.8.7] Electrical waste can also go to a civic amenity site. Never put it in the household bin, which the crossed-out wheelie bin symbol warns.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.b] A washing machine shows how the selection criteria apply to a large appliance, where cost, efficiency, size and installation all matter.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.0] Capacity: the drum size, stated in kilograms of dry laundry, should match the number of people in the household.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.1] Spin speed, measured in revolutions per minute (rpm), affects how dry clothes are after washing and so how much drying is needed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.2] Eco and low-temperature programmes reduce water and energy use, and the energy label shows the rating for the model.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.9.3] Installation needs: a water supply and drain, a suitable power point, and a level floor must all be available where the machine is placed.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.b] A kettle shows how the criteria apply to a small appliance, where capacity, cleaning and safety features matter most.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.0] Capacity is stated in litres; buy a kettle that holds the amount the household needs, not more, because heating extra water wastes energy.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.1] A concealed element in the base is easier to clean than an exposed element, where limescale collects.
[hom-appliances-equipment-s2.10.2] Automatic cut-off switches the kettle off when the water boils, and boil-dry protection switches it off if the water level is too low.

CANDIDATES hom-cooking-methods-s3: 3. Choice of cooking method
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.b] Cooking methods fall into three groups according to what carries the heat to the food. The right choice depends on the food, the eater and the result you want.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.0] Moist methods use water, stock or steam as the medium. Dry methods use hot air or radiant heat (heat given off as rays from a hot element or flame) with little or no added liquid. Fat-based methods use fat or oil as the medium.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.1] The food: tender cuts and delicate foods such as fish, eggs and vegetables suit quick methods, because they need little time to become tender. Tough, fibrous cuts need long, moist methods, which soften the fibres.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.2] Nutrition: steaming, stir-frying and grilling use little or no water and short times, so they conserve water-soluble vitamins. Boiling loses them into the cooking water unless that liquid is used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.3] Palatability (how pleasant the food is to eat): dry and fat-based methods brown and crisp the surface, adding colour and flavour, while moist methods give a soft, tender texture.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.4] Diet: steaming, poaching, boiling and grilling on a rack add little or no fat, which suits a diet that limits fat. Deep-fat frying adds fat and energy because the food absorbs fat.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.5] Consumer needs: an older person who has difficulty chewing or swallowing needs soft, moist, easily digested food, so a moist method is the better choice.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.6] Time and equipment: a pressure cooker shortens cooking time, while stewing and braising are long, slow methods in a covered pan or oven.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.0.7] Cost: slow moist cooking makes an inexpensive cut of meat tender and palatable, so you do not have to buy an expensive cut.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.b] In moist methods the food is cooked in water, stock or steam. The temperature of the liquid is what separates them: boiling is hottest, simmering is just below it and poaching is lower again.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.0] Boiling: food is cooked in rapidly bubbling water at boiling point, 100 °C at sea level. It is quick and used for vegetables, pasta, rice, eggs and potatoes, but water-soluble vitamins leach (dissolve) into the water.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.1] Simmering: liquid is kept just below boiling point, with small bubbles breaking the surface. It is gentler than boiling and is used for stocks, soups, stews and stewed fruit.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.2] Stewing: food is cooked slowly and gently in a small amount of liquid in a covered pan or casserole, on the hob or in the oven. It suits tough meat and vegetables, as in Irish stew, a traditional stew.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.3] Stewing keeps nutrients because the liquid is served with the food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.4] Steaming: food is cooked by the steam from boiling water without touching the water, in a steamer, on a plate over a pan or in a covered bowl in a pan. No fat is added, and nutrients and shape are kept. It suits fish, chicken, vegetables, rice and steamed puddings.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.5] Poaching: food is cooked gently in liquid kept slightly below simmering point, about 85 °C, on the hob or in the oven. It suits delicate foods that would break up or toughen in fiercer heat, such as eggs, fish and fruit like pears.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.6] Braising: tough meat or vegetables are browned in fat, then cooked slowly with a little liquid in a covered dish, in the oven or on the hob. The long, slow, moist cooking tenderises tough cuts.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.1.7] Pressure cooking: food is cooked in a sealed pan in which steam builds up pressure. This raises the temperature above 100 °C and shortens the cooking time. It is used for stocks, stews, tough meat and pulses.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.b] Dry methods cook food with hot air or radiant heat rather than liquid. They brown the surface, which adds colour and flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.0] Roasting: meat, poultry, potatoes or vegetables are cooked uncovered in the oven in a roasting tin, usually with a little fat. The surface browns while the centre cooks by conduction and convection.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.1] Baking: food is cooked by the dry heat of an oven, mainly by convection (hot air circulating) and radiation. It is used for cakes, bread, pastry, fish, pasta and potato dishes, as with fish pie and lasagne.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.2] Grilling: food is cooked by radiant heat from an element or flame above or below it. The heat seals the surface, so it is a fast method. It suits tender cuts, steaks, chops, thin fish, sausages, rashers, tomatoes, mushrooms, peppers and kebabs.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.3] Barbecuing: this is grilling outdoors on a grid over burning charcoal or gas, using radiant heat, which gives a smoky flavour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.2.4] Grilling lowers the fat content of food, because fat drips away from it.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.b] Grilling is fast and the heat is fierce, so food can burn outside while it is raw inside, or dry out. These rules prevent that.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.0] Preheat the grill to the required temperature so that the surface seals on contact.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.1] Oil the grid or the food to prevent sticking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.2] Use thin pieces, so that heat reaches the centre before the surface burns.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.3] Do not add salt before grilling, because salt draws moisture to the surface.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.4] Turn the food with tongs rather than a fork, so that its juices stay inside instead of running out through holes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.3.5] Use the correct cooking time, since grilled food dries out and overcooks quickly.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.b] Fat-based methods, usually called frying, cook food in hot fat or oil. They give a crisp, golden surface, but the amount of fat used varies a great deal from one method to another.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.0] Shallow frying: food is cooked in hot fat in a shallow pan, partly covered by the fat and turned once or twice, giving a crisp surface. It is used for eggs, fish, chops and pancakes.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.1] Deep-fat frying: food is fully immersed in hot oil in a deep pan or fryer, which seals the surface quickly. It is used for chips and fish in batter. The food absorbs some fat, so it is higher in fat and energy than grilled food.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.2] Stir-frying: small, thin pieces are tossed and stirred constantly over high heat in a wok or large pan with a little oil. Because the cooking is so quick, it keeps colour, crispness and vitamin C.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.3] Dry frying: food is cooked in a non-stick pan without added fat, using the fat already in foods such as bacon rashers and sausages.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.4.4] Pot roasting: meat is cooked in a little fat in a covered pan on the hob, so it cooks in its own juices and steam and stays moist. It is not done in the oven and not uncovered.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.b] A microwave oven is a fast method that suits reheating and cooking small amounts. It works differently from the other methods, so its advantages and limits are worth knowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.0] Microwave ovens cook and reheat quickly with little added fat and water, so vitamins are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.1] They do not brown food unless a grill or combination mode is used, so the surface stays pale.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.5.2] Containers must be microwave-safe, and metal must not be used.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.b] Examiners often ask you to give dishes or foods for a method. These are the examples that are accepted.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.0] Baking: apple crumble, sponge, soda bread, fish pie, lasagne, pizza and muffins.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.1] Grilling: kebabs, chicken satay, burgers, fish, rashers, sausages, tomatoes, mushrooms and bruschetta.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.2] Steaming: fish, chicken, rice, Christmas pudding (a traditional steamed pudding), fruit and vegetables. Roasting: meat, potatoes and vegetables.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.3] Frying has four types: dry, shallow, deep and stir-fry. The moist methods are boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.6.4] Classifying methods: moist covers boiling, stewing, steaming, poaching, braising and pressure cooking. Dry covers baking, grilling, roasting and barbecuing. Frying covers dry, shallow, deep and stir-frying.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.b] Steaming is a good choice for an older person because it is simple and gives soft food that is easy to eat. It also has weaknesses, so a full evaluation covers ease, nutrition and palatability.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.0] Ease: steaming needs little attention or equipment, a whole meal can cook in a tiered steamer, and it suits small portions.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.1] Texture: the moist, soft food is easy to chew and swallow, which suits an older person with difficulty chewing or swallowing.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.2] Nutrition: steaming adds no fat, so it suits a diet that limits fat. Because the food does not sit in water, water-soluble vitamins (B and C) are kept.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.3] Palatability: steamed food keeps its colour and moisture, but it can taste bland and does not brown. Overcooking spoils its texture and colour.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.4] Fish, chicken, rice and vegetables suit steaming; steamed puddings are a traditional example.
[hom-cooking-methods-s3.7.5] Herbs, lemon juice and a sauce add flavour without added salt, which suits a diet that limits salt.
