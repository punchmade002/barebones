TARGET hom-social-change-s3: 3. Education and social welfare
[hom-social-change-s3.0.b] Access to education has widened across generations, so more children and adults now complete school and continue into further study. A hundred years ago many children left school at primary level, and second-level and university education were mainly for those who could pay.
[hom-social-change-s3.0.0] Today every child has free primary and second-level education and a wide choice of routes after school, so schooling no longer depends on what a family can afford.
[hom-social-change-s3.0.1] Free second-level education was announced in 1966 by the Minister for Education, Donogh O'Malley, and has been in effect since 1967. It removed the fees that had kept many children from completing school.
[hom-social-change-s3.0.2] School attendance is compulsory from age 6 to 16, or until three years of second-level education have been completed (Education (Welfare) Act 2000).
[hom-social-change-s3.0.3] Third-level tuition fees for eligible full-time undergraduates were abolished from 1996 (the free fees initiative), widening access to colleges and universities. A student contribution (registration charge) remains.
[hom-social-change-s3.0.4] Grants to meet living and other costs at third level are paid by SUSI (Student Universal Support Ireland) and are based on means, so students from lower-income families receive more help.
[hom-social-change-s3.0.5] The free pre-school programme, the Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) programme, began in 2010 and now provides two years of free pre-school before primary school, for children of the eligible age.
[hom-social-change-s3.0.6] Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses, further education, apprenticeships and adult and community education give adults a second route to qualifications for work or further study.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.b] Free fees alone do not keep every child in school. Further supports help pupils who need extra help, who are at risk of leaving early, who come from disadvantaged areas, or who want a different route.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.0] Special needs assistants and resource teaching, for example, support pupils with special educational needs within mainstream classes, so these children can learn alongside their classmates.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.1] The School Completion Programme targets students at risk of leaving school early, through extra supports such as homework and out-of-school activities, which helps them stay on to finish.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.2] The DEIS programme (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) supports schools in disadvantaged areas, because pupils there face more barriers to staying in school.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.3] Leaving Certificate Applied is a modular, practical programme assessed over two years, so it suits students who learn by doing, preparing students for work and adult life.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.4] Transition Year and Youthreach offer practical, work-based and personal development learning to young people in different circumstances. Youthreach is for early school leavers, so it gives them a second chance.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.5] The Back to Education Allowance is an income support, not a course. It helps people on certain social welfare payments to take approved courses at second or third level.
[hom-social-change-s3.1.6] The Higher Education Access Route, HEAR, improves third-level access for socially disadvantaged students, and the Disability Access Route to Education, DARE, does the same for students with disabilities.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.b] Education changes what parents can do for their children and what a family can earn. Most effects are positive, but there are costs as well.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.0] Better-educated parents are better able to help with homework and to encourage children's progress and achievement.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.1] Educational attainment affects job prospects and earnings, so better qualifications raise the family's standard of living.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.2] Women's education has increased their financial independence and their participation in paid employment, because qualifications open up better-paid jobs.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.3] Adult education lets parents return to work after raising children, adding a second income to the household.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.4] As education widens choices, families tend to be smaller and people marry later, and there is less poverty across generations.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.5] Negative effects include the cost of books, uniforms, transport and college living away from home, and exam pressure on students.
[hom-social-change-s3.2.6] Children may leave the area for college or work.
[hom-social-change-s3.3.b] Social welfare is income support from the State, through the Department of Social Protection, to people who cannot earn enough: those who are unemployed, ill or disabled, retired, widowed, parenting alone, caring for others or on low pay. It is funded from taxes and PRSI (pay-related social insurance) contributions.
[hom-social-change-s3.3.0] Social insurance payments (contributory) depend on PRSI contributions paid while working and on a record of those contributions. Examples are Jobseeker's Benefit, the contributory State pension and maternity benefit.
[hom-social-change-s3.3.1] Social assistance payments (non-contributory) are means-tested, meaning they depend on an assessment of household income and savings, so they are targeted at households in need and paid from taxation.
[hom-social-change-s3.3.2] Examples of means-tested payments are Jobseeker's Allowance, the non-contributory State pension, Supplementary Welfare Allowance, One-Parent Family Payment, Disability Allowance and Carer's Allowance.
[hom-social-change-s3.3.3] Universal payments are paid to everyone in a category regardless of income. Child Benefit is the main example, paid for each child regardless of parental income.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.b] Several payments are aimed at families with children, because children are costly and parents with the main responsibility for them may have low or interrupted incomes.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.0] Child Benefit is a monthly payment for each child, paid up to age 16, or up to 18 where the child is in full-time education. It is universal. It was called Children's Allowance until it was renamed.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.1] The Working Family Payment (formerly Family Income Supplement) supports employees on low pay who have children, making work more worthwhile.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.2] One-Parent Family Payment supports parents who have the main responsibility for children on their own.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.3] The Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance is paid once a year, means-tested, to help families on low incomes meet the cost of school clothing.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.4] Maternity, paternity and parent's benefits support workers who take leave to care for a child, so they do not lose all income while away.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.5] Medical cards (and GP visit cards) give eligible families free GP visits and free or low-cost prescribed medicines. They depend on means or age.
[hom-social-change-s3.4.6] Other supports are the Fuel Allowance, the Household Benefits Package and the free travel pass for older people.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.b] Welfare payments give families a safety net, but they also have drawbacks and a cost. A good answer weighs both.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.0] Welfare payments reduce child poverty by giving families a regular income when a parent is out of work, ill or on low pay, so children can stay in school and have more activities.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.1] Old age pensions give older people financial independence, so they do not depend on their adult children.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.2] Carer's payments allow a family member to care for an elderly or disabled relative at home, for example without giving up all income.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.3] Lone-parent families can support their children because of payments such as One-Parent Family Payment, which replace part of a second income.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.4] Conditions attached to payments, such as job-seeking and activation requirements for jobseekers, means tests and contribution records, aim to avoid long-term dependence on welfare.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.5] Criticisms are that payments can be low relative to living costs, that people can become dependent on welfare, and that extra earnings can reduce payments, a trap called the poverty trap.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.6] Critics argue that welfare can discourage people from taking up low-paid work, which is why work incentives form part of many schemes.
[hom-social-change-s3.5.7] Some people feel stigma in claiming, and welfare is a cost to taxpayers because it is funded from taxes and PRSI.
[hom-social-change-s3.6.b] Comparing who attended school a hundred years ago with today shows how education has changed within a family's own history.
[hom-social-change-s3.6.0] Ask an older relative how many years they attended school, how many children were in their class and whether they stayed on after the minimum school-leaving age.
[hom-social-change-s3.6.1] Record the same details for a present-day young person and compare the answers.
[hom-social-change-s3.6.2] Note which supports, such as special needs assistants, the School Completion Programme and free pre-school, were not available to the older relative.

CANDIDATES hom-education-s3: 3. The provision of education in Ireland
[hom-education-s3.0.b] Pre-school (early childhood) education is provided by private, community and voluntary services before a child starts primary school. It is not compulsory, but it gives children a head start, so the State funds some of it.
[hom-education-s3.0.0] The Early Childhood Care and Education (ECCE) scheme provides a free pre-school year to children before they enter primary school, delivered through approved providers. It is a government scheme, so it costs families nothing.
[hom-education-s3.0.1] Early Start is a one-year preventative pre-school programme for three- and four-year-olds, run in selected primary schools in disadvantaged areas. It aims to give children a positive start and prevent later school failure.
[hom-education-s3.0.2] Early Start is staffed by primary teachers working with a child care assistant, so the programme is led by trained teachers.
[hom-education-s3.0.3] The Access and Inclusion Model (AIM) supports children with disabilities to take part in the free pre-school programme, so they are not left out.
[hom-education-s3.0.4] Naíonraí are pre-schools where children learn through the Irish language.
[hom-education-s3.0.5] Montessori schools, which are generally privately run, use trained teachers and encourage children to learn through play at their own pace.
[hom-education-s3.0.6] Playschools give children of a similar age the opportunity to play together and learn to share, take turns and follow group rules.
[hom-education-s3.0.7] Pre-school builds language, social skills and the routines a child needs for the transition to primary school, so children settle in more easily when they start.
[hom-education-s3.1.b] Primary school is the first stage of compulsory education. Exams ask for the ages involved, the types of school to choose from and the alternatives open to parents.
[hom-education-s3.1.0] Children may enrol in primary school from age four and must begin by age six, because school is compulsory from six.
[hom-education-s3.1.1] Primary education is an eight-year cycle, from junior infants to sixth class, and most pupils finish between the ages of 12 and 14.
[hom-education-s3.1.2] Primary schools are state-funded or private. A state-funded school is paid for by the State but run by its patron (the religious body, Educate Together or other group that runs it), so it is not state-run and not private.
[hom-education-s3.1.3] State-funded schools may be religious, multidenominational or non-denominational. Educate Together is an example of a non-denominational school, and it is not a private school.
[hom-education-s3.1.4] Specialist types include Educate Together schools, Gaelscoileanna (Irish-medium schools, where subjects are taught through Irish), Montessori schools and single-sex schools.
[hom-education-s3.1.5] Parents may educate a child at home instead of enrolling them in a school. Home-educated children are registered with Tusla.
[hom-education-s3.2.b] Second-level education follows primary school and is made up of several programmes. Each has a set length, a particular kind of curriculum and its own form of assessment, which is how exams ask you to describe them.
[hom-education-s3.2.0] Junior Cycle is a three-year programme that uses classroom-based assessments and ends with a state examination at the end of third year, marked by the State Examinations Commission. Its award is the Junior Cycle Profile of Achievement, which replaced the old Junior Certificate.
[hom-education-s3.2.1] Transition Year is an optional year between Junior Cycle and Leaving Certificate with no state examination. It focuses on personal development, work experience and self-directed learning (students taking charge of their own learning).
[hom-education-s3.2.2] The Leaving Certificate Programme is a two-year programme that ends with state examinations at the end of sixth year.
[hom-education-s3.2.3] Leaving Certificate subjects are offered at higher and ordinary level, and foundation level exists only in Irish and mathematics. Grades are converted to points for third-level entry.
[hom-education-s3.2.4] Leaving Certificate Applied (LCA) is a two-year modular programme (made up of separate units) with practical, written and oral assessment. It suits students who prefer practical learning and can lead to Post Leaving Certificate courses.
[hom-education-s3.2.5] The Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (LCVP) has two link modules: Preparation for the World of Work and Enterprise Education. Work experience is part of Preparation for the World of Work.
[hom-education-s3.3.b] Work experience is a placement in a workplace. It lets a student try out working life while still at school, and the benefits are a common exam question.
[hom-education-s3.3.0] Work experience is part of the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme (inside the Preparation for the World of Work link module) and is a standard element of Transition Year.
[hom-education-s3.3.1] A placement lets students test their career preferences and see what a job actually involves, so they can make a better choice about their future.
[hom-education-s3.3.2] Students learn workplace co-operation and how working life differs from school.
[hom-education-s3.3.3] Work experience develops communication, teamwork, planning, organisation, punctuality and problem solving.
[hom-education-s3.3.4] Students learn the expectations of a workplace, including timekeeping, appearance and health and safety.
[hom-education-s3.3.5] Completing a placement builds confidence and responsibility, and gives the student a work record for a curriculum vitae.
[hom-education-s3.4.b] Third level is education after second level. It includes degree courses in colleges and further education courses, and students need a way in and a way to pay.
[hom-education-s3.4.0] Third-level institutions include universities, technological universities and institutes of technology, which offer full-time degree and diploma courses. TU Dublin, created under the Technological Universities Act 2018, is an example of a technological university.
[hom-education-s3.4.1] Most full-time undergraduate places are allocated through the Central Applications Office (CAO) using Leaving Certificate points, which are based on the grades a student earns.
[hom-education-s3.4.2] Post Leaving Certificate (PLC) courses are full-time further education courses, usually delivered by Education and Training Boards in second-level schools and colleges. They provide training with work experience and can give access to third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.3] The Student Universal Support Ireland (SUSI) scheme provides means-tested grants (based on family income) towards the costs of approved third-level courses.
[hom-education-s3.4.4] The Free Fees Initiative pays tuition for eligible undergraduates, so students pay only a student contribution.
[hom-education-s3.4.5] The Higher Education Access Route (HEAR) and the Disability Access Route to Education (DARE) are reduced-points entry routes for students from disadvantaged backgrounds or with disabilities, so these students can enter with fewer points.
[hom-education-s3.5.b] Adult and second-chance education lets people return to learning at any age, whatever their earlier educational level.
[hom-education-s3.5.0] Adult education courses are available full-time, part-time, modular, day, evening and weekend, and by distance learning, so adults can fit study around work and family.
[hom-education-s3.5.1] Second-chance education gives people who left school early a route to qualifications, such as Youthreach.
[hom-education-s3.5.2] Education and Training Boards (ETBs) run adult, community and further education courses; they replaced the Vocational Education Committees in 2013.
[hom-education-s3.5.3] SOLAS, the Further Education and Training Authority, funds and co-ordinates further education and training programmes across the country.
[hom-education-s3.5.4] Adult literacy schemes provide basic reading, writing and numeracy courses for adults.
[hom-education-s3.5.5] Adult education builds skills for work, supports career change, and helps adults keep pace with new workplace technology.
[hom-education-s3.6.b] Students with special educational needs are supported through a combination of planning, specialist staff and school settings. The aim is that each student gets the help that suits their need.
[hom-education-s3.6.0] An Individual Education Plan (IEP) sets out the goals, teaching approach and supports for a student with special needs, drawn up with the school and the parents.
[hom-education-s3.6.1] The National Council for Special Education (NCSE) plans and co-ordinates special educational provision, and Special Educational Needs Organisers allocate supports to schools.
[hom-education-s3.6.2] Special Needs Assistants (SNAs) provide care and support in class for students who have care needs or who need help to take part in lessons.
[hom-education-s3.6.3] Special education teachers (formerly called learning support and resource teachers) give withdrawal teaching, taking students out of class individually or in small groups, to students with learning difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.6.4] Special classes in mainstream schools, such as autism classes, have a reduced number of pupils per teacher, so each pupil gets more attention.
[hom-education-s3.6.5] Special schools provide for students with significant general or specific learning difficulties, physical disabilities, or sensory impairments.
[hom-education-s3.6.6] Visiting teachers advise and support students who are blind or vision impaired, or deaf or hard of hearing, and the teachers who teach them.
[hom-education-s3.6.7] Assistive technology, such as text-to-speech software, helps students with reading or writing difficulties.
[hom-education-s3.7.b] Beyond the school, specialist professionals assess needs and provide therapy, and the examinations themselves are adjusted for students who need it.
[hom-education-s3.7.0] Educational psychologists assess students' needs and recommend supports, which helps the school decide what help to give.
[hom-education-s3.7.1] The Health Service Executive (HSE) provides speech and language therapy and occupational therapy to children with special needs.
[hom-education-s3.7.2] The State Examinations Commission provides reasonable accommodations in state examinations, such as a reader, a scribe or extra time, so that a student's disability does not stop them showing what they know.

CANDIDATES hom-personal-finance-s1: 1. Household income
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.b] A household is a group who share a home and make shared decisions about income, spending and saving. It is the basic financial unit of the economy, because everything the economy produces is ultimately bought, taxed or worked for by households.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.0] Households spend on goods and services, which supports the businesses that supply them and the jobs those businesses provide.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.1] Households pay taxes, which fund public services and State welfare payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.2] Households buy property, and property purchases generate stamp duty, a tax that is income for the State.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.3] Households supply the workforce: children who grow up in families become the future workers, so families that teach children money management also prepare the next generation.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.0.4] Families usually run their finances independently, but they may need State aid, such as welfare payments or help from MABS, when income falls short of need.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.b] Households receive money in different forms depending on whether, and how, their members work. The main sources are wages, salaries, pensions and State payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.0] Wages: payment for hours worked or output, usually paid weekly or fortnightly, often for manual or hourly work. Because pay follows hours, a week with fewer hours means a smaller wage.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.1] Salaries: a fixed annual amount paid in regular instalments, usually for non-manual or professional work, so the pay is the same each period.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.2] Pensions: income after retirement from an employer's scheme, a personal pension fund or the State. Money paid into a pension while working is a voluntary deduction.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.3] Social welfare allowances and benefits: payments made by the State to people who are unemployed, ill, disabled, caring, retired, widowed or raising children.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.1.4] Other sources, in one line: profit from self-employment, rent from property, interest on savings, gifts and maintenance payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.b] Two households with similar jobs can still have very different incomes. Age, gender, social class, culture and employment pattern all shape how much a household earns and what it must spend.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.0] Age: income tends to rise with experience and promotion, then falls at retirement unless a pension has been arranged.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.1] Age: teenagers can earn part-time income, which eases the financial strain on the family.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.2] Gender: equal-pay law requires equal pay for the same work, but women are still under-represented in senior management, which men hold more of in many sectors. Women are also more likely to work part-time or take career breaks for caring.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.3] Social class: low qualifications or early school leaving can lead to low pay or unemployment, while higher education widens access to better-paid work.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.4] Culture: cultural attitudes shape whether women take paid work, which occupations are seen as suitable, and which spending priorities a household sets.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.5] Employment pattern: the number of family members in paid work, and whether hours are full-time, part-time, job-sharing, flexible or seasonal, all change household income.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.2.6] Disability, illness or addiction can reduce earning capacity and raise household costs, so income falls while spending needs rise.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.b] The pay a person earns is not the pay they take home, because deductions are taken off first. Knowing which figure is which matters for both exams and household planning.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.0] Gross income is income earned before any deductions are made.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.1] Net income is take-home pay: gross income after deductions have been made.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.2] Compulsory deductions from gross pay are PAYE, PRSI and USC. The law requires them, so the employee cannot choose to avoid them.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.3] Voluntary deductions are chosen by the employee and include union subscriptions, pension contributions, additional voluntary contributions (AVCs, extra payments into a pension), health insurance, savings, social club fees and holiday fund payments.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.3.4] Budgets are planned from net income, because net income is the money a household actually has to spend and save.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.b] The three compulsory deductions each work differently and each pays for something different. Between them they take money from gross pay for the State.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.0] PAYE (Pay As You Earn) is income tax deducted by the employer from each pay, based on the employee's earnings, and paid to the Revenue Commissioners (the State). It rises with income through a standard rate and a higher rate.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.1] Revenue works out the employee's credits and rate band, and the employer uses these details to calculate the PAYE to deduct.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.2] PAYE and USC go to the State to pay for public services such as health, education, roads and welfare.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.3] PRSI (Pay Related Social Insurance) is a compulsory percentage of pay, so it is related to income. It is paid by the employee, through deduction, and separately by the employer, and it funds social insurance benefits.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.4] Different PRSI classes exist, and self-employed people pay PRSI too. Employees earning below a set threshold are exempt from paying employee PRSI.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.5] USC (Universal Social Charge) is a compulsory charge on gross income, calculated at rates that rise in bands as income rises. It is separate from income tax.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.4.6] Employees with gross income below a set threshold are exempt from paying USC.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.b] A tax credit lowers the tax bill itself, so it is worth the same as an equal amount of tax saved. It is not a reduction in the pay the tax is worked out on.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.0] A tax credit reduces the amount of income tax a person has to pay. Examples are the personal credit and the employee (PAYE) credit.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.1] Income tax is worked out on gross income first, and the person's tax credits are then subtracted from the tax calculated. A credit does not reduce income before tax is worked out.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.2] Revenue works out the employee's credits and standard rate cut-off point (the income up to which the lower rate applies), and the employer uses them when deducting PAYE.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.5.3] Tax credits do not reduce the USC, which is charged separately on gross income.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.b] The State pays welfare in two ways. One is earned through PRSI contributions; the other is paid from taxes to people who do not qualify for the first, and it checks their means.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.0] Social insurance payments are paid to people who have made the minimum PRSI contributions, so the person has earned them, and they are not means tested.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.1] Examples of social insurance payments: Jobseeker's Benefit, Illness Benefit, Maternity Benefit, Carer's Benefit and the contributory State pension.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.2] Social assistance payments are paid from taxes to people whose PRSI contributions are insufficient to qualify for social insurance payments, and they are means tested.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.3] Examples of social assistance payments: Jobseeker's Allowance, Disability Allowance, Carer's Allowance and the non-contributory State pension. Carer's Benefit is the PRSI-based alternative to Carer's Allowance.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.6.4] A means test assesses the claimant's income and capital (usually including a partner's) against a set limit. The payment is reduced or refused as means rise above it.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.b] Some payments follow neither pattern exactly. A universal payment is paid to everyone who meets one condition, without a means test or a contribution record.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.0] Child Benefit: a monthly universal payment for each qualifying child. It is not means tested and does not depend on PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.1] Working Family Payment: a weekly payment to employees on low pay who have children, and means tested against a family-size income limit. It replaced the name Family Income Supplement.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.2] Supplementary Welfare Allowance: means tested, and paid to people whose means cannot meet their basic needs or those of their dependants.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.3] Supplementary Welfare Allowance can also be a temporary payment for people awaiting another benefit while their claim is being processed.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.4] Maternity Benefit: a social insurance payment for employees on maternity leave.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.7.5] Other payments a student may name: Carer's Allowance, Disability Allowance, Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance and Fuel Allowance.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.b] There are two State pensions for older people, and the difference between them is the same as the difference between social insurance and social assistance.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.0] The contributory State pension (formerly called the old age pension) is based on the claimant's record of PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.1] The contributory pension is not means tested, so other income does not normally remove entitlement to it.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.2] The non-contributory State pension is means-tested social assistance for an older person who lacks enough PRSI contributions.
[hom-personal-finance-s1.8.3] Eligibility for the non-contributory pension depends on means and on residence conditions, including habitual residence in the State (a settled, normal home there).

CANDIDATES hom-unemployment-poverty-s6: 6. Statutory responses
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.b] The State pays income support to unemployed people through two routes, and the route decides what a claimant has to prove. All social welfare payments come from the Department of Social Protection.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.0] Social insurance payments (also called benefits) depend on a record of PRSI contributions paid while in work, and they are not means-tested. They are funded from those PRSI contributions.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.1] Social assistance payments are means-tested, which means the household's income and savings are assessed, and no contribution record is required, because they are paid on the basis of need. They are funded from general taxation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.2] Jobseeker's Benefit is the main social insurance payment for an unemployed person who has enough paid PRSI contributions, so the claimant's own contributions earn the right to it.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.3] Jobseeker's Allowance is the main social assistance payment for an unemployed person who does not qualify for Jobseeker's Benefit or whose benefit has ended, so it acts as a safety net.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.4] A Jobseeker's Allowance claimant must be unemployed, capable of work, available for full-time work and genuinely seeking employment. This shows the payment supports people who want work, not people who have chosen not to work.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.0.5] Supplementary Welfare Allowance is a means-tested payment for people with no or very low income whose basic needs are not met. It also pays exceptional needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.b] Families with children face extra costs, so several payments are aimed at them. Some reach every family, and others are limited to low-income households.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.0] Child Benefit is paid for each qualifying child regardless of the parents' income. It is universal, not means-tested, so it reaches families on every income level.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.1] Payments for lone parents are means-tested, because they are aimed at those in need, and recognise that one adult is carrying the full cost of the household.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.2] The Working Family Payment (formerly Family Income Supplement) is a tax-free, means-tested weekly payment for low-paid workers who have a dependent child. It is an in-work payment, so it makes work pay and eases the poverty trap.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.3] Means-tested payments are reduced as income rises, so the household gains less from extra earnings than the wage itself.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.1.4] The Fuel Allowance is paid weekly to qualifying low-income households during the winter season to help with heating costs, which rise in cold weather.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.b] Besides cash payments, the State lowers the cost of essentials such as health, school, housing and childcare for families on low incomes. Each scheme either pays for something or removes a cost.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.0] Medical card: gives free GP visits and access to many health services, run by the HSE. Eligibility depends on income and, in some cases, health needs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.1] Back to School Clothing and Footwear Allowance: a once-yearly payment, because the cost falls at the start of the school year, that helps qualifying families meet the cost of school uniforms and shoes.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.2] Household Benefits Package: help with electricity or gas and a free television licence for qualifying older people and people with certain disabilities or long-term illnesses.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.3] Housing supports: local authority (social) housing is let at rents related to income. The Housing Assistance Payment (HAP) is paid through the local authority to help households pay rent in the private rented sector, and the tenant's rent contribution is based on income.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.4] Education supports: the school meals programme and free school book schemes ease the cost of school, and the free pre-school place (ECCE), an early-learning place before primary school, reduces childcare costs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.2.5] Money Advice and Budgeting Service (MABS): a free, confidential, State-funded service, working under the Citizens Information Board, that helps people in debt, mainly low-income households, to budget and deal with creditors.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.b] These initiatives work through training, business supports and job creation rather than income support alone. A statutory body is one set up by law or run by the State.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.0] SOLAS is the State further education and training authority. It funds, plans and coordinates further education and training through the Education and Training Boards (ETBs).
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.1] SOLAS supports apprenticeships, vocational courses and literacy, numeracy and digital training, and it provides skills training and upskilling, matching the skills of unemployed people to vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.2] Enterprise Ireland is the State agency that helps Irish-owned companies increase sales, exports and employment through grants, market advice and training. More sales and exports create jobs.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.3] Local Enterprise Offices (LEOs) in each county are the first stop shop for people starting or growing small businesses, giving training, mentoring, advice and grants and encouraging job creation.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.4] Intreo is the Department of Social Protection's public employment service. It gives job-seekers advice, job-matching and referral to training, so unemployed people can be linked to vacancies.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.5] Work-based learning and CV and job-search preparation give unemployed people the experience that employers ask for, which makes them more employable.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.6] Community Employment (CE) offers temporary part-time work placements on local community projects for long-term unemployed and other eligible people. It is a Department of Social Protection scheme, not a SOLAS one.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.3.7] Other schemes that support a return to education and training are the Vocational Training Opportunities Scheme (VTOS), the Back to Education Allowance and Youthreach, an ETB programme for early school leavers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.b] Foreign direct investment (FDI) is investment by an overseas company that sets up or expands operations in Ireland. The State encourages it because it creates jobs, but it also brings risks.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.0] IDA Ireland is the State agency responsible for attracting overseas companies and for helping foreign companies already here to expand, through grants, site selection and advice.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.1] A low corporation tax rate is a key incentive for multinational companies to locate in Ireland: 12.5%, and 15% for the largest multinational groups.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.2] Other attractions are a young, skilled, flexible, educated and English-speaking workforce, grant assistance, good telecommunications and access to the European Union's single market.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.3] Foreign investment, for example a new plant, creates direct jobs in the foreign firms and indirect jobs in suppliers and services, so it reduces unemployment.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.4] It also brings tax revenue and new skills and technology into local labour markets, so workers can move on to other employers.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.5] Dependence on a few large multinationals is a weakness: if a parent company closes or moves a plant, a local area can lose many jobs at once.
[hom-unemployment-poverty-s6.4.6] Multinational employment tends to be concentrated in larger towns and cities, so rural areas benefit less from it. IDA Ireland aims to spread investment to regions outside the main cities.
