TARGET hom-lipids-s5: 5. Functions and energy value
[hom-lipids-s5.0.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do far more in the body than provide energy. Each function below follows from where fat sits in the body or what it does as it passes through the digestive system.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.0] Lipids are a concentrated source of energy, supplying the body with more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein, so a small amount of fat gives a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.1] Excess energy from any food is stored as adipose tissue (the body's stored fat), which is a reserve of energy for when food intake is low.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.2] Adipose tissue lies under the skin and insulates the body against heat loss, which helps to keep body temperature constant.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.3] Adipose tissue around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against knocks and pressure, and holds them in place.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.4] Lipids slow the emptying of the stomach, which helps to delay hunger, so meals containing fat are more satisfying.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.b] Beyond energy and body protection, lipids carry nutrients, build cell structures and affect how food tastes.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.0] Lipids carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K and help the body absorb them, because these vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water. Dairy fat, oily fish and egg yolk supply them.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.1] Lipids are a source of essential fatty acids, the fatty acids the body cannot make for itself and must get from food.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.2] Phospholipids (fat-like lipids containing phosphorus) and cholesterol form part of the structure of every cell membrane, the thin layer around each cell.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.3] Fat also adds flavour and a smooth texture to food, although this is a cooking benefit and not a biological function.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.4] Digestion link: the enzyme lipase digests triglycerides (the main fats in food) to fatty acids and glycerol, and any excess is stored as adipose tissue.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.b] Cholesterol is a lipid that is often blamed for heart disease, yet the body needs it. It is different from the saturated fat in food, and students often confuse the two.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.0] Cholesterol is a fat-like substance called a sterol, found only in animal foods such as egg yolk, offal (liver and kidney), meat, butter, cream, cheese, prawns and other shellfish. Plant foods contain none.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.1] The liver makes cholesterol itself, in fact most of what the body has, so the amount eaten has less effect on blood cholesterol than the saturated fat in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.2] Saturated fat in the diet raises blood LDL cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol does. A plant oil such as coconut oil is cholesterol-free but can still be high in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.3] The body uses cholesterol to make cell membranes, bile salts that emulsify fat (break it into tiny droplets) for digestion, and steroid hormones such as oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone and cortisol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.4] Vitamin D is made in the skin from a derivative of cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.5] Too much cholesterol in the blood, especially LDL, builds up on artery walls and narrows them, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke. It is also a main constituent of most gallstones.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.6] To lower blood cholesterol, eat less saturated and trans fat and more unsaturated fat, oily fish, oats, pulses, fruit and vegetables (their fibre helps). Keep to a healthy weight, be active and do not smoke.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.7] Functional spreads containing plant stanols or sterols also help to lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.b] Foods high in saturated fat are energy-dense and raise blood LDL cholesterol. A diet high in them over years raises the risk of several diseases, and each has its own mechanism.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.0] Obesity: saturated-fat foods are energy-dense, and surplus energy from them is stored as adipose tissue, which can lead to obesity.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.1] Coronary heart disease and high blood pressure: raised LDL cholesterol narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis), increasing the risk of coronary heart disease, high blood pressure (hypertension) and stroke.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.2] Type 2 diabetes: excess body fat reduces the effectiveness of insulin (the hormone that controls blood sugar), which is called insulin resistance, so the risk of Type 2 diabetes increases.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.3] Gallstones: cholesterol is a main component of gallstones, which can form in the gall bladder.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.4] Varicose veins and infertility are also linked to a high-fat diet; the first four effects carry the most weight.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.5] Lifestyle changes recommended for someone at risk of CHD: a low-fat, low-cholesterol, low-salt diet; losing weight; not smoking; limiting alcohol; exercise; and managing stress.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.6] Irish healthy eating guidelines from the Department of Health advise replacing saturated fats with unsaturated (mono- and polyunsaturated) fats and using fats and oils in small amounts, which is the fats, spreads and oils shelf of the Food Pyramid.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.7] Other advice: choose lean meat and low-fat dairy, and grill, steam or bake instead of frying. Saturated fat should supply less than 10% of daily energy (HSE and Department of Health guidelines).
[hom-lipids-s5.4.b] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal). Fat has the highest energy value per gram of the three nutrients, which is why fatty foods are so energy-dense.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.0] Fat provides 37 kJ or 9 kcal of energy per gram, which is more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein. The true/false statement that 1 gram of fat provides 20 kilocalories is false.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.1] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ or 3.75 kcal per gram; protein provides 17 kJ or 4 kcal per gram; alcohol provides 29 kJ or 7 kcal per gram.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.2] Energy from fat is calculated by multiplying the grams of fat by 37 kJ, or by 9 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.3] Worked example (sample data): 20 g of fat gives 20 × 37 = 740 kJ, or 20 × 9 = 180 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.4] A large part of the energy in the average Irish diet comes from fat, much of it hidden in processed and convenience foods, meat products, dairy, cakes, pastries, biscuits, chocolate, fried foods and takeaways, and much of that is saturated.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.5] Guidance is to reduce total fat, especially saturated and trans fat, replacing them with unsaturated fat and complex carbohydrate. Over-consumption of fat leads to obesity because fat is energy-dense and stores readily.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s1: 1. Fatty acids
[hom-lipids-s1.0.b] Fatty acids are the building blocks of fats and oils (lipids). They are sorted into three classes by the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain, and that count decides how the fat behaves.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.0] A fatty acid is a long chain of carbon atoms with hydrogen attached, made of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen only. It has a carboxyl group (–COOH) at one end and a methyl group (–CH₃) at the other.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.1] Saturated fatty acids have no double bonds between carbon atoms, so every carbon carries its full quota of hydrogen atoms. Saturated means saturated with hydrogen.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.2] Monounsaturated fatty acids have one double bond (C=C) in the carbon chain, so two neighbouring carbons each hold one hydrogen fewer.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.3] Polyunsaturated fatty acids have two or more double bonds in the carbon chain.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.4] Saturated fats are generally solid at room temperature, while fats rich in monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fatty acids are generally liquid oils. Coconut oil and palm oil are plant oils that are rich in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.0.5] Examples: stearic acid (or butyric acid) is saturated, oleic acid is monounsaturated, and linoleic acid (or linolenic acid) is polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.b] The shape of the carbon chain explains why some fats are solid and others are liquid. Higher Level questions ask you to describe and draw each type.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.0] Saturated fatty acids contain only single C–C bonds and are fully saturated with hydrogen. The chain is straight and flexible, so the chains pack tightly together and the fat is solid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.1] Each cis C=C double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid puts a kink (bend) in the chain, so the molecules pack less tightly and the fat stays liquid.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.2] A monounsaturated fatty acid has one double bond and so one kink. A polyunsaturated fatty acid has two or more double bonds, so it has one kink for each cis double bond and is liquid at room temperature.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.3] Omega-3 fatty acids are polyunsaturated, with the first double bond between the third and fourth carbon atoms counted from the methyl (omega) end. Omega-6 fatty acids have their first double bond at the sixth carbon from the methyl end.
[hom-lipids-s1.1.4] Diagram: draw a zig-zag or a simple row of carbons for a saturated, a monounsaturated and a polyunsaturated fatty acid. Label the –COOH (carboxyl) end, the –CH₃ (methyl) end and mark each C=C double bond.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.b] A double bond can hold its two hydrogen atoms in two different arrangements. The arrangement changes the shape of the chain and how the fat acts in the body.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.0] Cis and trans describe the hydrogen atoms at one double bond in an unsaturated fatty acid, not how many double bonds there are.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.1] In a cis fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on the same side of the carbon chain (both above or both below), so the chain bends.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.2] In a trans fatty acid the hydrogen atoms on either side of the double bond lie on opposite sides, so the chain stays nearly straight, like a saturated fatty acid.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.3] Most naturally occurring unsaturated fatty acids are cis, for example those in plant oils such as cooking oils and in oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.2.4] Trans fatty acids form when oils are partially hydrogenated in food manufacture, so they are found in some processed foods such as biscuits, pastries and fried takeaway foods. Small natural amounts occur in the fat of ruminant animals, in dairy fat and in beef and lamb fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.b] Most fatty acids can be made by the body, but a few cannot. These essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated and must come from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.0] Essential fatty acids are polyunsaturated fatty acids that the body cannot manufacture (or cannot make in enough quantity), so they must be obtained from food.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.1] Linoleic acid (an omega-6 fatty acid) and linolenic acid (an omega-3 fatty acid) are the two essential fatty acids most often named.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.2] Arachidonic acid is also counted as essential by examiners. Strictly the body forms it only from linoleic acid, when enough linoleic acid is eaten.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.3] Essential fatty acids are needed for growth, for the structure of cell membranes, for the nervous system and brain, and for the immune response.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.4] The body uses essential fatty acids to make prostaglandins, hormone-like compounds that regulate blood clotting, blood pressure and inflammation.
[hom-lipids-s1.3.5] Good sources include vegetable oils such as sunflower, corn, soya and rapeseed oil, nuts and seeds, and oily fish.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.b] Omega-3 fatty acids are a family of essential polyunsaturated fats with several health benefits. They come from a limited range of foods, so it helps to know the sources by name.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.0] Omega-3 fatty acids help reduce the risk of heart attack, stroke, circulatory disease and the formation of blood clots, and they can help raise HDL cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.1] Omega-3 fatty acids are linked with healthy brain activity and reduced inflammation, and they may reduce the risk of certain cancers.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.2] Oily fish are the main animal source: salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.3] Plant sources include walnuts, flaxseed (linseed), soya beans and rapeseed (canola) oil.
[hom-lipids-s1.4.4] Some eggs and milk are enriched with omega-3 by changing the diet of the hens or cows, and fish-oil supplements are also accepted as a source.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.b] The type of fatty acid in the diet affects the amount of cholesterol carried in the blood. This is why the balance of saturated, unsaturated and trans fats matters for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.0] Cholesterol travels in the blood bound to proteins, forming lipoproteins (carriers of cholesterol). LDL and HDL are these carriers, not types of cholesterol itself.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.1] LDL (low-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol through the blood to the tissues and can deposit it on artery walls, where it narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis) and raises the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.2] HDL (high-density lipoprotein) removes cholesterol from the circulation, carrying it away from the tissues to the liver for removal.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.3] Saturated fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD).
[hom-lipids-s1.5.4] Unsaturated cis fatty acids, both monounsaturated and polyunsaturated, reduce LDL cholesterol and help to raise HDL cholesterol, so they are generally good for the heart.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.5] Trans fatty acids raise LDL cholesterol and lower HDL cholesterol, increasing the risk of CHD. They are thought to do more harm than saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.6] Monounsaturated and polyunsaturated fats can lower total cholesterol when they replace saturated fats in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s1.5.7] Essential fatty acids must be eaten because the body cannot make them, and omega-3 fatty acids add the heart and brain benefits.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s2: 2. Triglycerides and the classification of lipids
[hom-lipids-s2.0.b] Lipids are the fats and oils in our food. Most of the lipid we eat is in one form, the triglyceride, so knowing what a triglyceride is made of explains most of this topic.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.0] Fats are solid at room temperature and oils are liquid, but both are lipids. Most dietary lipid is in the form of triglycerides.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.1] Fats and oils (triglycerides) are made of carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) only. They contain no nitrogen, so, unlike protein, they are not a source of nitrogen.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.2] Lipids are made up of fatty acids and glycerol, which join together to form the triglyceride.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.3] Other lipids exist, such as phospholipids like lecithin and sterols like cholesterol, but triglycerides are the lipids studied here.
[hom-lipids-s2.0.4] In digestion the enzyme lipase, made in the pancreas and carried in pancreatic juice, breaks triglycerides down into fatty acids and glycerol.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.b] Most fats and oils in food are triglycerides: one glycerol molecule combined with three fatty acid molecules. The name tells you this, because tri means three.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.0] A triglyceride contains only carbon (C), hydrogen (H) and oxygen (O) atoms, with no nitrogen.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.1] Glycerol is an alcohol, with the formula C₃H₈O₃. It carries three hydroxyl (–OH) groups, one on each of its three carbon atoms, which gives three places for a fatty acid to attach.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.2] The three fatty acids in one triglyceride can be the same or different, and may be saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated. This mixture decides whether the fat is solid or liquid.
[hom-lipids-s2.1.3] The carboxyl group of each fatty acid joins to one hydroxyl group of glycerol, forming an ester bond (–COO–).
[hom-lipids-s2.2.b] Each fatty acid is joined to glycerol by the same reaction, repeated three times. The result is a glycerol backbone with a fatty acid chain hanging from each of its carbons.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.0] Each ester bond forms by condensation, a reaction in which two molecules join and one molecule of water (H₂O) is eliminated.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.1] Three water molecules are removed when one glycerol and three fatty acids combine to form one triglyceride, because three ester bonds are made.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.2] A triglyceride is therefore a glycerol backbone with three fatty acid chains hanging from it.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.3] Digestion runs this in reverse: lipase breaks the ester bonds by adding water back, which is hydrolysis, releasing the fatty acids and glycerol.
[hom-lipids-s2.2.4] Diagram: a glycerol backbone with three fatty acid chains attached by ester (–COO–) links, labelling glycerol, the fatty acid chains and the ester bonds, and marking the three water molecules released during formation.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.b] One way to sort lipids is by where they come from. There are three classes: animal, vegetable (plant) and marine.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.0] Animal fats come from land animals, for example butter, lard, suet, dripping, cheese, egg yolk and the fat in meat.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.1] Vegetable (plant) oils come from seeds, nuts and fruits, for example olive, sunflower, corn, soya and rapeseed oils, and avocado oil.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.2] Marine lipids come from fish and other sea creatures, for example the oil in oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, herring, sardines and trout, and fish-liver oils such as cod-liver oil.
[hom-lipids-s2.3.3] Palm oil and coconut oil come from plants but are high in saturated fat, so they behave like animal fats in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.b] The second way to sort lipids is by the type of fatty acid they contain. Every fat and oil is a mixture, so the class depends on which type is present in the largest proportion.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.0] A lipid is classed as saturated, monounsaturated or polyunsaturated according to which type of fatty acid makes up the largest proportion of its fatty acids.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.1] Olive oil is classed as monounsaturated because most of its fatty acids have one double bond. Rapeseed oil and avocado are also mainly monounsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.2] Sunflower, corn and soya oils are classed as polyunsaturated because most of their fatty acids have more than one double bond. The oils of oily fish are also mainly polyunsaturated.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.3] Butter, lard, suet, dripping, meat fat, cheese, coconut oil and palm oil are classed as saturated because saturated fatty acids form the largest proportion of their fatty acids.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.4] Most fats contain a mixture of all three fatty acid types, so classification depends on the predominant type. Butter has some monounsaturated fat and olive oil has some saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s2.4.5] The fatty acid type explains the state: a lipid that is mostly saturated is a fat, solid at room temperature, while one that is mostly unsaturated is an oil.

CANDIDATES hom-energy-nutrition-s3: 3. Energy value of the nutrients
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.b] Only three nutrients supply energy in food: protein, carbohydrate and fat (lipid). Each releases a fixed amount of energy, measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal), for every gram eaten, and these values are the basis of every energy calculation.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.0] Protein supplies 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, carbohydrate supplies 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram and fat (lipid) supplies 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram. A simple table of these three values is the best way to learn them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.1] Fat is the most concentrated energy source, giving more than twice the energy per gram of protein or carbohydrate. This is why a high-fat diet is energy-dense and contributes strongly to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.2] Vitamins, minerals and water supply no energy, so they are not energy nutrients, even though the body cannot work without them.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.3] Alcohol is not a nutrient, but it does supply energy, at 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. It supplies no protein, vitamins or minerals, so it is a source of empty kilocalories (energy with no nutrition).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.4] Fibre is a carbohydrate that the body cannot digest, so it supplies very little usable energy. Do not count it with the starches and sugars, and do not give it 16 kJ per gram.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.0.5] 1 kcal is about 4.2 kJ, so dividing kJ by 4.2 gives kcal. Check: 37 kJ is about 9 kcal, 17 kJ is about 4 kcal, 16 kJ is about 3.75 kcal and 29 kJ is about 7 kcal.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.b] A food's energy value is worked out from how many grams of each energy nutrient it contains. Food composition tables and labels give these amounts per 100 g, so the method is multiply, add, then state the unit.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.0] 1. Find the grams of protein, carbohydrate and fat in the amount eaten, from the food label or a food composition table (which gives values per 100 g).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.1] 2. Multiply the grams of each nutrient by its energy value per gram: protein by 17, carbohydrate by 16 and fat by 37.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.2] 3. Add the three results to give the total energy value of the food.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.3] 4. Give the unit, kJ, and convert to kcal if asked by dividing by 4.2.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.4] Scaling to a portion: if the table gives values per 100 g and the portion is 150 g, multiply each per-100 g value by 150 divided by 100 (or 1.5).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.1.5] Without scaling, the answer is for 100 g, not for the portion eaten.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.b] Seeing the method on sample figures shows where the marks are: each multiplication, the total and the unit. These are invented sample foods, not facts about any named product.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.0] Worked example (sample data): 100 g of a food with 12 g protein, 60 g carbohydrate and 8 g fat gives 12 x 17 = 204 kJ, 60 x 16 = 960 kJ and 8 x 37 = 296 kJ.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.1] These add to a total of 1460 kJ. In kcal this is about 348 (1460 divided by 4.2).
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.2] Second worked example (sample data): 100 g of a food with 20 g protein, 50 g carbohydrate and 10 g fat gives 20 x 17 = 340 kJ, 50 x 16 = 800 kJ and 10 x 37 = 370 kJ.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.3] These add to a total of 1510 kJ, which is about 360 kcal.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.2.4] Percentage contribution: divide the energy from one nutrient by the total energy, then multiply by 100. In the 1460 kJ sample, fat gives 296 out of 1460, which is about 20 per cent of the energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.b] Section B data questions give a table of composition per 100 g for several foods, such as protein, fat, carbohydrate, fibre, calcium and iron. The sum is only half the answer, because you must also say what the figures mean for a diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.0] To rank foods by energy value, calculate the kJ for each food using the method above, then put them in order and name the highest and the lowest.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.1] A high-fibre food helps bowel health, because fibre adds bulk and keeps waste moving through the gut.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.2] A high-fat food is a disadvantage on a low-fat diet, because fat has the most energy per gram and contributes to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.3] A high-carbohydrate food is a good source of energy for the body.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.3.4] The 2007H bread comparison is an example of this type of question: calculate, rank, then comment on how suitable each bread is for a given diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.b] Carbohydrate supplies the largest share of energy in the diet. It is the body's main and most readily available energy source.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.0] Sugars and starches are digested to glucose, which cells use for energy. Any glucose not needed straight away is stored as glycogen in the liver and muscles.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.1] Starchy foods such as bread, potatoes, rice and pasta supply carbohydrate together with fibre, vitamins and minerals, so they give energy with useful nutrition. Cereals and fruit are also sources.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.2] Sugary foods and drinks supply carbohydrate with little nutrition, so they are a source of empty kilocalories.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.4.3] Healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods, preferably wholegrain, with less from added sugars.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.b] Fat packs far more energy into each gram than the other nutrients, so it contributes strongly to the energy in the diet even when it is a smaller part of the weight of the food. It is the second largest source of energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.0] Fats and oils are lipids, mostly triglycerides, made of glycerol and fatty acids.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.1] Fat supplies the most energy per gram of the three energy nutrients, more than twice that of carbohydrate or protein.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.2] Surplus energy from fat is stored as adipose tissue, an energy reserve, which is why high-fat diets contribute strongly to weight gain.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.3] Saturated fats come mainly from animal foods and some plant oils, such as palm oil. Unsaturated fats come from vegetable oils, nuts, seeds and oily fish.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.5.4] Sources of fat include butter, oils, fatty meat, nuts, cheese and oily fish.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.b] Protein can supply energy, but this is not its main job. It makes the smallest contribution to the energy in the diet.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.0] Protein is made of amino acids. The body uses it mainly for growth and repair of tissue, but it can also supply energy.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.1] Protein is not stored in the body as protein, so surplus protein is broken down and used for energy or converted to fat.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.2] When carbohydrate and fat are short, protein is used for energy at the expense of its building role.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.3] High biological value (HBV) protein, from meat, fish, eggs, milk and cheese, contains all essential amino acids. Beans are also a protein source.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.6.4] Protein is not a major energy source in a healthy diet: its value per gram is close to that of carbohydrate, but it is eaten in smaller amounts.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.b] How much each nutrient contributes to a diet depends on how much of it is eaten and on how much energy each gram of the food carries.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.0] Energy density is the energy per gram of a food. Foods rich in fat and sugar are the most energy-dense.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.1] Wholegrain starchy foods, fruit, vegetables and lean protein give nutrients with less energy per gram, so they are a better choice than empty kilocalories.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.2] Healthy eating guidelines aim for most energy to come from starchy foods, preferably wholegrain, with less from fat, especially saturated fat, and less from added sugars.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.3] The typical Irish diet gets more of its energy from saturated fat and from added sugar in drinks and processed foods than the guidelines recommend.
[hom-energy-nutrition-s3.7.4] Calculating the contribution of each nutrient: multiply the grams eaten in a day by the energy value per gram, then divide each result by the total and multiply by 100 to give its percentage share.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s1: 1. Fat-soluble vitamins
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.b] Vitamins are organic compounds that the body needs in very small amounts. They are grouped by what they dissolve in, and that one difference decides how they are absorbed, stored and lost.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.0] Vitamins are needed for normal growth, health and chemical reactions in the body. The body cannot make enough of most of them, so they must come from food.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.1] There are two classes: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E and K) and water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C). For example, vitamin A is fat-soluble and vitamin C is water-soluble.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.0.2] RDA (recommended dietary allowance) is the daily amount that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It differs with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, because needs change at different life stages.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.b] Vitamins A, D, E and K dissolve in fat, so they are absorbed together with dietary fat. This also explains why they are stored in the body and why too much can be harmful.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.0] Fat-soluble vitamins are absorbed in the small intestine along with dietary fats and are carried in the lymphatic system (the body's drainage network) before entering the blood.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.1] Because they need fat to be absorbed, a diet very low in fat, or a condition that stops fat being absorbed, can reduce vitamin intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.2] The liver and fatty (adipose) tissue store fat-soluble vitamins, so the body keeps a reserve between meals and deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.3] Because they are stored, excess intake from supplements or large amounts of liver can build up to toxic levels (toxicity). Water-soluble vitamins are mostly excreted in urine instead.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.1.4] Excess vitamin A (retinol) is harmful in pregnancy because it can harm the developing baby, so high-dose vitamin A supplements and large amounts of liver are avoided during pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.b] Vitamin A comes in two forms: one ready-made in animal foods and one made from a plant pigment. Its main jobs are vision, healthy surface tissues and protection of cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.0] Retinol is the active, ready-made (preformed) form of vitamin A, found only in animal foods: liver, fish liver oils, oily fish, dairy products (butter, full-fat milk and cheese), eggs and fortified margarine (margarine with vitamin A added).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.1] Beta-carotene is a plant pigment and a provitamin (a substance the body converts into a vitamin). The body converts it into retinol in the intestine, so beta-carotene is not itself vitamin A.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.2] Good beta-carotene sources include carrots, sweet potatoes, apricots, mango, red and orange peppers, tomatoes and dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach and kale, where the green hides the orange pigment.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.3] Retinol combines with the protein opsin to form rhodopsin (visual purple), the light-sensitive pigment in the rod cells of the retina. This allows vision in dim light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.4] Vitamin A keeps the cells lining the eyes, mouth, respiratory tract, urinary tract and skin healthy and moist, which protects them against infection.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.5] Vitamin A supports growth, cell repair and a healthy immune system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.2.6] Beta-carotene acts as an antioxidant, neutralising free radicals (unstable molecules that damage cells).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.b] Without enough vitamin A, the eye pigment cannot be rebuilt and the body's moist surface linings break down. The eyes show the first signs.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.0] Night blindness is the earliest sign: poor vision in dim light because rhodopsin is not regenerated.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.1] Xerophthalmia: the eyes become dry and damaged, and in severe cases the cornea (the clear front of the eye) is destroyed and blindness follows.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.2] Dry, rough skin and dry, brittle hair, because the skin and other surface tissues are no longer kept healthy and moist.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.3] Poor growth, because vitamin A supports growth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.4] Lowered resistance to infection, because the mucous membranes (moist linings) dry out, crack and lose their protective role.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.3.5] In severe cases deficiency causes blindness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.b] Vitamin D is unusual because the body can make it in the skin when sunlight falls on it, which is why it is called the sunshine vitamin. It is the vitamin that lets the body use calcium to build bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.0] Cholecalciferol (vitamin D3) forms in the skin when ultraviolet B from sunlight acts on a cholesterol derivative in the skin. It is also found in animal foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.1] Ergocalciferol (vitamin D2) is the plant and fungal form, made when mushrooms and yeast are exposed to ultraviolet light.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.2] Dietary sources include oily fish such as salmon, mackerel, sardines and herring, egg yolk, liver, butter, fortified margarine and spreads, fortified milk and some fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.3] Vitamin D increases the absorption of calcium and phosphorus from the small intestine, which is why it is the vitamin needed to absorb calcium.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.4] Vitamin D raises blood calcium levels, working with parathormone (the parathyroid hormone) when blood calcium falls, so blood calcium stays at the right level.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.5] Vitamin D supports the mineralisation of bones (the deposit of calcium and phosphorus that hardens them) and the formation of teeth, so it builds and maintains strong bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.4.6] In Ireland, weak winter sunlight means that dietary sources of vitamin D matter most in the winter months. People who cover up, stay indoors or have dark skin also make less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.b] Low vitamin D means too little calcium reaches the bones, so they stay soft. The condition has a different name in children and in adults.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.0] Rickets in children: bones soften and bend, causing bow legs, delayed growth and poor tooth formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.1] Osteomalacia in adults: bones become soft, weak and painful, and fractures can occur, because they are not properly mineralised.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.5.2] Low vitamin D lowers calcium absorption, which is a contributing factor in osteoporosis, where bone density falls, and in dental decay.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.b] Vitamin E is the fat-soluble vitamin whose main job is as an antioxidant: it stops oxidation damaging fatty parts of the body's cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.0] Tocopherols are the active forms of vitamin E. Alpha-tocopherol is the form the human body uses most, and the main form in the body.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.1] Rich sources are vegetable oils such as sunflower, wheatgerm and olive oil, nuts and seeds such as almonds and sunflower seeds, wholegrain cereals, avocado, green leafy vegetables and egg yolk.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.2] Vitamin E is an antioxidant that protects the membranes of cells and polyunsaturated fatty acids from oxidation (damage by free radicals).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.3] Vitamin E also helps protect the vitamin A and vitamin C in the body, and supports healthy skin and immune function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.6.4] Deficiency is rare in healthy people. In people who cannot absorb fat, and in premature babies, it can cause nerve and muscle damage and the early breakdown of red blood cells (a form of anaemia).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.b] Vitamin K is the clotting vitamin: the K comes from the German word for coagulation. It has two forms, one from plants and one made by bacteria in the gut.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.0] Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone) comes from plants: green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, cabbage, kale and sprouts, and vegetable oils and liver.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.1] Vitamin K2 (menaquinones) is made by bacteria in the large intestine and is also found in dairy products, eggs and meat. The gut bacteria are therefore a second source.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.2] The liver needs vitamin K to make the clotting factors, including prothrombin, that allow blood to clot. This is its primary function.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.3] Vitamin K activates osteocalcin, a protein that binds calcium into bone, so it supports bone formation.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.4] Deficiency impairs blood clotting, causing bleeding and easy bruising. Long courses of antibiotics or poor fat absorption can also reduce vitamin K.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.7.5] The HSE offers every newborn baby a vitamin K injection within hours of birth, because their gut bacteria are not yet established and they have low stores, so they risk bleeding. It is the parents' choice.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.b] All four fat-soluble vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water, but they differ in what destroys them: heat, oxygen, light or alkalis. Knowing this explains how storage and cooking affect each one.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.0] Vitamin A is insoluble in water but soluble in fats and organic solvents.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.1] Vitamin A is heat-stable in normal cooking but is destroyed by prolonged high temperatures.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.2] Vitamin A is destroyed by oxygen (it is oxidised), so foods kept exposed to air lose it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.3] Vitamin D is insoluble in water and is relatively heat-stable in normal cooking and storage, so cooking oily fish loses little.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.4] Vitamin E is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking, but it is destroyed by oxidation, including by rancid fats, by light and air, and by frying at high temperature for long periods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.5] Vitamin K is insoluble in water and heat-stable in normal cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.8.6] Vitamin K is destroyed by light (ultraviolet) and by alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.b] To judge how good a food is as a source of vitamins, use food composition tables and food labels, and think about whether the food is fresh, processed or fortified.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.0] Food composition tables give the vitamin content of foods, usually per 100 g, so fresh and processed foods can be compared on the same basis (like for like).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.1] The mandatory nutrition declaration on a food label lists energy, fat, saturates, carbohydrate, sugars, protein and salt, under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. Vitamins appear only when added or when a claim is made, so not listing vitamins does not mean the food has none.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.2] Where vitamins are listed, the label states the amount per 100 g (or per serving) and as a percentage of the reference intake.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.3] In Ireland, the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014 give effect to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011. The Food Safety Authority of Ireland (FSAI) advises on food labelling and nutrition in Ireland.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.4] Fortified foods have vitamins added that the natural food does not contain, or that were lost in processing. Examples are margarine with added vitamins A and D and fortified breakfast cereals.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.5] Fresh produce loses vitamin C in storage, while frozen vegetables, blanched before freezing, usually retain more vitamin C than long-stored fresh vegetables.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s1.9.6] Canning and prolonged cooking destroy heat-sensitive vitamins, so a processed food may contain less of a vitamin than its fresh form.
