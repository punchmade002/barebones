[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.b] Coronary heart disease (CHD) is the narrowing of the coronary arteries, the vessels feeding the heart muscle, by fatty deposits. The aim of the CHD diet is to lower LDL cholesterol and blood pressure and to reduce the strain on the heart and arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.0] Replace saturated animal fats with mono- and polyunsaturated oils such as olive, rapeseed and sunflower oil, because saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol, the type that clogs arteries. Use low-cholesterol products where possible.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.1] Choose low-fat dairy products and lean meat, and trim visible fat from meat before cooking. Eat more fish and skinless chicken in place of fatty red meat.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.2] Eat oily fish such as salmon, mackerel and sardines, which supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.3] Increase soluble fibre from oats, beans, lentils, fruit and vegetables, which helps lower LDL cholesterol. Eat more fruit and vegetables in general.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.4] Reduce salt, which helps keep blood pressure down, and use herbs, spices and lemon juice for flavour.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.5] Reduce refined carbohydrate and sugar, such as white bread, sugary foods and sugary drinks, and reduce alcohol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.6] Use low-fat cooking methods: grill, bake, steam or boil food rather than frying it, avoid creamy sauces, and skim fat from stock and gravy.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.0.7] A sample day's menu: porridge with low-fat milk and fruit; a wholegrain sandwich with chicken and salad; grilled oily fish with vegetables and potatoes; low-fat yoghurt or fruit.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.b] Cholesterol is the substance behind the CHD diet. The body needs some of it, but the type and amount in the blood decide whether it helps or harms the arteries.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.0] Cholesterol is a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell, made mainly by the liver and also obtained from animal foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.1] Cholesterol forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed for some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.2] Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is associated with saturated fat and deposits cholesterol in artery walls, hardening them. This is why LDL is called the harmful cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.3] High-density lipoprotein (HDL) is associated with unsaturated fat and carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.1.4] Lifestyle changes that reduce CHD risk include maintaining a healthy weight, not smoking, avoiding excess alcohol, exercising regularly and reducing stress.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.b] Some foods are designed to do more than feed you. These functional foods can be added to a CHD menu to help protect the heart.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.0] A functional food has health benefits beyond its basic nutritive value, so it does more than supply energy and nutrients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.1] Plant sterol and stanol spreads, yoghurts, cheese spreads and drinks reduce cholesterol absorption and so lower LDL cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.2] Omega-3 enriched eggs and milk supply omega-3 fatty acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.3] Prebiotic and probiotic milk and yoghurt improve digestion.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.2.4] Fortified foods have vitamins and minerals added to them, so they supply nutrients the diet may otherwise lack.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.b] A lacto-vegetarian eats no meat, poultry, fish or eggs, but includes milk and dairy products. Without meat, careful choices are needed to get enough protein, iron, calcium and vitamins.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.0] Protein comes from milk, cheese and yoghurt (high biological value protein), and from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products such as tofu and textured vegetable protein (TVP), and mycoprotein. Combining plant proteins across the day gives a good range of amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.1] Calcium comes from milk, yoghurt and cheese, and from green leafy vegetables, sesame seeds and fortified soya products. Vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus help calcium absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.2] Iron comes from green leafy vegetables, pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit and fortified cereals; vitamin C from fruit and vegetables at the same meal improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.3] Vitamin B12 comes from dairy products, vitamin B group from wholegrains, and vitamin D from fortified spreads and cereals and from sunlight. Omega-3 fats come from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.4] Some cheeses are made with animal rennet, and some mycoprotein products contain egg white, so labels must be checked.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.5] Eggs are excluded, so the diet relies on dairy and pulses for protein and on fortified foods for nutrients that eggs would normally supply.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.6] Other types of vegetarian: lacto-ovo includes eggs and dairy, ovo includes eggs, and pesco includes fish. Some sources also allow eggs in a lacto-vegetarian diet, but the strict definition is dairy yes, eggs no.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.3.7] People become vegetarian for religious, cultural, moral, health, economic or environmental reasons. A sample day: orange juice, porridge with milk and wholemeal toast; vegetable soup, brown bread, yoghurt and fruit; vegetarian lasagne or a lentil dish with green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.b] A vegan diet excludes all animal-derived foods, including meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and honey. Because dairy and eggs are gone too, a vegan must plan protein, calcium, iron and vitamin B12 from plant and fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.0] Protein comes from pulses, nuts, seeds, soya products, vegan mycoprotein, seitan and tempeh; eating pulses with grains or nuts over the day provides all the essential amino acids.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.1] Vitamin B12 has no reliable plant source, so it must come from fortified foods such as soya milk, cereals and yeast extract, or from a supplement.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.2] Calcium comes from fortified soya milk and flour, calcium-set tofu, sesame seeds in tahini, nuts, vegan cheese, and broccoli, spinach and other dark green vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.3] Iron comes from pulses, wholegrains, dried fruit, dark green vegetables and fortified cereals; vitamin C from peppers, tomatoes, berries and citrus improves absorption.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.4] Vitamin D comes from fortified foods and sunlight, and omega-3 fats from flaxseed, walnuts and rapeseed oil.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.5] Hidden animal ingredients can turn up in stock cubes, some spreads, some bread and sweets (gelatine) and in cheese made with rennet, so choose labelled vegan products and check for gelatine, milk powder, egg, rennet or honey.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.4.6] A vegan menu example uses vegan mycoprotein mince, lentils or beans, vegetables and fruit, which excludes animal products and still supplies protein, iron, calcium and fortified B12.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.b] Coeliac disease is a lifelong intolerance to gluten, the protein found in wheat, barley and rye. The only treatment is a strict gluten-free diet.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.0] Gluten damages the villi, the finger-like lining of the small intestine, so food is not properly absorbed and deficiencies of iron, calcium and folate can develop.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.1] Avoid bread, cakes, biscuits, pastries, pizzas and pies made with wheat, ordinary pasta and couscous, seitan, and processed foods such as sausages, some soups, sauces and gravies. Wheat, barley, rye and malt are all sources of gluten.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.2] Build meals around naturally gluten-free staples such as rice, corn (maize), potatoes, buckwheat, soya products and lentils, plus meat, fish, eggs, dairy, fruit and vegetables. Gluten-free versions of bread, pasta and flour are also available.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.3] Check ingredient lists for hidden gluten in stock, sauces, malt products and processed meats, and look for the gluten-free symbol or declaration on products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.4] A gluten-free claim means the food as sold contains no more than 20 mg of gluten per kilogram (Regulation (EU) No 828/2014, FSAI guidance). Wheat and oats used must still appear in the ingredients.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.5] Avoid cross-contamination in the kitchen, for example by using a separate toaster and utensils, so crumbs of gluten do not reach safe food. Oats are naturally gluten-free but often contaminated, so use only oats labelled gluten-free.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.6] Gluten-free breads and cereals are often low in fibre, so choose brown rice, beans and other high-fibre gluten-free foods.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.5.7] Replace the iron, calcium and folate normally supplied by wheat products with other naturally gluten-free or fortified sources.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.b] The diabetic diet aims to keep blood glucose stable, maintain a healthy weight and reduce the risk of heart disease. It does not mean no carbohydrate; it means carbohydrate that is low-GI, regular and controlled.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.0] Regular meals and snacks at set times prevent large swings in blood glucose; skipping meals is avoided.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.1] Low-glycaemic-index (GI) carbohydrates such as wholegrain bread, oats, pasta and pulses release glucose slowly, so blood glucose rises gradually. High-fibre foods and plenty of vegetables also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.2] Refined sugar, sugar-sweetened soft drinks, sweets and sugar-laden processed foods are avoided or limited, and low-calorie sweeteners can replace sugar.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.3] Fibre-rich fruit and vegetables and low-sugar fruits are chosen, as high-sugar fruit should be limited.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.4] Saturated fat and salt are reduced, and unsaturated oils are used instead, because diabetes raises the risk of heart disease. Keeping a healthy weight and exercising also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.6.5] Insulin dose is matched to the carbohydrate eaten in people using insulin, so meal timing and portions must be consistent.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.b] People on modified diets need suitable foods that are easy to find and easy to use. Manufacturers and retailers both respond to this.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.0] Manufacturers produce specialised ranges such as gluten-free, diabetic, vegetarian and coronary heart disease products.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.1] Manufacturers give detailed labels and packet recipes, and offer smaller portion sizes for people who need them.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.2] Retailers provide specialist shops or sections, designated shelves and wider ranges of special-diet foods, so customers can find suitable products quickly.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.7.3] Retailers run in-store cookery demonstrations to show customers how to use special-diet products, and provide information and websites.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.b] Labels let people on special diets check quickly whether a food is suitable. What the label shows, and how far it can be trusted, is what exams focus on.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.0] The name of the food and ingredients must be listed in descending order of weight, so the main ingredients are easy to identify.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.1] Under Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011, given effect in Ireland by the European Union (Provision of Food Information to Consumers) Regulations 2014, allergens must be highlighted in the ingredient list.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.2] Nutrition information shows the energy, fat, saturated fat, sugar, salt and fibre content of the food.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.3] Symbols and claims show gluten-free, vegetarian and vegan status and whether a food is fortified, functional, low-fat or reduced-sugar, which helps people with specific needs choose.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.4] Labels give cooking and storage instructions, the net quantity, the use-by or best-before date, the origin and any GM ingredients, helping people choose foods safely.
[hom-meal-planning-s4.8.5] Labels are useful but must be read critically: claims can mislead, small print can hide gluten, and 'may contain' warnings need checking.

CANDIDATES hom-meal-planning-s3: 3. Diet-related problems
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.b] Obesity develops when the energy taken in as food regularly exceeds the energy the body uses, and the surplus is stored as fat (adipose tissue).
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.0] Body mass index (BMI) is weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared. A BMI of 30 or more in adults is classed as obese, and 25 to under 30 as overweight.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.1] Fatty and sugary foods are energy-dense, which means a small portion supplies a large amount of energy, so it is easy to take in more than the body needs.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.2] Sugary drinks, cream sauces, fried foods, large portions, snacking, late-night eating, too much processed food and a low-fibre diet all increase energy intake.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.3] Not all causes are dietary. Inactivity, heredity, stress and emotional eating also contribute, and a sedentary lifestyle means less energy is used up.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.4] Obesity increases the risk of coronary heart disease, Type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and gallstones, as well as joint strain and arthritis, breathing problems and low self-esteem.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.5] Weight-loss guidance is a balanced low-energy diet: lower fat (especially saturated fat), reduced sugar and salt, extra fibre, fruit, vegetables and water, lean protein and less processed food.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.0.6] Low-fat cooking methods, removing visible fat from meat, avoiding cream sauces, smaller portions, avoiding alcohol and late-night eating, and regular exercise all help, because they cut energy in and raise energy used.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.b] Coronary heart disease (CHD) happens when the coronary arteries, which supply the heart muscle with blood, become narrowed or blocked by fatty deposits (atherosclerosis), which can cause angina or a heart attack.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.0] Saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol, which is deposited in artery walls and narrows them, so less blood reaches the heart muscle.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.1] Trans fats, found in some hard margarines and in fried and processed foods, raise LDL and lower HDL cholesterol, so they are doubly harmful.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.2] Refined carbohydrates and sugar contribute to raised blood triglycerides (blood fats), reduced HDL cholesterol and weight gain.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.3] Excess salt raises blood pressure, which damages artery walls and makes the heart work harder.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.4] A diet low in fibre, fruit and vegetables offers little protection against CHD, because these foods help lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.5] Other causes are smoking, lack of exercise, obesity, excess alcohol, stress, high blood pressure, heredity, age and gender.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.6] Cholesterol is a soft, wax-like substance found in every body cell. The liver makes most of it, with some coming from animal foods. It forms part of cell membranes and bile, is needed to make some hormones, and helps transport fats in the blood.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.1.7] LDL cholesterol (associated with saturated fat) deposits cholesterol in artery walls, hardening and narrowing them. HDL cholesterol (associated with unsaturated fat) carries cholesterol away from the arteries to the liver.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.b] Diabetes means the level of glucose in the blood is raised. It happens because of a problem with insulin, a hormone made by the pancreas that lets glucose move from the blood into body cells.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.0] Type 1 diabetes (insulin-dependent): the pancreas produces little or no insulin. It often starts in childhood, adolescence or young adulthood, may be hereditary, is controlled with insulin, diet and exercise, and cannot be reversed.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.1] Type 2 diabetes (non-insulin-dependent): insulin is produced but cells respond poorly to it, or too little is made. It usually develops later in life, although some young people now develop it, and it accounts for about 90 per cent of cases.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.2] Excess weight and inactivity are the main risk factors for Type 2 diabetes. Diets high in sugar and refined carbohydrate, combined with excess weight, raise the risk. Sugar alone is not the cause.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.3] Type 2 can often be controlled with diet, weight loss, exercise and tablets, so losing weight can improve it.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.4] Gestational diabetes develops during pregnancy and usually resolves after the birth.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.2.5] Symptoms include frequent urination, thirst, hunger, weight loss and tiredness. Long-term complications can affect the eyes, kidneys, nerves and heart.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.b] A diet low in fibre and fluid makes stools hard and dry, and the effort of passing them puts the large intestine under strain. Several bowel problems follow from this in a chain.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.0] Constipation: hard, dry stools are passed with difficulty, usually caused by too little fibre and fluid and too little activity.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.1] Diverticular disease: small pouches called diverticula form in the wall of the large intestine when the pressure from straining pushes the lining outwards.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.2] Diverticulitis: inflamed or infected diverticula cause pain and need medical treatment.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.3] Haemorrhoids: swollen veins around the anus, caused by straining during constipation.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.4] A low-fibre diet also raises the risk of bowel disorders, including bowel cancer.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.5] Prevention: eat wholegrain cereals, brown bread, wholegrain rice, pulses, nuts and seeds, and fruit and vegetables with their skins on, eaten raw where possible, because skins and raw plant foods keep most of their fibre.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.6] Whole fruit and vegetables supply more fibre than juice, and fresh or dried fruit is a better snack than processed alternatives. Plenty of fluid, exercise and regular meals also help.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.3.7] A high-fibre diet reduces the risk of bowel disease.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.b] Bones are living tissue that is built up in childhood and lost slowly in later life. Osteoporosis is a thinning and weakening of the bone, which becomes porous, brittle and prone to fracture.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.0] Bone mass peaks in early adulthood, after which bone is gradually lost, so building strong bones in childhood and adolescence gives a bigger store to draw on later.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.1] Too little calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C or phosphorus over life raises the risk. Vitamin D helps the gut absorb calcium, and vitamin C is needed for the collagen framework of bone.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.2] Phytates, oxalates, excess fibre and tannins in some foods can reduce calcium absorption, because they bind calcium in the gut.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.3] Excess caffeine and alcohol increase calcium loss, and excess saturated fat can also reduce calcium availability.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.4] Risk factors include older age, the fall in oestrogen after menopause (which puts women at higher risk), smoking and inactivity.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.5] Weight-bearing exercise helps to maintain bone strength.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.4.6] Prevention: increase calcium, vitamin D, vitamin C and phosphorus in the diet, build bone in childhood and adolescence, take weight-bearing exercise, and limit the inhibitors of calcium absorption listed above.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.b] Teeth are damaged by acid made from sugar in the mouth. Dental caries (tooth decay) and gum disease are separate problems, although both start with plaque.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.0] Bacteria in plaque ferment sugars in the mouth to produce acid, which dissolves enamel and causes dental caries.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.1] Frequent snacking, sticky sweets and sugary drinks keep the mouth acidic for longer, which increases the risk of decay.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.2] Fluoride strengthens enamel and makes it more resistant to acid; fluoride toothpaste is used twice a day.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.3] Calcium, phosphorus and vitamin D support the formation of enamel and dentine, the teeth's hard tissues.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.4] Periodontal (gum) disease is inflammation of the gums and the tissues around the teeth, caused by a build-up of plaque.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.5.5] Protection: limit sugary food to mealtimes, brush, have dental check-ups, and eat cheese and crunchy fruit and vegetables.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.b] Diet problems rarely come singly. A surplus of one nutrient or too little of another can lead to several conditions, and some conditions lead on to others.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.0] Excess energy leads to obesity, which increases the risk of Type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease and high blood pressure.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.1] Excess salt raises blood pressure, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.2] A diet high in sugar contributes to both obesity and dental caries.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.3] A low-fibre, low-fluid diet can cause constipation, which in turn can lead to diverticular disease.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.4] Too little calcium and vitamin D leads to osteoporosis, and too little iron leads to anaemia.
[hom-meal-planning-s3.6.5] A poor diet in a teenager can cause several problems at once: overweight, CHD risk, tooth decay, constipation, anaemia, diabetes risk and osteoporosis later.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s7: 7. Biological value
[hom-proteins-s7.0.b] Not all protein foods are equally useful to the body. Biological value (BV) measures how fully a protein food supplies the essential amino acids (the amino acids the body cannot make for itself) in the right proportions for growth and repair.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.0] High biological value (HBV) protein, also called complete protein, contains all the essential amino acids in adequate amounts, so the body can use it to build and repair tissue.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.1] HBV sources are mainly animal foods: meat, fish, poultry, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.2] Soya beans are the main plant exception: they contain all the essential amino acids, so soya beans and soya products are classed as HBV.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.3] Low biological value (LBV) protein, also called incomplete protein, lacks one or more essential amino acids or contains them in small amounts.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.4] LBV sources are mainly plant foods: cereals such as wheat, rice and maize, and pulses (dried peas, beans and lentils), along with nuts and seeds. Peas, beans and lentils are LBV on their own.
[hom-proteins-s7.0.5] To sort foods quickly: eggs, fish, meat and milk are HBV; peas, rice, wheat and maize are LBV.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.b] A low biological value food is not a dead end. Two LBV foods eaten together can supply all the essential amino acids, because each makes good what the other lacks.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.0] This is called complementation, or supplementary value: one protein food makes up the deficiency of another, so the combined meal provides protein of high biological value. The foods are eaten in the same meal or the same day.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.1] Beans on toast is the classic example: bread supplies methionine, which beans lack, while beans supply lysine, which bread lacks, so together they give a complete set of essential amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.2] Hummus with pitta bread and dhal with chapatti are further complementary pairs, as are rice with lentils, lentil soup with wholemeal bread and bolognese with beans.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.3] Adding a small amount of an HBV food such as milk, cheese or egg to a cereal or pulse also raises its value, for example in macaroni cheese, rice pudding or cereal with milk.
[hom-proteins-s7.1.4] Complementation lets vegetarians and vegans meet their protein needs from plant foods alone, makes meals cheaper, and lets pulses stretch meat as meat extenders.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.b] Complementation is easy to put into practice, because everyday meals can be built around a pair of foods that fill each other's gaps. When you plan a simple meal, say which two foods complement each other and why.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.0] Pair a cereal (rice, pasta, bread or oats) with a pulse (lentils, chickpeas, beans or peas) in the same meal, for example lentil soup with wholemeal bread, because the cereal and pulse lack different essential amino acids.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.1] Add a small amount of egg, milk, cheese or yoghurt to a plant-based meal to raise its biological value.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.2] Soya products such as tofu and textured vegetable protein are HBV, so they can provide protein alone in a vegetarian meal.
[hom-proteins-s7.2.3] Use pulses as meat extenders, adding them to minced meat dishes such as bolognese or chilli to stretch the meat, which makes the meal cheaper.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.b] Protein is often a large part of a food bill, so value depends on cost per usable portion rather than the price of the pack.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.0] Compare unit prices, such as price per kilogram or per 100 g, to judge value across different pack sizes and brands.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.1] Allow for waste: bone, gristle, fat and cooking losses reduce the usable portion of meat and fish, so a cheap-looking pack may give less to eat.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.2] Cheaper cuts of meat for stewing and braising are as nutritious as expensive cuts and suit slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.3] Eggs, milk, cheese, dried or tinned pulses, tinned fish such as mackerel, tofu, textured vegetable protein and mycoprotein are low-cost sources of protein.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.4] Fish is usually cheaper when in season, for example mackerel.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.5] Own-brand products and special offers save money only if the extra food can be used before it spoils.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.6] Bulk buying saves money when the food is divided into portions and frozen, so that only the amount needed is thawed.
[hom-proteins-s7.3.7] Plan meals and use a shopping list, and use pulses or TVP to extend mince. Low-cost protein foods also fit the Food Pyramid shelf of meat, poultry, fish, eggs, beans and nuts, where two servings a day are recommended.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.b] Protein foods such as meat, fish and dairy spoil quickly and can carry bacteria, so safe buying and storing matter as much as price.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.0] Buy from a clean, reputable retailer, and check the use-by date, and the quality assurance and traceability information on the label, before buying.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.1] Chilled meat, fish and dairy should be kept at 5 degrees Celsius or below, so they are bought last and carried home in a cool bag.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.2] Frozen protein foods should be solid when bought and stored at −18 °C, and packs that have thawed and refrozen should be avoided.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.3] Packaging should be sealed and undamaged, and cans should not be bulging.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.4] Fresh fish has firm flesh, bright eyes and red gills; fresh meat has a fresh colour and smell.
[hom-proteins-s7.4.5] At home, refrigerate protein foods quickly, keep raw and cooked foods apart, defrost in the fridge and cook thoroughly.

CANDIDATES hom-proteins-s4: 4. Sources
[hom-proteins-s4.0.b] Protein comes from both animal and plant foods, so everyone can meet their needs whatever they eat. Exam answers earn marks for naming a variety of foods from each group.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.0] Animal protein sources are meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt. Cheese and yoghurt belong in the list because they are made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.1] Plant protein sources are soya beans and soya products, pulses (lentils, peas and beans), cereals (wheat, rice, maize and oats), nuts and seeds.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.2] Cereals such as wheat, rice and maize supply protein as well as carbohydrate, so everyday foods like wheat, bread and pasta are plant protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.0.3] Potatoes and vegetables contain only small amounts of protein, so they are not counted as main protein sources.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.b] Albumin is the protein most people meet first, in the white of an egg. It is a good example of a protein that is tied to one particular food.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.0] Albumin is the main protein of egg white (ovalbumin), and it is also found in lean meat.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.1] Lactalbumin, a related albumin, is found in the whey of milk, the watery part left when the casein is removed.
[hom-proteins-s4.1.2] Albumin is a soluble globular protein. It dissolves in water and coagulates (sets) when heated, which is why raw egg white turns solid and white on cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.b] Casein is the main protein of milk, so it follows milk into every food made from milk. Cheese-making depends on changing it.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.0] Casein (the older term is caseinogen) is the main protein of milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.1] Casein is present in cheese and yoghurt, the products made from milk.
[hom-proteins-s4.2.2] Casein is coagulated by the enzyme rennin, which is the first step in making cheese because it forms the curd. Acid also coagulates it, which is why milk curdles when it sours.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.b] Gelatine is the protein behind jelly, but it is not found in raw meat. It is made when another protein, collagen, is heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.0] Gelatine is not present in raw meat; it forms when collagen from animal bones, skin and connective tissue is heated in moisture.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.1] Gelatine sets to a gel when it cools, so it is used in jelly, aspic, gelatine-set mousses and desserts, marshmallows and some sweets.
[hom-proteins-s4.3.2] Gelatine comes from animal sources, so foods set with it are not vegetarian.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.b] Gluten is the protein that gives wheat bread its structure. It does not exist in dry flour; it is formed when the flour is wet.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.0] Gluten is the protein of wheat and wheat flour.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.1] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin, both already present in the flour, are mixed with water. Flour itself contains gliadin and glutenin, not formed gluten.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.2] Gluten is elastic and stretchy, and kneading develops it. It gives bread dough its elastic, stretchy texture and traps the carbon dioxide made by yeast, so the dough rises.
[hom-proteins-s4.4.3] Foods that depend on wheat gluten include bread, pasta, cakes, pastry and other flour products.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.b] Each of these proteins is tied to a food or food group. Pairing every named protein with one food is the quickest way to answer a naming question.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.0] Albumin: found in egg white and meat, and it sets when heated.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.1] Casein: found in milk, cheese and yoghurt, and it is coagulated by rennin.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.2] Gelatine: found in set desserts such as jelly, and it is made from collagen.
[hom-proteins-s4.5.3] Gluten: found in wheat flour and bread, and it traps gas in dough.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.b] Meat and fish are made of muscle and connective tissue, and each contains its own proteins. Higher Level questions ask you to name them.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.0] Myosin and actin are the contractile proteins of muscle, which means they contract to move the muscle. They are found in the flesh of meat and fish.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.1] Collagen is the main protein of connective tissue and is found in meat, fish and bones.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.2] Collagen in meat and fish is the source of gelatine when it is heated in moisture; this is why cheap tough cuts of meat tenderise on slow, moist cooking.
[hom-proteins-s4.6.3] Elastin in meat and globulin in egg are also acceptable answers when asked for proteins in meat or egg.

CANDIDATES hom-cereals-grains-s3: 3. Milling and flour
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.b] Milling turns the whole wheat grain into flour by separating the bran, germ and endosperm and then grinding the endosperm finely. Each stage has a purpose, and an account of production should give the stages in order with the reason for each.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.0] 1. Intake and cleaning: the wheat is sampled, graded and may be blended to give the quality needed. It is then passed through sieves, air currents and magnets to remove stones, dust, chaff and metal, so that none of these end up in the flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.1] 2. Conditioning (tempering): a controlled amount of water is added and the grain is left to rest. This toughens the bran so it separates in large pieces rather than shattering into the flour, and it softens the endosperm (the starchy centre of the grain) ready for milling.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.2] 3. Break rolling: corrugated (grooved) rollers crack open the grain and release the endosperm, while the bran and germ stay in larger pieces because the bran is not crushed.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.3] 4. Sifting (plansifting): plansifters, which are stacks of sieves, separate the bran, germ and endosperm particles by size, so each stream can be dealt with separately.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.4] 5. Reduction rolling: smooth rollers grind the endosperm repeatedly, with a sieving step after each pass, until it becomes fine white flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.5] 6. Finishing: flour from different batches is blended for consistent quality, such as gluten strength, and may be treated with permitted improvers. It may be fortified (have extra nutrients added) and is tested for moisture, gluten strength and colour before packing.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.6] Extraction rate is the percentage of the original grain that ends up in the flour. White flour is about 75% and wholemeal flour is 100%, because it keeps all three parts of the grain. Wheatmeal falls between the two.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.7] Stone-ground flour is made by grinding the grain between millstones instead of rollers, and it is accepted as a type of flour.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.0.8] Diagram: a flow chart of the milling stages in order (intake and cleaning, conditioning, break rolling, sifting, reduction rolling, finishing), with the bran, germ and endosperm streams branching off at the sifting step.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.b] The difference between white and wholemeal flour comes down to how much of the grain is kept during milling. This changes the colour, nutrition, texture of baked foods and how long the flour keeps.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.0] White flour is made from the endosperm only, because the bran and germ are removed. It is lighter in colour and texture, and has about 75% extraction.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.1] White flour has less fibre and fewer B vitamins and minerals than wholemeal, because these are mostly lost with the bran and germ. It has a longer shelf life.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.2] Wholemeal flour is made from the whole grain, with 100% extraction, so it is brown and higher in fibre, B vitamins and minerals.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.1.3] Wholemeal flour makes denser baked products. It has a shorter shelf life because of the oil in the germ, which goes off over time.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.b] Flours differ in how much of the grain they contain, how much gluten-forming protein they have and whether anything has been added. This decides which job each one does best.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.0] Plain white (cream) flour has a medium gluten content. It is used for pastry, cakes (creamed or whisked), biscuits, sauces and roux, pizza, scones and batters.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.1] Self-raising flour is plain flour with a raising agent, usually baking powder, added, so it is not a different wheat. It is used for cakes, sponges, scones and biscuits.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.2] Strong flour is made from hard wheat and is high in gluten-forming protein. It is used for yeast breads, pizza dough and puff pastry because the gluten traps gas, such as the carbon dioxide produced by yeast, for a good rise.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.3] Wholemeal (wholewheat) flour is milled from the whole grain, so it contains bran, germ and endosperm. It is used for brown bread, scones, muffins and wholemeal pastry, and gives a denser texture because the bran cuts the gluten strands.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.4] Wheatmeal (brown) flour is partly refined, with part of the bran removed. It is used for brown bread and soda bread.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.5] Cornflour is a fine starch made from the endosperm of maize, not a wheat flour, so it does not contain gluten. It is used as a thickener for sauces, custards and gravy.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.2.6] Rice flour and other gluten-free flours (rice, maize and other blends) are used in baking for people with coeliac disease, who cannot eat gluten.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.b] Gluten is the protein network that gives bread and pastry their structure, and its development is controlled by the way flour is handled.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.0] Gluten forms when the proteins gliadin and glutenin in wheat flour are mixed with water.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.1] Gluten is elastic and stretchy: it traps gas and gives bread its structure.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.2] Kneading develops gluten, producing a strong, elastic dough, whereas rubbing in fat and mixing lightly limit gluten, giving tender pastry.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.3.3] Strong flour contains more gluten-forming protein than plain flour, so it gives a more elastic dough.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.b] Flour is the syllabus's example of a food that undergoes extensive processing. The grain is cleaned, separated, ground, blended, treated and packed, and the packaging and label then protect and inform the buyer.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.0] Flour is a processed food: milling turns the whole grain into a refined ingredient with a different nutrient profile.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.1] Packaging: flour is packed in strong, sealed, food-grade bags, paper or plastic, that protect it from moisture, insects, contamination and odours, and are easy to handle and store.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.2] Labelling: the label gives the name of the food (the type of flour), the ingredients list, the net quantity, the nutrition declaration, the best-before date, storage instructions and the name and address of the food business. It may also carry cooking or recipe guidance.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.3] Allergens: wheat and gluten must be emphasised in the ingredients list, for example in bold type, so people with allergies or coeliac disease can spot them. This is set by Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.4] Traceability: a batch (lot) code and date mark allow the manufacturer to trace the flour back through production, so it can be recalled if there is a problem.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.5] Fortified flour: any added nutrients are shown on the nutrition declaration.
[hom-cereals-grains-s3.4.6] Labelling also lets shoppers make a safe choice, compare products and follow the storage guidance.

CANDIDATES hom-lipids-s5: 5. Functions and energy value
[hom-lipids-s5.0.b] Lipids (fats and oils) do far more in the body than provide energy. Each function below follows from where fat sits in the body or what it does as it passes through the digestive system.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.0] Lipids are a concentrated source of energy, supplying the body with more energy per gram than carbohydrate or protein, so a small amount of fat gives a lot of energy.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.1] Excess energy from any food is stored as adipose tissue (the body's stored fat), which is a reserve of energy for when food intake is low.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.2] Adipose tissue lies under the skin and insulates the body against heat loss, which helps to keep body temperature constant.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.3] Adipose tissue around organs such as the kidneys and heart cushions them against knocks and pressure, and holds them in place.
[hom-lipids-s5.0.4] Lipids slow the emptying of the stomach, which helps to delay hunger, so meals containing fat are more satisfying.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.b] Beyond energy and body protection, lipids carry nutrients, build cell structures and affect how food tastes.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.0] Lipids carry the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K and help the body absorb them, because these vitamins dissolve in fat and not in water. Dairy fat, oily fish and egg yolk supply them.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.1] Lipids are a source of essential fatty acids, the fatty acids the body cannot make for itself and must get from food.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.2] Phospholipids (fat-like lipids containing phosphorus) and cholesterol form part of the structure of every cell membrane, the thin layer around each cell.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.3] Fat also adds flavour and a smooth texture to food, although this is a cooking benefit and not a biological function.
[hom-lipids-s5.1.4] Digestion link: the enzyme lipase digests triglycerides (the main fats in food) to fatty acids and glycerol, and any excess is stored as adipose tissue.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.b] Cholesterol is a lipid that is often blamed for heart disease, yet the body needs it. It is different from the saturated fat in food, and students often confuse the two.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.0] Cholesterol is a fat-like substance called a sterol, found only in animal foods such as egg yolk, offal (liver and kidney), meat, butter, cream, cheese, prawns and other shellfish. Plant foods contain none.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.1] The liver makes cholesterol itself, in fact most of what the body has, so the amount eaten has less effect on blood cholesterol than the saturated fat in the diet.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.2] Saturated fat in the diet raises blood LDL cholesterol more than dietary cholesterol does. A plant oil such as coconut oil is cholesterol-free but can still be high in saturated fat.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.3] The body uses cholesterol to make cell membranes, bile salts that emulsify fat (break it into tiny droplets) for digestion, and steroid hormones such as oestrogen, progesterone, testosterone and cortisol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.4] Vitamin D is made in the skin from a derivative of cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.5] Too much cholesterol in the blood, especially LDL, builds up on artery walls and narrows them, which increases the risk of coronary heart disease (CHD) and stroke. It is also a main constituent of most gallstones.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.6] To lower blood cholesterol, eat less saturated and trans fat and more unsaturated fat, oily fish, oats, pulses, fruit and vegetables (their fibre helps). Keep to a healthy weight, be active and do not smoke.
[hom-lipids-s5.2.7] Functional spreads containing plant stanols or sterols also help to lower blood cholesterol.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.b] Foods high in saturated fat are energy-dense and raise blood LDL cholesterol. A diet high in them over years raises the risk of several diseases, and each has its own mechanism.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.0] Obesity: saturated-fat foods are energy-dense, and surplus energy from them is stored as adipose tissue, which can lead to obesity.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.1] Coronary heart disease and high blood pressure: raised LDL cholesterol narrows the arteries (atherosclerosis), increasing the risk of coronary heart disease, high blood pressure (hypertension) and stroke.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.2] Type 2 diabetes: excess body fat reduces the effectiveness of insulin (the hormone that controls blood sugar), which is called insulin resistance, so the risk of Type 2 diabetes increases.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.3] Gallstones: cholesterol is a main component of gallstones, which can form in the gall bladder.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.4] Varicose veins and infertility are also linked to a high-fat diet; the first four effects carry the most weight.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.5] Lifestyle changes recommended for someone at risk of CHD: a low-fat, low-cholesterol, low-salt diet; losing weight; not smoking; limiting alcohol; exercise; and managing stress.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.6] Irish healthy eating guidelines from the Department of Health advise replacing saturated fats with unsaturated (mono- and polyunsaturated) fats and using fats and oils in small amounts, which is the fats, spreads and oils shelf of the Food Pyramid.
[hom-lipids-s5.3.7] Other advice: choose lean meat and low-fat dairy, and grill, steam or bake instead of frying. Saturated fat should supply less than 10% of daily energy (HSE and Department of Health guidelines).
[hom-lipids-s5.4.b] Energy is measured in kilojoules (kJ) or kilocalories (kcal). Fat has the highest energy value per gram of the three nutrients, which is why fatty foods are so energy-dense.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.0] Fat provides 37 kJ or 9 kcal of energy per gram, which is more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate or protein. The true/false statement that 1 gram of fat provides 20 kilocalories is false.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.1] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ or 3.75 kcal per gram; protein provides 17 kJ or 4 kcal per gram; alcohol provides 29 kJ or 7 kcal per gram.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.2] Energy from fat is calculated by multiplying the grams of fat by 37 kJ, or by 9 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.3] Worked example (sample data): 20 g of fat gives 20 × 37 = 740 kJ, or 20 × 9 = 180 kcal.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.4] A large part of the energy in the average Irish diet comes from fat, much of it hidden in processed and convenience foods, meat products, dairy, cakes, pastries, biscuits, chocolate, fried foods and takeaways, and much of that is saturated.
[hom-lipids-s5.4.5] Guidance is to reduce total fat, especially saturated and trans fat, replacing them with unsaturated fat and complex carbohydrate. Over-consumption of fat leads to obesity because fat is energy-dense and stores readily.

CANDIDATES hom-carbohydrates-s6: 6. Energy value and dietary fibre targets
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.b] Every nutrient releases a set amount of energy per gram, so we can work out how much energy a food supplies from its nutrient content. Carbohydrate is the main energy nutrient in most diets, so you need its figure and how to use it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.0] Carbohydrate provides 16 kJ (3.75 kcal) per gram. Sugars and starch have the same energy value per gram, so a gram of sugar gives no more energy than a gram of starch.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.1] For comparison, protein provides 17 kJ (4 kcal) per gram, fat 37 kJ (9 kcal) per gram and alcohol 29 kJ (7 kcal) per gram. Fat gives more than twice the energy of carbohydrate for the same weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.2] Fibre (non-starch polysaccharide) is not digested by human enzymes, so it contributes little usable energy. Say 'little', not 'none'.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.3] To calculate the energy from a food, multiply the grams of carbohydrate by 16 to get kilojoules (kJ), or by 3.75 to get kilocalories (kcal).
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.4] Worked example: 30 g of sugar provides 30 × 16 = 480 kJ, or 30 × 3.75 = 112.5 kcal. One kilocalorie is about 4.2 kilojoules (1 kcal = 4.184 kJ), which is why the kcal figure is smaller.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.5] Label point: the nutrition declaration on a food label states carbohydrate and, of which, sugars; fibre is an optional item. The energy figure is calculated by the manufacturer, so it may not match your own calculation exactly.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.6] Settings, comparative study: compare two breakfast cereals, one with added sugar and one without, using the label's carbohydrate, sugar and energy per 100 g. Discuss which supplies more energy and which supplies more fibre.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.0.7] Settings, activity comparison: work out the energy in a food such as a bar of chocolate and compare it with the time an activity such as walking or running takes to use it up. Use the energy values from the source given with the question.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.b] Carbohydrate, mostly as starch, is the main source of energy in most diets, and it should supply the largest share of the energy in the diet, with fat and protein supplying the rest. The kind of carbohydrate matters, because some carries other nutrients and some does not.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.0] Healthy eating guidelines use the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid (Department of Health and HSE, 2016). Its base shelf is vegetables, salad and fruit, and the second shelf up is wholemeal cereals, breads, potatoes, pasta and rice.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.1] The top shelf holds foods high in fat, sugar and salt. These are eaten in small amounts and not every day, so they should not be the main source of energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.2] Choose wholemeal and wholegrain starchy foods, because they supply energy together with fibre, vitamins and minerals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.3] Foods with a lot of added sugar supply energy but few other nutrients, so they are described as energy-dense and nutrient-poor ('empty calories').
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.1.4] Replacing sugary foods and refined starchy foods with wholegrain starchy foods, fruit and vegetables raises the intake of fibre and nutrients without raising the energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.b] Fibre is the part of plant foods that humans cannot digest, and adults are advised to eat a set amount each day. Many Irish adults do not reach it, so you should know the target and the everyday ways to meet it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.0] The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) recommends a daily intake of dietary fibre for adults of 25 g a day. This is its adequate intake, set in 2010 for normal bowel function.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.1] Irish healthy eating guidance from the Department of Health and the HSE (the Healthy Ireland Food Pyramid) also advises eating wholegrain cereals, fruit and vegetables every day.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.2] Breakfast: choose a high-fibre cereal (look for at least 3 g of fibre per 100 g on the label), porridge, bran or wholegrain bread or toast. Check the fibre per 100 g when choosing cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.3] Choose wholegrain or wholemeal bread, brown rice, wholemeal pasta and brown flour in baking in place of white versions, because the fibre is in the outer part of the grain that white versions have lost.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.4] Add pulses such as beans, lentils, peas and chickpeas to soups, stews and salads. Half a tin of beans gives about 7.5 g of fibre, and chickpeas, kidney beans and butter beans give about 10 g.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.5] Add two tablespoons of seeds a day, such as sunflower, sesame, pumpkin and linseed, or bran or porridge oats, to cereal, yoghurt, bread and salads.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.6] Eat vegetables at lunch and dinner, about a third of each meal; frozen vegetables are as good as fresh. Leave the skins on fruit and vegetables, and bake or boil potatoes in their jackets, since most of the fibre in a potato is in the skin.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.2.7] Eat 2-3 pieces of fruit a day (a piece gives about 2 g of fibre), and snack on fresh fruit, dried fruit or a handful of nuts instead of biscuits or crisps. Increase fibre gradually and drink plenty of water, because fibre absorbs water.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.b] Added sugar supplies energy with few other nutrients, so cutting it down is a main healthy eating target. Strategies fall into two groups: what you buy, and how you prepare and eat food.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.0] Purchasing: read labels and ingredient lists, because added sugars appear under names such as sucrose, glucose syrup, fructose, honey, cane sugar, brown sugar, corn syrup and fruit juice concentrate.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.1] Choose reduced-sugar, lower-sugar or no-added-sugar versions of drinks, jams, cereals and yoghurts, choose fruit canned in juice rather than syrup, and avoid sugar-coated cereals.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.2] Front-of-pack colour (traffic-light) labelling, where used, shows at a glance whether a food is high, medium or low in sugars. It is voluntary, so not every product carries it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.3] Preparing: reduce the sugar in recipes and use spices, vanilla or ripe fruit for flavour. Pureed fruit, banana, dates or apple can sweeten recipes instead of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.4] Do not add sugar to tea, coffee or breakfast cereals. Replace fizzy drinks with water, or with fruit juice diluted with sparkling water, which cuts the sugar per glass.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.5] Eat whole fruit rather than fruit juice or smoothies. Juice contains little fibre, and the sugar in juices and smoothies is released from the fruit's cells so it counts as free sugar, whereas whole fruit keeps its fibre and fills you up.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.3.6] Snacks and treats: limit sweets, biscuits, cakes, sweet sauces and sugary drinks, and keep fresh fruit, fruit or vegetable sticks, nuts and natural yoghurt as snacks instead.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.b] A diet high in sugar causes harm in several ways, some directly on the teeth and others through the energy and nutrients the diet supplies. Each effect needs its reason to earn marks.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.0] Tooth decay: bacteria in plaque (the sticky film on teeth) ferment sugar to produce acid, which dissolves the enamel of the teeth.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.1] Weight gain and obesity: sugary foods are energy-dense and nutrient-poor, so regular intake adds to excess energy intake.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.2] Type 2 diabetes: a diet high in sugar and energy is a risk factor for type 2 diabetes, which is linked to excess body weight.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.3] Heart disease and high blood pressure: high sugar intake is associated with a higher risk of heart disease and raised blood pressure. High fructose intake has been linked with higher blood pressure, and refined carbohydrate with excess insulin that can raise LDL cholesterol.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.4] Nutritional deficiency: foods high in sugar displace fruit, vegetables and other nutrient-rich foods from the diet, so vitamins and minerals fall short.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.4.5] Sugary foods are also low in fibre, so they do little for bowel health.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.b] Artificial sweeteners give a sweet taste without the energy of sugar, or with less of it. They fall into two types, intense and bulk, and each has its uses and limits.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.0] Intense sweeteners, such as aspartame and saccharin, are many times sweeter than sugar, so only tiny amounts are needed and they add almost no energy.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.1] Bulk sweeteners, such as sorbitol, mannitol and xylitol, have a similar bulk to sugar and provide some energy, though less than sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.2] Uses in food manufacture: they sweeten and improve palatability, and they are used in low-calorie, sugar-free, slimming and reduced-sugar foods, soft drinks and confectionery.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.3] Sorbitol and other bulk sweeteners act as humectants: they retain moisture and stop products drying out.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.4] Bulk sweeteners are used where sugar's bulk is needed, as in confectionery and fudge. Xylitol and sorbitol do not cause tooth decay, so they are used to avoid it.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.5] Sweeteners cannot replace all of sugar's functions: they do not provide the bulk, browning, preserving or bulk-texture roles of sugar.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.6] Sweeteners are food additives with E numbers. Labels must say 'contains sweetener', and aspartame carries a warning for people with phenylketonuria.
[hom-carbohydrates-s6.5.7] Excess bulk sweeteners can have a laxative effect, so their labels carry a warning.

CANDIDATES hom-vitamins-minerals-s2: 2. Water-soluble vitamins C, B12 and folate
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.b] Vitamin C, vitamin B12 and folate are water-soluble vitamins, so they behave differently from vitamins that dissolve in fat. Knowing this explains why they are needed every day and why cooking can ruin them.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.0] Water-soluble vitamins (the B group and C) dissolve in water and are not stored in useful amounts in the body. Any excess is lost in urine, so they are needed regularly in the diet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.1] The exception is vitamin B12, which the liver stores, so a store builds up that can last for several years.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.2] Because they dissolve in water, these vitamins are lost into cooking water and are easily destroyed during storage, preparation and cooking.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.0.3] The RDA (recommended daily allowance) is the daily amount of a nutrient that meets the needs of almost everyone in a group. It varies with age, sex, pregnancy and breastfeeding, and smokers are advised more vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.b] Vitamin C is the most-asked vitamin in the exam, and it comes mainly from fruit and vegetables. Fresh, raw and in-season produce has the most.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.0] Citrus fruit (oranges, lemons, grapefruit), blackcurrants, rosehips, kiwi fruit and strawberries are rich sources of vitamin C.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.1] Peppers, broccoli, sprouts, cabbage, tomatoes, new potatoes and peas are good vegetable sources.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.1.2] Fresh fruit juices and smoothies, and fortified drinks, also supply vitamin C, and a supplement can too. Any fruit or vegetable is accepted as a source in an answer.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.b] Vitamin C does several different jobs in the body, and exam questions usually ask for two or three of them. The key one is collagen, because so many other effects follow from it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.0] Vitamin C is needed to make collagen, the protein of connective tissue that gives strength to skin, blood vessels, gums, cartilage, bones and teeth.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.1] By supporting collagen formation, vitamin C speeds up the healing of wounds, because new tissue can be built.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.2] Vitamin C aids iron absorption. It converts non-haem iron (the iron in plant foods) from the ferric form (Fe³⁺) to the ferrous form (Fe²⁺), which is absorbed more readily.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.3] Vitamin C also aids calcium absorption, which marking schemes accept.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.4] Vitamin C is an antioxidant: it neutralises free radicals (damaging particles) and so protects cells from damage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.2.5] Vitamin C supports the immune system, helping the body resist infection, and it prevents scurvy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.b] If the diet lacks vitamin C for a long time, collagen cannot be made properly and the body's tissues start to fail. The deficiency disease is scurvy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.0] Scurvy develops after a severe and prolonged lack of vitamin C in the diet.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.1] Weak collagen causes bleeding, sore and swollen gums, loose teeth, easy bruising and painful joints.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.2] Wounds heal slowly because new tissue cannot be formed properly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.3] Reduced iron absorption can lead to anaemia, with tiredness and pale skin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.3.4] The body becomes more susceptible to infection because its resistance is lowered.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.b] Vitamin C is easily lost, but the loss depends on method and time. Cooking does not destroy all of it: steaming, microwaving and short cooking keep most.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.0] Buy fresh produce and use it quickly, and eat it raw where possible.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.1] Wash vegetables before cutting rather than soaking them, prepare them just before cooking and cut them in large pieces, so less of the surface is exposed.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.2] Cook in a little boiling water for a short time with the lid on, or steam, microwave or stir-fry, so less vitamin C dissolves or is destroyed.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.3] Do not use bicarbonate of soda or copper pans, which destroy the vitamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.4.4] Serve at once rather than keeping the food hot, and use the cooking water in soups and sauces to recover the vitamin that dissolved into it.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.b] Ascorbic acid is a very unstable vitamin, and its properties explain every rule for keeping it in food. Higher Level answers on vitamin C add these properties to sources, functions and deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.0] Vitamin C is a white, crystalline, acidic substance with a sweet-sour taste, and it is very soluble in water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.1] Because it is water-soluble, vitamin C is lost into cooking water when vegetables are boiled or soaked.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.2] Vitamin C is destroyed by prolonged heat, by light, by alkalis such as bicarbonate of soda, and by oxidation in air.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.3] The enzyme oxidase is released when plant cells are cut or bruised and destroys vitamin C, so cut vegetables should be cooked promptly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.4] Copper and brass pans destroy vitamin C, and it is also lost during long storage and dehydration.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.5.5] Vitamin C is an antioxidant, which means it acts as a reducing agent (it donates electrons), and this is how it changes ferric iron to ferrous iron.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.b] Vitamin B12 is unusual because only animal foods contain it naturally. Its absorption also needs a helper made in the stomach.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.0] Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in animal foods: liver and kidney, meat, fish and oily fish, eggs, milk, cheese and yoghurt.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.1] Plant foods contain no natural vitamin B12, so vegans rely on fortified foods such as some breakfast cereals, plant milks and yeast extract, or on supplements.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.2] The lining of the stomach makes intrinsic factor, a protein that binds vitamin B12 so that it can be absorbed in the ileum (the end of the small intestine).
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.6.3] The liver stores vitamin B12, and the store can last for several years, so deficiency develops slowly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.b] B12 is needed wherever cells are being made or nerves are working, which is why its deficiency affects both the blood and the nervous system.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.0] Vitamin B12 works with folate to form healthy red blood cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.1] Vitamin B12 is needed for the synthesis of DNA, so it is needed for cell division, especially in cells that divide rapidly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.2] Vitamin B12 maintains the myelin sheath, the fatty layer that insulates nerve fibres and keeps nerve impulses working normally, so it keeps nerve cells healthy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.7.3] Vitamin B12 is also involved in the body's use of fatty acids and amino acids.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.b] A lack of B12 harms both the blood and the nerves. The cause can be the diet or a failure to absorb the vitamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.0] B12 deficiency causes megaloblastic anaemia, in which red blood cells are too large and immature and cannot carry oxygen effectively.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.1] Symptoms of this anaemia include tiredness, weakness, pale skin and breathlessness.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.2] Nerve damage causes numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, poor balance and memory problems.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.3] A strict vegan diet without fortified foods or supplements can cause deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.4] Pernicious anaemia is the form of B12 deficiency caused by a lack of intrinsic factor, often through an autoimmune condition, which prevents B12 absorption even when the diet is adequate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.8.5] Failure to absorb B12 can also come from stomach or gut disease, and older people absorb less.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.b] B12 is more stable than vitamin C, but it is not indestructible.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.0] Vitamin B12 is water-soluble, so some is lost into cooking water.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.1] Vitamin B12 is a red crystalline compound that contains the mineral cobalt, which gives it the name cobalamin.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.9.2] Vitamin B12 is stable to heat in ordinary cooking but is destroyed by light and by strong acids and alkalis.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.b] Folate is the natural form of vitamin B9, and folic acid is the synthetic form. Folate is needed for making new cells, which matters most in early pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.0] Natural folate is found in dark green leafy vegetables such as spinach, broccoli, sprouts and cabbage, in pulses such as chickpeas, lentils and beans, and in asparagus, oranges, liver and yeast extract.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.1] Wholegrain and fortified breakfast cereals also supply folate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.2] Folic acid is the synthetic form used in supplements and in fortified foods such as breakfast cereals. It is not a different vitamin from folate.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.3] Folate is needed to synthesise DNA and RNA, so it is essential for cell division and growth, including the growth of the foetus.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.4] Folate works with vitamin B12 to form red blood cells.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.10.5] In the first weeks of pregnancy, folate is essential for the formation of the neural tube, the early brain and spinal cord, and it protects against neural tube defects such as spina bifida and anencephaly.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.b] The neural tube forms in the first weeks of pregnancy, before many women know they are pregnant, so folate advice is aimed at women before they conceive.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.0] A lack of folate causes megaloblastic anaemia, with the same large, immature red blood cells as B12 deficiency, and tiredness, weakness, pale skin, breathlessness and a sore tongue.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.1] Neural tube defects, such as spina bifida and anencephaly, can develop in the unborn baby if the mother lacks folate in early pregnancy.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.2] Poor growth is also an effect of folate deficiency.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.11.3] Women who may become pregnant are advised, in HSE advice, to take a folic acid supplement before conception and during early pregnancy to reduce the risk of neural tube defects, as well as eating folate-rich foods.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.b] Folate is fragile in the kitchen, which is why fortified foods use the more stable synthetic form.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.0] Folate is water-soluble and is sensitive to heat, light and oxygen, so it is destroyed by prolonged cooking and storage.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.1] Folate is lost into cooking water during prolonged boiling.
[hom-vitamins-minerals-s2.12.2] Synthetic folic acid is more stable than natural folate, which is why it is used in fortification.
