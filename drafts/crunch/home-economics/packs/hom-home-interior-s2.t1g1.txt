[hom-home-interior-s2.0.b] Before choosing a floor covering you need to know what lies beneath it, because the structure decides what the covering has to cope with. There are two structures: solid and suspended.
[hom-home-interior-s2.0.0] A solid floor is a concrete slab laid on hardcore (a layer of broken stone) with a damp-proof layer (a membrane or course) to stop moisture rising, and finished with a thin screed (a smooth fine-concrete top coat). It is usually found on the ground floor of modern homes.
[hom-home-interior-s2.0.1] A suspended floor is made of joists (timber, concrete or steel beams) carrying tongue-and-groove boards or planks, with an air space beneath. The air circulates and lets moisture escape, so the boards stay dry. It is usually found on upper floors.
[hom-home-interior-s2.0.2] A damp-proof course (a waterproof layer) can be added in a suspended floor to prevent dampness reaching the boards.
[hom-home-interior-s2.0.3] Boards in a suspended floor can be sanded and varnished. Gaps and loose boards in a suspended timber floor must be fixed before a new covering is laid, because they would show through and make the covering uneven.
[hom-home-interior-s2.0.4] Diagram: draw cross-sections of both floors. For the solid floor label the screed, concrete slab, damp-proof layer and hardcore. For the suspended floor label the boards, joists and air space.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.b] Floor coverings are grouped as hard, semi-hard and soft. Hard coverings include clay and stone tiles, glass and resin mosaics and safety vinyl, so they last well and clean easily, but they tend to be cold and unforgiving underfoot.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.0] Ceramic tiles are made from clay, minerals and water, can be glazed or unglazed, and come in many sizes and shapes. Terracotta and glazed stone tiles are related tile types.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.1] Ceramic tiles are hard-wearing, easy to clean, stain resistant and resistant to water, so they suit wet areas. They are cold and hard underfoot and can crack or chip if something heavy falls on them.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.2] Quarry tiles are unglazed clay tiles that are hard-wearing, which is why they suit hallways and kitchens.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.3] Flagstones are cut natural stone with uneven textures and natural colours, such as in a hallway. They are hard-wearing but cold underfoot and chip easily.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.4] Slate is a fine-grained, foliated (layered) metamorphic rock. Its main points are that it is hard-wearing and easy to clean. It is also warm underfoot and slightly absorbent.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.5] Marble and travertine are natural limestone-type stone, refined and cut into tiles and slabs, with flecks of colour. They are attractive but chip easily and are cold underfoot.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.6] Stone carpeting is a gravel-based mixture poured on the subfloor and sealed when set. Mosaics are made of glass, metal, resin and stone in varied textures and colours, and are decorative and hard-wearing.
[hom-home-interior-s2.1.7] Safety flooring is a slip-resistant vinyl covering, used where slipping is a risk such as in bathrooms and hospitals. It is also hard-wearing and hygienic.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.b] Semi-hard coverings, such as wood, vinyl, linoleum, cork, coir and sisal, are firm but have more give than tile or stone. Each is chosen for particular properties, so learn what each is made of and its main strength and weakness.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.0] Strip wood flooring is made of tongue-and-groove strips of hardwood such as oak, beech or maple, or softwood such as pine or spruce. It may be solid.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.1] Block parquet is made of hardwood blocks laid in a herringbone pattern, and mosaic parquet uses blocks laid in a basket-weave pattern.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.2] Wood flooring is attractive, durable and low maintenance, and most boards are sold ready sealed, so they need little finishing. It can be expensive.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.3] Wood flooring is noisy, cold underfoot, and marked or scratched easily. Softwood boards dent more readily than hardwood.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.4] Laminated wood flooring is laminated, with a plastic veneer (a thin surface layer) over the wood. It is hard-wearing and low maintenance, but hard underfoot and may scratch.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.5] Vinyl is made from polyvinyl chloride (PVC), a plastic developed from petroleum, mixed with resin, plasticiser, pigment and filler, and put together under heat and pressure on a backing. It comes in sheets or tiles in many colours and designs, such as marbled, flecked, embossed, veined or striped.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.6] Vinyl is durable (varying with thickness), quiet, easy to clean, hygienic, stain resistant and resistant to water, acids and alkalis. It can be marked by heels or furniture.
[hom-home-interior-s2.2.7] Foam-backed vinyl is softer and warmer underfoot because of its foam layer, but vinyl may scorch or melt if exposed to high heat.
[hom-home-interior-s2.3.b] These four coverings are made from natural plant materials. Each has a different set of properties, so do not give one the properties of another.
[hom-home-interior-s2.3.0] Linoleum is a natural floor covering made from linseed oil, cork dust and resin on a jute backing. It is resilient (springs back after pressure), quiet underfoot and easy to care for.
[hom-home-interior-s2.3.1] Cork is made from granules of the bark of the cork oak bonded with polyurethane resin, or sliced directly from the bark. It is sold sealed or unsealed, and is warm and quiet underfoot but not very hard-wearing.
[hom-home-interior-s2.3.2] Coir is made from coconut fibre that is softened in water, beaten, spun and woven. It is tough and hard-wearing.
[hom-home-interior-s2.3.3] Sisal is made from the fibre of an agave plant, a dark green spiky plant. It is hard-wearing, easy to clean and anti-static (it does not build up static electricity).
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.b] Soft coverings are carpets and rugs. They are the warmest and quietest choice underfoot, but they need more care than hard floors.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.0] Carpet is soft, warm and resilient underfoot, insulates against heat and absorbs sound. It comes in many colours and patterns and is durable.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.1] Carpet needs regular vacuuming because it dirties easily, and it can be expensive, depending on fibre and quality.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.2] Woven carpets have a fabric pile woven onto a hessian backing. Axminster has a cut pile and allows an almost unlimited range of colours and designs.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.3] Wilton is woven from a continuous strand of yarn. It is thicker than Axminster but limits the number of colours.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.4] Tufted carpets have tufts inserted into a woven backing, held in place with adhesive and usually backed with foam.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.5] Fibre blends include 100 per cent wool, acrylic and polypropylene. Carpets made of 80 per cent wool and 20 per cent nylon combine the warmth and resilience of wool with the durability of nylon.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.6] Carpet tiles are sealed-edge squares backed with PVC or rubber and are stain resistant. A damaged tile can be replaced individually, but the range of colours and patterns is limited. They are graded light, medium, heavy and general.
[hom-home-interior-s2.4.7] Underlay sits beneath carpet, so it adds cushioning and insulation and extending the life of the carpet. Rugs are loose and can be moved, but they need a non-slip backing to prevent slipping on hard floors.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.b] The right covering depends on the room it is going into and the people who use it, so work through a set of factors. Each factor needs a reason when you answer a question.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.0] Function and traffic flow decide durability: hallways and kitchens need hard-wearing surfaces, while bedrooms can take soft coverings.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.1] Subfloor condition and type must be checked, as a poor subfloor will show through thin coverings and make laying difficult.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.2] Warmth underfoot matters in bedrooms and bathrooms. Cold surfaces such as tile or stone can be made warmer with underfloor heating or rugs.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.3] Maintenance, cleaning and hygiene: a sealed or non-porous covering is easier to keep clean than an unsealed one.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.4] Noise is reduced by soft coverings and by underlay, which is important in flats and upper-floor rooms. Safety also counts, so a non-slip surface suits wet areas.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.5] Cost includes the purchase price, laying and upkeep, so a cheaper covering that needs frequent replacement may cost more over time.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.6] Décor, the size of the room, the flooring in adjoining rooms and the occupants also influence the choice.
[hom-home-interior-s2.5.7] Typical matches: hallway (ceramic, quarry tile, flagstone, wood or vinyl); kitchen (tiles, vinyl or linoleum); bathroom (ceramic, slate, vinyl or safety flooring, which are durable, water resistant and non-slip); sitting room (wood, carpet or vinyl); bedroom (carpet); home office (carpet or carpet tiles).
[hom-home-interior-s2.6.b] Paint is the most common wall finish because it is cheap and quick to apply. The main choice is between water-based and solvent-based paint, and between finishes that reflect light and ones that absorb it.
[hom-home-interior-s2.6.0] Emulsion paint is water-based, quick drying, easy to apply, low odour and inexpensive, and is available in a wide variety of colours and shades. It is durable and washable.
[hom-home-interior-s2.6.1] Silk vinyl emulsion is a washable emulsion with a slight sheen, suitable for children's rooms, kitchens and hallways.
[hom-home-interior-s2.6.2] Solvent-based gloss or satin paint is hard-wearing and washable and copes with high condensation, so it is often used on woodwork and in kitchens and bathrooms. It has a strong smell and dries slowly.
[hom-home-interior-s2.6.3] Gloss paint reflects light and is washable. Matt paint absorbs light and hides surface flaws, but it marks more easily and is harder to wipe clean.
[hom-home-interior-s2.6.4] Textured paint contains plastic fibres that create a rough, textured finish. Specialised paints include flame retardant paint and mould resistant paint for bathrooms.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.b] Where paint is not enough, a wall can be papered, tiled, panelled or decorated. Each choice suits different rooms and conditions.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.0] General-purpose wallpaper has a design printed on paper, varies in thickness and hides faults. Embossed wallpaper has a design stamped into the paper and can be painted.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.1] Woodchip wallpaper is paper with wood chips in it, hides faults and can be painted. Flock wallpaper has a raised, velvet-like pattern.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.2] Vinyl or washable wallpaper is plastic-coated, so it is washable, strong and hard-wearing and withstands condensation. It has a huge choice of designs, camouflages faults, can be patterned or textured and makes rooms cosier.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.3] Lining paper is a plain paper applied before paint or wallpaper to give a smooth, even surface.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.4] Ceramic or porcelain wall tiles are waterproof, easy to clean and hygienic, which makes them suitable behind sinks and cookers, around baths and in showers. They come glazed or unglazed in many colours, sizes and patterns.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.5] Tiling needs a sound wall, waterproof adhesive and grout (the filler between tiles). It is costly to install and can feel cold.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.6] Panelling in wood or medium-density fibreboard adds texture and warmth to a wall, such as in a living room.
[hom-home-interior-s2.7.7] Stencils and motifs can be added to a painted wall to add decoration.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.b] Choosing a wall finish means matching it to the room's conditions, the people using it and the budget. Give a reason with each factor.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.0] The function of the room and its conditions decide the finish: a hallway needs a washable surface, a kitchen or bathroom has moisture, while a bedroom can have a matt, softer finish.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.1] Durability depends on traffic flow and the age of the occupants, since young children are more likely to mark walls. Non-toxic products matter in children's rooms.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.2] The size and shape of a room and the condition of its walls affect the choice. Cracks, damp or uneven plaster must be treated, and damaged walls may need filling or lining before decoration.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.3] Light reflectance: gloss and pale finishes reflect light and brighten a room, while matt and dark finishes absorb it.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.4] Maintenance and cleaning: a washable finish saves time and cost over the life of the wall.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.5] Budget must cover materials, preparation and professional labour, which adds to the cost of tiling and wallpapering.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.6] Environmental impact: water-based paints release fewer volatile organic compounds (harmful vapours given off as paint dries) than solvent-based paints.
[hom-home-interior-s2.8.7] Décor and trends, texture and finish, ease of application, coverage and aesthetics (how the finish looks) also affect the choice.
[hom-home-interior-s2.9.b] Furniture is expensive and used for years, so choose it with several factors in mind. Think about the room, the user and the cost.
[hom-home-interior-s2.9.0] Furniture style may be antique, traditional or contemporary, and the choice should suit the room's décor and the occupants' preferences. The function of the room and the principles of design, such as proportion and balance, also matter.

[hom-home-interior-s2.9.2] Freestanding furniture can be moved and replaced easily, while built-in units make best use of alcoves and awkward spaces.

[hom-home-interior-s2.9.5] Safety labels, quality marks and guarantees indicate that a product has been tested and is backed by the manufacturer. Choose furniture and upholstery that is fire resistant and carries safety labels.
[hom-home-interior-s2.9.6] Budget: a well-made piece costs more initially but lasts longer, so cost must be weighed against quality. Availability also matters.
[hom-home-interior-s2.9.7] For a bedroom, consider storage, size, durability and safety. For an office, consider desk size and shape, the chair, built-in drawers and shelving, with both functional and aesthetic guidelines.

[hom-home-interior-s2.10.0] Storage furniture includes wall units, filing cabinets, TV cabinets, furniture chests, toy boxes and storage boxes and baskets.

[hom-home-interior-s2.10.6] Display shelves show photographs, trophies and ornaments, which enhances the appearance of a room.

CANDIDATES hom-home-interior-s1: 1. Elements and principles of design and factors in interior design
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.b] The elements of design are the visual building blocks of a room, the things you can actually see and touch when you walk in. The marking schemes list colour, line, shape, pattern and texture, and form (the 3-D version of shape) may be added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.0] You should be able to name three elements and explain two of them, each with an example of how it is used in a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.0.1] Each element does a job: line leads the eye, shape and form give a room its feel, colour sets mood, pattern adds interest and texture adds warmth or a clean look.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.b] Line and shape are the structure of a room. They decide whether it feels tall or wide, soft or crisp, before any colour is added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.0] Line is the path the eye follows; horizontal lines make a room feel wider and calmer, while vertical lines make it feel taller.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.1] Lines come from skirting boards, picture rails, curtain edges, cabinet edges and the joints between tiles or floorboards, so a room always contains them.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.2] Shape is the two-dimensional (flat) outline of an object or area; form is its three-dimensional volume, such as the bulk of a sofa.
[hom-home-interior-s1.1.3] Rounded shapes look soft and informal, while angular shapes look crisp and formal.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.b] Colour is the element with the biggest effect on how a room feels. To use it well you need the colour wheel vocabulary and to know how warm and cool colours behave.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.0] Primary colours are red, yellow and blue; they cannot be made by mixing other colours.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.1] Secondary colours are orange, green and violet, each made by mixing two primaries.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.2] Tertiary colours are made by mixing a primary with a neighbouring secondary colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.3] Warm colours (red, orange and yellow) advance visually, which means they seem to come towards you, so a room feels cosy and smaller. Cool colours (blue, green and violet) recede, so a room feels spacious and calm.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.4] Pastels are pale, soft colours that feel restful, and neutrals are colours such as white and cream that go with almost anything.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.5] Pale colours reflect more light and make a room appear larger; dark colours absorb light and make a room appear smaller and more intimate.
[hom-home-interior-s1.2.6] Pale ceilings look higher than dark ones.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.b] Changing how light or dark a colour is gives a range from one hue (a pure colour such as blue). Choosing which colours sit together then gives a colour scheme.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.0] A tint is a colour with white added, a tone has grey added, and a shade has black added.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.1] A monochromatic scheme uses tints, tones and shades of a single colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.2] A complementary (contrasting) scheme pairs colours opposite each other on the colour wheel, such as blue and orange, for contrast.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.3] A harmonious (analogous) scheme uses colours that sit next to each other on the wheel, such as green, blue and violet, for a restful effect.
[hom-home-interior-s1.3.4] An accent colour is a strong colour used in small amounts, such as cushions or a single feature wall, to draw the eye.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.b] Colour can do four jobs in a room, and the marking schemes list all four. Which colours you choose also depends on the way the room faces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.0] Colour can link rooms, so that a repeated colour makes one space flow into the next.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.1] Colour can alter the apparent proportions of a room, for example pale colours to make it look larger and dark colours to make it feel smaller and cosier.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.2] Colour can highlight a room's good features, or disguise poor ones.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.3] Colour can create atmosphere, such as pastels for a restful bedroom or warm colours for a cosy sitting room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.4.4] Aspect (the direction a room faces) matters: a north-facing room receives little daylight, so warm colours suit it, while cool colours suit a south-facing room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.b] Pattern adds variety and interest, but the amount and scale you use decide whether a room feels lively or fussy.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.0] Pattern is a repeated or decorative design, such as stripes, checks, florals or geometric motifs.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.1] Large-scale or bold patterns dominate a room and make it feel busy and can shrink it; small-scale patterns recede and create a calmer effect.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.2] Several patterns can work together if they are linked by a shared colour, texture or motif, which ties the room together.
[hom-home-interior-s1.5.3] Too much pattern makes a room fussy, while too little makes it bland.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.b] Texture is how a surface feels, or looks as if it feels. It affects both the comfort of a room and how easy it is to keep clean.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.0] Texture is the surface quality of a material, perceived by touch and by the way light falls on it.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.1] Smooth textures such as glass, mirror, glazed tile, paint and polished steel look cool and are easy to keep hygienic, so they suit kitchens and bathrooms.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.2] Rough or soft textures such as carpet, upholstery, brick and textured wallpaper or paint add warmth and comfort to a sitting room or bedroom.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.3] Matt surfaces absorb light and hide surface flaws, while gloss surfaces reflect light and show marks.
[hom-home-interior-s1.6.4] Contrasting textures, such as smooth glass beside rough wool, add interest without adding colour.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.b] The principles of design are the rules for putting the elements together. The four the marking schemes use are proportion, balance, emphasis and rhythm.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.0] Proportion is the size relationship between the parts of a room, such as furniture, windows, doors and wall areas, and between those parts and the room itself.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.1] Scale is the size of one object relative to the room around it; oversized furniture in a small room makes the room look crowded.
[hom-home-interior-s1.7.2] Proportion guides furniture choice: a large sofa suits a large room, while a small room suits low, slim pieces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.b] Balance is about sharing visual weight around a room so that it feels settled rather than lopsided.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.0] Balance creates harmony, so that no single feature dominates and colour, pattern and texture are shared out evenly.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.1] Formal balance is symmetrical, with matching items placed either side of a central point, such as a pair of lamps beside a fireplace.
[hom-home-interior-s1.8.2] Informal balance places items of different shapes but similar visual weight on either side of a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.b] Emphasis gives the eye somewhere to go first. That place is called the focal point, and it stops a room looking like a jumble of equal features.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.0] Emphasis creates a focal point: the feature the eye goes to first, such as a fireplace, a window view, a feature wall or a large painting.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.1] A focal point can be created with colour, lighting, a strong pattern or the position of furniture.
[hom-home-interior-s1.9.2] A room works best with one focal point; two or more features competing for attention make the room feel disjointed.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.b] Rhythm is what makes the eye travel smoothly round a room instead of stopping and starting.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.0] Rhythm is created by repeating a colour, shape, style, line, pattern or texture so that the eye moves easily around the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.10.1] Repeating a cushion colour on the sofa and in a rug, for example, links the two areas and carries the eye across the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.b] Evaluate a room by naming each element used, then checking whether each principle is achieved and where it fails. Designing one follows a sensible order, starting from what the room is for.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.0] Start from the room's function, such as a living room for relaxing and watching television, and choose a colour scheme that suits that function.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.1] Place the focal point first, then arrange seating and storage so that the focal point is visible from the main entrance.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.2] Use proportion to choose furniture: a large sofa suits a large room, while a small room suits low, slim pieces.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.3] Then arrange furniture and traffic routes, and check each principle in turn.
[hom-home-interior-s1.11.4] Diagram: a labelled plan of one room showing furniture placement, windows, doors, radiator, the focal point and the main traffic route.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.b] Aesthetic factors are about how a room looks and who decides that. They explain why two families can furnish the same room very differently.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.0] Aesthetic factors are the occupants' personal taste, current trends, the style of the house, and the function and décor of the room, which together set the look of a room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.1] Trends change quickly, so a room decorated to the latest fashion can date within a few years; neutral base colours with accessories that are easy to change avoid this.
[hom-home-interior-s1.12.2] Trends such as open-plan living, neutral schemes and natural materials show how fashion shapes interior design.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.b] A room can look beautiful and still be unpleasant to live in. Comfort factors make sure it works for the people using it.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.0] Comfort depends on heating, lighting and ventilation, so a room needs to be warm, well lit and able to be aired.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.1] Seating and work surfaces should be comfortable and at suitable heights for the people using them.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.2] Comfort also depends on the room's function (working versus relaxing), its aspect, the space available and the layout of the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.3] A north-facing room receives little direct sunlight, so warm colours help to make it feel bright and cosy.
[hom-home-interior-s1.13.4] Quiet and noisy zones should be separated, so that a family member working or studying is not disturbed by people watching television.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.b] Ergonomics is about the fit between people and the layout of a room, so that they can move, reach and work with least effort and strain. It is not the same as how the room looks.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.0] Ergonomics is the design of a room so that its layout suits how people move, reach and work.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.1] The work triangle places the sink, cooker and fridge at the three points of an imaginary triangle, so that movements between them are short and efficient.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.2] Work zones group related tasks: preparation, cooking, washing up and storage, arranged in the order they are carried out.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.3] Traffic flow should keep the main routes through a room clear of furniture, so that no one has to walk around a sofa or across a doorway.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.4] Storage and work surfaces should sit at heights that avoid bending and stretching, with frequently used items between shoulder and knee height.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.5] Work surfaces with few joints and crevices are quicker to clean and resist the build-up of food and dirt.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.6] Sockets and switches should be easy to reach.
[hom-home-interior-s1.14.7] Diagram: a kitchen plan showing the work triangle, with sink, cooker and fridge labelled.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.b] A home has to fit the people living in it. Their number, ages and way of life decide how a room is planned.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.0] The number, ages and stage of the family determine the number of bedrooms, the storage needed and the amount of living space required.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.1] Young children need safe, easy-to-clean surfaces, rounded furniture edges and a play area that can be supervised from the kitchen.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.2] Teenagers may need a study area with good lighting and a door that gives privacy.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.3] A home office or hobby space affects the layout when a family member works from home or pursues a craft.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.4] Income and work patterns also shape what a family needs from its rooms.
[hom-home-interior-s1.15.5] Future needs should be allowed for, such as a room that can later become a bedroom or an attic that can be converted.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.b] Some people need features that most homes do not have. Good design removes barriers, so that a person with limited mobility, weak grip or poor sight can live safely and independently.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.0] A wheelchair user needs wider doorways, clear turning space, lowered worktops and lowered switches and sockets.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.1] Level thresholds and ramps remove steps at entrances; grab rails and a raised toilet seat help people with limited mobility.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.2] Easy-to-operate fittings such as lever handles and easy-close curtains suit people with weak grip or limited strength.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.3] Good contrast between floor, walls and furniture helps a person with poor sight to find doorways, steps and edges.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.4] Non-slip floor finishes and good lighting on stairs and in bathrooms reduce the risk of falls.
[hom-home-interior-s1.16.5] Adaptable design, which can be changed as needs change, means the home can keep suiting the person.
[hom-home-interior-s1.17.b] Budget limits every design. Thinking about the whole cost of an item, not just the price tag, leads to better choices.
[hom-home-interior-s1.17.0] Budget covers the initial purchase, fitting and maintenance of each item, and running costs where an item uses energy or water.
[hom-home-interior-s1.17.1] A limited budget can still produce a good design by choosing one focal point and spending most of the money on it.
[hom-home-interior-s1.17.2] Painting walls and updating soft furnishings changes a room's style at much lower cost than replacing furniture or flooring.
[hom-home-interior-s1.17.3] Cheaper materials may wear out sooner, so their true cost includes how often they will need replacing; weigh cost against quality.
[hom-home-interior-s1.17.4] Buying in stages allows a room to be completed as money becomes available, provided the first purchases fit the final plan.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.b] Every material has an effect on the environment in how it is made, used and thrown away. Environmentally aware design chooses items with a low impact at each stage.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.0] Consider a material's impact in production, use and disposal, and its origin and transport.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.1] Choose timber from sustainably managed forests, and natural or recycled materials where they perform the job well.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.2] Low-emission paints (low-VOC) reduce the release of volatile organic compounds, gases given off by paint, into the air of the room.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.3] Reuse and repurposing, such as restoring an old wooden dresser, avoid the energy and waste involved in making replacements.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.4] Energy-efficient LED lighting uses less electricity than traditional bulbs and lasts longer.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.5] Light colours and well placed windows reduce the need for artificial lighting during the day.
[hom-home-interior-s1.18.6] Durable furniture and finishes that last many years have a smaller environmental impact than cheap items replaced often.

[hom-home-interior-s1.19.0] An advantage is expertise, a coordinated scheme and fewer costly mistakes.
[hom-home-interior-s1.19.1] A disadvantage is that the designer's fees reduce the budget available for the room itself.

CANDIDATES hom-home-interior-s3: 3. Materials used in the home
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.b] No material is best for everything, so each one is judged against how the room is used and what the household needs before it is chosen. These criteria give you a checklist for justifying any choice.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.0] Durability: resistance to wear, impact, moisture and heat determines how long a material will last in a given room, so a kitchen worktop needs much more than a bedroom shelf.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.1] Cost: the purchase price, fitting costs and upkeep over the life of the material all count, because a cheap material that needs replacing often can cost more in the end.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.2] Appearance and texture: the material must suit the style, colour scheme and décor of the room.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.3] Ease of cleaning and maintenance: non-porous, sealed or washable materials need less upkeep because dirt and spills do not soak in.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.4] Safety in use: slip resistance on floors, safe edges on furniture and toughened or laminated glass where people could be injured.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.5] Insulation: poor conductors such as wood, plastics and fabric feel warmer to touch and reduce heat loss; good conductors such as metal feel cold.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.6] Environmental impact: the source of the material, how far it travels, whether it can be recycled and how long it lasts.
[hom-home-interior-s3.0.7] Suitability for the job: the material must have the strength and the resistance to water and heat that its use demands.
[hom-home-interior-s3.1.b] Wood is a natural material used for many parts of the home, from floors to furniture. Solid woods fall into two groups, hardwoods and softwoods, which differ in cost, durability and use.
[hom-home-interior-s3.1.0] Solid hardwoods such as ash, beech, elm, oak, mahogany, rosewood and walnut are expensive, durable and resistant to decay and warping, and they have an attractive grain.
[hom-home-interior-s3.1.1] Solid hardwoods are used for floors, stairs, furniture, doors, architraves (the decorative frames around doors), windows, skirting boards and decking.
[hom-home-interior-s3.1.2] Softwoods such as pine and spruce (also called deal) are cheaper and easier to work, but they dent and scratch more readily than hardwoods. They are used for floorboards, joists, roof timbers, doors, windows, furniture and shelving.
[hom-home-interior-s3.1.3] Hardwood and softwood describe the type of tree, not how hard the wood is. Hardwoods are usually denser and more durable, but not every hardwood is harder than every softwood.
[hom-home-interior-s3.1.4] Example of one solid wood: oak is a hardwood that is hard-wearing, durable and expensive, and it is used for floors, stairs and furniture.
[hom-home-interior-s3.2.b] Because wood comes from a living tree, it keeps reacting to its surroundings after it is cut. This is why it needs preparation and a protective finish.
[hom-home-interior-s3.2.0] Wood is a natural material that shrinks, swells and warps with changes in humidity, so it must be well seasoned (dried) before use.
[hom-home-interior-s3.2.1] Varnish, lacquer or sealant protects wood from moisture and wear and keeps its surface easy to clean. Stain or paint can be used for the same protection.
[hom-home-interior-s3.2.2] Wood is a poor conductor of heat, so it feels warm to touch.
[hom-home-interior-s3.2.3] Wood is renewable if it is sustainably sourced, which helps its environmental impact.
[hom-home-interior-s3.3.b] Solid wood is expensive, so wood is also made into boards. These give a large, stable surface at lower cost, and they are manufactured boards, not solid wood.
[hom-home-interior-s3.3.0] Veneer is a thin layer of attractive hardwood glued over a cheaper core, giving the look of solid wood at lower cost.
[hom-home-interior-s3.3.1] Plywood is made of thin wood layers glued together with the grain of each layer at right angles to the next, which makes it strong, stable and resistant to warping.
[hom-home-interior-s3.3.2] Medium-density fibreboard (MDF) is made from wood fibres bonded with resin; it has a smooth surface that takes paint well but swells if it gets wet.
[hom-home-interior-s3.3.3] Chipboard is made from wood chips bonded with resin; it is cheap and widely used for kitchen units and flooring, but it is weak when wet.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.b] Metals are chosen where strength, heat conduction or a hygienic surface matter most. Each metal has its own strengths, which decide where it is used in the home.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.0] Metals used in the home include steel, stainless steel, aluminium, copper, brass and cast iron.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.1] Metals are strong and hard-wearing, and they are good conductors of heat and electricity, which is why they feel cold to touch.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.2] Ordinary steel rusts unless it is coated or painted; stainless steel contains chromium, which resists corrosion, so it is hygienic and easy to keep clean.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.3] Stainless steel is used for sinks, worktops, cooker hoods and appliance fronts in kitchens, and for cutlery.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.4] Aluminium is lightweight and resists corrosion because it forms a protective oxide layer; it is used for window frames, saucepans and fittings.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.5] Copper is malleable (easily shaped) and a good conductor, so it is used for hot water pipes, cylinders and saucepan bases.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.6] Brass is an alloy (a mixture of metals) of copper and zinc that resists corrosion and is used for taps, door handles and other decorative fittings.
[hom-home-interior-s3.4.7] Cast iron is heavy and brittle and retains heat; it is used for fireplaces, radiators and cooking pots.
[hom-home-interior-s3.5.b] Glass lets light into a room while keeping weather out, and its surface is easy to keep clean. Its brittleness and its poor heat retention when used as a single pane are why special types of glass are made.
[hom-home-interior-s3.5.0] Glass is made mainly from sand heated with other materials.
[hom-home-interior-s3.5.1] It is transparent, non-porous, hygienic and easy to clean, and it is hard but brittle.
[hom-home-interior-s3.5.2] Single glazing loses heat easily, whereas double glazing traps a layer of air or argon and reduces heat loss.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.b] Plain glass breaks into sharp pieces and lets heat escape, so different types have been developed to make glass safer, warmer or more private.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.0] Float glass is smooth and clear and is the basic glass used in windows and in mirrors, which are made by coating it with a reflective layer.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.1] Toughened glass is heat treated so that it shatters into small blunt pieces when broken and withstands heat and stress better; it is used for shower screens, glass doors, low-level glazing and oven doors.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.2] Laminated glass is two sheets bonded with a plastic interlayer, so that it holds together when broken and resists break-ins; it is used for windscreens and security glazing.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.3] Double glazing has two panes with a sealed gap of air or argon, which reduces heat loss and noise.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.4] Low-emissivity (low-E) glass has a thin coating that reduces heat escaping through the window while still letting in light.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.5] Frosted or patterned glass lets light through while obscuring the view, which suits bathrooms and hall doors.
[hom-home-interior-s3.6.6] Glass bricks refract light, bending it as it passes through, and let light through partitions and walls; frosted glass diffuses light so that it spreads evenly across the room.
[hom-home-interior-s3.7.b] Plastics are moulded into many household items. They fall into two groups, thermoplastic and thermosetting, that behave very differently when heated.
[hom-home-interior-s3.7.0] Plastics are made from polymers, which are long chains of molecules, usually from petroleum.
[hom-home-interior-s3.7.1] They are lightweight, waterproof, easy to clean and easily coloured and moulded, and they are poor conductors of heat and electricity.
[hom-home-interior-s3.8.b] A thermoplastic can be softened by heat again and again, like wax, which makes it easy to shape but means it cannot take high temperatures.
[hom-home-interior-s3.8.0] Thermoplastics are soft and flexible, soften when heated, can be moulded and remoulded, and harden again when cooled.
[hom-home-interior-s3.8.1] They are damaged by solvents but not by most acids and alkalis.
[hom-home-interior-s3.8.2] Polyethylene is a thermoplastic used for bottles, food containers, toys and bowls.
[hom-home-interior-s3.8.3] PTFE, also called Teflon, is a fluorocarbon thermoplastic with a non-stick finish used on saucepans and frying pans, and it is still classed as thermoplastic even though it tolerates cooking heat.
[hom-home-interior-s3.8.4] Polyvinyl chloride (PVC) is a thermoplastic used for vinyl floor and wall coverings, pipes and window frames.
[hom-home-interior-s3.9.b] A thermosetting plastic sets permanently the first time it is moulded, like a baked cake that cannot be melted back into batter. This is why it can withstand high temperatures.
[hom-home-interior-s3.9.0] Thermosetting plastics are hard and rigid and cannot be remoulded once set; they withstand high temperatures but blister and crack if overheated.
[hom-home-interior-s3.9.1] Thermosetting plastics are damaged by strong acids and alkalis but not by solvents, the opposite of thermoplastics.
[hom-home-interior-s3.9.2] Melamine formaldehyde is a thermosetting plastic used for cups, plates, saucers and laminated worktops.
[hom-home-interior-s3.9.3] Phenol formaldehyde is a thermosetting plastic used for the interior paddles of top-loading washing machines.
[hom-home-interior-s3.9.4] Bakelite is a thermosetting plastic used for light switches, telephones, utensil handles and cooker control knobs.
[hom-home-interior-s3.10.b] Fabrics soften and decorate a home, and each use puts different demands on the material. Judge a fabric by the same kind of criteria as any other material.
[hom-home-interior-s3.10.0] Fabrics in the home are used for curtains, upholstery, carpets, bed linen and cushions.
[hom-home-interior-s3.10.1] Criteria for choosing a fabric are durability, drape (how it hangs), colour fastness and fade resistance, cleanability, flame resistance, comfort and cost.
[hom-home-interior-s3.10.2] Upholstery fabric should be durable, comfortable, resilient, stain resistant, hard-wearing and fire resistant.
[hom-home-interior-s3.10.3] Woven fabrics are stable and strong, while knitted fabrics stretch and recover their shape.
[hom-home-interior-s3.11.b] Natural fibres come from plants and animals. Each has a typical set of strengths and weaknesses.
[hom-home-interior-s3.11.0] Cotton is absorbent and washable, and it is cool, but it creases easily and shrinks unless it is pre-shrunk.
[hom-home-interior-s3.11.1] Linen is strong and absorbent and feels cool to touch, but it creases easily.
[hom-home-interior-s3.11.2] Wool is warm, resilient and insulating, but it can shrink or felt if washed incorrectly.
[hom-home-interior-s3.11.3] Silk is soft and lustrous and drapes well, but it is delicate and expensive.
[hom-home-interior-s3.12.b] Man-made fibres are manufactured to give properties that natural fibres lack, such as crease resistance and strength.
[hom-home-interior-s3.12.0] Polyester is strong, resists creasing and dries quickly, and it is often blended with cotton to combine comfort and durability.
[hom-home-interior-s3.12.1] Nylon is very strong and resists abrasion, which makes it suitable for carpets and upholstery that receive heavy wear.
[hom-home-interior-s3.12.2] Acrylic is a man-made fibre that feels like wool, takes bright colours and resists fading in sunlight.
[hom-home-interior-s3.12.3] Velvet is a pile fabric made from several fibres, not a man-made fibre.
[hom-home-interior-s3.12.4] Blends combine the advantages of each fibre, so one fibre's weakness is covered by another's strength.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s1: 1. Textiles as a household resource
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.b] A textile is a material made from fibres or yarns (the threads spun from fibres) that has been woven, knitted or otherwise made into fabric. Textiles are a household resource, like food or fuel: they are bought, used, cared for and eventually replaced.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.0] Textiles serve household purposes, such as bedding, curtains, carpets, towels and upholstery, and clothing purposes.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.1] Because textiles cost money and need care, choosing well saves money and effort over the life of the item.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.0.2] Most household textiles do several jobs at once. A curtain, for example, gives privacy, decoration and some warmth.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.b] Textiles are used throughout the home for practical and decorative purposes, often several at once. Examiners often ask for a list of functions, so learn each one with its example item.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.0] Warmth and insulation: blankets, duvets, throws and thick curtains trap still air within their fibres and layers, reducing heat loss from the body.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.1] Sound absorption (sound insulation): carpets, rugs, curtains and upholstery absorb sound and reduce echo in a room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.2] Privacy and light control: curtains, net curtains and blinds screen the room from view and control the light entering it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.3] Comfort: cushions, mattress covers, bed linen and upholstery give softness and support.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.4] Protection: oven gloves and aprons protect the user from heat and spills, carpets protect floors from wear, and mattress protectors protect the mattress.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.5] Absorbency: towels, tea towels, dishcloths and bath mats take up water from the body, from dishes or from the floor, which makes washing and drying easier.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.6] Decoration and aesthetics: curtains, cushions, wall hangings, bedspreads and rugs add colour, pattern and texture to a room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.1.7] Hygiene: washable textiles can be laundered to remove dirt and micro-organisms. Safety is a further function, because fire-resistant finishes make textiles safer.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.b] A textile is chosen by weighing the properties of the fabric against the job it has to do, the money available and the household's preferences. Examiners often ask for three or four of these criteria, each with an explanation.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.0] Fitness for purpose: the fabric's properties must suit its job, for example a towel needs absorbency and a curtain needs to drape well and resist fading.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.1] Suitability in use: the fabric must withstand the wear, washing and conditions of the room it is used in, such as a kitchen, a living room or a child's bedroom. Purpose is the job; use is the wear and conditions, and both come under fitness.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.2] Cost: the price must fit the budget and the expected life of the item, so a durable fabric can be better value. Wool and silk cost more than most other fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.3] Personal choice: likes and dislikes, colour and style, comfort, a preference for natural or easy-care synthetic fabrics, and current trends.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.4] Aesthetic appeal: appearance, drape, weight, texture, lustre, colour and pattern, because the textile must look right in the room.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.5] Care and cleaning: whether the fabric is washable or dry clean, how much ironing it needs, and its fade resistance, stain resistance and shrinkage, which affect how practical it is for the household.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.6] Safety: a flame-retardant finish or fire-resistance label is wanted, and a loose or pile surface is avoided because it burns more easily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.2.7] In a longer Higher Level answer, durability (how long the fabric lasts), finishes (treatments added to improve a fabric) and sustainability (the environmental effect of the fabric) are also accepted, with one developed line on each.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.b] Each property below describes how a fabric behaves in use. Define each in plain words and give an example fabric, since examiners ask what a property means and which fabric has it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.0] Absorbent: the fabric takes in and holds moisture. Cotton and linen are absorbent, which makes them suitable for towels, and an absorbent fabric also takes up dye easily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.1] Flammable: the fabric ignites and burns readily when exposed to a flame, creating a fire risk. Cotton burns easily, so flammability is not wanted in household textiles.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.2] Crease-resistant: the fabric resists wrinkling in wear and washing and returns towards its smooth shape, so less ironing is needed. Polyester and wool resist creasing, but cotton and linen crease unless finished.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.3] Colourfast: the colour resists running in water and fading in light, so it stays bright after washing, because the dye is fixed firmly to the fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.4] Durable (hard wearing): the fabric resists abrasion, tearing and wear over a long period of use.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.5] Drape: the way a fabric hangs in folds. A good drape suits curtains and loose clothing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.6] Other properties examiners accept as desirable are warm, strong, washable, easy to dye, shrink resistant, stain resistant, quick drying and resilient (springs back into shape).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.3.7] Static: synthetic fibres build up static electricity, so the fabric clings, and fabric conditioner reduces this. Wool can shrink in hot washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.b] Choosing a fabric means matching its properties to the job of the item. When you recommend a fabric for a stated item, give the fabric and a reason from its properties.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.0] Cotton towels: absorbent, strong when wet and able to withstand hot washing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.1] Linen tea towels: absorbent, strong and quick drying, but they crease easily unless treated.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.2] Polyester curtains: drape well, resist fading, wash easily, dry quickly and resist creasing.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.3] Cotton curtains: easy to launder and dye, with a crisp appearance and strong when wet, but they shrink if not pre-shrunk.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.4] Wool blankets: warm, insulating, absorbent and elastic (resilient), but they can shrink and felt (the fibres mat together) if washed at high temperatures or carelessly.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.5] Carpets in nylon or wool: strong, hard wearing and resistant to abrasion from foot traffic.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s1.4.6] Bed linen in cotton or polyester-cotton: washable, strong and easy to iron.

CANDIDATES hom-textiles-fibres-s2: 2. Safety in household textiles
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.b] Household textiles such as curtains, bedding and sofas can feed a fire, so fire risk is a key safety check when buying. How likely a textile is to catch light depends on the structure of the fabric, the fibres in it and the filling inside it.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.0] Loosely spun yarns (yarns twisted only lightly, so they trap plenty of air) ignite very easily, so loosely spun fabrics are a poor choice where fire risk is high.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.1] Fabrics with a raised pile or nap (a fluffy surface of upstanding fibres) ignite more easily than smooth, tightly woven fabrics, because flames can run quickly across the loose surface fibres.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.2] Blends of man-made and natural fibres can burn more quickly than either fibre on its own, so a blend is not automatically safer than a single fibre.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.3] Foam and other fillings in cushions and upholstery burn fiercely and give off toxic fumes when they burn, so check what the filling is and whether it is fire resistant.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.4] Flame-retardant treatment lowers the risk of ignition. Inherently flame-retardant fabrics keep their resistance because the fibre itself resists ignition, while coated or finished fabrics rely on a treatment applied to the fabric.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.5] Read the labels for fire-resistance information, for example a statement that the materials meet the requirements for resistance to cigarette and match ignition. Read the care label too, so that a flame-retardant finish is not damaged by washing or cleaning.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.0.6] Higher-risk items in the home are children's nightwear, upholstered furniture, curtains near heat and protective workwear. Keep textiles away from heaters and cookers.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.b] A flame-retardant finish is a chemical treatment that reduces how easily a fabric ignites and burns. Proban is one such finish; Pyrovatex is another.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.0] Identification: Proban is used on cotton, linen and other cellulosic fabrics (fabrics made from plant cellulose).
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.1] Phosphorus and nitrogen compounds are fixed within the fibre structure, so the protection is built into the fibre rather than sitting on the surface as a coating.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.2] You cannot tell by looking or touching whether a fabric has the finish. Its presence is shown on the product label or in the manufacturer's information.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.3] Effect 1: it reduces the flammability of the fabric, which lowers the risk of ignition.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.4] Effect 2: when taken away from a flame the fabric self-extinguishes (goes out by itself), because an insulating char forms around the fibres instead of the fabric burning on. The fabric neither melts nor keeps burning once it is taken away from the flame.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.5] Drawback 1: the finish may cause an allergic reaction in some people.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.6] Drawback 2: the fabric may need special care in cleaning, so follow the care instructions to avoid damaging the finish by washing or cleaning.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.1.7] Uses: children's nightwear, protective workwear, upholstered furniture, military clothing and curtains, all items where a fire would be particularly dangerous.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.b] Upholstered furniture is sat on every day, so its fabric has to cope with constant use while staying comfortable and safe.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.0] Durable and hard wearing: the fabric resists abrasion and rubbing from constant use, so it keeps its appearance and does not wear through or tear.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.1] Resilient: the fabric springs back after being sat on, so cushions keep their shape. Wool is resilient, while cotton loses its shape more readily.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.2] Comfortable: the fabric has a pleasant handle (the feel of the fabric) and is not harsh against the skin.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.3] Stain resistant: spills are less likely to soak in and wipe off more easily. Fabric that is also fade resistant and easy to clean stays looking good for longer.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.4] Fire resistant: the fabric and the filling should resist ignition, and this is shown on a label.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.2.5] A label stating that the materials meet the requirements for resistance to cigarette and match ignition tells the consumer that the covering has been tested and resists catching fire from a lit cigarette or a match.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.3.b] The rules on the fire safety of domestic upholstered furniture changed in July 2026. The Irish fire safety order for domestic furniture that the syllabus names was revoked (by S.I. 357/2026), and these products now fall under EU product-safety law.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.3.0] From July 2026, the fire safety requirements for domestic upholstered furniture are those of the EU General Product Safety Regulation (GPSR), Regulation (EU) 2023/988.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.3.1] The GPSR is an EU regulation, so it applies directly in Ireland without needing an Irish Act, and it has applied since 13 December 2024.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.3.2] Its core duty is that only safe products may be placed on the market. Producers must assess the risks of their products, and sellers, including online sellers, must not supply unsafe goods.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.3.3] In practice, furniture that meets EU product-safety requirements may be sold to consumers in Ireland. The separate Irish fire label is no longer a legal requirement, though consumers can still look for fire-resistance information from the supplier.
[hom-textiles-fibres-s2.3.4] The Competition and Consumer Protection Commission (CCPC) is the Irish body for consumer information and complaints (ccpc.ie).
