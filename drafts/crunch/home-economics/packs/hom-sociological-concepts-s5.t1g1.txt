TARGET hom-sociological-concepts-s5: 5. Social change
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.b] Societies never stand still. Social change is a significant alteration over time in the structures, institutions, relationships, values or behaviour of a society, in other words in the way a society is organised.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.0] In exam terms, social change is change in society caused by law reform, technology or major events. The internet and divorce legislation are good examples.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.1] Change can be gradual, such as the slow shift in attitudes to the role of women or to marriage over several decades, or sudden, such as a new law, a war or a pandemic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.2] Change can be planned, such as a law or a policy, or unplanned. It happens in every society, but the speed differs from one society to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.0.3] Cultural lag is the gap in time between the appearance of a new material invention and the adjustment of norms, values and institutions to it, because people and organisations take time to catch up. The concept was developed by the sociologist William Ogburn.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.b] Many of the biggest changes come from shifts in how people think and live. These social causes feed one another: more education leads to new attitudes, and new attitudes lead to new family patterns.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.0] Changing attitudes and values: there is more acceptance of cohabitation, separation, divorce, lone parenthood, same-sex relationships and diversity, less stigma attached to children born outside marriage, and the Church has less influence than before.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.1] Greater equality for women through better education, careers, financial independence and legal equality changes gender roles at home. The marriage bar, the rule forcing women to leave their jobs on marrying, ended in the civil service in 1973.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.2] Urbanisation is the movement of people from rural areas to towns and cities. It weakens close kin ties and extended family contact and brings new lifestyles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.3] Towns have better services and facilities, but also more pressure, traffic and anti-social behaviour. Rural depopulation means rural areas can lose services and older people can become isolated.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.4] Education: more people reach second and third level, which changes attitudes, expectations and the value placed on qualifications. Wider access also links to social mobility.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.5] Demographic change means changes in the make-up of the population: falling birth rates, smaller families, later marriage and parenthood, longer life expectancy and an ageing population. These alter the structure of households and the demand for services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.6] Migration, the movement of people into and out of Ireland, brings new cultures, languages and religions. Cultural diffusion, the spread of ideas, fashions and practices between societies through travel, migration and the media, has a similar effect.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.1.7] Globalisation, the growth of economic and cultural links between countries, means events and ideas in one country affect others. Social movements and pressure groups also campaign for change, for example for equality, and gain support for their aims.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.b] Law reform both reflects change in attitudes and drives it, because a new law changes what people may do and what they come to expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.0] Divorce: the referendum was held in November 1995 and passed narrowly. The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 then set up the legal process. Say referendum 1995, Act 1996, not that divorce was available in 1995.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.1] Same-sex marriage was approved by referendum in 2015 and given effect by the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.2] The Civil Partnership Act 2010 provided civil partnership for same-sex couples. This was the earlier arrangement, because since 2015 same-sex couples can marry.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.2.3] Other legal changes that alter family life include maternity and paternity leave and equality legislation, which support parents at work and promote equal treatment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.b] How a society earns its living shapes family life, because work decides who is at home, how much money the household has and where people live.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.0] Industrialisation moved many workers from farms and rural trades into factories and towns. Service industries have since grown, and work patterns have changed to include flexible hours, job sharing and working from home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.1] Growth of female employment has produced dual-earner households. These have more income but more need for childcare, and can bring role overload and role conflict, which are effects, not causes.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.2] Unemployment, recession, emigration and returning emigrants change family life by splitting relatives apart and bringing them back. High housing costs delay home ownership, marriage and parenthood, while higher standards of living improve comfort.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.3.3] State welfare: improved social welfare, such as child benefit and pensions, reduces dependence on the family and eases financial strain.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.b] New technology changes daily life faster than norms and institutions can adjust, which is cultural lag in action. Its effects can be positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.0] Modern communications technology, including mobile phones, the internet and social media, changes how families communicate, how children learn and how people work, and it changes how people spend leisure time. Examples are children skilled in IT, video calls and apps.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.1] Positive effects include access to information and contact with distant relatives. Negative effects include addiction, cyber-bullying and less face-to-face time.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.2] Labour-saving appliances free time and make shared housework easier, which supports more equal roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.3] Contraception and medical advances affect family size and life expectancy, because families can plan the number of children and people live longer.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.4.4] Transport shortens distances, so relatives and workers can live further apart and still keep in touch.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.b] Social change affects the family, institutions and everyday life, and its effects can be both positive and negative.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.0] Changing gender roles: more women take part in paid employment, and household tasks are more often shared between partners, giving more equal roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.1] Smaller families: many couples have fewer children or have them later in life, and fewer marriages, and later ones, are taking place.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.2] Changing family structures: lone-parent, blended and cohabiting families have become more common, so family structures are more diverse, and the extended family has declined.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.3] An ageing population increases demand for health and care services and for support for older people, and the roles of older people change. More families use childcare.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.4] Changes in values, such as greater acceptance of diversity, change the norms people expect of one another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.5] Effects can be positive, such as more choice and freedom, or negative, such as stress and strain, role overload, loss of community and social problems. Rapid change can leave some groups behind, for example people whose skills are no longer needed in changing industries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.6] Change affects institutions: the Church loses influence, the State takes on more services, and schools adapt to diversity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.5.7] Social change can cause tension and conflict between groups that hold different values, for example older and younger generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.b] Not everyone welcomes change, and several forces can slow it down or stop it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.0] Vested interests are groups who benefit from the existing system, so they may oppose changes that would reduce their advantages.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.1] Tradition and religious belief can resist changes in norms and values that conflict with long-held teachings.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.2] Fear of the unknown and uncertainty about the consequences of change make people reluctant to accept it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.6.3] The cost of change, in money, time and disruption, can slow or prevent it, and generations may disagree about it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.b] Ireland has seen rapid change in recent decades, and each example below links a cause to an effect on family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.0] Divorce: Irish voters approved divorce by referendum in 1995, and the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 provided for it. Marriage equality followed, with a referendum in 2015 and the Marriage Act 2015.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.1] Women in employment: the marriage bar in the civil service ended in 1973, and more married women have been in paid work since, which changed roles at home.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.2] Migration: the Celtic Tiger, a period of rapid economic growth in the late 1990s and 2000s, brought increased immigration and with it new languages, cultures and religions.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.3] Emigration in earlier decades and in the 2008 recession years, and return migration, moved relatives apart and then back together, affecting families and communities.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.7.4] Rural depopulation and urban growth shifted people from the countryside to towns, and the influence of the Church has fallen.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.b] Exam questions on social change usually ask for the impact on family life, and marks are given for each cause covered.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.0] Structure a discuss answer by cause (social, economic, technological) and then effect, with one Irish example for each point.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s5.8.1] Link each cause to a clear effect, for example urbanisation weakening extended family contact, or dual incomes increasing the need for childcare.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s1: 1. Society, culture, norms and mores, values
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.b] Sociology is the study of how people live together in groups and societies. Its starting point is the society itself, because human life is organised, not random.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.0] A society is a group of people who live in a defined territory and share a way of life, a set of institutions (the established organisations and arrangements of social life) and a sense of belonging.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.1] Social life in a society is organised: people depend on one another and follow common rules, which is why daily life is predictable rather than chaotic.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.0.2] The term applies at different scales, such as Irish society, a small rural community or Western society, so always be clear which group you mean.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.b] Culture is what makes the way of life of one society different from another. It is much wider than the arts, because it covers almost everything a group of people share and hand on.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.0] Culture is the whole way of life of a society, passed from one generation to the next. It includes language, customs, beliefs, values, norms, mores, music, art, dress, food, religion, technology and objects. Irish culture is a good example.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.1] Culture is learned, not inherited biologically, so a child raised in any society picks up that society's way of life through socialisation (the process of learning how to live in a society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.2] Material culture is the physical objects a society makes and uses, such as clothing, tools, buildings and housing. Non-material culture is the intangible side: language, beliefs, values, norms, customs and rituals.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.3] Cultures differ between societies, and a culture changes over time. Differences show up in language, dress, food, religious practice, wedding customs and attitudes to age and gender roles.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.4] Members of a society share common symbols and meanings, though not every member values every symbol.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.5] Safe Irish examples of culture include Irish traditional music, the GAA, the Irish language as a cultural symbol, St Patrick's Day, Christmas customs and Irish wedding customs. Symbols like these carry shared meanings for those who value them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.6] A subculture is a smaller group within a society with some distinct values, norms or styles of its own, such as a youth music scene, a sporting club or a religious minority. It still shares the main culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.1.7] Ethnocentrism is judging another culture by the standards of your own culture. Cultural relativism is understanding a culture on its own terms, by its own standards, rather than by those of another culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.b] Every society has rules about how to behave, most of them unwritten. These rules come in different strengths, from everyday habits to rules with serious moral weight.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.0] A norm is an accepted, expected way of behaving in a given situation. It is a rule, mostly unwritten. The standard example is forming a queue; others are greeting people, keeping quiet in a library, turning up on time and dressing suitably for an interview or a funeral.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.1] Norms vary between cultures, between social settings and over time. For example, what is acceptable at a match is not acceptable in church, and what was normal fifty years ago may not be now. Norms about punctuality, personal space and greeting also differ from one culture to another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.2] Mores (pronounced MOR-ays) are norms with strong moral weight, whose breach is seen as seriously wrong, such as the ban on theft, violence or disrespect to the dead. Breaking a more brings serious disapproval or punishment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.3] Customs, also called folkways, are everyday conventions and traditional ways of doing things, such as queueing, shaking hands, a birthday custom or a wedding custom. Breaking them is rude, not wrong, and brings only mild disapproval. This helps separate a norm from a more.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.2.4] Laws are norms that are written down and enforced by the State through officially appointed bodies such as the courts. Some mores become laws, such as the bans on theft and assault, but many norms are not laws, such as queueing.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.b] A norm only works if there is some pressure to follow it. Societies apply that pressure through sanctions, and the result is either conformity or deviance.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.0] A sanction is how a society enforces norms: a reward for conforming to a norm or a penalty for breaking one.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.1] Positive sanctions reward conformity, for example praise, a prize, an award or promotion.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.2] Negative sanctions punish breaking norms, for example a frown, ridicule, ostracism (being left out) or, at the formal end, fines and imprisonment.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.3] Informal sanctions come from family, friends and neighbours. Formal sanctions come from official bodies such as the courts, schools and employers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.4] Conformity means following norms. Deviance is behaviour that breaks a norm.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.3.5] What counts as deviance depends on the society and the time, because norms vary between cultures and change over time, so the same behaviour may be deviant in one place and normal in another.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.b] Values sit behind norms. A norm is the specific rule; a value is the broad belief that the rule expresses.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.0] Values are the beliefs and attitudes a society holds about what is right and wrong, good and bad, important and worth striving for.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.1] Values are general, abstract and long-lasting; norms are the specific rules that carry values into everyday behaviour. A value of respect for life lies behind the norm against violence, and a value of honesty lies behind the norm of not cheating in exams.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.2] Examples of values given in Irish and Western societies include family, education, honesty, fairness and equality, respect for others, religious belief, freedom, community and care for the vulnerable. These are examples of values, not a claim about every person.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.3] Core values are held strongly and change slowly; peripheral values are held less firmly and change more readily.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.4] Values are learned from the family first, then from school, peers, religion and the media. They differ between families, groups and generations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.5] Values change over time, for example attitudes to marriage, gender equality and diversity. The changes feed through into new norms and laws.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.4.6] Values can conflict, such as personal freedom against duty to family, or career against family time. Individuals must balance them, and societies resolve such conflicts through norms and laws.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.b] The terms in this subsection are not separate lists. They fit together as one system, with culture as the umbrella.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.0] Culture is the umbrella. Values, norms, mores, customs, language, beliefs and symbols are its parts.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.1] Values are the broad beliefs. Norms and mores are the rules that put them into practice.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.2] Sanctions enforce norms and mores, and laws are the formal, written version of them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.5.3] A chain to remember: a value of honesty leads to a norm of not cheating, and cheating brings a sanction such as a teacher's disapproval or a penalty.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.b] Exams often ask you to explain four or five terms together, such as culture, norms, values and role. Each term is marked separately, so a clear layout earns the marks.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.0] Give each term its own line or short paragraph so that each one can be credited separately.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.1] Open with a one-sentence definition that uses the correct sociological meaning. Use the wording the schemes credit: way of life of a society for culture, accepted way of behaving for norms, and beliefs about what is right and wrong for values.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.2] Add the point that separates the term from the one most often confused with it, such as folkways from mores or norms from values.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.3] Give a specific example from Irish or everyday life for each term, such as queueing for a norm or Irish culture for culture.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s1.6.4] Where a term links to another in the set, say how: for example, values underpin norms, and norms are enforced by sanctions. A three-mark term answer is one definition point, one example or extension point and, where terms are related, a link.

CANDIDATES hom-sociological-concepts-s4: 4. Social institutions
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.b] Every society has basic needs, such as raising children, passing on knowledge and organising work. A social institution is the lasting, organised way a society meets one of them, which is why institutions matter for understanding family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.0] A social institution is an established, organised set of norms (accepted rules of behaviour), roles and values built around a basic need of society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.1] The five main social institutions are the family, education, religion, the economy (work) and government (the State). Health and the media are sometimes added to this list.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.2] An institution is not a building or a single organisation. A school is an organisation within the institution of education, so the school down the road is part of the institution, not the institution itself.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.3] Institutions last across generations, while individual groups come and go. Because they carry norms and values, pass on culture and give structure to social life, each one is an agent of socialisation (a means by which people learn how to behave in society).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.4] Institutions are linked, so what happens in one affects the others. For example, the economy affects family life, and education prepares people for work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.5] Functionalism is the view that each institution performs functions that help keep society stable. This view describes what institutions do; it does not label any institution good or bad.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.6] A manifest function is an intended and recognised result of an institution, for example the school's function of educating young people.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.0.7] A latent function is an unintended and often hidden result, for example a school providing supervised care for children while parents are at work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.b] The family is the first and most basic institution, because it is where every person's care and learning begins. Over time other institutions take over or add to some of its work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.0] The family reproduces the population and provides for the care of children, older people and dependants (people who rely on others for support).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.1] The family socialises children, passing on language, norms, values and skills, so that children learn how to take part in society.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.2] The family is the basic economic unit of consumption (using goods and services) and provides economic support through shared income and household work.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.3] The family provides emotional security and a sense of belonging and identity.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.4] Other institutions adopt or supplement family functions. Schools take on much of children's education, the State provides welfare and health services, employers provide income, and religious bodies provide moral guidance.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.1.5] Examples of supplementing a family function: parenting courses support the emotional function, schooling from preschool to third level supports the educational function, welfare payments support the economic function, and medical services support the physical function.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.b] Education is how a society deliberately passes on what it knows, and it also shapes children's behaviour and future work. It works both in the classroom and in the everyday life of the school.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.0] Education transmits knowledge, skills and the culture of a society from one generation to the next. It does this formally through the curriculum (the subjects taught), from primary to third level.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.1] It also teaches informally through the hidden curriculum, the unwritten lessons of school life such as rules, punctuality, respect for authority, sharing and teamwork.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.2] Education socialises children, reinforcing the behaviour and values learned at home. It promotes social cohesion by teaching shared history, language and civic values, such as the Irish language and Irish history in Irish schools.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.3] Education prepares people for work through literacy and numeracy, qualifications, specialist skills and career guidance. Work experience comes through Transition Year, the Leaving Certificate Applied, the Leaving Certificate Vocational Programme and college.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.4] Education sorts and allocates people to positions in the economy by means of qualifications. It can support social mobility (moving to a higher social position) by opening access to skilled and professional occupations.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.2.5] In Ireland, education runs through primary, post-primary, further and higher education. The Leaving Certificate is the state examination at the end of second level, and most students use it for entry to third-level courses.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.b] Education is meant to give everyone a fair chance, but a family's circumstances can affect how far a child gets. Supports exist to narrow that gap.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.0] Socio-economic status (a family's income, occupation and social position) affects chances in education. Higher-status families often have more educational opportunities and go on to third level more often.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.1] Costs of books, uniforms and equipment can exclude low-income families, because they cannot afford what a child needs to take part fully.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.2] Grants, book rental schemes and allowances help families meet these costs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.3.3] DEIS (Delivering Equality of Opportunity in Schools) supports aim to reduce the gap for pupils at risk of being left behind.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.b] Religion is the institution that answers questions about meaning and right and wrong, and it gives people a community to belong to. Describe it neutrally: Ireland has many faiths and people of no religion.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.0] Religion provides a system of beliefs and values that gives meaning to life and explains events that cannot be explained by science alone.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.1] Religion provides moral codes that guide behaviour and reinforce many of a society's norms and values, for example rules against stealing or killing.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.2] Religious rituals mark key stages of life, such as birth, first communion or confirmation, marriage and death.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.3] Religious institutions provide community networks and social support to their members, so people feel they belong.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.4] Churches and other religious bodies have supported education, health care and charity. In Ireland many primary schools still have a religious patron.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.4.5] The influence of religion on Irish society has changed, with declining influence of the Church, more civil ceremonies and a more secular society (one not guided by religion).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.b] The economy is the institution that decides how a society produces and shares what people need. Work is how most families connect to it, which is why economic change reaches family life.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.0] The economic institution organises the production, distribution and consumption of goods and services.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.1] Employment is the main way the economy allocates status and income to individuals and families, and income decides a family's standard of living.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.2] Economic institutions include businesses, banks, markets and the systems of trade through which goods move between producers and consumers.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.3] Work is where people take on adult roles and learn workplace norms (the accepted behaviour at work).
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.4] Economic institutions shape family life, for example by determining whether one or both parents must work outside the home, and so hours of work and childcare needs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.5] Economic changes affect family income and roles. Unemployment lowers living standards and strains relationships, while growth of service industries creates jobs.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.5.6] Families are consumers, buying goods and services with the income that work provides.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.b] Government is the institution with the power to make rules for everyone and to provide services that individuals cannot organise alone. Its structure spreads that power so no single body holds all of it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.0] The political institution, the State, maintains order, makes laws, collects taxes and provides public services, including social welfare, health, education, housing and protection of the vulnerable.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.1] Government is typically divided into three branches: the legislature, which makes laws; the executive, which carries them out; and the judiciary (the courts), which interprets and applies them.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.2] This division is called the separation of powers, and it prevents any one branch from holding unchecked authority.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.3] In Ireland the Oireachtas, the national parliament, consists of the President, Dáil Éireann and Seanad Éireann. The Government is the executive, and the courts are the judiciary.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.4] Local authorities provide local services such as housing, planning and libraries.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.5] Citizens take part in government by voting in elections and referendums, electing representatives to the Dáil in general elections.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.6] The State supports the family in each function: welfare payments for the economic function, health services for the physical function, and schooling for the educational function.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.6.7] Child protection is part of this support. The State's agency for children is Tusla, the Child and Family Agency, which has exercised the Child Care Act 1991 functions since 2014.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.b] Health services and the media are often added to the five main institutions because they also shape everyday life. Treat them as additions, since the list of institutions is not fixed.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.0] The health institution promotes health and provides care and treatment for illness and injury.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.1] In Ireland the Health Service Executive (HSE) provides public health care.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.2] The media, including newspapers, radio, television and online platforms, inform the public and entertain it.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.3] The media also shape public opinion and attitudes and transmit norms and values, which makes them both an institution and an agent of socialisation.
[hom-sociological-concepts-s4.7.4] The legal system (courts and law) is part of government, so it needs no separate institution.
