TARGET hom-family-law-s4: 4. The Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989
[hom-family-law-s4.0.b] When a marriage breaks down and the couple cannot agree how to part, a court can step in. Judicial separation under the Judicial Separation and Family Law Reform Act 1989, as amended, is a court decree under which spouses live apart with the court's orders in place. It does not end the marriage.
[hom-family-law-s4.0.0] A decree (a formal order of the court) means the spouses are no longer required to live together, and the court sets out the arrangements for their maintenance and property.
[hom-family-law-s4.0.1] Because the marriage continues, neither spouse is free to remarry after a judicial separation. Only a divorce ends a marriage and allows remarriage.
[hom-family-law-s4.0.2] The application is made to the Circuit Court or the High Court, either of which can grant a judicial separation, and it is heard by a judge. It is not made to the District Court.
[hom-family-law-s4.0.3] A judicial separation is a legal status that the court grants, unlike informal separation, which spouses agree between themselves without a court order.
[hom-family-law-s4.0.4] It is needed in particular where the couple cannot agree the terms of separating. If they can agree, they use a separation agreement instead.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.b] A court will not grant a decree just because a spouse asks for one. The applicant (the spouse applying) must prove at least one ground, or reason, set out in the Act. The Act has six grounds, and the respondent is the other spouse.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.0] Adultery by the respondent spouse since the marriage.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.1] Unreasonable behaviour by the respondent that makes it unreasonable for the applicant to continue living with them.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.2] Desertion by the respondent for a continuous period of at least one year immediately before the application.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.3] The spouses have lived apart for at least one year and both consent to the decree being granted.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.4] The spouses have lived apart for at least three years, even if the other spouse does not consent.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.5] The marriage has broken down so that a normal marital relationship has not existed between the spouses for at least one year before the application. This is the ground most commonly used because it does not require either spouse to be at fault.
[hom-family-law-s4.1.6] The applicant must establish one of these statutory grounds (grounds set by law) before the court can grant the decree.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.b] A decree on its own only releases the spouses from living together. The court can add orders that settle money, property and children, so the practical questions are answered at the same time.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.0] Maintenance orders require one spouse to provide financial support (regular payments for living costs) for the other spouse and for the children.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.1] Property orders divide or transfer property between the spouses, including who lives in the family home (property adjustment), and orders can also be made about the use of the family home.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.2] Orders on the custody of children decide where a child lives and who has responsibility for their upbringing.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.3] Access orders set out the arrangements for a parent who does not have custody to see the child.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.4] The court may extinguish succession rights, which means a spouse loses their right to inherit from the other spouse. Without such an order, the right to inherit continues.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.5] A pension adjustment order is also possible.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.6] These orders can be made together with the decree, so that the financial and childcare arrangements are settled at once, which saves the family returning to court.
[hom-family-law-s4.2.7] Maintenance orders made on judicial separation can later be varied by the court if the circumstances of either spouse change, such as a change in income.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.b] Judicial separation sits between informal arrangements and divorce. Comparing it with a separation agreement and with divorce shows who decides in each case and whether the marriage ends.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.0] Judicial separation differs from divorce because divorce ends the marriage and allows both spouses to remarry, while judicial separation does not.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.1] Divorce requires the spouses to have lived apart for at least two of the previous three years (Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 as amended by the Family Law Act 2019), and it ends the marriage.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.2] A separation agreement (a deed of separation, sometimes called a legal separation) is a binding agreement made between the spouses, whereas judicial separation is a decree made by the court.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.3] A separation agreement is drawn up by solicitors and agreed by both spouses, often with a mediator. It is cheaper and avoids court, and it is legally binding.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.4] Judicial separation is used when the spouses cannot agree terms, because the judge decides for them.
[hom-family-law-s4.3.5] In both a separation agreement and judicial separation, the spouses are not required to live together, but they remain married.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s4: 4. When a marriage breaks down
[hom-marriage-s4.0.b] Counselling is the first option for a couple in difficulty. It helps partners talk through their problems while there is still a chance to put things right, so that separation or divorce does not become necessary.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.0] Counselling aims to help a couple resolve their problems before they become grounds for separation or divorce, which is why it is the first step rather than the last.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.1] The service is confidential, so partners can speak openly. They may attend together, separately or, where relevant, with their children.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.2] Several visits may be needed before the couple reach a resolution, because problems built up over time are rarely settled in one session.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.3] The counsellor facilitates the discussion and may offer advice, but does not decide for the couple. The couple work out their own way forward.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.4] Counsellors may refer a couple to a specialist counsellor for a particular problem, such as addiction or difficulties in sexual relations.
[hom-marriage-s4.0.5] Marriage counselling is provided by Accord and by other independent counselling services in Ireland.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.b] Mediation is an alternative to court for a couple who have already decided to separate or divorce. Instead of a judge deciding the outcome, the couple work out the terms themselves with help.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.0] The Family Mediation Service of the Legal Aid Board offers a confidential service to couples who have decided to separate or divorce. It was established in 1986, is State-funded and is free, whereas private mediators charge.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.1] An impartial, trained mediator meets the couple together and helps them negotiate their own agreement. The mediator does not take sides or decide for them.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.2] The issues negotiated include parenting arrangements, custody and access to children, finances and maintenance, and the family home and other property.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.3] Mediation keeps children out of conflict, encourages communication between the partners, and helps them accept the separation.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.4] Mediation is less costly, less adversarial and quicker than court proceedings, which pit the spouses against each other.
[hom-marriage-s4.1.5] The outcome is a written agreement that each partner can bring to their solicitor to be made legally binding.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.b] Legal separation is a legally binding agreement under which spouses live apart but remain married. It lets a couple settle practical arrangements without going to court.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.0] The spouses agree to live apart and set out the terms in a deed of separation, a legally binding written agreement.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.1] Typical terms are living apart, care and access arrangements for the children, maintenance, and what happens to the family home.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.2] A solicitor draws up the deed, and each spouse should have their own solicitor. A mediator may help them reach the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.3] Legal separation can be done without going to court if both spouses agree to the terms.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.4] The spouses are still married, so neither can remarry. Only a divorce or a nullity decree allows that.
[hom-marriage-s4.2.5] Where the spouses cannot agree, a court can grant a judicial separation. This is a court order, so it is different from a deed of separation, which the couple agree themselves.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.b] Nullity and annulment both declare that a marriage was never valid, but only a legal nullity decree has effect in the eyes of the State. Do not confuse either with divorce, which ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.0] Legal nullity is a declaration by a court (the Circuit Court or High Court) that the marriage was never valid in law. The parties are then free to marry other people.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.1] Couples married in a civil or State-recognised religious ceremony need a legal nullity decree before either spouse can remarry.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.2] A marriage may be void, for example where one party is already married or in a civil partnership, the parties are within the prohibited degrees of relationship, or the formalities were not followed.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.3] Nullity may also be granted for a lack of capacity or free consent, or where a marriage is voidable, for example because one spouse cannot sustain a normal marital relationship, including inability to consummate.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.4] Church annulment is granted by a Church marriage tribunal, which studies the marriage and declares it invalid in the eyes of the Church.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.5] A church annulment has no legal effect in the State, so the couple remain legally married. A civil nullity decree or a divorce is still needed before either can remarry civilly.
[hom-marriage-s4.3.6] Nullity says the marriage never validly existed. Divorce ends a marriage that was valid.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.b] Divorce is a court decree that legally ends a valid marriage. It was introduced in Ireland by the Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996, after the 1995 referendum.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.0] The Family Law (Divorce) Act 1996 was amended by the Family Law Act 2019, which shortened the living-apart period from four of the previous five years to two of the previous three, and came into force on 1 December 2019.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.1] First condition: the spouses must have lived apart for at least two of the previous three years. The couple can live apart under the same roof, and they do not have to be legally separated first.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.2] Second condition: there must be no reasonable prospect of reconciliation, meaning no realistic chance of the spouses getting back together.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.3] Third condition: proper provision has been or will be made for each spouse and for any dependent children, so that nobody is left without support.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.4] The couple must also have a link to Ireland: either living here permanently, or one of them having lived here for at least one year before applying.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.5] When granting a divorce, the court can make orders on custody and access, maintenance, the family home and other property, and succession rights, which extinguish a spouse's right to inherit from the other.
[hom-marriage-s4.4.6] A decree of divorce is final, and each spouse is then free to remarry.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s1: 1. Definition of marriage
[hom-marriage-s1.0.b] Marriage is both a social institution, recognised by society, and a legal status, recognised and protected by the State. It is a voluntary commitment that is intended to be lasting.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.0] A marriage is a union of two people who freely agree to it, entered into through a civil or religious ceremony that is legally recognised. Because both must agree freely, nobody can be forced into it.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.1] Marriage is a union to the exclusion of all others, so a person may be married to only one person at a time. Marrying again while the first marriage is still valid is bigamy, which is a crime.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.2] Living together as a couple and remaining faithful to each other are part of what marriage involves, and a full definition includes both.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.3] Marriage creates legal rights and duties between the spouses (husband and wife), including inheritance rights and a duty of financial support for the spouse and children. This is why it is a legal status as well as a personal commitment.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.b] The State gives marriage special protection in the Constitution, and the law has changed over time about who can marry and how couples are recognised.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.0] Article 41 of the Constitution (Article 41.3 deals with marriage) pledges the State to guard the institution of marriage, on which the family is founded, with special care, and to protect it against attack.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.1] After the marriage equality referendum on 22 May 2015, the Constitution says marriage may be contracted in accordance with law by two persons without distinction as to their sex.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.2] The Marriage Act 2015 gave this effect, in force from 16 November 2015, so same-sex couples marry on the same legal terms as opposite-sex couples.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.3] Civil partnership, created by the Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010, gave same-sex couples rights and duties similar to marriage. It is a different legal status, not marriage.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.4] Civil partnership is no longer available to new couples, but existing civil partnerships remain valid. Civil partners who marry each other end their civil partnership.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.5] A marriage can be a civil ceremony (conducted by a registrar or a civil solemniser) or a religious ceremony (by a priest, minister or other person registered as a solemniser). A secular or humanist ceremony is possible with a registered solemniser. Each has the same legal effect once registered.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.b] The law sets conditions so that a marriage is entered into freely, by people who are able to marry, and is properly recorded. A marriage that fails these conditions may be void or voidable, and a court can declare it invalid through legal nullity.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.0] Age: both people must be at least 18. There is no exception for 16- and 17-year-olds, because the minimum age is 18 for everyone.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.1] Free to marry: neither person may already be married or in a civil partnership, because a person can be married to only one person at a time.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.2] Consent: both must consent freely, and consent obtained by force, threat or pressure is not valid. Both must also understand what they are doing, so a person who cannot understand the nature of marriage cannot validly marry (this is called capacity).
[hom-marriage-s1.2.3] Prohibited degrees: the couple may not be too closely related, by blood or, in some cases, by marriage or adoption.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.4] Notice: both must give notice of their intention to marry, in person, to a civil registration service (the registrar of the area) at least three months before the wedding. This applies to civil, religious and secular ceremonies.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.5] They bring the documents the registrar requires with the notice, for example identification and a declaration that there is no impediment (nothing in law to stop the marriage).
[hom-marriage-s1.2.6] Formalities: the ceremony is conducted by an authorised person, a registrar or a registered solemniser. The venue is one the registrar has approved (for a civil ceremony), inspected in advance, or a place suitable for the religion. Two witnesses must be present and the required declarations made.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.7] Place: the wedding must take place in a registered building, such as a register office or registered church, or in an approved venue inspected in advance by a registrar.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.8] Registration: the couple and the two witnesses sign the register at the ceremony, and the marriage is then registered. A marriage certificate can be obtained afterwards. The Civil Registration Act 2004 (as amended) governs notice, solemnisers and registration.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.b] A marriage that breaks the legal requirements is not always treated the same way. The law separates marriages that were never valid from those that are valid until a court ends them.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.0] A void marriage was never valid in law, for example because one person was already married or in a civil partnership, they were within the prohibited degrees, one was under 18, or the formalities were not followed. A court decree is still sought to settle the status.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.1] A voidable marriage is valid until a court annuls it, for example because one spouse did not freely consent, or because one spouse is unable to sustain a normal marital relationship.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.2] A marriage that cannot be consummated (no sexual relations after the ceremony because of an inability to do so) can be annulled.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.3] Only the Circuit Court or High Court can grant a nullity decree, and an agreement between the couple cannot annul a marriage. The court process is called legal nullity.
