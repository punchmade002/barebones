TARGET hom-work-leisure-s4: 4. The Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act (1996)
[hom-work-leisure-s4.0.b] Young people are easily exploited at work and can lose out on school if they work too much. The Protection of Young Persons (Employment) Act 1996 sets limits so that work does not harm a young person's health, safety, education or development.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.0.0] The Act sets a minimum age for work, limits on working hours, rules on breaks and rest, and limits on night work, which stops employers exploiting young people and protects their schooling.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.0.1] The Act splits young workers into two age groups, because the rules differ for each: a child is under 16, and a young person is 16 or 17. Use the phrase 'under 18' only for the two groups together.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.0.2] The Act also sets out what employers must do before employing a young person, and what records they must keep.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.0.3] The Workplace Relations Commission (WRC) inspectors enforce the Act. Employers who break it commit an offence and can be prosecuted and fined.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.0.4] Employers are expected to know the Act's rules. The Act also applies in most cases to work done for family employers, with some limited exceptions.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.1.b] The younger the worker, the tighter the limits. The Act uses age to decide whether a person can work at all, and what kind of work is allowed.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.1.0] It is an offence to employ a child under 16 on a full-time basis, because full-time work would interfere with schooling.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.1.1] Children under 14 may not be employed at all, apart from licensed work in cultural, artistic, sporting or advertising settings, such as film, theatre, modelling or sport. These need authorisation from the relevant Minister, with conditions, in individual cases.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.1.2] Children of 14 may do light work during school holidays and approved work experience. Children of 15 may do this and also light work during the school term.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.1.3] Light work means work that is not harmful to health, safety or development and does not interfere with schooling.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.1.4] Employers must not give children work that is unsuitable or dangerous, because children are less able to judge risks.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.2.b] Limits on hours stop work from crowding out school and rest. The limits depend on age group and on whether it is school term or holiday time.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.2.0] During school holidays, 14- and 15-year-olds may do light work only, for a maximum of 7 hours in a day and 35 hours in a week.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.2.1] During the school term, only 15-year-olds may do light work, for a maximum of 8 hours a week. 14-year-olds cannot work during term, apart from approved work experience.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.2.2] Approved work experience as part of a school programme (at 14 or over), and approved training or work experience (at 15), allows up to 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.2.3] During the summer holidays, a child must have at least 21 days free from any work, so that they get a proper break.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.2.4] 16- and 17-year-olds may work up to 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.3.b] Young workers are still growing and studying, so the Act guarantees time to rest during the working day, between shifts and across the week.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.3.0] Children (under 16) must have at least 14 consecutive hours' rest in every 24 hours, and two days off in every seven, so that they can sleep and recover.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.3.1] Children must get a break of at least 30 minutes after 4 hours' work, so that they do not work for long stretches without rest.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.3.2] Young persons (16 and 17) must have at least 12 consecutive hours' rest in every 24 hours.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.3.3] Young persons must have two days off a week, consecutive where practicable, so that they get a proper rest.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.3.4] Young persons must get a 30-minute break after 4.5 hours' work. Breaks are unpaid.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.4.b] The Act bans work late at night and early in the morning, because tired young people cannot work or study safely.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.4.0] Children may not work between 8 p.m. and 8 a.m.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.4.1] Young persons (16 and 17) may not work between 10 p.m. and 6 a.m.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.4.2] A later window of 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. applies only in prescribed limited cases, and never before a school day during term.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.5.b] The Act puts responsibility on the employer to check the worker's age, get the right permission and keep proof that the rules were followed.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.5.0] Before employing anyone under 18, the employer must see a copy of their birth certificate or other satisfactory evidence of age, so that the right rules are applied.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.5.1] Before employing a child (under 16), the employer must have the written permission of a parent or guardian.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.5.2] The employer must keep a record (register) of each young person's name, date of birth, daily start and finish times and pay, and must keep it for at least three years.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.5.3] Employers may be inspected, and must give inspectors access to the records, so that inspectors can check the rules are being followed.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.5.4] Employers who break the Act can be prosecuted and fined.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.6.b] A part-time job brings benefits, but it also carries risks, and the Act aims to keep the balance in the young person's favour.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.6.0] Advantages of part-time work are income and experience, for example money of their own and skills for later jobs.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.6.1] Disadvantages are tiredness and an effect on study. The Act's hour, rest and night-work limits exist to reduce these risks.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.b] The Act is one of several laws that protect workers (five areas of wider employment protection). The Organisation of Working Time Act limits adults' hours, whereas the 1996 Act protects under-18s, so keep the two separate.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.0] Safety, Health and Welfare at Work Act 2005: employers must provide a safe workplace, assess risks, supply safety training and equipment, and have a safety statement. Workers have duties too, such as following safety rules, and the Health and Safety Authority (HSA) enforces it.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.1] National Minimum Wage Act 2000: sets a minimum hourly pay rate, so that no worker is paid below it. Reduced rates apply to young workers.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.2] Employment Equality Acts 1998–2015: prohibit discrimination in employment on nine grounds: gender, civil status, family status, age, disability, sexual orientation, race, religion and membership of the Traveller community.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.3] The Employment Equality Acts cover access to work, pay, conditions, training, promotion and dismissal, so discrimination is banned at every stage of a job.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.4] Organisation of Working Time Act 1997: limits weekly hours, sets rest breaks and daily and weekly rest, and gives paid annual leave and public holidays. It also has Sunday and night work provisions.
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.5] Other examples: the Unfair Dismissals Acts 1977–2015 (protection against unfair dismissal), the Maternity Protection Acts 1994 and 2004, the Parental Leave Act 1998, and the Terms of Employment (Information) Acts (written terms of employment).
[hom-work-leisure-s4.7.6] Disputes go to the Workplace Relations Commission. Trade unions also represent workers and negotiate pay and conditions.
