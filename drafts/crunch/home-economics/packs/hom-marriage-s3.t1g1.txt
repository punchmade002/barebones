TARGET hom-marriage-s3: 3. Preparing for marriage
[hom-marriage-s3.0.b] A wedding takes a day, but a marriage lasts years. Pre-marriage courses give engaged couples a structured chance to examine what married life involves before they commit.
[hom-marriage-s3.0.0] A pre-marriage course is run before the wedding and helps a couple understand what married life involves and prepares them for it.
[hom-marriage-s3.0.1] Courses explain the expectations and realities of marriage, so couples test their assumptions about married life before the wedding rather than discovering a mismatch afterwards.
[hom-marriage-s3.0.2] Accord, an Irish marriage and relationship support organisation founded by the Catholic Church, provides pre-marriage courses for couples in centres around Ireland. It is not a State body.
[hom-marriage-s3.0.3] Churches and other religious bodies also offer preparation for their own members, and some may require it before a religious wedding. The couple should check the requirements of their own religion.
[hom-marriage-s3.1.b] Courses cover a range of practical and personal topics, so couples can discuss them before the wedding.
[hom-marriage-s3.1.0] Topics include communication, conflict resolution (managing disagreements), relationships, family planning, child-rearing and parenting, financial responsibility and budgeting, and the legal requirements of marriage.
[hom-marriage-s3.1.1] Practical topics include buying or renting a home and managing the household budget as a married couple, so money and housing plans are agreed before they cause disputes.
[hom-marriage-s3.1.2] Couples discuss roles and expectations, such as who will do which household tasks and what each partner expects from the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s3.1.3] Couples examine their own personal qualities that help a marriage, such as honesty, openness, tolerance and commitment, and discuss issues that could harm the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s3.1.4] Courses prompt couples to discuss problems such as addiction (alcohol abuse, drugs and gambling), so they can recognise the warning signs and know where to seek help early.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.b] Exams ask how courses help, not only what they cover. Each benefit below follows from the topics: the course gives couples knowledge, skills and a safe place to talk.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.0] They explain the expectations and realities of marriage, so couples test their assumptions before committing.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.1] They encourage openness and honest communication between partners, so difficult subjects are raised before the wedding instead of after it.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.2] They give couples a forum to discuss shared expectations about roles, money, housing and having children, and to find out whether they agree.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.3] They give practical advice on money, housing, parenting and family planning, so the couple can plan ahead.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.4] They build the skills needed to resolve conflict before it damages the relationship, which reduces the risk of later breakdown, and they show couples where help is available.
[hom-marriage-s3.2.5] Meeting other engaged couples gives support and shows couples that others have the same questions.
[hom-marriage-s3.3.b] Pre-marriage courses are not the only help available. The State, schools, religious bodies and advice services all play a part in preparing people for marriage.
[hom-marriage-s3.3.0] The registrar of the area (the civil registration service) receives the written notice of intention to marry at least three months before the wedding, and the couple supply the required documents.
[hom-marriage-s3.3.1] The registrar also inspects proposed venues, so the ceremony location is approved, and registers the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s3.3.2] Religious bodies may offer preparation for couples before a religious wedding, and the couple should check the requirements of their own religion.
[hom-marriage-s3.3.3] Schools prepare young people for marriage: Home Economics classes examine the roles and responsibilities of partners in marriage and the laws relating to marriage, and SPHE classes discuss relationships and marriage.
[hom-marriage-s3.3.4] Information and advice services help with specific questions: Citizens Information, solicitors for legal questions such as property and wills, financial advice services such as MABS for budgeting, and the family's own support.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s1: 1. Definition of marriage
[hom-marriage-s1.0.b] Marriage is both a social institution, recognised by society, and a legal status, recognised and protected by the State. It is a voluntary commitment that is intended to be lasting.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.0] A marriage is a union of two people who freely agree to it, entered into through a civil or religious ceremony that is legally recognised. Because both must agree freely, nobody can be forced into it.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.1] Marriage is a union to the exclusion of all others, so a person may be married to only one person at a time. Marrying again while the first marriage is still valid is bigamy, which is a crime.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.2] Living together as a couple and remaining faithful to each other are part of what marriage involves, and a full definition includes both.
[hom-marriage-s1.0.3] Marriage creates legal rights and duties between the spouses (husband and wife), including inheritance rights and a duty of financial support for the spouse and children. This is why it is a legal status as well as a personal commitment.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.b] The State gives marriage special protection in the Constitution, and the law has changed over time about who can marry and how couples are recognised.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.0] Article 41 of the Constitution (Article 41.3 deals with marriage) pledges the State to guard the institution of marriage, on which the family is founded, with special care, and to protect it against attack.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.1] After the marriage equality referendum on 22 May 2015, the Constitution says marriage may be contracted in accordance with law by two persons without distinction as to their sex.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.2] The Marriage Act 2015 gave this effect, in force from 16 November 2015, so same-sex couples marry on the same legal terms as opposite-sex couples.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.3] Civil partnership, created by the Civil Partnership and Certain Rights and Obligations of Cohabitants Act 2010, gave same-sex couples rights and duties similar to marriage. It is a different legal status, not marriage.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.4] Civil partnership is no longer available to new couples, but existing civil partnerships remain valid. Civil partners who marry each other end their civil partnership.
[hom-marriage-s1.1.5] A marriage can be a civil ceremony (conducted by a registrar or a civil solemniser) or a religious ceremony (by a priest, minister or other person registered as a solemniser). A secular or humanist ceremony is possible with a registered solemniser. Each has the same legal effect once registered.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.b] The law sets conditions so that a marriage is entered into freely, by people who are able to marry, and is properly recorded. A marriage that fails these conditions may be void or voidable, and a court can declare it invalid through legal nullity.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.0] Age: both people must be at least 18. There is no exception for 16- and 17-year-olds, because the minimum age is 18 for everyone.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.1] Free to marry: neither person may already be married or in a civil partnership, because a person can be married to only one person at a time.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.2] Consent: both must consent freely, and consent obtained by force, threat or pressure is not valid. Both must also understand what they are doing, so a person who cannot understand the nature of marriage cannot validly marry (this is called capacity).
[hom-marriage-s1.2.3] Prohibited degrees: the couple may not be too closely related, by blood or, in some cases, by marriage or adoption.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.4] Notice: both must give notice of their intention to marry, in person, to a civil registration service (the registrar of the area) at least three months before the wedding. This applies to civil, religious and secular ceremonies.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.5] They bring the documents the registrar requires with the notice, for example identification and a declaration that there is no impediment (nothing in law to stop the marriage).
[hom-marriage-s1.2.6] Formalities: the ceremony is conducted by an authorised person, a registrar or a registered solemniser. The venue is one the registrar has approved (for a civil ceremony), inspected in advance, or a place suitable for the religion. Two witnesses must be present and the required declarations made.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.7] Place: the wedding must take place in a registered building, such as a register office or registered church, or in an approved venue inspected in advance by a registrar.
[hom-marriage-s1.2.8] Registration: the couple and the two witnesses sign the register at the ceremony, and the marriage is then registered. A marriage certificate can be obtained afterwards. The Civil Registration Act 2004 (as amended) governs notice, solemnisers and registration.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.b] A marriage that breaks the legal requirements is not always treated the same way. The law separates marriages that were never valid from those that are valid until a court ends them.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.0] A void marriage was never valid in law, for example because one person was already married or in a civil partnership, they were within the prohibited degrees, one was under 18, or the formalities were not followed. A court decree is still sought to settle the status.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.1] A voidable marriage is valid until a court annuls it, for example because one spouse did not freely consent, or because one spouse is unable to sustain a normal marital relationship.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.2] A marriage that cannot be consummated (no sexual relations after the ceremony because of an inability to do so) can be annulled.
[hom-marriage-s1.3.3] Only the Circuit Court or High Court can grant a nullity decree, and an agreement between the couple cannot annul a marriage. The court process is called legal nullity.

CANDIDATES hom-marriage-s2: 2. Cultural variations and the marriage relationship
[hom-marriage-s2.0.b] The arrangements around marriage are not the same everywhere. They differ in who may marry, who chooses the partner and how many partners a person may have.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.0] The minimum age for marriage varies between countries and cultures; in Ireland both partners must be at least 18.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.1] In an arranged marriage, parents or families choose the spouse on the basis of background and suitability, usually with the couple's consent. This contrasts with a marriage freely chosen by the couple themselves.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.2] Arranged marriage is traditional in some communities, for example Sikh, Japanese and Travelling communities, but not for every member of those communities.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.3] Some cultures restrict the choice of spouse by religion, social class or kinship (family relationship), meaning a person may not marry someone from a particular group.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.4] Monogamy, meaning one husband and one wife at a time, is the norm in Western countries, including Ireland, so one marriage at a time is also what the law there recognises.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.5] Polygamy, meaning more than one spouse at a time, is the general term and is not permitted in Ireland. Marrying again without the death or divorce of the first spouse is bigamy, which is a criminal offence.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.6] Polygamy has two forms: polygyny, where one man has more than one wife, and polyandry, where one woman has more than one husband.
[hom-marriage-s2.0.7] Same-sex marriage is legal in some countries, including Ireland since the Marriage Act 2015, and not recognised in others.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.b] The same legal marriage can be celebrated in different ways, depending on the couple's beliefs. The choice of ceremony is one more way marriage varies between people and cultures.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.0] Marriage may be a civil ceremony before a registrar or a religious ceremony; both are legally valid when the marriage is registered.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.1] Some couples choose a secular or humanist ceremony, which has no religious content.
[hom-marriage-s2.1.2] Interfaith marriages join partners of different religions, so the couple must agree how the ceremony and their family's religious life will be arranged, for example which traditions the children will follow.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.b] Weddings are full of customs that carry meaning for the people who follow them. These customs differ by culture and religion, in dress, symbols, venue and the form of the ceremony.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.0] Dress and symbols such as a veil and a wedding ring, and throwing rice or confetti, are wedding customs that have been popular for centuries in many cultures.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.1] Jewish weddings are held under a canopy, which symbolises the couple's future home.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.2] At the end of a Jewish wedding, a glass is broken under foot to recall the destruction of the ancient Temple in Jerusalem.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.3] Quaker couples marry at a public gathering with no priest or official present, because Quakers believe God alone joins a couple as husband and wife.
[hom-marriage-s2.2.4] The ceremony can take place in a church, mosque, synagogue, registry office or other approved location, depending on the couple's religion and the law of the country.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.b] Marriage is more than a private promise. It is a public commitment that carries legal obligations, and couples keep it going through how they live day to day.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.0] Spouses make a public commitment to each other, usually through vows or declarations before witnesses during the ceremony, sealed by registration of the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.1] Because marriage carries legal obligations, spouses owe each other the rights and responsibilities below, which the law can enforce.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.2] Commitment is maintained, so that the marriage lasts, by working on the relationship, communicating positively and remaining faithful and loyal.
[hom-marriage-s2.3.3] Commitment is also shown through sharing responsibility for the home, money and children, for example through shared finances and a shared home.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.b] A right is something each spouse is entitled to receive from the other because they are married. These rights are the other side of the responsibilities each partner takes on.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.0] Each other's company: spouses are entitled to live together as a couple.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.1] Sexual relations: each spouse is entitled to sexual relations with the other, and consummation is the basis of the marriage. An unconsummated marriage can be annulled (declared never to have been valid) in some circumstances.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.2] Faithfulness: each spouse is entitled to the other's loyalty and faithfulness within a monogamous marriage, because the marriage is a promise to one partner only.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.3] Inheritance: under the Succession Act 1965, a spouse who dies without a will (intestate) leaves the whole estate to the surviving spouse if there are no children.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.4] If there are children and no will, the surviving spouse takes two-thirds of the estate and the children share one-third.
[hom-marriage-s2.4.5] Legal right share: a surviving spouse can claim a share of the estate even where a will leaves them less, so a will cannot cut them out. The share is one half if there are no children and one third if there are children.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.b] Marriage also places duties on each partner, towards each other and towards their children. Some of these are backed by law and some depend on commitment.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.0] Financial support: each spouse must support the other (a dependent spouse) and any dependent children. The Family Law (Maintenance of Spouses and Children) Act 1976 allows a spouse to seek maintenance through the court.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.1] Joint guardianship: married parents are both guardians of their children and share responsibility for their care and upbringing, under the Guardianship of Infants Act 1964 as amended by the Children and Family Relationships Act 2015.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.2] Children's needs: parents must provide for their children's physical, emotional, social, moral, educational and spiritual needs.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.3] Commitment: spouses must work on the relationship, show loyalty and keep their commitment to the marriage.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.4] Positive communication: spouses must talk openly about decisions, finances and family matters to keep the marriage functioning.
[hom-marriage-s2.5.5] Caring for a sick spouse is also a responsibility, since marriage means looking after your partner when they are ill.
