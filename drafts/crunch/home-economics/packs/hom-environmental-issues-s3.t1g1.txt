MODE: cost and links
TARGET hom-environmental-issues-s3: 3. Pollution
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.0.b] Pollution is the release of substances or energy into the environment at a rate that harms living things or damages natural processes. It is usually grouped by where the harm happens: in the air, in water or through sound.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.0.0] Air pollution: gases and particles released into the atmosphere, mainly from burning fossil fuels, industry, traffic and domestic fires.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.0.1] Water pollution: harmful substances entering rivers, lakes, groundwater or the sea, from sewage, farming, industry and households.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.0.2] Noise pollution: unwanted or excessive sound from traffic, construction, industry and household equipment.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.0.3] Litter and land pollution: discarded waste on streets, in the countryside and in water, which harms wildlife and blocks drains.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.b] Most air pollution comes from burning something, whether fuel in a power station, a car engine or a home fire. Each pollutant comes from a particular kind of burning, so it helps to link each gas to its source.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.0] Carbon dioxide and sulphur dioxide are released when fossil fuels such as coal, oil, peat and gas are burned for electricity, transport and home heating; sulphur dioxide comes especially from coal, oil and peat.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.1] Nitrogen oxides are produced by vehicle engines and by any high-temperature combustion, because the heat makes nitrogen and oxygen in the air combine.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.2] Carbon monoxide is produced by incomplete combustion (burning without enough oxygen), for example in poorly maintained or faulty boilers, stoves and vehicle exhausts.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.3] Smoke and particulates (tiny solid specks in the air) come from solid fuels such as coal, wood and smoky turf burned in open fires and stoves, and from industry. Smoke and fog together form smog.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.4] CFCs (chlorofluorocarbons) released from old fridge refrigerants and foams, and from older aerosol sprays, destroy ozone in the upper atmosphere.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.5] Methane, a greenhouse gas, is released from landfill and from farm animals.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.1.6] Overuse of chemical sprays and cleaning products, and burning rubbish, add further pollutants to the air.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.b] Air pollutants cause three separate global problems, plus harm to health. Keep each cause with its own effect, because examiners mark the link between them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.0] Global warming and climate change: carbon dioxide, methane and other greenhouse gases trap heat in the atmosphere, raising average temperatures and sea levels and changing weather patterns and crops.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.1] Acid rain: sulphur dioxide and nitrogen oxides dissolve in rainwater to form acids, which lower the pH of soil and water, harm plants and fish, dissolve limestone and corrode metal and stone buildings.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.2] Ozone depletion: CFCs break down ozone in the stratosphere (the upper atmosphere), so more harmful ultraviolet radiation reaches the earth's surface.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.3] Health: particulates and smoke cause respiratory illness, aggravate asthma and place extra strain on the heart.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.4] Carbon monoxide is a poison that stops the blood carrying oxygen. It has no smell, which is why carbon monoxide detectors are used in homes.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.2.5] Smog and smoke reduce air quality and visibility.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.b] Water pollution happens when waste from homes, farms and industry reaches rivers, lakes and the sea, often through drains and run-off (rainwater washing material off land).
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.0] Untreated or poorly treated sewage is discharged into rivers and the sea.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.1] Fertiliser and slurry (liquid animal waste) are washed off farmland into waterways after heavy rain; together with sewage, these are the main sources of nutrients.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.2] Industrial effluent (liquid waste), chemical waste and pesticides are dumped or washed into water.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.3] Oil leaking from vehicles and heating oil tanks runs off into drains and streams.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.4] Household chemicals, paint and oil poured down drains also reach waterways.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.3.5] Phosphates in washing powders and detergents once entered waterways through household drains. EU rules now limit phosphorus in these detergents, so they are no longer the main source.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.4.b] The best-known effect is eutrophication, where too many nutrients set off a chain of events that ends with a lake or river short of oxygen. Other pollutants harm wildlife and people directly.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.4.0] Eutrophication: excess nitrates and phosphates feed rapid growth of algae, which forms an algal bloom on the water surface.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.4.1] The bloom blocks light, so submerged plants die; when the algae die, bacteria decompose them and use up dissolved oxygen.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.4.2] Low oxygen levels kill fish and other aquatic life, leaving lakes and rivers depleted.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.4.3] Toxic substances can accumulate up the food chain, because each animal eats many contaminated ones, affecting fish and birds that feed on contaminated organisms.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.4.4] Chemical contamination of drinking water sources threatens human health and can leave water unfit to drink.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.5.b] Noise is sound that is unwanted or too loud. Loud or constant noise damages health as well as comfort.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.5.0] Main sources: road and air traffic, construction and roadworks, industry, lawnmowers, music systems, televisions and alarms.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.5.1] Noise is measured in decibels (dB), a logarithmic scale, so an increase of 10 dB represents roughly a tenfold increase in sound intensity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.5.2] Effects of persistent noise: stress, tension, headaches, disturbed sleep and insomnia.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.5.3] Long-term exposure to loud sound causes permanent hearing damage.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.5.4] Noise reduces concentration in schools and workplaces.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.b] Individual choices add up. Consumers can cut air, water and noise pollution at home, on the road and when shopping.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.0] Air: walk, cycle, use public transport or share car journeys to reduce vehicle emissions.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.1] Air: drive an electric, hybrid or low-emission vehicle, and keep tyres correctly inflated and the engine serviced to reduce fuel use and emissions.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.2] Air: use smokeless fuels or renewable heat, such as heat pumps or solar, instead of smoky solid fuels, and do not burn rubbish.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.3] Air: choose energy-efficient appliances so that less fossil fuel is burned to generate the electricity they use. Dispose of old fridges and freezers properly so that CFCs are not released.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.4] Water: never pour paint, oil or chemicals down drains, use fertilisers and chemicals sparingly, and compost garden waste rather than burning it. Keep boilers and oil tanks maintained so they do not leak.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.5] Noise: keep music and television volumes down, avoid noisy garden work at unsocial hours, maintain equipment, and use double glazing and sound insulation.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.6.6] General: reduce, reuse and recycle, buy only what is needed, choose biodegradable or second-hand products, and avoid single-use packaging.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.b] Individuals cannot solve pollution alone, so the State uses laws, a regulator and charges that make polluting more expensive.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.0] The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), set up under the Environmental Protection Agency Act 1992, licenses large industrial and waste activities, monitors air and water quality and enforces environmental law.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.1] The Waste Management Act 1996 (as amended) controls waste collection, movement and disposal, and gives local authorities and the EPA roles in promoting reduction, recycling and reuse.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.2] Solid fuel regulations: the Air Pollution Act 1987 (Solid Fuels) Regulations 2022 ban the sale of smoky (bituminous) coal across the State from 31 October 2022 and restrict the retail sale of turf and of wet wood, to cut smoke and particulates from home heating. Local authorities enforce them.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.3] Levies and taxes make polluting choices dearer: the landfill levy (a charge per tonne of waste sent to landfill, encouraging recycling and recovery), the plastic bag levy (since 2002) and a carbon tax on fossil fuels (introduced in 2010), which encourages energy efficiency and renewable alternatives.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.4] Other clean-green schemes: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle; Green Dot (Repak); WEEE take-back; EU Ecolabel; and, since 2024, the Re-turn deposit return scheme.
[hom-environmental-issues-s3.7.5] Support for renewable energy and home energy upgrades, with grants through the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland (SEAI), reduces the fossil fuel burned.

CANDIDATES hom-environmental-issues-s1: 1. Renewable and non-renewable resources
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.b] A natural resource is a material or energy source taken from the environment, such as fuels, water, minerals, timber, wind and sunlight. Because some resources run out and others do not, what we use and how we use it matters for the future.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.0] Sustainable use means meeting present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs, so resources are still there for the people who come after us.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.1] Resources are classed by how quickly they are replaced: renewable resources are replenished naturally, while non-renewable resources are finite (limited in supply).
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.2] Consumers influence resource use through what they buy, how long they keep products in use and how they dispose of them, so everyday choices add up.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.0.3] Consumers conserve resources by buying only what is needed, choosing durable and repairable goods, and using items fully before replacing them, which means fewer new materials and less energy are used.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.b] A renewable resource is naturally replenished and is not used up by constant use, provided it is used no faster than it is replaced. Each renewable energy source works by turning a natural movement or heat into electricity or heat for buildings.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.0] Solar energy: photovoltaic (PV) panels convert sunlight directly into electricity, and solar thermal panels use sunlight to heat water. Passive solar design, which sites the house and its glazing towards the sun, uses sunlight for heat and light.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.1] Wind energy: turbines convert the kinetic energy (movement energy) of moving air into electricity; wind farms are usually sited on exposed upland and coastal land, or at sea, where the wind is strongest.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.2] Hydroelectric energy: falling or flowing water turns turbines, so the source depends on a steady flow of water and a difference in height. The Shannon Scheme at Ardnacrusha, opened in 1929, is an Irish example.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.3] Biomass: wood, straw, energy crops and plant or animal waste are burned, or converted to biogas, to release heat or electricity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.4] Geothermal energy: heat stored in the earth's crust is brought to the surface as hot water or steam to heat buildings or generate electricity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.5] Tidal energy uses the rise and fall of the tides to drive turbines; wave energy uses the motion of waves to drive devices.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.6] Renewable heat in the home comes from heat pumps (which take low-grade heat from the air, ground or water), solar thermal panels, and stoves burning wood from managed forests. Heat pumps count as renewable heat because most of their heat comes from air, ground or water.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.1.7] Hydrogen can also carry energy, but it is renewable only if it is made using renewable electricity.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.b] Renewable energy is attractive because it does not run out and is cleaner to produce, but it is not free of drawbacks. Exam answers need both sides.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.0] Renewable sources are sustainable: their supply does not run out on human timescales, so a well sited and maintained system can last indefinitely.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.1] Most produce little or no air pollution or carbon dioxide while generating energy, which reduces damage to air and water.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.2] Using renewables reduces dependence on imported fuel, which improves energy security (a dependable supply that does not rely on other countries).
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.3] Some renewables can be installed in a home, such as solar panels on a roof or a small wind turbine, so energy is produced where it is used.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.4] Building, maintaining and manufacturing renewable systems creates employment, and running costs are low once the system is installed.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.5] Limitation: wind and solar output varies with weather and daylight, so storage or a backup supply is needed.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.6] Limitation: installation costs can be high, and hydro and tidal systems need suitable sites.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.2.7] Limitation: wind farms and dams can affect the landscape and habitats.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.b] A non-renewable resource is limited in supply and cannot be replaced within a human lifetime once it is used up. Fuels and minerals dug out of the ground fall into this group.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.0] Fossil fuels are coal, oil, natural gas and peat, formed from the remains of plants and animals over very long periods: peat over thousands of years, coal, oil and gas over millions. Coke and anthracite (a type of coal) are other fuels in this group.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.1] Peat (turf) is cut from bogs for home heating and electricity generation. Bogs regrow far too slowly to count as renewable, so peat is a non-renewable fossil fuel, and bog habitats do not regenerate quickly.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.2] Nuclear energy uses uranium, a mined, finite fuel, so it is non-renewable. Ireland has no nuclear power station.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.3] Iron ore, copper ore and bauxite (the ore of aluminium) are non-renewable mineral resources because they are mined and finite; recycling extends the life of the metal they yield.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.4] For one non-renewable source used in the home, give the source and an advantage: fossil fuels are concentrated, give a lot of heat, and have an established supply and appliances.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.3.5] The disadvantages are that the fuel is finite, its price rises as reserves fall, burning it causes emissions and extracting it damages the land. For these reasons it is not sustainable.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.b] Using non-renewable resources harms the environment twice: when they are extracted and when they are burned. As they become scarce, the supply itself also becomes a problem.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.0] Burning fossil fuels releases carbon dioxide, the main greenhouse gas (a gas that traps heat in the atmosphere), which contributes to global warming and climate change.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.1] Burning coal, oil and peat releases sulphur dioxide, which forms acid rain.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.2] Mining, quarrying, drilling and peat extraction destroy habitats and landscapes, so the wildlife that lived there loses its home.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.3] Cutting bogs releases stored carbon, which adds to the carbon dioxide in the air.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.4.4] As reserves fall, fuel becomes harder and more costly to extract, so supplies become less secure.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.b] Consumers are responsible for how they use resources because their daily choices decide how much fuel is burned and how much material is thrown away. Practical steps cut demand for non-renewable resources and support renewable ones.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.0] Reduce energy demand, for example by insulating the home, using appliances efficiently and switching off standby, so less fossil fuel is burned.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.1] Choose a renewable electricity supplier, or install a renewable system such as solar panels or a heat pump, where practical. Smokeless or renewable fuel is also a better choice.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.2] Buy wood and paper products certified by a recognised scheme such as FSC (Forest Stewardship Council) or PEFC, which confirms that the timber came from managed forests.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.3] Reuse and recycle metals, glass and paper so that finite materials are used again.
[hom-environmental-issues-s1.5.4] Choose products made with recycled content, which means less new material has to be mined or felled.
